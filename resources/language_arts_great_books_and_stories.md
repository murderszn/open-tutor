### 🏺 Greek & Roman Mythology
**Why it matters:** Greek and Roman myths are the foundation of Western literature. Allusions to these stories appear everywhere — in books, movies, brand names, and everyday language.

| Story / Figure | Summary | Why You'll See It Again |
|----------------|---------|------------------------|
| **The Odyssey** (Homer, ~800 BC) | Odysseus (Ulysses) takes 10 years to sail home after the Trojan War, facing monsters, gods, and temptation. | The "epic journey" story template. The word *odyssey* now means any long, difficult journey. |
| **The Iliad** (Homer, ~800 BC) | The 10-year Trojan War — sparked by Helen's abduction — featuring Achilles, Hector, and the famous Trojan Horse. | Source of phrases like *"Achilles' heel"* and the concept of the epic hero. |
| **Prometheus** | Titan who stole fire from the gods and gave it to humans. Punished forever. | Symbol of human progress and the danger of defying authority. |
| **Icarus** | Boy given wax wings, flew too close to the sun, fell into the sea. | Symbol of overconfidence and ignoring good advice. |
| **King Midas** | King who was granted the wish that everything he touched turned to gold — including his food and daughter. | Symbol of greed and unintended consequences. |
| **Pandora's Box** | Woman given a box she was told never to open. She opened it, releasing all the world's evils. Hope alone remained. | Symbol of curiosity, temptation, and hope surviving disaster. |
| **Medusa** | A Gorgon whose gaze turned people to stone. Slain by Perseus using a mirrored shield. | Symbol of facing your fears indirectly; appears in countless stories. |
| **Hercules (Heracles)** | The strongest man alive, completed 12 impossible labors as penance. | The archetype of physical strength and enduring hardship. |
| **Orpheus** | Musician so talented he descended into the Underworld to rescue his wife Eurydice. He looked back and lost her. | Symbol of love, art, and the danger of doubt at the last moment. |

**Key Gods to Know:**
| God | Domain |
|-----|--------|
| Zeus (Jupiter) | King of the gods, sky, thunder |
| Hera (Juno) | Marriage, jealousy |
| Poseidon (Neptune) | Sea, earthquakes |

| Hermes (Mercury) | Messengers, travel, trickery |

### 📜 Aesop's Fables (~600 BC)
Short stories with animals as characters, each ending with a moral lesson. Every educated person knows these.

| Fable | Moral |
|-------|-------|
| **The Tortoise and the Hare** | Slow and steady wins the race. |
| **The Boy Who Cried Wolf** | Liars aren't believed even when they tell the truth. |
| **The Ant and the Grasshopper** | Prepare today; don't waste time. |
| **The Fox and the Grapes** | People often pretend not to want what they cannot have. ("Sour grapes.") |
| **The Lion and the Mouse** | Even the smallest person can help the greatest. |
| **The Crow and the Pitcher** | Intelligence and resourcefulness overcome obstacles. |

## Part 2: Shakespeare

William Shakespeare (1564–1616) was an English playwright and poet. He is considered the greatest writer in the English language. His 37 plays and 154 sonnets shaped literature for 400 years and counting.

**Key facts:**
- Wrote in **Early Modern English** (sounds older, but becomes readable with practice)
- His plays are divided into: **Tragedies**, **Comedies**, and **Histories**
- Invented or popularized over **1,700 English words**: bedroom, lonely, generous, swagger, eyeball, bedroom, manager, and many more
- Performed at the **Globe Theatre** in London

---

### 🗡️ Major Tragedies

| Play | One-Sentence Summary | Famous Quotes |
|------|---------------------|---------------|
| **Romeo and Juliet** | Two teenagers from feuding families fall in love and die tragically. | *"What's in a name? That which we call a rose by any other name would smell as sweet."* |
| **Hamlet** | A Danish prince struggles to avenge his murdered father while doubting himself. | *"To be, or not to be, that is the question."* |
| **Macbeth** | A Scottish general, urged by his wife and three witches, murders the king and descends into madness. | *"Fair is foul, and foul is fair."* |
| **Othello** | A noble general is manipulated by jealousy and deceived into murdering his innocent wife. | *"O, beware, my lord, of jealousy! It is the green-eyed monster."* |
| **King Lear** | An aging king divides his kingdom among his daughters with disastrous results. | *"How sharper than a serpent's tooth it is to have a thankless child."* |

---

### 😄 Major Comedies

| Play | Summary |
|------|---------|
| **A Midsummer Night's Dream** | Lovers, fairies, and actors get tangled in magical mischief in an enchanted forest. |
| **Much Ado About Nothing** | Two sets of lovers — one that hates each other, one that is tricked into loving each other. |
| **The Merchant of Venice** | A merchant's bond with a moneylender goes wrong; themes of mercy, justice, and prejudice. |
| **Twelfth Night** | A girl disguises herself as a boy, creating a chain of mistaken identities and love triangles. |

---

### 📜 Shakespeare's Sonnets
Shakespeare wrote 154 sonnets — 14-line poems following a specific rhyme scheme (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG).

- **Sonnet 18:** *"Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? Thou art more lovely and more temperate."*
- **Sonnet 116:** *"Let me not to the marriage of true minds admit impediments."* (about true love)

## Part 3: Classic American Literature

---

| Title & Author | Year | Summary | Themes |
|---------------|------|---------|--------|
| **The Adventures of Tom Sawyer** — Mark Twain | 1876 | A mischievous boy's adventures along the Mississippi River. | Childhood, freedom, morality |
| **Adventures of Huckleberry Finn** — Mark Twain | 1884 | A boy and an escaped slave raft down the Mississippi, questioning society's rules. | Freedom, race, morality, society |
| **The Call of the Wild** — Jack London | 1903 | A domesticated dog is taken to Alaska and rediscovers his wild instincts. | Nature vs. civilization, survival, identity |
| **To Kill a Mockingbird** — Harper Lee | 1960 | A young girl in the 1930s South watches her father defend a Black man unjustly accused of a crime. | Racial injustice, courage, empathy, growing up |
| **The Great Gatsby** — F. Scott Fitzgerald | 1925 | A mysterious millionaire pursues a lost love in the Roaring Twenties, revealing the emptiness of the American Dream. | The American Dream, class, illusion, love |
| **Of Mice and Men** — John Steinbeck | 1937 | Two migrant workers in the Great Depression dream of owning a farm; it ends in tragedy. | Friendship, dreams, loneliness, mercy |
| **The Old Man and the Sea** — Ernest Hemingway | 1952 | An aging fisherman battles a giant marlin alone at sea. | Perseverance, dignity, man vs. nature |
| **The Outsiders** — S.E. Hinton | 1967 | Two rival gangs — the Greasers and the Socs — clash, and a teenager learns about loyalty and class. | Class, identity, loyalty, growing up |

## Part 4: Classic British & World Literature

| Title & Author | Year | Summary | Themes |
|---------------|------|---------|--------|
| **Oliver Twist** — Charles Dickens | 1837 | An orphan boy navigates crime and poverty in Victorian London. | Poverty, injustice, identity, good vs. evil |
| **A Tale of Two Cities** — Charles Dickens | 1859 | Set during the French Revolution; *"It was the best of times, it was the worst of times."* | Sacrifice, revolution, resurrection, duality |
| **Pride and Prejudice** — Jane Austen | 1813 | Elizabeth Bennet navigates society, love, and marriage while refusing to be treated as less than she is. | Pride, social class, independence, love |
| **Jane Eyre** — Charlotte Brontë | 1847 | An orphaned girl becomes a governess and falls in love with a brooding, secretive man. | Independence, morality, love, class |
| **Treasure Island** — Robert Louis Stevenson | 1883 | A young boy, a treasure map, and a cast of pirates including the legendary Long John Silver. | Adventure, greed, loyalty, good vs. evil |
| **Animal Farm** — George Orwell | 1945 | Farm animals overthrow their farmer — but the pigs slowly become as corrupt as the humans. | Power, corruption, revolution, propaganda |
| **1984** — George Orwell | 1949 | A dystopian world where "Big Brother" watches everyone and even truth is controlled by the government. | Totalitarianism, freedom, truth, surveillance |
| **Lord of the Flies** — William Golding | 1954 | Boys stranded on an island without adults descend from civilization into savagery. | Human nature, order vs. chaos, power |

## Part 5: Famous Poems Every Student Should Know

| Poem | Poet | What It's About |
|------|------|-----------------|
| **"The Road Not Taken"** | Robert Frost | Choosing one path over another in life; often misread as celebrating nonconformity. |
| **"O Captain! My Captain!"** | Walt Whitman | Written after Lincoln's assassination; mourning a great leader lost. |
| **"Still I Rise"** | Maya Angelou | Defiance, dignity, and triumph over oppression. |
| **"Harlem (A Dream Deferred)"** | Langston Hughes | What happens to dreams that are put off — do they wither, or explode? |
| **"I Heard a Fly Buzz — When I Died"** | Emily Dickinson | Death seen from the dying person's perspective, oddly focused on a buzzing fly. |
| **"The Raven"** | Edgar Allan Poe | A man is haunted by a raven that only says "Nevermore" after the death of his beloved. |
| **"If—"** | Rudyard Kipling | Advice from a father to a son on what it means to be a person of integrity. |
| **"Invictus"** | William Ernest Henley | A poem of unconquerable will written while the author was gravely ill. |
| **"Nothing Gold Can Stay"** | Robert Frost | Beauty and innocence are fleeting — everything perfect fades. (Quoted in *The Outsiders.*) |

## Part 7: Essential Literary Movements to Know

| Movement | Era | Key Idea | Example Authors |
|----------|-----|----------|-----------------|
| **Classical** | Ancient Greece/Rome | Order, reason, beauty, heroism | Homer, Virgil, Sophocles |
| **Renaissance** | 1400s–1600s | Humanism, individual power, beauty | Shakespeare, Milton |
| **Romanticism** | 1780s–1850s | Emotion, nature, imagination over reason | Wordsworth, Keats, Poe |
| **Realism** | 1850s–1900s | Depicting life as it truly is | Twain, Dickens, Tolstoy |
| **Modernism** | 1900s–1940s | Breaking old forms, stream of consciousness | Hemingway, Fitzgerald, Woolf |
| **Harlem Renaissance** | 1920s–1930s | Black American art, culture, and identity in the North | Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston |
| **Postmodernism** | 1960s–present | Questioning truth, playful with structure | Vonnegut, Toni Morrison |
