## 🗂️ Vocabulary (kid-friendly)

- **Macronutrient:** A nutrient the body needs in large amounts for energy and building — the Big Three are carbohydrates, proteins, and fats.
- **Micronutrient:** Vitamins and minerals the body needs in small amounts (not covered in depth here, but found on every nutrition label).
- **Carbohydrate:** The body's main fuel source. Broken down into glucose — simple sugar the cells burn for energy. Found in bread, rice, fruit, pasta, sugary foods.
- **Protein:** The body's builder. Made of chains of amino acids. Repairs muscle, builds new cells, powers the immune system. Found in meat, eggs, beans, dairy, nuts.
- **Fat:** Long-term energy storage, brain health, insulation. Also helps absorb vitamins A, D, E, and K (fat-soluble vitamins). Found in oils, butter, nuts, avocado, meat.
- **Calorie:** A unit measuring how much energy a food provides. (Technically a kilocalorie — the number on the label.) Carbs and protein have 4 calories per gram; fat has 9.
- **Serving size:** The amount the nutrition facts are based on — *not* necessarily the whole package.
- **% Daily Value (%DV):** What percentage of the recommended daily amount of a nutrient is in one serving. Based on a 2,000-calorie diet. 5% or less = low; 20% or more = high.
- **Digestion:** The process of breaking food into smaller molecules the body can absorb and use.
- **Absorption:** When nutrients pass through the wall of the small intestine into the bloodstream.
- **Metabolism:** All the chemical reactions in the body that turn food into energy and building materials.
- **Glucose:** The simple sugar that cells use as fuel in cellular respiration.
- **Amino acids:** The 20 building blocks of protein. 9 are "essential" — you must get them from food because your body can't make them.
- **Saturated fat:** Solid at room temperature (butter, coconut oil). Limit these in the diet.
- **Unsaturated fat:** Liquid at room temperature (olive oil, avocado). Healthier fats.
- **Fiber:** A type of carbohydrate the body can't fully digest — but it feeds good gut bacteria and helps digestion. Found in vegetables, fruits, whole grains.
- **Sodium:** Salt. Found in almost every processed food. Too much raises blood pressure.

## 🧬 The Three Macronutrients — Deep Dive

### 🍞 Carbohydrates

**What they are:** Long chains of sugar molecules. Simple carbs (fruit, candy, white bread) break down fast — quick energy spike. Complex carbs (oats, brown rice, sweet potato) break down slower — steadier energy.

**What the body does with them:**
1. Chewing + saliva starts breaking starch into sugar in the mouth.
2. The stomach and small intestine finish the job — breaking carbs down to glucose.
3. Glucose enters the bloodstream. The pancreas releases insulin to help cells absorb it.
4. Cells use glucose in **cellular respiration** to make ATP — the body's actual energy currency.
5. Extra glucose gets stored in the liver and muscles as glycogen. Overflow is stored as fat.

**Why they matter:** Every cell in the body — especially brain and muscle cells — runs on glucose. No carbs = brain fog and low energy.

---

### 🥩 Proteins

**What they are:** Long chains of amino acids folded into complex shapes. There are 20 amino acids total; 9 must come from food (essential amino acids).

**What the body does with them:**
1. Stomach acid and enzymes (pepsin) break proteins into shorter chains called peptides.
2. The small intestine finishes breaking peptides into individual amino acids.
3. Amino acids are absorbed into the bloodstream and carried to cells throughout the body.
4. Cells reassemble amino acids into new proteins — muscles, enzymes, antibodies, hormones, and more.

**Why they matter:** Proteins aren't just for muscle. They're also enzymes (speed up chemical reactions), hormones (insulin, growth hormone), and antibodies (immune defense). Almost everything in a cell is made of protein.

---

### 🥑 Fats

**What they are:** Molecules made of glycerol + fatty acid chains. Types: saturated (solid at room temp, limit these), unsaturated (liquid, heart-healthy), trans fats (mostly artificial, avoid).

**What the body does with them:**
1. Bile (made in the liver, stored in the gallbladder) breaks fat into tiny droplets — a process called emulsification.
2. Enzymes called lipases break the droplets down into fatty acids and glycerol.
3. Absorbed through the small intestine's lining into the lymphatic system, then the bloodstream.
4. Used for: building cell membranes, making hormones, storing energy, insulating nerves.

**Why they matter:** The brain is about 60% fat by dry weight. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) can only be absorbed when fat is present in the meal.

## 🧾 Reading a Nutrition Label

Every packaged food in the U.S. has a Nutrition Facts label. Here's how to read it:

```
┌──────────────────────────────┐
│  Nutrition Facts             │
│  Serving size  1 cup (240g)  │  ← ALWAYS check this first
│  Servings per container  2   │  ← The bag has 2 servings!
│                              │
│  Calories         230        │  ← Per serving
│                    % DV*     │
│  Total Fat     8g   10%      │
│   Saturated Fat 1g   5%      │
│   Trans Fat    0g            │
│  Cholesterol  0mg    0%      │
│  Sodium     160mg    7%      │
│  Total Carbs  37g   13%      │
│   Fiber        4g   14%      │
│   Total Sugars 1g            │
│  Protein       3g            │
│                              │
│  Vitamin D    10%            │
│  Calcium       8%            │
│  Iron         45%            │
│  Potassium     6%            │
│                              │
│  *% Daily Value based on     │
│   2,000-calorie diet         │
└──────────────────────────────┘
```

**Key moves when reading a label:**
1. **Check serving size first.** If a bag says 230 calories but has 2.5 servings, you're eating 575 calories if you eat the whole bag.
2. **Look at % DV.** 5% or less = low; 20% or more = high. Aim high on fiber, vitamins, minerals. Aim low on saturated fat, sodium, added sugar.
3. **Find the Big Three.** Where are the calories coming from — fat, carbs, or protein? Use the grams × calories formula:
   - Fat: g × 9 = calories from fat
   - Carbs: g × 4 = calories from carbs
   - Protein: g × 4 = calories from protein

## 🔥 The Digestive Journey (Step by Step)

| Stop | Organ | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Mouth** | Chewing breaks food into smaller pieces; saliva starts digesting starch |
| 2 | **Esophagus** | Muscular tube pushes food to stomach via peristalsis (wave contractions) |
| 3 | **Stomach** | Acid (pH ~2) and enzymes break down protein; churning makes chyme |
| 4 | **Small intestine** | ~20 feet long; most digestion and ALL nutrient absorption happens here; villi + microvilli massively increase surface area |
| 5 | **Liver / Gallbladder** | Liver makes bile; gallbladder stores + releases it to emulsify fats |
| 6 | **Pancreas** | Releases enzymes (amylase, lipase, protease) and bicarbonate into small intestine |
| 7 | **Large intestine** | Absorbs water; gut bacteria process remaining fiber; forms waste |
| 8 | **Rectum / Anus** | Stores and eliminates waste |

**Key vocabulary:** Peristalsis · Chyme · Villi · Microvilli · Emulsification · Bile · Enzyme · Absorption

## 🍳 Cooking Changes Food (Science)

Cooking is applied chemistry and biology. Here's what actually happens:

| What you observe | The science |
|---|---|
| Egg white turns solid when cooked | **Protein denaturation** — heat unfolds and re-bonds protein chains into a solid network |
| Bread crust turns brown | **Maillard reaction** — amino acids + sugars react under high heat to create hundreds of new flavor compounds |
| Pasta softens in water | **Starch gelatinization** — water molecules penetrate and expand starch granules, softening the texture |
| Vegetables turn bright green then dull when overcooked | Chlorophyll is released and then broken down by heat + acid |
| Baking soda makes things rise | Baking soda (a base) reacts with acidic ingredients to produce CO₂ gas — same gas in your breath |
| Vinegar + baking soda fizzes | Acid + base → CO₂, water, and salt — a classic chemical reaction |

## 💪 Nutrition for Strength & Exercise

Food isn't just about staying alive — it's about *performing*. Whether you're lifting, running, playing chess for 2 hours straight, or coding through a tough bug, what you eat before and after changes how well your body handles the work and how fast it recovers.

### How Each Macro Fuels Your Body During Exercise

**Carbohydrates → Immediate Fuel**

Carbs are the body's preferred energy source during moderate-to-high intensity activity. Here's the chain:

1. You eat carbs → digested to glucose → glucose enters blood
2. Muscles pull glucose in and convert it to ATP via cellular respiration
3. When blood glucose drops, the body taps stored glycogen (in muscle + liver)
4. When glycogen runs out → fatigue, heavy legs, "hitting the wall"

> **Takeaway:** If you're doing something intense (basketball, a long run, a heavy workout), you want carbs on board beforehand. Whole grains, oats, fruit, rice — all good sources.

**Protein → Repair and Build**

Muscle tissue is made of protein. When you exercise — especially strength training — you create tiny tears in muscle fibers. This is normal and good. Protein is what repairs those tears and makes the muscle come back slightly stronger than before. That process is called **muscle protein synthesis (MPS)**.

Key facts:
- The body can only use ~20–40g of protein at a time for MPS — eating 100g in one meal isn't more effective
- Timing matters: consuming protein within ~2 hours after exercise maximizes repair
- Leucine is the amino acid that most strongly triggers MPS — it's high in eggs, milk, chicken, beef, fish

> **Takeaway:** After a workout, get protein in. It doesn't have to be a shake — eggs, Greek yogurt, chicken, or even peanut butter all work.

**Fat → Long-Duration Fuel**

Fat is the primary fuel source for low-intensity, long-duration activity (walking, light hiking, easy bike rides). Fat provides more than twice the calories per gram as carbs or protein, making it the body's deep reserve tank.

Fat is NOT useful for quick explosive efforts — fat metabolism is slow. Sprint athletes and weightlifters primarily run on carbs + glycogen.

> **Takeaway:** For everyday activity and endurance, fat is important. For explosive strength training, carbs are king.

---

### Pre-Workout Nutrition

**Goal:** Top off glycogen stores and give the body available energy. Avoid anything that sits heavy in the stomach.

| Timing | What to eat | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 2–3 hours before | Full balanced meal — moderate carbs, moderate protein, low fat | Digests fully; fuels the whole session |
| 30–60 min before | Small snack — fast carbs, minimal fat/fiber | Quick energy; fiber slows digestion (bad right before exercise) |
| Examples | Banana + peanut butter, oatmeal + fruit, rice + chicken | Easy to digest, good carb-to-protein ratio |

**Avoid right before:** High-fat meals, large amounts of fiber, dairy if it upsets your stomach during exercise.

---

### Post-Workout Nutrition (The Recovery Window)

**Goal:** Replenish glycogen, trigger muscle protein synthesis, start recovery.

The **anabolic window** — the ~30–60 minutes after training when your muscles are most receptive to nutrients — is real but not as narrow as old fitness culture suggested. Eating within 2 hours is the practical goal.

| Nutrient | Post-workout target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Protein | 20–40g | Triggers MPS; repairs micro-tears |
| Carbohydrates | 0.5–1g per lb bodyweight | Restores glycogen; also boosts insulin which helps amino acid uptake |
| Fluids | 16–24oz per lb of sweat lost | Rehydration is as important as food |

**Good post-workout combos:**
- Chocolate milk (seriously — carb + protein + fluid in one)
- Greek yogurt + fruit + granola
- Eggs + rice + vegetables
- Protein smoothie + banana

### Strength Training Basics (How Muscle Actually Grows)

Muscle growth = **progressive overload + adequate protein + rest**.

1. **Progressive overload:** Gradually increasing the demand on the muscle (heavier weight, more reps, less rest). Without this, muscles adapt and stop growing.
2. **Protein synthesis:** After training, the body rebuilds damaged muscle fibers slightly thicker. This is how muscle is "built."
3. **Rest:** Muscle doesn't grow during the workout — it grows *between* workouts. Sleep is when most repair happens. Training the same muscle group 2–3x per week with rest days is optimal for beginners.
4. **Caloric surplus:** To *add* new muscle tissue, you need to eat slightly more calories than you burn. You can't build something from nothing.

**Calorie needs scale with activity:**
- Sedentary (desk, school): base calories (roughly 14–16 × body weight in lbs)
- Moderate activity (3–4 workouts/week): ~17–19 × body weight in lbs
- High activity (daily intense training): 20+ × body weight in lbs

> These are rough estimates. A registered dietitian gives personalized numbers.

### Protein Targets by Goal

| Goal | Protein per lb of bodyweight/day |
|---|---|
| General health (no training) | 0.36g / lb |
| Active teens / moderate training | 0.6–0.8g / lb |
| Building muscle (strength training) | 0.7–1.0g / lb |
| Maximum muscle gain | ~1.0g / lb (upper limit for most people) |

*Example: A 120 lb person doing strength training 3x/week needs roughly 85–120g of protein per day.*

### Hydration and Exercise

Water is not a macronutrient, but it might as well be — dehydration tanks performance fast.

- Lose just **2% of body weight in water** → strength and endurance drop noticeably
- Lose **5%** → significant performance and cognitive impairment
- Urine color check: pale yellow = good; dark yellow = drink more; clear = slightly over-hydrated

**Electrolytes** (sodium, potassium, magnesium) are lost in sweat and need to be replaced during long or intense sessions. Sports drinks are one option; so are bananas (potassium), nuts (magnesium), and a pinch of salt in water.

### Quick Reference: Macros for Athletes

| Macro | Role in training | Best sources for athletes |
|---|---|---|
| Carbs | Primary fuel for intense work; restores glycogen post-workout | Oats, rice, sweet potato, fruit, whole grain bread |
| Protein | Builds and repairs muscle; most important post-workout | Chicken, eggs, Greek yogurt, beef, fish, beans + rice |
| Fat | Long-duration energy; supports hormones and joint health | Avocado, nuts, olive oil, fatty fish (salmon) |
| Water | Transports nutrients, regulates temperature, enables every reaction | Water, milk, fruits with high water content |
