# Grade 11 Science

Independent, grade-specific materials in the public OpenTutor curriculum library.
This track is a **draft in progress**: the audit and scope-and-sequence are
delivered; the eight units are planned, not yet written. See
[scope-and-sequence.md](scope-and-sequence.md).

**Do not refer a learner to another grade track.** This folder is self-contained
for one grade-11 learner at a time; a guiding adult adapts pacing, materials,
and supports. Keep completed learner work, names, photos, schedules, and grades
in private storage — never in this public repository.

See also: [Grade 11 track](../README.md), [curriculum index](../../README.md).

## What this track covers

A full-year (about 36 weeks) grade-11 science course following the **Physics**
pathway: measurement and uncertainty carried forward from chemistry;
two-dimensional vectors and the trigonometry that resolves them; one- and
two-dimensional kinematics including projectile motion; forces, free-body
diagrams, and Newton's laws; work, energy, and conservation; momentum, impulse,
and collision safety; uniform circular motion, gravitation, and orbital models
built from real planetary data; mechanical and electromagnetic waves, sound,
and optics; DC circuits, electric and magnetic fields, and electromagnetic
induction; thermal systems and the direction of energy spreading; qualitative
modern physics (photons, nuclear energy scale, relativity); and an engineering
capstone in which the learner designs, builds, and refines an energy-conversion
or wave-based device.

Sessions run about 50 minutes, five per week: explicit concept lessons with
worked examples, adult-supervised bench investigations using household and
low-cost apparatus, PhET interactive simulations as the default alternative
whenever a procedure is hazardous or materials are unavailable, data-analysis
and problem-solving practice, and review. All bench work follows the safety
rules in the scope-and-sequence (§6): no mains electricity (battery/low-voltage
DC only), eye protection for projectile and rotating apparatus, adult preview
of every procedure, and simulation or structured observation substituted
whenever supervision or materials are inadequate.

## Track objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the year the learner will be able to:

1. Measure length, time, and mass with the correct instrument; record readings
   to the instrument's precision; apply significant-figure rules in
   calculations; distinguish accuracy from precision; and report a reasonable
   uncertainty for a single measurement or a repeated-trial average.
2. Represent vectors graphically and algebraically; add and subtract vectors by
   components using right-triangle trigonometry; and compute the magnitude and
   direction of a resultant.
3. Analyze motion in one and two dimensions: interpret position–time,
   velocity–time, and acceleration–time graphs; apply the constant-acceleration
   kinematic equations; and predict a projectile's range, maximum height, and
   flight time from its launch conditions.
4. Draw complete free-body diagrams and apply Newton's three laws to solve for
   net force, acceleration, friction, tension, and normal force, including
   objects on inclines and in connected (pulley) systems.
5. Calculate work, kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy, and elastic
   potential energy; apply the work–energy theorem and conservation of
   mechanical energy to systems with and without nonconservative forces; and
   evaluate the efficiency of an energy conversion.
6. Calculate momentum and impulse; apply conservation of momentum to elastic
   and inelastic collisions in one dimension; and evaluate how extending
   collision time reduces peak force in a safety design.
7. Explain uniform circular motion as accelerated motion toward the center;
   calculate centripetal force; apply Newton's law of universal gravitation to
   compute orbital speeds and periods; and describe how satellites stay in
   orbit.
8. Describe mechanical and electromagnetic waves by wavelength, frequency,
   amplitude, and speed; use v = fλ to make quantitative predictions; explain
   sound production, pitch, loudness, and the Doppler effect; trace light
   through reflection and refraction; and predict image location and size for
   mirrors and lenses with ray diagrams.
9. Build and analyze simple DC circuits with batteries, resistors, bulbs,
   switches, and meters; measure current and voltage; apply Ohm's law; compute
   equivalent resistance and power in series and parallel circuits; and explain
   short circuits and fuses as safety devices.
10. Map electric and magnetic fields around charges, magnets, and
    current-carrying wires; and explain how electromagnets, electric motors,
    and generators work through electromagnetism and electromagnetic induction.
11. Describe thermal energy transfer by conduction, convection, and radiation;
    use specific heat capacity in calorimetry calculations; explain the
    direction thermal energy spreads in everyday systems; and describe, at a
    qualitative level, the photoelectric effect, the scale of nuclear energy
    release, and time dilation as consequences of modern physics.
12. Plan and carry out a safe physics investigation: write a testable question,
    identify variables, choose apparatus, record data in tables with units,
    represent relationships with graphs, evaluate sources of error and
    uncertainty, and communicate evidence-based conclusions — with the adult
    approving every procedure before it begins.

## Safety summary

Physics investigations use projectiles, rotating apparatus, heat, and
electricity, so this track's labs are adult-supervised by design. The full
rules — personal protective equipment, electrical limits, laser and projectile
rules, heat handling, and simulation alternatives — are in the scope-and-sequence
(§6). No investigation in this track uses mains electricity, open flames,
concentrated chemicals, or toxic materials; anything more hazardous is replaced
by a PhET simulation or a structured observation.
