# Lesson 02 — Centripetal Force: Who Provides It?

**Unit 05 · Session S3 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** unit objective 2 (track objective 7)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can calculate the centripetal force
`F_c = m v² / r`; I can name the real force that supplies it in a
horizontal circle, a vertical circle, a banked curve, and an orbit;
and I can explain why there is no outward "centrifugal force" on
the moving object.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, calculator. For the Session S4 demonstration:
a hollow tube (or a short length of pipe), a light string, a small
rubber stopper or ball, and a few washers; safety glasses; a clear
area. Adult-supervised.

## Readiness check (5 min)

2.1. A 0.50 kg ball on a string is whirled in a horizontal circle
of radius 0.80 m at 2.0 revolutions per second. Before
calculating: name the force that pulls the ball toward the center,
and say whether that force does work on the ball. (Hint: is the
force along the motion or across it?)
2.2. You are a passenger in a car turning left. You feel pressed
against the right-hand door. A learner says, "The centrifugal
force pushes me outward." What is actually happening, in terms of
your inertia and the door? Answer in two sentences.

## Explicit teaching

Lesson 01 found the acceleration: `a_c = v² / r`, toward the
center. Newton's second law turns that acceleration into a force
requirement:

`F_c = m a_c = m v² / r`

**Centripetal force is not a new kind of force.** It is a *role*:
whatever real force happens to point toward the center plays the
centripetal role. The name tells you the direction, not the source.
The routine is the same diagram-first routine from Unit 02:

1. Draw the free-body diagram for the moving object alone.
2. Ask which real forces (tension, friction, normal force,
   gravity, …) have a component toward the center.
3. Their sum toward the center must equal `m v² / r`.
4. Solve, carry units, and check the answer against common sense.

Four situations to sort every problem into:

- **Horizontal circle (string, whirling stopper):** tension
  provides the centripetal force. `T = m v² / r`.
- **Car on a flat curve:** static friction between tires and road
  provides it. `f_s = m v² / r`. If the required force exceeds
  the maximum static friction, the car slides outward — not
  because something pushes it out, but because nothing is left
  to pull it in.
- **Banked curve:** the normal force from the road tilts inward;
  its horizontal component provides the centripetal force, so the
  car needs less friction (or none at the design speed).
- **Vertical circle (yo-yo, loop):** gravity and tension share
  the role, and their shares change around the circle. At the top,
  both point toward the center: `T + m g = m v² / r`. At the
  bottom, tension must beat gravity: `T − m g = m v² / r`.
- **Orbit:** gravity provides the centripetal force. That is
  Lesson 05; the pattern is already visible here.

**There is no centrifugal force on the moving object.** What you
feel in a turning car is your body's inertia: your body wants to
keep going straight, and the car (through the seat and the door)
has to push you inward to make you turn. The push you feel is
real — it is the inward centripetal force, felt from the inside.
Calling it an outward force on you gets the free-body diagram
wrong: an outward arrow on the passenger would predict
acceleration *away* from the center, and the passenger does not
accelerate outward. (In a rotating *frame* physicists sometimes
use a fictitious centrifugal term as a bookkeeping device. This
unit works in the ground frame, where no such force exists.)

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — The car on the curve.** A 1,200 kg car rounds a
flat curve of radius 50 m at 15 m/s. What force must friction
provide, and in which direction?

*Reasoning:* The centripetal acceleration is
`a_c = v² / r = (15 m/s)² / 50 m = 4.5 m/s²`, toward the center
of the curve. By Newton's second law, the net force toward the
center is `F_c = m a_c = 1,200 kg × 4.5 m/s² = 5,400 N`. The
only horizontal force available is static friction, so friction
must provide 5,400 N toward the center. Check: a tire's maximum
static friction is roughly `μ_s m g`; with `μ_s ≈ 0.7` that is
about 8,200 N, so 5,400 N is plausible and the car holds the
curve. If the speed doubled to 30 m/s, the required force would
quadruple to 21,600 N — beyond the tires — and the car would
slide. That `v²` is why speed limits on curves are strict.

**Example 2 — The whirling ball.** A 0.50 kg ball on a 0.80 m
string is whirled horizontally at 2.0 revolutions per second.
Find the tension in the string.

*Reasoning:* The frequency is `f = 2.0 Hz`, so the speed is
`v = 2π r f = 2π × 0.80 m × 2.0 /s = 10.1 m/s`. The centripetal
acceleration is `a_c = v² / r = (10.1 m/s)² / 0.80 m = 127 m/s²`
(about 13 g — the string works hard). The tension is the only
horizontal force, so `T = m a_c = 0.50 kg × 127 m/s² = 63 N`.
The string pulls inward on the ball; the ball pulls outward on
the string with an equal third-law force (Lesson 02 of Unit 02) —
that outward pull on the *string* is real, but it acts on the
string, not on the ball, so it never appears in the ball's
free-body diagram.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult or a partner; draw the free-body
diagram first, then write the equation.

2.3. A 900 kg car rounds a flat curve of radius 40 m at 12 m/s.
What static friction force is needed? If the tires can provide
at most 6,000 N, does the car hold the curve?
2.4. A 0.20 kg ball on a 0.60 m string is whirled horizontally
with a period of 0.50 s. Find the speed and the tension.
2.5. A 0.30 kg yo-yo on a 0.90 m string moves in a vertical
circle at 5.0 m/s. Find the tension at the top and at the bottom
of the circle. (Use `g = 9.80 m/s²`.) Explain in one sentence
why the two tensions differ.

## Independent practice

2.6. A satellite's centripetal force is gravity. A learner draws
a free-body diagram of the satellite with two arrows: gravity
toward Earth and a "centrifugal force" away from Earth, balanced.
Explain the two errors in that diagram.
2.7. A banked curve is designed so that at 20 m/s a car needs no
friction at all. Explain, with a free-body diagram described in
words, which force provides the centripetal force and why the
banking helps.
2.8. (Error analysis.) A learner writes: "The tension in the
string is the centrifugal force pulling the ball outward."
Identify the error and rewrite the sentence correctly, naming
what the tension actually does and what pulls outward on the
string.

## Application

A 1,500 kg truck rounds a flat curve of radius 60 m. The road is
wet, so the tires can provide at most 5,000 N of static friction.
(a) What is the fastest speed the truck can take the curve
without sliding? (b) The driver takes the curve at 15 m/s. Does
the truck hold the curve? Show the calculation. (Numbers are
practice values.)

## Exit check

Name the force that provides the centripetal force in each case:
(a) a child on a merry-go-round holding a rail, (b) a planet
orbiting the Sun, (c) a car on a banked curve at the design speed.
In which case, if any, is there an outward force on the moving
object?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** say "the inward pull the turn
  needs" instead of "centripetal force" until the idea is secure,
  then attach the word.
- **Alternate response mode:** act out the car-turn case with a
  rolling ball on a tray: tilt the tray to turn the ball and
  narrate which push provides the turn.
- **Extension:** for the vertical circle, find the minimum speed
  at the top for the string to stay taut (`T = 0`), using
  `m g = m v² / r` with `r = 0.90 m`; explain what happens to the
  yo-yo if it goes slower.

## Teacher note

The durable errors are "centrifugal force is a real outward force
on the object" and "the string pushes the ball outward." Both are
diagram errors, so the fix is always: draw the free-body diagram
for the moving object alone, and check every arrow against "what
object exerts this force on this object?" The Session S4 whirling
demonstration (stopper through a tube, washers as the counterweight)
makes the `F_c = m v² / r` pattern visible: more washers, or a
faster spin, and the radius changes. Adult runs the demonstration;
eye protection for everyone nearby; a clear area; stop immediately
if the string frays. Answer-key reference: items 2.1–2.8 and the
application.
