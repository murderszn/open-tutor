# Answer Key — Unit 05: Circular Motion, Gravitation, and Orbital Models

**Adults only.** Every numbered item in the lessons, practice
sets, quiz, unit assessment, and project sample values is solved
here, independently of the lesson text. Never link this file from
a student question. Values are rounded to three significant
figures unless stated; unrounded values were used between steps.

## Lesson 01 — Uniform circular motion

**1.1.** Yes — the car is accelerating. Its speed is constant,
but its velocity changes direction around the roundabout, and
acceleration is any change in velocity.

**1.2.** Along a straight line tangent to the circle at the point
of the cut. Once the string is gone there is no inward force, so
the stone keeps the velocity it had at that instant (Newton's
first law).

**1.3.** `a_c = v² / r = (15 m/s)² / 50 m = 4.5 m/s²`, toward the
center of the curve.

**1.4.** `T = 1 / f = 1 / 2.0 Hz = 0.50 s`.
`v = 2π r / T = 2π × 0.35 m / 0.50 s = 4.4 m/s`.
`a_c = v² / r = (4.4 m/s)² / 0.35 m = 55 m/s²` toward the center.

**1.5.** The inside car (smaller radius) has the larger
acceleration: `a_c = v² / r`, so at the same speed `a_c ∝ 1 / r`.
Factor: `60 / 40 = 1.5` — the inside car's acceleration is 1.5
times the outside car's.

**1.6.** `v = 2π r / T = 2π × 8.0 m / 5.0 s = 10 m/s`.
`a_c = v² / r = (10 m/s)² / 8.0 m = 13 m/s²` toward the center —
about 1.3 times `g`. A rider feels pressed outward with about
1.3 times their weight.

**1.7.** Error: confusing constant speed with constant velocity.
Correct: "The object moves at constant speed, but its direction
keeps changing, so its velocity changes and it accelerates toward
the center."

**1.8.** `v = 2π r / T = 2π × 6.38 × 10⁶ m / 86,400 s = 464 m/s`.
`a_c = v² / r = (464 m/s)² / 6.38 × 10⁶ m = 0.034 m/s²` toward
Earth's axis — about 0.3% of `g`. It is far too small to feel,
which is why you do not feel Earth's rotation.

**Application.** (a) `T = 1 / 3.0 Hz = 0.333 s`.
`v = 2π r f = 2π × 0.25 m × 3.0 /s = 4.7 m/s`.
`a_c = v² / r = (4.7 m/s)² / 0.25 m = 89 m/s²`.
(b) `89 / 9.80 ≈ 9.1` — about 9.1 g. The drum wall pushes the
clothes inward with about nine times their weight, and the water
— which is not held by the drum — flies outward through the holes
by inertia.

**Exit check.** Yes, it is accelerating, toward the center of
the circle. If that acceleration vanished, the satellite would
fly off in a straight line tangent to its orbit (Newton's first
law).

## Lesson 02 — Centripetal force

**2.1.** Tension in the string pulls the ball toward the center.
It does no work on the ball: the force is perpendicular to the
motion, so it changes the direction of the velocity, not the
speed.

**2.2.** Your body wants to keep going straight (inertia). The
car turns left, so the door must push you inward (to the left) to
make you turn with it. The push you feel is that inward force,
felt from the inside — not an outward force on you.

**2.3.** `F_c = m v² / r = 900 kg × (12 m/s)² / 40 m = 3,240 N`
toward the center. 3,240 N < 6,000 N, so the car holds the curve.

**2.4.** `v = 2π r / T = 2π × 0.60 m / 0.50 s = 7.5 m/s`.
`Tension = m v² / r = 0.20 kg × (7.5 m/s)² / 0.60 m = 19 N`.

**2.5.** `F_c = m v² / r = 0.30 kg × (5.0 m/s)² / 0.90 m = 8.3 N`.
At the top: `T + m g = F_c`, so
`T = 8.3 N − 0.30 kg × 9.80 m/s² = 8.3 − 2.9 = 5.4 N`.
At the bottom: `T − m g = F_c`, so `T = 8.3 + 2.9 = 11 N`.
They differ because gravity helps provide the centripetal force
at the top (pointing toward the center) and opposes it at the
bottom.

**2.6.** Two errors: (1) there is no centrifugal force on the
satellite in the ground frame — the only force is gravity;
(2) the diagram is therefore unbalanced in the wrong way: gravity
alone *is* the centripetal force, `F_g = m v² / r`, and that is
what bends the path. A correct diagram has one arrow: gravity
toward Earth.

**2.7.** On a banked curve the normal force from the road is
tilted inward (perpendicular to the road surface). Its horizontal
component points toward the center of the curve and provides the
centripetal force, so at the design speed no friction is needed
at all. The banking supplies the inward push that friction would
otherwise have to supply.

**2.8.** Error: tension is not centrifugal, and it does not pull
outward. Correct: "The tension in the string pulls the ball
*toward the center* — it is the centripetal force. The ball pulls
outward on the *string* with an equal third-law force, but that
force acts on the string, not on the ball."

**Application.** (a) `v_max = √(F_max r / m) = √(5,000 N × 60 m /
1,500 kg) = √200 = 14 m/s`. (b) At 15 m/s the required force is
`F = m v² / r = 1,500 kg × (15 m/s)² / 60 m = 5,625 N`, which
exceeds the 5,000 N the tires can provide — the truck slides.

**Exit check.** (a) Friction between the child's shoes (or hands)
and the merry-go-round — static friction toward the center.
(b) Gravity. (c) The horizontal component of the normal force
from the banked road. In none of the three cases is there an
outward force on the moving object.

## Lesson 03 — Universal gravitation

**3.1.** Mass does not change: it is still about 71 kg on the
Moon. Weight does change: it falls to about one sixth. A bathroom
scale measures force (weight) and divides by Earth's `g`, so on
the Moon it would read about 12 kg — a wrong mass reading.

**3.2.** Four times as weak. Gravity (and magnetism at this level
of analogy) follows an inverse-square law: doubling the distance
quarters the force.

**3.3.** `F = G M m / r² = 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × 50 × 50 / (1.0)²
= 1.7 × 10⁻⁷ N`. It is unnoticeable because `G` is tiny and the
masses are small — about ten million times weaker than the weight
of a grain of sand.

**3.4.** `g_mars = G M / R² = 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6.39 × 10²³ /
(3.39 × 10⁶)² = 3.71 m/s²`. Weight: `W = 60.0 kg × 3.71 m/s²
= 223 N` (vs. 588 N on Earth).

**3.5.** Doubling the distance quarters the force:
`3.54 × 10²² / 4 = 8.85 × 10²¹ N`. Doubling the Sun's mass
doubles the force: `7.08 × 10²² N`. Check by calculation:
`F = G M_sun M_earth / (2r)² = (G M_sun M_earth / r²) / 4`.

**3.6.** Scaling: at `2 R_earth` the force is one quarter of the
surface weight: `9,800 / 4 = 2,450 N`. Direct check:
`g = G M / (2R)² = 9.80 / 4 = 2.45 m/s²`;
`F = 1,000 kg × 2.45 m/s² = 2,450 N`. Agrees.

**3.7.** `g` falls as `1 / r²`, and the mountaintop is farther
from Earth's center. `g_top = 9.80 × (6.371 / 6.376)²
= 9.80 × 0.9984 = 9.79 m/s²` — a change of about 0.015 m/s²,
or 0.16%. A 70 kg person's weight changes by about 1 N: far too
small to notice on a bathroom scale.

**3.8.** Error: the force does not halve — it quarters. Correct:
"Gravity follows an inverse-square law: at twice the distance
the force is one quarter as strong (in general, at `n` times the
distance it is `1 / n²` as strong)."

**Application.** (a) `g = G M_earth / r² = 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ ×
5.9722 × 10²⁴ / (6.771 × 10⁶)² = 8.69 m/s²`.
(b) `W = 75 kg × 8.69 m/s² = 652 N`, which is `652 / 735 = 0.89`
— about 89% of their surface weight. (c) The astronaut floats
not because gravity is gone — it is still 89% as strong — but
because the astronaut and the station are in free fall together:
both fall toward Earth at the same rate, so there is no floor
pushing on the astronaut.

**Exit check.** In words: every mass attracts every other mass
with a force proportional to the product of the masses and
inversely proportional to the square of the distance between
their centers. In symbols: `F = G M m / r²`. At three times the
distance the pull falls to one ninth. The planet's mass does not
change; its weight (the pull on it) falls to one ninth.

## Lesson 04 — Coulomb's law

**4.1.** The electrostatic force holds the balloon to the wall:
rubbing gives the balloon a net charge, which attracts opposite
charge in the wall. Electrostatics beats gravity here because the
balloon's weight is tiny and a small net charge produces a
comparatively large electric force.

**4.2.** No. Like charges repel and opposite charges attract.
Gravity is always attractive because mass is always positive;
charge comes in two signs.

**4.3.** `F = k q₁ q₂ / r² = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (2.0 × 10⁻⁶) ×
(3.0 × 10⁻⁶) / (0.50)² = 0.22 N`. The charges have opposite
signs, so the force is attractive.

**4.4.** `F = k e² / r² = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹)² /
(1.0 × 10⁻⁹)² = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ N`, repulsive.

**4.5.** The error is calling gravity the strongest force. The
hydrogen-atom comparison shows the electric force between an
electron and a proton is about `10³⁹` times stronger than their
gravitational attraction. Gravity holds the solar system together
not because it is strong, but because planetary matter is
electrically neutral — its charges cancel, leaving gravity as the
only force that adds up over astronomical distances.

**4.6.** Electric: `F_e = k e² / r² = 8.99 × 10⁹ ×
(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹)² / (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰)² = 2.3 × 10⁻⁸ N`
(attractive). Gravitational:
`F_g = G m_e m_p / r² = 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ ×
1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ / (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰)² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴⁷ N`. The electric
force is about `2 × 10³⁹` times stronger.

**4.7.** The distance triples, so the force falls to one ninth:
`0.36 / 9 = 0.040 N`. Check: `q = √(F r² / k) = √(0.36 × 0.040 /
8.99 × 10⁹) = √(1.6 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.3 × 10⁻⁶ C` — the charges are
about 1.3 µC each.

**4.8.** Error: Coulomb's law covers both attraction and
repulsion. Correct: "Coulomb's law gives the electrostatic force
between two charges: opposite charges attract and like charges
repel, with the size `F = k q₁ q₂ / r²` — unlike gravity, which
is always attractive."

**Application.** (a) `F_e = k q² / r² = 8.99 × 10⁹ ×
(5.0 × 10⁻⁹)² / (0.020)² = 5.6 × 10⁻⁴ N`, repulsive.
(b) `F_g = G m² / r² = 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × (2.0 × 10⁻⁹)² /
(0.020)² = 6.7 × 10⁻²⁵ N`. (c) The electric force is larger by
a factor of about `8 × 10²⁰`. Dust grains do not clump by gravity
because their gravitational attraction is utterly negligible next
to electrostatic forces — and even the electric force here is
only about half a millinewton.

**Exit check.** `F = G M m / r²` and `F = k q₁ q₂ / r²`. Alike:
both are inverse-square laws acting at a distance. Different:
(1) gravity is always attractive, electrostatics attracts or
repels; (2) gravity's source is mass, electrostatics' source is
charge. A neutral planet and a neutral star attract by gravity
but (nearly) not electrostatically because their positive and
negative charges cancel, while their masses add.

## Lesson 05 — Orbits and Kepler's laws

**5.1.** Mercury moves faster (about 48 km/s vs. Neptune's
5.4 km/s) because it is closer to the Sun — `v = √(G M / r)`.
Neptune takes far longer: 60,190 days (about 165 years) vs.
Mercury's 88 days.

**5.2.** `G M m / r² = m v² / r` — the gravitational force on the
satellite equals the centripetal force it needs.

**5.3.** `r = 2 × 6.371 × 10⁶ m = 1.274 × 10⁷ m`.
`v = √(G M / r) = √(6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.9722 × 10²⁴ /
1.274 × 10⁷) = 5.59 × 10³ m/s = 5.59 km/s`.
`T = 2π r / v = 2π × 1.274 × 10⁷ / 5.59 × 10³ = 1.43 × 10⁴ s
= 3.98 h`. Check: at the surface `v = 7.91 km/s`, and
`5.59 = 7.91 / √2`, as expected since `r` doubled.

**5.4.** Mercury: `r = 57.9 × 10⁹ m`, `T = 88 × 86,400 s
= 7.60 × 10⁶ s`. `T² / r³ = (7.60 × 10⁶)² / (57.9 × 10⁹)³
= 5.78 × 10¹³ / 1.94 × 10²⁹ = 2.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ s²/m³`. Neptune:
`r = 4.4951 × 10¹² m`, `T = 60,190 × 86,400 s = 5.20 × 10⁹ s`.
`T² / r³ = (5.20 × 10⁹)² / (4.4951 × 10¹²)³ = 2.70 × 10¹⁹ /
9.08 × 10³⁷ = 2.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ s²/m³`. Both agree with each other
and with the predicted `2.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ s²/m³` to within rounding.

**5.5.** `T² = r³ = 4³ = 64`, so `T = 8 years`. The shortcut works
because `T² = 4π² r³ / (G M_sun)` with `r` in AU and `T` in
years makes the constant 1 for the Sun; for a different star the
constant would be `M_sun / M_star` in those units, so the period
would differ.

**5.6.** `v = √(G M_earth / r) = √(6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.9722 ×
10²⁴ / 3.844 × 10⁸) = 1.02 × 10³ m/s = 1.02 km/s` — matches
Lesson 01's value. `T = 2π r / v = 2π × 3.844 × 10⁸ / 1.02 × 10³
= 2.37 × 10⁶ s = 27.5 days`, vs. the actual 27.3 days. The ~0.5%
gap is the model's approximation: the two-body formula should
use `M_earth + M_moon`, and the Sun also pulls on the Moon.

**5.7.** From `T² = 4π² r³ / (G M_earth)`:
`r³ = G M_earth T² / 4π² = 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.9722 × 10²⁴ ×
(86,164 s)² / 4π² = 7.50 × 10²² m³`, so `r = 4.22 × 10⁷ m`.
Altitude: `4.22 × 10⁷ − 6.371 × 10⁶ = 3.58 × 10⁷ m ≈ 35,800 km`.

**5.8.** Two errors: (1) it is distance from the star, not the
planet's size or mass, that decides orbital speed — the planet's
mass cancels in `v = √(G M / r)`; (2) "bigger" is ambiguous and
irrelevant here. Correct: "Planets farther from the Sun orbit
slower, because orbital speed falls as `1 / √r`."

**Application.** (a) `T² = r³ = 0.50³ = 0.125`, so
`T = 0.354 years ≈ 129 days`. (b) `r = 0.50 × 1.496 × 10¹¹ m
= 7.48 × 10¹⁰ m`; `v = √(G M_sun / r) = √(6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ ×
1.989 × 10³⁰ / 7.48 × 10¹⁰) = 4.21 × 10⁴ m/s = 42.1 km/s`.
(c) It moves faster than Earth's 29.8 km/s because it is closer
to the Sun — orbital speed grows as `1 / √r`.

**Exit check.** `F_gravity = F_centripetal` gives
`G M m / r² = m v² / r`; cancel `m` and one `r` to get
`v² = G M / r`, so `v = √(G M / r)`. Kepler's third law, in
words: the square of a planet's period is proportional to the
cube of its distance from the Sun; in symbols:
`T² = 4π² r³ / (G M)`. The dataset shows `T² / r³ ≈
2.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ s²/m³` for all eight planets, confirming the law.

## Lesson 06 — Satellites, escape, and orbital models

**6.1.** The claim is wrong: `g` at 400 km is about 8.7 m/s²,
about 89% of surface gravity. The astronaut floats because the
astronaut and the station are falling together around Earth —
free fall, not zero gravity.

**6.2.** The ball would keep falling toward Earth but never land:
the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls, so it
falls around the planet — that is an orbit.

**6.3.** `v_esc = √(2 G M / R) = √(2 × 6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ ×
7.342 × 10²² / 1.7374 × 10⁶) = 2.38 × 10³ m/s = 2.38 km/s` —
about one fifth of Earth's 11.2 km/s, because the Moon is much
less massive and smaller.

**6.4.** `v = √(G M_earth / r) = √(6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.9722 ×
10²⁴ / 4.22 × 10⁷) = 3.07 × 10³ m/s = 3.07 km/s`. Check:
`T = 2π r / v = 2π × 4.22 × 10⁷ / 3.07 × 10³ = 86,400 s
= 24 h`. It must orbit above the equator because a geostationary
orbit has to lie in Earth's equatorial plane: the satellite must
circle Earth's rotation axis at the same rate Earth turns, and
only an equatorial orbit keeps it above one fixed point — any
inclined orbit would drift north and south each day.

**6.5.** The claim is wrong on both counts. Gravity at 400 km is
about 8.7 m/s² (89% of surface gravity), so gravity is very much
present. The astronauts float because they and the station are in
free fall together — both falling toward Earth at the same rate,
so no floor pushes on them.

**6.6.** `r = 3 × 6.371 × 10⁶ m = 1.911 × 10⁷ m`.
Orbital speed: `v = √(G M / r) = √(6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.9722 ×
10²⁴ / 1.911 × 10⁷) = 4.57 × 10³ m/s = 4.57 km/s`. Escape
velocity there: `v_esc = √(2 G M / r) = √2 × 4.57 = 6.46 km/s`.
Yes — escape is `√2` times orbital speed at the same distance,
because `v_esc² = 2 G M / r` while `v_orb² = G M / r`.

**6.7.** Any three honest omissions with a real consequence, for
example: (1) elliptical orbits — Mercury's orbit (eccentricity
0.21) is poorly described by a circle; (2) other bodies' gravity
— Jupiter's pull perturbs asteroid orbits; (3) the atmosphere —
drag at 400 km slowly decays the ISS orbit, requiring reboosts;
(4) Earth's equatorial bulge — slowly rotates satellite orbital
planes; (5) relativity — GPS clocks need relativistic corrections.

**6.8.** Error: there is no centrifugal force balancing gravity.
Correct: "A satellite stays up because it is in free fall with
enough sideways speed to keep missing the ground — gravity is the
centripetal force bending its path into a circle, and nothing
pushes outward on it."

**Application.** (a) `v = √(G M / r) = √(6.67430 × 10⁻¹¹ ×
7.3 × 10¹⁰ / 1.0 × 10³) = √(4.87 × 10⁻³) = 0.070 m/s` — about
7 cm/s. (b) `v_esc = √2 × 0.070 = 0.099 m/s` — about 10 cm/s.
(c) Orbiting Bennu is slow, delicate work because its gravity is
so weak that orbital speeds are centimeters per second — a
walking-speed nudge (about 1 m/s) is ten times escape velocity,
so a small mistake sends a probe drifting away forever.

**Exit check.** A satellite is in continuous free fall toward
Earth, but its sideways speed is so large that the curved surface
falls away beneath it as fast as it falls — so it falls around
Earth instead of onto it. Escape velocity is
`v_esc = √(2 G M / r)`; for Earth at its surface it is 11.2 km/s.
