# Practice — Unit 05: Circular Motion, Gravitation, and Orbital Models

Practice Sets A–F follow Lessons 01–06. Each set mixes new
material with spaced retrieval from earlier lessons and units.
Work with the adult or a partner where marked; solve
independently where marked. All items are solved in
`answer-key.md`. Numbers are practice values unless labeled as
real reference data.

## Set A — Uniform circular motion (Lessons 01; Sessions S2)

A1. A carousel of radius 3.0 m makes one turn in 6.0 s. Find the
period, frequency, speed, and centripetal acceleration of a rider
at the edge.
A2. A car takes a curve of radius 25 m at 10 m/s, then the same
curve at 20 m/s. By what factor does the centripetal acceleration
grow? Explain why using `a_c = v² / r`.
A3. (Retrieval, Unit 02.) A 5.0 kg box is pushed with a net force
of 20 N. What is its acceleration? State the law you used.
A4. A washing-machine drum (radius 0.30 m) spins at 5.0 rev/s.
Compute the centripetal acceleration of a point on the drum wall
and express it as a multiple of `g`.
A5. A point on a CD (radius 0.060 m) has centripetal acceleration
120 m/s². Find its speed. Then find its period.
A6. Explain in two sentences why a satellite in a circular orbit
at constant speed is accelerating, even though its speed never
changes.

## Set B — Centripetal force (Lesson 02; Session S4)

B1. A 0.40 kg ball on a 0.50 m string is whirled horizontally at
3.0 rev/s. Find the tension in the string.
B2. A 1,000 kg car rounds a flat curve of radius 80 m at 18 m/s.
What friction force is needed? If the tires can provide at most
4,500 N, does the car hold the curve?
B3. (Retrieval, Lesson 01.) A merry-go-round of radius 2.5 m has
a period of 5.0 s. Find the speed and centripetal acceleration of
a rider at the edge.
B4. A 0.25 kg mass on a 1.0 m string moves in a vertical circle
at 4.0 m/s. Find the tension at the top and at the bottom.
B5. A banked curve is designed for 25 m/s with no friction
needed. A car takes it at 15 m/s instead. Does it need friction
now — and if so, in which direction (up or down the banking)?
Explain with a free-body diagram described in words.
B6. A learner draws a free-body diagram of a child on a
merry-go-round and adds an outward "centrifugal force" arrow.
Correct the diagram in words: which arrows belong, and which
does not?

## Set C — Universal gravitation (Lesson 03; Session S6)

C1. Compute the gravitational force between two 80 kg people
standing 2.0 m apart (center to center). Why is it unnoticeable?
C2. A 50.0 kg student weighs how much on Earth? On Mars
(`g_mars = 3.71 m/s²`)? On the Moon (`g_moon = 1.62 m/s²`)?
State the mass in each case.
C3. The gravitational force between two bodies is 800 N. What is
it if (a) the distance doubles, (b) the distance triples,
(c) one mass doubles and the distance doubles?
C4. (Retrieval, Lesson 02.) A 1,200 kg car needs 6,000 N of
centripetal force to hold a curve. What is its centripetal
acceleration?
C5. Compute `g` on the surface of Venus
(`M_venus = 4.87 × 10²⁴ kg`, `R_venus = 6.052 × 10⁶ m`) and
compare with Earth's 9.80 m/s². (Known value to check against:
about 8.87 m/s².)
C6. A satellite's weight at Earth's surface is 4,900 N. What is
the gravitational force on it at `r = 4 R_earth`? Use the scaling
rule, then confirm by direct substitution with `g = G M / r²`.

## Set D — Coulomb's law (Lesson 04; Session S8)

D1. Two charges, `+4.0 µC` and `+4.0 µC`, sit 0.20 m apart.
Compute the force and state attract or repel.
D2. Two charges, `+6.0 µC` and `−2.0 µC`, sit 0.30 m apart.
Compute the force magnitude and state attract or repel.
D3. The electrostatic force between two charges is 1.80 N at
0.10 m apart. What is it at 0.30 m apart? Use the scaling rule.
D4. (Retrieval, Lesson 03.) Two masses attract with 100 N at
1.0 m apart. What is the force at 5.0 m apart?
D5. An electron and a proton sit `2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m` apart. Compute
the electric force between them. (Use `e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C`.)
D6. Explain in three sentences why a charged balloon sticks to a
wall but a neutral planet does not stick to another planet
electrostatically — and which force does hold planets together.

## Set E — Orbits and Kepler's laws (Lesson 05; Session S12)

E1. Use the planet dataset: Mars is at 227.9 million km with a
period of 687 days. Compute its orbital speed in km/s.
E2. Use the dataset: Jupiter is at 778.6 million km with a period
of 4,333 days. Compute `T² / r³` in SI units and compare with
`2.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ s²/m³`.
E3. A satellite orbits Earth at `r = 1.5 R_earth`. Compute its
orbital speed and its period in minutes.
E4. (Retrieval, Lesson 03.) A 70 kg astronaut weighs 686 N on
Earth. What is their weight at `r = 3 R_earth`? Use the scaling
rule.
E5. A planet orbits its star at 9 AU. Find its period in years
using `T² = r³`. Then explain why this shortcut only works when
the star's mass equals the Sun's.
E6. The ISS orbits at 7.67 km/s. A satellite in a higher orbit
moves at 5.0 km/s. Which orbit has the longer period, and why?
Answer with `v = √(G M / r)` and `T = 2π r / v`.

## Set F — Satellites, escape, and orbital models (Lesson 06; Session S14)

F1. Compute the escape velocity from the surface of Venus
(`M_venus = 4.87 × 10²⁴ kg`, `R_venus = 6.052 × 10⁶ m`).
Compare with Earth's 11.2 km/s.
F2. A satellite orbits Earth at `r = 4 R_earth`. Compute its
orbital speed and its escape velocity at that distance.
F3. (Retrieval, Lesson 05.) The Moon's orbital speed is about
1.02 km/s. Its distance is `3.844 × 10⁸ m`. Compute its period
in days from `T = 2π r / v`.
F4. A spacecraft in low Earth orbit loses altitude because of
drag. Does its speed increase or decrease as it sinks? Explain
with `v = √(G M / r)` and name the energy trade-off.
F5. Name two things the two-body circular-orbit model leaves out,
and for each, name a real mission or situation where the omission
matters.
F6. (Spaced retrieval, whole unit.) Write both inverse-square
laws from memory, state the centripetal-force equation, and
explain in two sentences why a satellite is in free fall.
