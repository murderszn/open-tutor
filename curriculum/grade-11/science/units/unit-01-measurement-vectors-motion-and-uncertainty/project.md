# Project — Launch Lab: Predict and Test Projectile Range

**Unit 01 · Session S17 · ~50 minutes (+ S20 portfolio check)**
**Unit objective 5 (track objective 12). Adult approves the procedure
before any launch.**

## The challenge

Build a small launcher, *predict* where its projectile will land using
the Lesson 05 methods, then test the prediction. Your grade rests on
the honesty of the comparison — not on hitting the target.

## Safety (adult reads first, per the track safety rules)

- Safety glasses for the learner and anyone nearby; a clear range with
  a backstop (a cardboard box or a wall with a sheet); low-speed
  launcher only.
- The adult previews and approves the full procedure — launcher
  design, range layout, and backstop — before the first launch.
- Never aim at people, pets, or breakables. Keep the launch speed low
  (a few m/s).
- If materials or supervision are inadequate, use the PhET simulation
  alternative below — same data-analysis demand, no bench needed.

## Testable question

P1. Write one testable question, e.g.: "Does the horizontal-launch
range equation R = v_x·√(2y₀/9.80) predict the measured landing
position of a ball launched from height y₀ at speed v_x?"

## Variables

P2. Identify and record:
- Independent variable (what you set): launch height y₀ and/or launch
  speed v_x.
- Dependent variable (what you measure): horizontal range R.
- Controlled variables (what you hold fixed): launch angle
  (horizontal, 0°), ball type and mass, release method, landing
  surface, air stillness.

## Materials (low-cost)

- Launcher: a toy-car ramp, a rolled-paper tube taped to a stack of
  books, or a ruler "diving board" — anything giving a repeatable
  *horizontal* launch. A small ball (marble, ping-pong ball).
- Measuring tape or meter stick; stopwatch or phone timer; safety
  glasses; a cardboard-box backstop; masking tape to mark the launch
  point's floor position (plumb line: a string with a weight finds the
  point directly below the launch).
- Notebook for the data table.

## Procedure

1. **Adult approves** the launcher, range layout, and backstop.
2. Measure the launch height y₀ (launch point to floor) three times;
   record each reading with units.
3. Determine the launch speed v_x: time the ball rolling across a
   known horizontal distance on the launcher (or use the PhET
   alternative's set speed). Take five trials; compute mean ±
   uncertainty (Lesson 02 method).
4. **Compute the predicted range before launching:** t = √(2y₀/9.80),
   R_predicted = v_x·t. Keep three significant figures. Write it down
   — this is your prediction, and it may not be changed after step 5.
5. Mark the predicted landing point with tape. Launch five times;
   measure each landing distance from the plumb-line mark. Record all
   five with units.
6. Compute the measured mean range and its uncertainty (half the
   range of the five landings).
7. (Mass check) Repeat three launches with a *different-mass* ball at
   the same settings. Record the mean landing position.

## Deliverables (portfolio)

P3. A data table with units on every column: trial number, launch
height readings, launch-speed trials, five range trials, and the
different-mass trials.
P4. The full prediction computation with units, significant figures,
and the uncertainty carried from the y₀ and v_x measurements.
P5. A comparison: predicted range vs. measured mean ± uncertainty;
percent difference = |measured − predicted| / predicted × 100%;
a written error discussion naming at least two specific error
sources (e.g., launch angle not exactly 0°, air resistance, speed
measurement timing error) and saying which direction each would push
the result.
P6. A conclusion answering P1's question with evidence, plus one
concrete improvement for a repeat run. Include the mass-check result
and what it says about mass in the prediction.

## Rubric

| Criterion | 3 — Proficient | 2 — Developing | 1 — Beginning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Testable question & variables (P1–P2) | Question is testable and answered by the data; all three variable types correctly identified | Question testable but vague; one variable type misidentified | No testable question or variables confused |
| Data table (P3) | Every column labeled with units; all trials recorded; no missing data | Table complete but a unit missing or one trial unrecorded | Table missing, unlabeled, or invented after the fact |
| Prediction (P4) | Computed *before* launching; units cancel correctly; 3 sig figs; uncertainty carried from measurements | Prediction done but after some launches, or a unit/sig-fig error | No pre-launch prediction, or prediction without units |
| Comparison & error discussion (P5) | Percent difference computed; measured ± uncertainty compared honestly to prediction; ≥2 specific, directional error sources | Comparison present but percent difference missing or error sources generic ("human error") | No comparison, or claim of confirmation contradicted by the numbers |
| Conclusion & mass check (P6) | Conclusion follows the evidence; mass-check result stated and interpreted; one concrete improvement named | Conclusion overclaims ("proved") or mass check missing | No evidence-based conclusion |

Score: 13–15 = objective 5 secure; 10–12 = revise one criterion;
below 10 = repeat the analysis session with adult support.

## PhET simulation alternative

Use the PhET **"Projectile Motion"** simulation
(https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulation/projectile-motion — verified
2026-10-14; the adult previews it first). Same analysis, no bench:

1. Set the cannon height to a value of your choice (e.g., 10 m),
   initial speed (e.g., 15 m/s), angle 0°, air resistance OFF.
2. **Predict** the range by hand first (t = √(2y₀/9.80), R = v_x·t).
3. Fire five times, recording the range each time with the sim's
   measurement tool; compute mean ± uncertainty.
4. Repeat at a different height; repeat with a different-mass
   projectile (e.g., golf ball vs. Buick — the sim offers both).
5. Complete deliverables P3–P6 with the sim data: same table, same
   prediction-first rule, same error discussion (name sim-specific
   sources, e.g., measurement-tool placement, rounding in the sim's
   readout).
