# Unit 03 — Energy, Work, Power, and Conservation

**Grade 11 · Science · Physics pathway · Weeks 11–14 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-27 for issue #51; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Unit 02 taught the learner to predict motion from forces. This unit
answers the next question: **where does the energy go, and what can
it do?** The answer is built in four moves:

1. **Work** — energy transferred by a force acting through a
   distance. Only the force component along the displacement
   counts, which is why a sideways force and a held-still load do
   no work at all.
2. **Kinetic and potential energy** — the energy of motion
   (`KE = ½mv²`) and the energy of position or configuration
   (`PE_grav = mgh`, `PE_elastic = ½kx²`). Only *changes* in
   potential energy are physically meaningful; the reference level
   is a free choice.
3. **Conservation** — when only conservative forces act, kinetic
   plus potential energy stays constant, so speeds and heights can
   be predicted without tracking the motion step by step. When
   friction or other nonconservative forces act, the missing
   mechanical energy is accounted for as thermal (and sound)
   energy — never lost without a trace.
4. **Power and energy budgets** — power is the *rate* of energy
   transfer, and a spreadsheet energy budget is a computational
   model of a system: given the changes in two components, the
   third is determined. The unit ends by building and refining a
   small energy-conversion device under stated constraints.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **if I
keep a careful energy account for a system, can I predict what it
will do — and can I explain honestly where every joule went?**

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–02 and the Weeks 1–2 diagnostic (re-taught in context
where insecure):

- **From Unit 01:** significant figures and the `value ±
  uncertainty` report; `g = 9.80 m/s²`; vector components with
  sine and cosine (needed for work at an angle).
- **From Unit 02:** free-body diagrams; Newton's second law; the
  idea that friction opposes motion. Energy reasoning does not
  replace force reasoning — Lesson 06 shows where each is the
  faster tool.
- **From grade-10 math:** rearranging formulas; square roots;
  reading bar graphs and tables.

Session S9 re-checks the work–energy theorem and reference-level
choices before nonconservative forces begin; anyone missing them
gets targeted warm-ups there.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Calculate the work done by a constant force from `W = Fd
   cos θ`, including the sign of the work, and explain why a
   perpendicular force and a zero displacement both give zero
   work. (track objective 5)
2. Calculate kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy, and
   elastic potential energy; choose a reference level and explain
   why only potential-energy *changes* matter. (track objective 5)
3. Apply the work–energy theorem (`W_net = ΔKE`) and conservation
   of mechanical energy to predict speeds and heights in systems
   where only conservative forces act. (track objective 5)
4. Account for nonconservative forces with `W_nc = ΔE_mech`;
   compute the energy converted to thermal energy and the
   efficiency of an energy conversion. (track objective 5)
5. Calculate average power (`P = W/t`) and instantaneous power
   (`P = Fv` for a constant force along the motion); and build a
   spreadsheet energy budget — a computational model of a two- or
   three-component system — that finds an unknown component change
   from the known ones. (track objective 5)
6. Plan and carry out a safe energy-conversion device build: state
   constraints, measure inputs and outputs, keep an energy
   account, refine the device once from measured evidence, and
   communicate an evidence-based conclusion — with the adult
   approving the procedure first. (track objective 12)

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS),
high school (9–12) band. Codes and descriptions were re-verified
2026-10-27 against the NSTA NGSS performance-expectation pages and
the AMNH NGSS card deck for HS-PS3, which restate the Achieve
performance expectations; descriptions here are short paraphrases,
not reproductions of the standards text. **This crosswalk is a
planning reference, not a claim of state adoption, accreditation,
or certification of alignment.**

- **HS-PS3-1** — create a computational model to calculate the
  change in the energy of one component of a system when the
  changes in the other components and the energy flows in and out
  are known. This unit is its main home: Lesson 06 builds the
  spreadsheet energy budget, and the unit assessment's budget
  table applies it. Note the assessment boundary carried in the
  standard: basic algebraic expressions or computations; systems
  of two or three components; thermal energy, kinetic energy,
  and/or the energies in gravitational, magnetic, or electric
  fields. Every budget in this unit has at most three components.
- **HS-PS3-2** — develop and use models to illustrate that energy
  at the macroscopic scale can be accounted for as a combination
  of energy associated with the motions of particles (objects)
  and energy associated with their relative positions. Lessons
  03–04 (energy bar charts, including the deterministic SVG chart
  in Lesson 04) and the project build this model.
- **HS-PS3-3** — design, build, and refine a device that works
  within given constraints to convert one form of energy into
  another form of energy. The project (Week 14) is its home:
  a rubber-band car or a waterwheel, with qualitative and
  quantitative evaluation and one measured refinement.
- Science and engineering practices used in this unit: developing
  and using models (energy bar charts, budgets, Lessons 03–06);
  planning and carrying out investigations (project, S16–S17);
  analyzing and interpreting data (Lessons 02, 05, project);
  using mathematics and computational thinking (every lesson;
  the spreadsheet budget in Lesson 06); constructing explanations
  from evidence (Lessons 04–05, project).
- `g = 9.80 m/s²` throughout (three significant figures), as in
  Units 01–02. All practice numbers are given practice values,
  not measured material constants, unless the project measures an
  effective value and says so.
