# Lesson 05 — Nonconservative Forces, Thermal Energy, and Efficiency

**Goal:** You will be able to account for energy when friction
acts, and compute the efficiency of an energy conversion.
**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, calculator.
**Readiness check (3 minutes):** A slide's top is 2.5 m up. A
30 kg child reaches the bottom at 4.0 m/s instead of the
frictionless prediction. Did energy disappear — or go
somewhere? (Answer: somewhere — into warming the slide, the
child's clothes, and the air. This lesson puts a number on
"somewhere.")

## Teaching

Lessons 03–04 kept a perfect account because only conservative
forces acted. Real systems add **nonconservative forces** —
friction, air resistance, a pushing hand — whose work depends
on the path taken. Slide twice as far against the same friction
and friction does twice as much negative work. The accounting
rule becomes:

```
W_nc = ΔE_mech = (KE_final + PE_final) − (KE_initial + PE_initial)
```

The work of nonconservative forces equals the change in
mechanical energy. When `W_nc` is negative (friction, air
resistance), mechanical energy decreases — and the missing
energy is not destroyed. It becomes **thermal energy** (warmer
surfaces and air) and sometimes sound. Energy is still
conserved; the *mechanical* account just no longer closes on
its own. That is the precise meaning of "energy is lost to
friction": lost from the mechanical account, found in the
thermal account.

**Efficiency** measures how good a conversion is at delivering
useful energy:

```
efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input
```

Efficiency is usually written as a percentage and is always
between 0% and 100% for a real device. A 70%-efficient motor
turns 70 J of every 100 J of electrical energy into useful
motion; the other 30 J becomes thermal energy (and a little
sound). No device reaches 100%: some energy always spreads into
thermal form. A device claiming over 100% efficiency — a
perpetual-motion claim — is claiming energy from nothing, and
the energy account is the tool that exposes it.

### Worked example 1 — a block on a rough ramp

A 4.0 kg block is released from rest at a height of 1.5 m on a
rough ramp and reaches the bottom moving at 4.0 m/s. How much
mechanical energy was converted to thermal energy?

Initial mechanical energy (all potential):
`PE = (4.0)(9.80)(1.5) = 58.8 J`, `KE = 0`, so `E_initial =
58.8 J`.
Final mechanical energy (all kinetic):
`KE = ½(4.0)(4.0)² = 32 J`, `PE = 0`, so `E_final = 32 J`.
Change: `ΔE_mech = 32 − 58.8 = −26.8 J ≈ −27 J`.
So `W_nc = −27 J`: friction did 27 J of negative work, and
27 J of mechanical energy became thermal energy. Without
friction the block would have reached the bottom at
`√(2 × 9.80 × 1.5) = 5.4 m/s` — the shortfall from 5.4 to
4.0 m/s *is* the 27 J, in speed form.

### Worked example 2 — motor efficiency

An electric motor takes in 800 J of electrical energy and
delivers 560 J of useful mechanical energy. (a) Find its
efficiency. (b) How much energy becomes thermal energy (and
sound)?

(a) `efficiency = 560 / 800 = 0.70 = 70%`.
(b) `800 − 560 = 240 J` leaves as thermal energy and sound.
Note the accounting habit: input = useful + wasted, always.
If a problem's numbers don't add up that way, something is
missing or wrong.

### Worked example 3 — a slide as a converter

A 30 kg child starts from rest at the top of a slide 2.5 m
high and reaches the bottom at 4.0 m/s. (a) How much
mechanical energy became thermal energy? (b) As an energy
converter (PE → KE), what is the slide's efficiency?

(a) Initial: `PE = (30)(9.80)(2.5) = 735 J`.
Final: `KE = ½(30)(4.0)² = 240 J`.
Missing: `735 − 240 = 495 J ≈ 5.0 × 10² J` became thermal
energy (warm slide, warm clothes, warmed air).
(b) `efficiency = useful output ÷ input = 240 / 735 = 0.327 ≈
33%`. Only a third of the starting potential energy became
motion; the rest warmed the world a little. That is normal
for a slide — and it is why the frictionless prediction in
Lesson 04's applied task overestimates real slide speeds.

## Guided practice

1. A system starts with 100 J of mechanical energy and ends
   with 70 J. How much energy became thermal energy?
2. A light bulb takes in 60 J of electrical energy and gives
   out 6.0 J of light. Find its efficiency.
3. A car engine takes in 5000 J of fuel energy and delivers
   1250 J of useful motion. Find its efficiency and the wasted
   energy.
4. A 5.0 kg block slides down a rough ramp from 2.0 m and
   reaches the bottom at 5.0 m/s. Find the initial PE, the
   final KE, and the energy converted to thermal energy.
   (Note: 5.0 m/s is *slower* than the frictionless 6.3 m/s —
   check that your thermal number is positive.)

## Independent practice

1. A skateboarder starts with 1200 J of potential energy at a
   ramp top and reaches the bottom with 900 J of kinetic
   energy. How much energy became thermal energy?
2. A water pump takes in 2000 J and delivers 1500 J of useful
   lifting energy. Find its efficiency.
3. Two motors: motor A takes in 2000 J and delivers 1500 J;
   motor B takes in 3000 J and delivers 1800 J. Which is more
   efficient? Which delivers more useful energy? (They can be
   different answers — say both.)
4. **Explain your reasoning.** A learner says friction
   "destroys energy." Write three sentences correcting this,
   naming where the energy goes and what evidence would show
   it (warmer surfaces after sliding).

## Applied task — the home energy audit

List three energy conversions in a home (for example: a
toaster, a phone charger, a car engine, a light bulb, a
furnace). For each, name the input form, the useful output
form, and the wasted form. Then rank the three by *likely*
efficiency — highest to lowest — and give one reason per
ranking. No numbers are required; the ranking reasons are the
product. The adult checks that every conversion's wasted form
is thermal energy (plus sound or light where honest) and that
no conversion is claimed at 100%.

## Exit check

1. A system loses 40 J of mechanical energy to friction. The
   thermal energy gained is ____ J.
2. A device takes in 500 J and delivers 125 J usefully.
   Efficiency = ____%.
3. True or false: "A 100%-efficient motor is the engineering
   goal." Correct it if false.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the accounting sentence "input = useful
  + wasted" fits every problem in this lesson — write it at the
  top of your working.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer the applied task as a
  table (conversion | input | useful | wasted) before writing
  ranking sentences.
- **Extension:** a 1500 kg car brakes from 20 m/s to rest.
  How much kinetic energy becomes thermal energy in the brakes,
  tires, and road? (Answer: the full KE, 300,000 J = 3.0 × 10⁵
  J — which is why brakes get hot.)

## Teacher note

Misconceptions to watch: "friction destroys energy" (it
converts it); efficiency computed as input ÷ output (inverted);
efficiency over 100% accepted without challenge. The answer key
solves every item independently. Next lesson: the rate of
transfer — power — and the spreadsheet budget that turns this
accounting into a computational model.
