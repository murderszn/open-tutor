# Lesson 01 — Work: Force Through Distance

**Goal:** You will be able to calculate the work done by a constant
force and explain why some forces do no work at all.
**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, calculator.
**Readiness check (3 minutes):** A force of 10 N pushes a box 2 m.
A second force of 10 N pushes an identical box 5 m. Which push
transfers more energy to its box, and how do you know? (Answer:
the second — the force acts over a longer distance. If you said
"they are equal because the forces are equal," keep that thought:
this lesson is about why distance matters.)

## Teaching

In everyday language, "work" means effort: holding a heavy box
feels like work. In physics, **work** has a narrower meaning:
**work is energy transferred to or from an object by a force
acting through a distance.** If nothing moves, no energy is
transferred, and the physics work is zero — however tired your
arms get.

For a constant force `F` acting while an object moves a distance
`d` in a straight line, with angle `θ` between the force and the
displacement:

```
W = F · d · cos θ
```

Work is measured in **joules (J)**: `1 J = 1 N·m`. Work is a
scalar — it has size and sign, but no direction. Three cases
cover almost every problem in this unit:

1. **Force along the motion** (`θ = 0°`, `cos θ = 1`):
   `W = Fd`. Positive work — energy goes *into* the object.
2. **Force opposite the motion** (`θ = 180°`, `cos θ = −1`):
   `W = −Fd`. Negative work — energy is taken *out of* the
   object.
3. **Force perpendicular to the motion** (`θ = 90°`,
   `cos θ = 0`): `W = 0`. The force changes the object's
   direction, not its energy.

Two traps to avoid. First, **work is not effort**: carrying a box
at constant height across a room does zero work on the box in the
vertical direction (your upward force is perpendicular to the
horizontal motion), even though your muscles burn energy
internally. Second, **only the component along the displacement
counts**: a force at an angle does `F cos θ` worth of work per
metre, not the full `F`.

### Worked example 1 — force along the motion

A learner pushes a crate with a constant horizontal force of
60 N, and the crate slides 8.0 m across the floor. How much work
does the push do?

The force is along the displacement, so `θ = 0°` and
`cos θ = 1`:

```
W = Fd cos θ = (60 N)(8.0 m)(1) = 480 J
```

The push transfers 480 J into the crate's motion (and, as
Lesson 05 will show, into warming the floor — but the *push*
does +480 J).

### Worked example 2 — force at an angle

A rope pulls a sled with a force of 100 N at 30° above the
horizontal, and the sled moves 5.0 m horizontally. How much work
does the rope do?

Only the horizontal component of the pull acts along the
displacement: `F cos θ = (100 N)(cos 30°) = (100 N)(0.866) =
86.6 N`. Then:

```
W = (86.6 N)(5.0 m) = 433 J
```

Or in one step: `W = (100 N)(5.0 m)(cos 30°) = 433 J`
(three significant figures). The vertical component lifts
slightly but moves nothing vertically, so it does no work.

### Worked example 3 — negative work and zero work

A crate slides 4.0 m across a floor while kinetic friction of
25 N opposes the motion. (a) How much work does friction do?
(b) How much work does the normal force do?

(a) Friction points opposite the displacement, so
`θ = 180°` and `cos θ = −1`:

```
W_friction = (25 N)(4.0 m)(−1) = −100 J
```

Friction removes 100 J from the crate's motion.

(b) The normal force points straight up; the displacement is
horizontal. `θ = 90°`, `cos θ = 0`, so `W_normal = 0 J`.
The normal force holds the crate up but transfers no energy.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult or a partner; check each answer against the
answer key only after writing your reasoning.

1. A force of 40 N pushes a box 6.0 m in the direction of the
   push. Find the work.
2. A force of 80 N acts at 60° to the displacement while an object
   moves 10 m. Find the work. (`cos 60° = 0.500`.)
3. A learner holds a 150 N weight motionless overhead for 20 s.
   How much physics work is done on the weight? Explain in one
   sentence.
4. A 2.0 kg book falls 3.0 m straight down. Gravity pulls down
   with force `mg = 19.6 N`. How much work does gravity do?
   Positive or negative?
5. A crate is pushed 3.0 m by a 50 N horizontal force while
   friction of 30 N opposes the motion. Find the work done by
   the push, by friction, and the net work (their sum).

## Independent practice

1. A gardener pulls a cart with 25 N along its path for 12 m.
   Find the work.
2. A force of 200 N acts at 45° to the displacement while a sled
   moves 8.0 m. Find the work. (`cos 45° = 0.707`.)
3. A car drives at constant velocity on a level road. How much
   work does the normal force do on the car? Why?
4. A mover pushes a box 6.0 m with 90 N while friction of 40 N
   opposes. Find the work of the push, of friction, and the net
   work.
5. A 5.0 kg box is lifted straight up 2.0 m at constant velocity.
   Gravity does negative work; the lifting force does positive
   work. Find both, and the net work. What does a net work of
   zero tell you about the box's kinetic energy? (Lesson 02
   answers this fully — make a prediction now.)

## Applied task — the work audit

Pick one everyday action (dragging a suitcase, pushing a
shopping cart, pulling a sled). List every force acting on the
object, state the direction of each force relative to the motion,
and say whether its work is positive, negative, or zero — with
one sentence of reasoning per force. No numbers are required;
the reasoning is the product. The adult checks that no force is
missing (weight, normal force, applied force, friction) and that
no "effort" argument is used as a substitute for displacement.

## Exit check

1. A force of 30 N pushes an object 5.0 m in the direction of
   motion. Work = ?
2. True or false: "Holding a heavy box overhead all day is hard
   work in physics." Correct the statement if it is false.
3. A force acts at 90° to an object's displacement. Its work is
   ____ J. Why?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the three cases (along / opposite /
  perpendicular) are the whole lesson — re-read them before each
  practice item rather than memorizing the cosine values.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer the guided practice orally,
  drawing a small arrow diagram for each force and its angle,
  before writing numbers.
- **Extension:** a force of 120 N pushes a crate 4.0 m, but the
  crate's speed does not change. What must be true about the
  other forces' work? (Their total must be −480 J, canceling the
  push — preview of Lesson 02's theorem.)

## Teacher note

Misconceptions to watch: "work = effort" (holding still),
"work = force" (ignoring distance), and "any force at any angle
does full work" (ignoring the component). The answer key gives
full solutions; the traps are flagged there. Next lesson: what
net work *does* to an object — the work–energy theorem.
