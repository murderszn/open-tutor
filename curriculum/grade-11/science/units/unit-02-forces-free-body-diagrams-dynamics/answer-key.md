# Answer Key — Unit 02 (adults only)

Every numbered item in the six lessons, `practice.md`, `project.md`
(alternative dataset), the week-04 quiz, and the unit assessment,
solved independently. The student files contain no answers.
`g = 9.80 m/s²` throughout; three significant figures unless noted.

## Lesson 01 (items 1.1–1.8 and application)

**1.1.** Weight down (`W = mg`) and normal force up from the table.
The book is not accelerating, so the net force is zero.
**1.2.** The coin drops (nearly) straight down into the cup: it
keeps its horizontal velocity (zero) by inertia once the card is
gone, and gravity pulls it down.
**1.3.** Disagree. With engines off and (ideally) no net force, the
first law says the spacecraft keeps its velocity of 8,000 m/s; it
does not slow down on its own.
**1.4.** The passenger's body tends to keep its forward velocity
(inertia) while the car slows around them; the belt applies the
backward net force that changes the passenger's velocity. There is
no forward force pushing the passenger — the forward motion is the
motion being preserved, not a force.
**1.5.** The loaded (10. kg) cart is harder to get moving; mass
measures inertia. The answer does not change at 3.0 m/s: changing
an already-moving object's motion is also harder for the larger
mass.
**1.6.** The reasoning is wrong: the book does have forces — weight
down and normal force up — which balance, so the net force is zero.
"No acceleration" means "net force zero," not "no forces."
**1.7.** Friction with the grass (and air resistance) is the
unbalanced backward force that changes the ball's velocity. The
first law survives: a nonzero net force produces the slowing.
**1.8.** Error: inertia is not a force. Correct: "Inertia is an
object's resistance to a change in motion; a moving object keeps
moving because no net force acts to stop it."
**Application.** Cruising phase: net force zero (3,500 N forward
balanced by 3,500 N resistance); velocity constant. Accelerator
phase: net force = 5,000 − 3,500 = 1,500 N forward; the van
accelerates forward.

## Lesson 02 (items 2.1–2.8 and application)

**2.1.** Yes: the wall pushes back with 50 N, west (opposite the
push), by Newton's third law.
**2.2.** Not enough: "equal and opposite" alone does not make a
pair. Check that the forces act on two different objects and come
from one interaction. Weight (Earth on book) and normal force
(table on book) fail both tests.
**2.3.** Pair: swimmer's hands push water backward; water pushes
swimmer forward (same magnitude). The water's forward push on the
swimmer accelerates the swimmer.
**2.4.** Pair: hammer pushes nail downward with 200 N; nail pushes
hammer upward with 200 N. The nail moves because the net force on
the nail (200 N down minus wood resistance, and its small mass)
accelerates it strongly; the hammer stops because the net force on
the hammer (200 N up plus the hand's grip) decelerates its larger
effective system. Equal pair forces, different net forces.
**2.5.** (a) Weight's pair: the person pulls up on Earth with a
gravitational force equal to their weight. (b) Normal force's pair:
the person pushes down on the floor with a contact force equal to
the normal force. Each member acts on a different object from its
partner.
**2.6.** (a) Pair: tire pushes road backward; road pushes tire
(car) forward, equal magnitude. (b) Motion is decided per object:
the net force on the car is the road's forward push minus
resistance; when it is forward, the car accelerates forward. The
backward push on the road acts on the road (and Earth), not on
the car, so it never cancels the car's forward push.
**2.7.** (a) Both feel 120 N — a pair is always equal. (b) The
50 kg skater's velocity changes more: same force, smaller mass,
larger acceleration (`a = F/m`), and less inertia.
**2.8.** Two errors: (1) weight and normal force are not a
third-law pair (same object, different interactions); (2) the
third law is not about forces canceling — cancellation is a
net-force statement about one object. Correct: "Weight and normal
force on the book balance, so the net force on the book is zero
and it stays at rest (first law). The weight's pair is the book's
pull on Earth; the normal force's pair is the book's push on the
table."
**Application.** The spectator confuses the pair with the net
force. The rope's motion is decided by the net force on the rope:
if one team pulls harder than the other (or than the other team's
grip/friction can resist), the net force on the rope is toward the
stronger team and that team wins. Equal pulls mean zero net force
and no motion — not a law that pulls must always be equal.

## Lesson 03 (items 3.1–3.8 and application)

**3.1.** Forces on the crate: weight down, normal force up, applied
push right, kinetic friction left. The horizontal balancers are
the push and friction (equal, since velocity is constant).
**3.2.** After release, only weight acts (air resistance ignored).
The throwing force ended at release — a force needs contact (or a
field) acting now, not in the past.
**3.3.** Forces: weight `W = 5.00 × 9.80 = 49.0 N` down; tension
`T = 49.0 N` up (at rest, net force zero). Net force zero.
**3.4.** Forces: weight 49.0 N down; tension 60.0 N up. Net force =
60.0 − 49.0 = **11.0 N upward** (so the box accelerates upward).
**3.5.** Forces on the book: weight straight down; normal force
perpendicular to the ramp, away from the surface; static friction
parallel to the ramp, up the slope (opposing the downhill slide
that would otherwise occur).
**3.6.** Forces: weight down, normal force up. The person
accelerates upward, so the net force is upward: normal force
**greater than** weight (`N − W = ma > 0`).
**3.7.** Forces: weight down, normal force perpendicular to the
surface, tension angled 30.0° above horizontal, kinetic friction
horizontal backward (opposing motion). The rope's upward
component carries part of the weight, so the surface need only
supply `N = W − T sin 30.0° < W`.
**3.8.** Errors: (1) the "throwing force" is invented — contact
ended at release; (2) a force larger than weight upward would mean
upward acceleration, but the ball actually accelerates downward.
Correct diagram: weight down only; net force downward; the ball
slows on the way up because its acceleration is downward.
**Application.** First diagram (not moving, 6.00 N pull): weight
`W = 2.00 × 9.80 = 19.6 N` down; normal 19.6 N up; pull 6.00 N;
**static** friction 6.00 N opposite the pull; net force zero.
Second diagram (sliding at constant velocity, 12.0 N pull):
weight 19.6 N down; normal 19.6 N up; pull 12.0 N; **kinetic**
friction 12.0 N opposite the pull (constant velocity ⇒ net
force zero, so friction equals the pull).

## Lesson 04 (items 4.1–4.8 and application)

**4.1.** `a = 12.0 / 4.00 = 3.00 m/s²` in the direction of the net force.
**4.2.** Normal force = 98.0 N (no vertical acceleration). The
mistake: "N = 98.0 kg" confuses a force (newtons) with a mass
(kilograms); the block's mass is 98.0 / 9.80 = 10.0 kg.
**4.3.** Net force = `3.00 × 2.50 = 7.50 N`. If the applied force
is 15.0 N, friction = 15.0 − 7.50 = **7.50 N** opposing the motion.
**4.4.** (a) Mass = 49.0 / 9.80 = **5.00 kg**. (b) `N = 49.0 N`;
`f_k = 0.250 × 49.0 = 12.3 N` (12.25 → 12.3 N, three significant
figures).
**4.5.** On a ramp, `N = mg cos θ`, not `mg`, so `f = μN` must use
`N = mg cos θ`. Using weight overstates friction (and the normal
force) whenever `θ ≠ 0`. Correct: `f_k = μ_k mg cos θ`.
**4.6.** (a) `a = 1.50 / 0.500 = 3.00 m/s²`, opposite the motion
(deceleration). (b) `t = 12.0 / 3.00 = 4.00 s` to stop.
**4.7.** (a) `N = 8.00 × 9.80 = 78.4 N`. (b) `f_k = 0.300 × 78.4 =
23.5 N`. (c) Net force = 60.0 − 23.5 = 36.5 N; `a = 36.5 / 8.00 =
4.56 m/s²` forward.
**4.8.** Errors: (1) `10 × 9.80` is the object's *weight*, not a
general `F = ma` result; (2) 98.0 N is a force, not an acceleration
(the units m/s² are wrong for that number). Correct for free fall:
net force = weight = 98.0 N, so `a = 98.0 / 10 = 9.80 m/s²`
downward.
**Application.** Drive force needed: `F − 40.0 = 25.0 × 1.20 =
30.0 N`, so `F = 70.0 N`. Loaded: `F − 80.0 = 50.0 × 1.20 =
60.0 N`, so `F = 140 N`. The answer exactly doubles here (both the
mass term and the friction term doubled in this constructed case);
in general it need not double, because friction need not scale
with mass in the same way — the lesson is to compute both terms,
not to scale the total by intuition.

## Lesson 05 (items 5.1–5.8 and application)

**5.1.** Weight = `10.0 × 9.80 = 98.0 N` down; normal force =
**98.0 N** up (flat surface, no vertical acceleration).
**5.2.** x-component (adjacent, cosine) = `100 × cos 30.0° =
86.6 N`; y-component (opposite, sine) = `100 × sin 30.0° =
50.0 N`.
**5.3.** (a) Diagram: weight straight down; normal force
perpendicular to the ramp; kinetic friction up the slope
(opposing downhill sliding). (b) Weight = `12.0 × 9.80 = 118 N`
(117.6 N); normal force = `118 × 0.906 = 107 N`; downhill
component = `118 × 0.423 = 49.9 N` (49.7 N unrounded).
**5.4.** Weight = 39.2 N; `N = 39.2 × 0.940 = 36.8 N`;
`a = g sin 20.0° = 9.80 × 0.342 = 3.35 m/s²`. An 8.00 kg block has
the *same* acceleration: mass cancels in `a = g sin θ`.
**5.5.** (a) Downhill component = `29.4 × 0.574 = 16.9 N`.
(b) Static friction = **16.9 N** up the slope (equilibrium along
the slope). (c) `μ_s` is not needed: equilibrium determines the
friction force directly; `μ_s` would only give the *maximum*
possible static friction.
**5.6.** Weight = 58.8 N; `N = 58.8 × 0.906 = 53.3 N`;
`f_k = 0.150 × 53.3 = 7.99 N`; downhill = `58.8 × 0.423 =
24.9 N`; net = 24.9 − 7.99 = 16.9 N; `a = 16.9 / 6.00 =
2.81 m/s²` downhill.
**5.7.** Weight = `70.0 × 9.80 = 686 N`; `N = 686 × 0.985 =
676 N`; `a = 9.80 × 0.174 = 1.70 m/s²`. Mass does not appear
because both the driving force (`mg sin θ`) and the inertia
(`m`) scale with mass, so mass cancels in `a = F/m`.
**5.8.** Error: the normal force on a slope is `mg cos θ`, not
`mg`. Correct: `N = 49.0 × 0.866 = 42.4 N`;
`f_k = 0.200 × 42.4 = 8.48 N`. The learner's friction is too
large because it was computed from the full weight instead of
the (smaller) normal force.
**Application.** (a) Total weight = `105 × 9.80 = 1,030 N`
(1,029 N); downhill force = `1,029 × 0.0872 = 89.7 N`;
`a = 89.7 / 105 = 0.854 m/s²`. (b) Even at 5.00°, the downhill
force is about 90 N — roughly the weight of a 9 kg object
pulling continuously — so without a brake the chair steadily
gains speed; the small angle does not make the force negligible.

## Lesson 06 (items 6.1–6.8 and application)

**6.1.** `a = 12.0 / 6.00 = 2.00 m/s²` for the whole unit.
**6.2.** At rest, tension = weight = `2.00 × 9.80 = 19.6 N`.
Accelerating upward at 2.00 m/s², tension *rises*:
`T = m(g + a) = 2.00 × 11.8 = 23.6 N` (the rope must support
weight and provide upward acceleration).
**6.3.** (a) `a = (5.00 − 3.00) × 9.80 / 8.00 = 2.45 m/s²`.
(b) Lighter mass up: `T = 3.00 × (9.80 + 2.45) = 36.8 N`;
heavier mass down: `T = 5.00 × (9.80 − 2.45) = 36.8 N`. Agree.
**6.4.** (a) `a = 2.00 × 9.80 / 8.00 = 2.45 m/s²`. (b) Hanging:
`T = 2.00 × (9.80 − 2.45) = 14.7 N`; table block:
`T = 6.00 × 2.45 = 14.7 N`. Agree. (c) Held at rest,
`T = 2.00 × 9.80 = 19.6 N` — larger, because the hanging mass
is in equilibrium then, not accelerating downward.
**6.5.** (a) `a = 25.0 / 5.00 = 5.00 m/s²`. (b) Front block alone:
contact force = `3.00 × 5.00 = 15.0 N`. (c) Rear block alone:
`25.0 − F = 2.00 × 5.00 = 10.0 N`, so `F = 15.0 N`. Agree.
**6.6.** (a) Downhill component = `39.2 × 0.500 = 19.6 N`.
(b) Hanging weight = `3.00 × 9.80 = 29.4 N`, larger than the
downhill component, so the system accelerates *up* the slope:
`a = (29.4 − 19.6) / 7.00 = 1.40 m/s²`. (c) Hanging mass:
`T = 29.4 − 3.00 × 1.40 = 25.2 N`; incline block:
`T = 19.6 + 4.00 × 1.40 = 25.2 N`. Agree.
**6.7.** System: total mass 20.0 kg; net external force along
motion = 60.0 − 20.0 = 40.0 N; `a = 40.0 / 20.0 = 2.00 m/s²`.
Second sled alone: tension = `10.0 × 2.00 = 20.0 N`. (Check on
the first sled: 60.0 − 20.0 − 20.0 = 20.0 N = 10.0 × 2.00. ✓)
**6.8.** Error: the lighter mass holds the heavier one back, so
the system does not fall freely. Correct:
`a = (4.00 − 1.00) × 9.80 / 5.00 = 5.88 m/s²` — less than `g`
because the total mass being accelerated includes the lighter
mass, while only the mass *difference* drives the motion.
**Application.** (a) Elevator side falls: net force =
`(800 − 700) × 9.80 = 980 N`; total mass 1,500 kg;
`a = 980 / 1,500 = 0.653 m/s²`. (b) Counterweight (rising):
`T = 700 × (9.80 + 0.653) = 7.32 × 10³ N` (7,317 N); check on
the elevator: `7,840 − 800 × 0.653 = 7,320 N` (rounding).
(c) The motor must overcome real friction, accelerate the
system on demand (including starting from rest and stopping
precisely at floors), and hold the car against unbalanced
loads — the idealized Atwood model has no friction, no motor
torque, and no braking or safety systems.

## Practice Set A

**A1.** (a) Net force zero (constant velocity). (b) Backward force
= **1,200 N**: the engine's push balances resistance; net force is
the *sum*, and balanced forces sum to zero however large they are.
**A2.** Pair: astronaut pushes wall (station) one way; station
pushes astronaut the opposite way, equal magnitude. The astronaut
moves visibly because their mass is small; the station's
acceleration from the same force is imperceptibly small
(`a = F/m` with huge `m`).
**A3.** The true pair of the book's weight (Earth pulls book down)
is the book pulling Earth up with the same gravitational force.
The table's push on the book is a different interaction acting on
the same object as the weight — not a pair.
**A4.** Forces during flight: weight down only (air resistance
ignored). No — the club's force ended at contact; nothing in
flight continues to push the ball.
**A5.** (a) Each feels **150 N**. (b) P (60.0 kg) changes velocity
more: same force, smaller mass, larger acceleration.
**A6.** (a) Pair: truck pushes car; car pushes truck, 20,000 N
each, opposite directions. (b) Equal forces produce very different
accelerations (`a = F/m`): the car's acceleration is about 6.7
times the truck's, and its structure absorbs far more deformation
per unit mass. Equal force does not mean equal damage.

## Practice Set F

**F1.** Accept any accurate one-sentence statements: (1) an object
keeps its velocity unless a net force acts (example: a puck on
ice); (2) `F_net = ma` (example: pushing a heavy cart vs. a light
one); (3) forces come in equal, opposite pairs on two objects
(example: jumping — feet push ground down/back, ground pushes
feet up/forward). Reject examples that reuse lesson examples
verbatim where the item asks for new ones, and credit novel
examples that are physically correct.
**F2.** (a) Diagram: weight 39.2 N down; normal 39.2 N up; push
30.0 N; kinetic friction `0.200 × 39.2 = 7.84 N` opposing.
(b) Net = 22.2 N; `a = 22.2 / 4.00 = 5.54 m/s²`.
**F3.** Not sound. "Not moving relative to the elevator" does not
mean "not accelerating": the elevator (and book) accelerate upward
together, so the net force on the book is upward and `N > W`.
Relative rest and inertial rest are different claims.
**F4.** Weight = 68.6 N; `N = 68.6 × 0.866 = 59.4 N`; downhill =
34.3 N; `f_k = 0.100 × 59.4 = 5.94 N`; net = 28.4 N;
`a = 28.4 / 7.00 = 4.05 m/s²`. Frictionless: `9.80 × 0.500 =
4.90 m/s²`. Friction cost the block **0.85 m/s²** of acceleration.
**F5.** The massless, frictionless-pulley assumption is violated
(pulley friction / rotational inertia). The two-way tension check
fails in the simple form: tension now differs on the two sides of
the pulley, because part of the weight difference goes into
overcoming pulley friction (and spinning the pulley) rather than
accelerating the masses.
**F6.** The action partner is the exhaust gases: the rocket pushes
exhaust backward (down), and the exhaust pushes the rocket
forward (up) with equal magnitude. The rocket accelerates because
the net force *on the rocket* (thrust minus weight, in a gravity
field) is nonzero — it never needed an external surface to push
against.

## Project — simulation alternative dataset (fictional practice data)

Mean accelerations from `a = 2Δx / t²`, `Δx = 0.800 m`:

| θ | a_predicted (m/s²) | a_measured (m/s²) | Percent difference | Implied μ_k |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10° | 1.70 | 1.11 | 34.7% below | 0.061 |
| 15° | 2.54 | 1.67 | 34.3% below | 0.092 |
| 20° | 3.35 | 2.27 | 32.4% below | 0.118 |

(Implied `μ_k = tan θ − a / (g cos θ)`.) Judgment: a single
constant friction coefficient does **not** explain these data
well — the implied value roughly doubles from 10° to 20°, so
friction alone, with one `μ_k`, is an incomplete explanation;
timing bias, a changing surface, or angle error must also be
considered. The consistent ~33% shortfall does, however, rule out
the frictionless prediction: measured values are systematically
below `g sin θ`, not scattered around it.
