# Lesson 02 — Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

**Unit 02 · Session S3 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** unit objective 2 (track objective 4)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can name a third-law pair correctly
— both forces, both objects, both directions; I can explain why
a pair never cancels on one object; and I can explain why weight
and normal force on the same object are generally not a pair.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil. (Optional: two bathroom scales or spring
balances, adult-supervised, for the readiness demonstration.)

## Readiness check (5 min)

2.1. You push a wall with 50 N. Does the wall push back? With how
much force, and in which direction?
2.2. A book rests on a table. A learner says, "The book's weight
down and the table's normal force up are a third-law pair,
because they are equal and opposite." Is that reasoning enough?
What else would you need to check?

## Explicit teaching

**Newton's third law** says:

> When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts a
> force on object A that is equal in magnitude and opposite in
> direction.

The two forces are a **third-law pair** (an action–reaction pair).
A pair always satisfies all four of these tests:

1. **Two different objects.** One force acts on A, the other on B.
2. **Same type of interaction.** Both are gravitational, both are
   contact pushes, both are friction — the pair is one interaction
   seen from two sides, never two different interactions.
3. **Equal magnitude, opposite direction.** Always — whether the
   objects are moving, at rest, big, or small.
4. **Same moment in time.** The pair exists together; one does not
   "cause" the other later.

Because the two forces act on **different objects**, they never
cancel each other. Cancellation only happens when two forces act
on the *same* object and are added into that object's net force.
This dissolves the famous "paradoxes":

- **The horse and cart.** The horse pulls the cart forward; the
  cart pulls the horse backward with an equal force. If those were
  the only forces, nothing would move — but they are not. The
  horse also pushes backward on the *ground*, and the ground
  pushes the horse forward. The cart moves because the net force
  *on the cart* is forward (the horse's pull minus friction); the
  horse moves because the net force *on the horse* is forward
  (the ground's push minus the cart's pull). Decide motion one
  object at a time.
- **Walking.** Your foot pushes backward on the ground; the
  ground pushes you forward. Without that forward push from the
  ground, you could not accelerate forward — which is why walking
  on ice is hard: the ground cannot push back as hard.

**The weight–normal trap.** A book on a table has weight pulling
down (Earth on book) and a normal force pushing up (table on
book). They are equal and opposite *when the book is at rest* —
but they are not a third-law pair. They act on the *same* object
(the book), and they are different interactions (gravity vs.
contact). The real pairs are:

- Weight's pair: the book pulls *up* on Earth with a gravitational
  force equal to its weight.
- Normal force's pair: the book pushes *down* on the table with a
  contact force equal to the normal force.

Proof that weight and normal are not a pair: put the book on a
table in an elevator accelerating upward. The normal force grows
larger than the weight — but the book's gravitational pull on
Earth does not change. A true pair always stays equal; these two
do not.

**Magnitude does not follow size.** A truck and a bicycle collide.
The truck exerts the same magnitude of force on the bicycle as
the bicycle exerts on the truck. The bicycle is wrecked and the
truck barely slows — not because the forces differ, but because
the same force produces a much larger acceleration on the much
smaller mass (Lesson 04: `a = F_net / m`). Equal forces, unequal
effects.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — The hand and the wall.** You push a wall with
50 N to the east.

*Reasoning:* The pair is: (a) your hand pushes the wall east
with 50 N; (b) the wall pushes your hand west with 50 N. Two
objects (hand, wall), one interaction (contact push), equal
magnitude, opposite direction. Why doesn't the wall move? The
net force *on the wall* includes the ground's friction and the
building's structure holding it — your 50 N is not the wall's
whole story. Why don't *you* move backward? Because friction on
your shoes balances the wall's 50 N push on you. If you stood on
ice, that same 50 N pair would send you sliding backward.

**Example 2 — The rocket.** A rocket engine throws exhaust gases
downward at high speed.

*Reasoning:* The pair is: (a) the rocket pushes exhaust gases
down; (b) the exhaust gases push the rocket up, with equal
magnitude. The rocket does not need air or ground to "push
against" — the reaction partner is the exhaust itself, which the
rocket carries with it. That is why rockets work in the vacuum
of space. The upward force on the rocket is one force on the
rocket; whether the rocket accelerates upward depends on the
net force on the rocket (thrust minus weight), not on the pair
canceling anything.

## Guided practice

For each item, write the pair in the form: "X on Y (direction,
magnitude if given); Y on X (opposite direction, same magnitude)."

2.3. A swimmer pushes water backward with their hands. Name the
pair, and say which member of the pair accelerates the swimmer.
2.4. A hammer hits a nail downward with 200 N. Name the pair.
Explain why the nail moves into the wood while the hammer stops
— without claiming the forces were unequal.
2.5. A 60 kg person stands on the floor. Name the pairs for (a)
the person's weight and (b) the floor's normal force on the
person. State which object each member acts on.

## Independent practice

2.6. A car tire pushes backward on the road. (a) Name the pair.
(b) Explain, one object at a time, why the car moves forward
even though the pair is equal and opposite.
2.7. Two ice skaters, one 50 kg and one 80 kg, push off each other
from rest with a pair of 120 N forces. (a) Which skater feels the
larger force? (b) Which skater's velocity changes more, and why?
(Use Lesson 01's inertia idea; the calculation is Lesson 04.)
2.8. (Error analysis.) A learner writes: "The book's weight and
the table's normal force cancel, so the book stays still. That
is Newton's third law." Identify the two errors in that sentence
(what cancels, and what the third law actually says), and
rewrite it correctly.

## Application

A tug-of-war team pulls a rope with 800 N; the opposing team
pulls back with 800 N, and the rope does not move. A spectator
says, "The third law says the pulls are equal, so tug-of-war can
never have a winner." Explain the spectator's error: what would
have to change for one team to win, and which object's net force
decides the rope's motion? (Numbers are practice values.)

## Exit check

Name the third-law pair when a magnet pulls a paperclip toward
it. State both forces, both objects, and why the paperclip
moves while the magnet (held in a hand) does not fly to the
paperclip.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** use the sentence frame "___ pushes
  ___; ___ pushes ___ back" for every pair until the two-object
  habit is automatic.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw two boxes, one per object, and
  draw one arrow on each box — never two arrows on one box for a
  pair.
- **Extension:** explain, in a paragraph, how a jet engine's
  third-law pair differs from a rocket's (what is thrown backward
  in each case), and why both work without touching the ground.

## Teacher note

Insist on the two-box habit from the first practice item: most
third-law errors are really bookkeeping errors — both arrows
drawn on one object. The elevator counterexample is the cleanest
test for the weight–normal trap; use it whenever a learner
argues from "equal and opposite" alone. Answer-key reference:
items 2.1–2.8 and the application.
