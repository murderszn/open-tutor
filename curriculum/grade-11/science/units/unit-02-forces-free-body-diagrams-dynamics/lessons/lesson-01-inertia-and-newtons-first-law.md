# Lesson 01 — Inertia and Newton's First Law

**Unit 02 · Session S1 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** unit objective 1 (track objective 4)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can state Newton's first law in my own
words; I can explain why a moving object does not need a force to
keep moving; and I can decide, for a described situation, whether
the net force on an object is zero or nonzero.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, calculator. A coin and a flat card (for the
readiness demonstration, adult-supervised).

## Readiness check (5 min)

1.1. A book sits at rest on a table. Name two forces acting on the
book, and say whether the book is accelerating.
1.2. A coin sits on a card over a cup. The card is flicked away
quickly. Predict what the coin does, and why, in one sentence.
(You will test this after the teaching — do not flick anything yet.)

## Explicit teaching

**A force is a push or a pull on an object, measured in newtons
(N).** It is a vector: it has a magnitude and a direction. But the
quantity that decides motion is not any single force — it is the
**net force**, the vector sum of all the forces on one object:

- If the forces on an object balance, the net force is **zero**.
- If they do not balance, the net force is **nonzero**, and it
  points in the direction of the imbalance.

**Newton's first law** (the law of inertia) says:

> An object keeps its velocity — its speed *and* direction —
> unless a nonzero net force acts on it. An object at rest stays
> at rest; an object in motion keeps moving at the same velocity.

Read that sentence again, because everyday language fights it.
Nothing in the law says a moving object "runs out of force" or
"needs a push to keep going." A hockey puck sliding on nearly
frictionless ice keeps sliding not because some force carries it
along, but because almost no net force acts on it. Friction and air
resistance are the forces that slow real pucks down — remove them,
and the puck would keep going.

**Inertia** is the name for this resistance to a *change* in
motion. Inertia is not a force, and it is not something an object
"has more of" when it moves faster. Inertia grows with **mass**:
a loaded shopping cart is harder to start, harder to stop, and
harder to turn than the same cart empty, at any speed. Mass is the
measure of inertia.

Three cases to sort every first-law question into:

1. **At rest, net force zero** — stays at rest. A book on a table.
2. **Moving at constant velocity, net force zero** — keeps that
   velocity. A car cruising with the engine's forward push exactly
   balancing friction and air resistance. The engine is still
   working — but the *net* force is zero, so velocity does not change.
3. **Net force nonzero** — velocity changes (speed up, slow down,
   or turn). This is acceleration, and it is Lesson 04's subject.
   Note the trap: case 2 has forces acting, and case 3 is the only
   one with acceleration. "Forces are acting" and "net force is
   nonzero" are different claims.

**Frames of reference.** The first law holds in an *inertial
frame* — a frame that is itself not accelerating. A bus cruising
at constant velocity is (nearly) an inertial frame; a bus braking
hard is not, which is why you lurch forward when it brakes: your
body is obeying the first law while the bus is not. In this unit,
the ground (or a steadily moving vehicle) is our frame unless a
problem says otherwise.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — The coin and the card.** The coin sits on a card
over a cup. The card is flicked away horizontally.

*Reasoning:* The flick applies a horizontal force to the *card*,
not to the coin. Horizontally, almost no force acts on the coin
(only tiny friction from the card, acting for a very short time).
By the first law, the coin keeps its horizontal velocity — zero.
It does not follow the card sideways. Vertically, gravity pulls
it down once the card is gone, so it drops straight into the cup.
The coin's "refusal" to move sideways is inertia, not a force
holding it in place.

**Example 2 — The cruising car.** A 1,200 kg car travels at a
constant 20 m/s on a flat road. The engine pushes forward with
2,000 N. What is the net force, and what resists the car?

*Reasoning:* Constant velocity means velocity is not changing,
so by the first law the net force must be zero. The forward
2,000 N must be exactly balanced by 2,000 N backward from
friction and air resistance combined. If the engine pushed
harder, the net force would be forward and the car would speed
up; if the driver lifted off, friction would be the unbalanced
force and the car would slow down. "The car needs 2,000 N to
keep moving" is wrong: it needs 2,000 N to *balance* the
resisting forces. With no resistance at all, it would need no
forward push to keep moving.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult or a partner; say the case (1, 2, or 3)
out loud before answering.

1.3. A spacecraft far from any planet, engines off, drifts at
8,000 m/s. A learner says, "With the engines off, it will slow
down and stop." Agree or disagree, and explain using the first law.
1.4. A seatbelt holds a passenger during a sudden stop. Explain,
in terms of inertia, why the passenger keeps moving forward when
the car stops — and why the belt, not "a forward force," is what
stops them.
1.5. Two carts, one empty (2.0 kg) and one loaded (10. kg), are
both pushed from rest. Which is harder to get moving, and what
property decides that? Would the answer change if both were
already rolling at 3.0 m/s?

## Independent practice

1.6. A book rests on a table in a train moving at constant
velocity. A learner says the book has no forces on it "because
it is not accelerating." Correct the reasoning: name the forces,
and state the net force.
1.7. A soccer ball rolls across grass and stops. A learner says,
"This breaks the first law, because nothing pushed it." Explain
what actually slowed it, and why the first law survives.
1.8. (Error analysis.) A learner writes: "Inertia is the force
that keeps a moving object moving." Identify the error, and
rewrite the sentence correctly.

## Application

A delivery van cruises at constant velocity on a highway while its
engine produces a forward force of 3,500 N. The driver then
presses the accelerator, and the forward force rises to 5,000 N
while resistance stays at 3,500 N. Describe the van's motion in
each phase, and state the net force in each phase. (Numbers are
practice values.)

## Exit check

A crate slides across a smooth warehouse floor at constant
velocity after a single push. Is a continuing forward force
needed to keep it moving? Explain in two sentences, using the
words *net force* and *inertia*.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** say "velocity stays the same"
  instead of "inertia" until the idea is secure, then attach the
  word to the idea you already stated.
- **Alternate response mode:** act out the three cases with a
  rolling ball — at rest, rolling steadily, rolling into a cushion
  — and narrate which case each one is.
- **Extension:** research, in one paragraph, why a rotating frame
  (such as a merry-go-round) is not an inertial frame, and name
  one everyday effect that shows it.

## Teacher note

The durable errors are "motion needs a force" and "inertia is a
force." Both return in Lessons 04–06 whenever a learner invents a
force to explain steady motion. The coin demonstration is worth
doing live (adult-supervised, eye protection if the card is
flicked hard) — it beats any paragraph. Answer-key reference:
items 1.1–1.8 and the application.
