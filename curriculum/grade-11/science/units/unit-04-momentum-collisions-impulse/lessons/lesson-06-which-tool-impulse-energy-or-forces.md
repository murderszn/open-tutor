# Lesson 06 — Which Tool: Impulse, Energy, or Forces?

**Unit 04 · Session S13 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** unit objectives 2, 3, 4, 5 (track objective 6); midyear
synthesis of Units 01–04

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can choose, for a new problem, whether
impulse–momentum, work–energy, or Newton's laws is the right tool —
and I can check one tool's answer with another where both apply.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, calculator.

## Readiness check (5 min)

6.1. A problem gives you a stopping **time**. A second problem
gives you a stopping **distance**. Which quantity — impulse or work —
fits each problem naturally, and why?

6.2. A cart's velocity changes in a collision. Two of the unit's
tools describe that change. Name them, and say what extra
information each one needs.

## Explicit teaching

Units 01–04 have now given the learner three ways to attack a
mechanics problem. They are not rivals; they answer different
questions, and a strong solution picks the tool that matches the
given information:

| Given / asked | Best first tool | Key equation |
|---|---|---|
| Force and time, or a velocity change | Impulse–momentum | `J = FΔt = Δp` |
| Force and distance, or a speed from a height | Work–energy | `W = Fd = ΔKE`; `PE = mgh` |
| Acceleration, or forces on one object | Newton's laws / free-body diagram | `F_net = ma` |
| Two objects colliding, one unknown velocity | Conservation of momentum | `m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁′ + m₂v₂′` |
| Whether a collision was elastic | Momentum **and** kinetic-energy test | Both totals, before and after |

**Two checks that catch errors.** First, a problem solvable two ways
must give the same answer both ways: a stopping cart's time (from
impulse) and its distance (from work) describe the same stop, and
inconsistent answers mean an arithmetic error somewhere. Second,
momentum answers carry signs and energy answers do not: if a
momentum solution ignores direction, or an energy solution invents
one, something is wrong.

**Systems and rockets — the idea behind the extension.** A rocket
is a momentum-conservation story: the rocket plus its exhaust form
a system with (ideally) no net external force, so as exhaust gas
gains backward momentum, the rocket gains equal forward momentum.
No external push is needed, which is why rockets work in space.
The same reasoning explains recoil in Lesson 03: the system total
stays put while its parts move apart.

**Midyear checkpoint preview.** Session S19's cumulative problems
mix all four units: a projectile's range (U01), a free-body diagram
and incline acceleration (U02), an energy budget (U03), and a
collision (this unit). The habit this lesson builds — name the
given quantities, pick the tool, check with a second tool where
possible — is the checkpoint's real subject.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — One stop, two tools.** A 2.0 kg cart moves at 6.0 m/s
and is stopped by a constant friction force of 30 N. (a) How long
does the stop take? (b) How far does the cart travel while stopping?

*Reasoning:* Part (a) gives a force and asks a time — impulse.
Part (b) gives a force and asks a distance — work–energy. The two
answers must describe the same stop.

- (a) `Δp = (2.0 kg)(0 − 6.0 m/s) = −12 kg·m/s`; magnitude 12.
  `Δt = |Δp| / F = 12 / 30 = 0.40 s`.
- (b) `KE = ½(2.0)(6.0)² = 36 J`. Work done by friction:
  `Fd = 36 J`, so `d = 36 / 30 = 1.2 m`.

Check: average speed during a uniform stop from 6.0 m/s is
3.0 m/s, and `3.0 m/s × 0.40 s = 1.2 m` ✓. The tools agree.

**Example 2 — Drop, then impact.** A 0.50 kg ball is dropped from
2.0 m and hits the ground. (a) How fast is it moving at impact?
(b) What is its momentum at impact?

*Reasoning:* Part (a) gives a height — energy. Part (b) follows
from the definition of momentum.

- (a) Energy: `v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.80 × 2.0) = √39.2 = 6.3 m/s`
  (6.26 rounded to two significant figures in the inputs' spirit;
  the answer key carries 6.3 m/s).
- (b) `p = mv = (0.50 kg)(6.3 m/s) = 3.1 kg·m/s` downward.

A learner who tried impulse here would be stuck: the fall time is
not given. The given quantity chooses the tool.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult or a partner; name the tool before
solving.

6.3. A 0.20 kg ball moves right at 5.0 m/s, bounces off a wall, and
moves left at 5.0 m/s. Find its momentum change, and its kinetic
energy before and after. Explain why impulse is nonzero while
kinetic-energy change is zero.

6.4. A 2.0 kg cart at 5.0 m/s sticks to a 3.0 kg cart at rest, and
the joined carts then slide to rest under a 5.0 N friction force.
(a) Find the common velocity after the collision (momentum tool).
(b) Find the sliding distance (work–energy tool, using the joined
carts' kinetic energy).

6.5. **Rockets, in words.** A rocket in deep space fires its engine.
Using conservation of momentum for the rocket-plus-exhaust system,
explain why the rocket accelerates forward even though nothing
pushes on it from outside, and say what gains the balancing
backward momentum.

## Independent practice

6.6. A 1.0 kg object moves at 4.0 m/s and is stopped by a 20 N
force. Find (a) the stopping time (impulse tool) and (b) the
stopping distance (work–energy tool), and check the two answers
against each other with average speed.

6.7. A 3.0 kg cart moves right at 2.0 m/s and sticks to a 1.0 kg
cart at rest. Find the common velocity, the kinetic energy before
and after, and the energy lost. State which tool gave each number.

6.8. **Error analysis.** A learner solves a stick-together
collision by conserving kinetic energy and gets a common velocity
larger than the momentum answer. Explain the error, name the law
that is actually conserved here, and say what happened to the
"missing" kinetic energy.

## Application

6.9. **Tool-choice table.** For each scenario, name the best first
tool and the equation, and say what a second-tool check would be:
(a) a car braking over a known distance; (b) a ball hit by a bat
during a known contact time; (c) two skaters pushing apart;
(d) a block sliding down a frictionless incline, asked for its
speed at the bottom; (e) a crash-test dummy's average force, given
the crash duration.

## Exit check

6.10. Complete the sentence in two versions: "When a problem gives
me a time, I reach for ___ because ___." and "When a problem gives
me a distance, I reach for ___ because ___."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Before solving, write "Given: …,
  Asked: …, Tool: …" as a header on every problem.
- **Alternate response mode:** A learner may present the tool
  choice as a flowchart (time? → impulse; distance? → work;
  collision? → momentum conservation) instead of a table.
- **Extension:** A 1,000 kg rocket in space expels 10 kg of exhaust
  backward at 2,000 m/s relative to the rocket's initial frame, from
  rest. By momentum conservation, `1,000v_rocket + 10(−2,000) = 0`
  in the simplified fixed-mass picture gives `v_rocket = 20 m/s`
  forward. Explain why this simplified picture overstates a real
  rocket's gain (the rocket's mass falls as exhaust leaves, and
  velocities must be tracked in a consistent frame), and why the
  direction of the conclusion — rocket forward, exhaust backward —
  is still correct.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: reaching for `F = ma` when no acceleration
is asked; using energy conservation in a sticky collision; treating
"check with a second tool" as optional decoration rather than the
error-catching step it is. Answers: see the answer key, Lesson 06.
The extension's 20 m/s is a simplified-model value, labelled as
such in the text; do not present it as a real rocket calculation.
