# Lesson 01 — Momentum: Mass Times Velocity

**Unit 04 · Session S1 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** unit objective 1 (track objective 6)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can calculate momentum with `p = mv`,
give its sign for direction and its units (kg·m/s), and explain why a
heavy slow object can be harder to stop than a light fast one.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, calculator. No bench apparatus is needed.

## Readiness check (5 min)

1.1. A bowling ball and a tennis ball roll at the same speed. Which
one would you rather try to stop with your hands, and what property
besides speed makes the difference?

1.2. A 1,000 kg car travels at 10 m/s, then later at 20 m/s. By what
factor does its "hardness to stop" change when its speed doubles, if
its mass does not change?

## Explicit teaching

**Momentum** is the quantity that measures how hard a moving object is
to stop. It combines the two things that matter — how much stuff is
moving, and how fast — into one number:

> `p = m × v`

where `p` is momentum, `m` is mass in kilograms, and `v` is velocity
in metres per second. The unit of momentum is therefore
kilogram-metres per second, kg·m/s. There is no special name for it;
write the full unit every time.

Momentum is a **vector**: it points in the direction of motion. In
this unit we work in one dimension, so direction is carried by a sign.
Our convention for the whole unit, stated once and used everywhere:

> **Right is positive. Left is negative.**

A 2.0 kg cart moving right at 3.0 m/s has momentum `+6.0 kg·m/s`.
The same cart moving left at 3.0 m/s has momentum `−6.0 kg·m/s`.
The magnitudes are equal; the momenta are not the same, because they
point opposite ways. That sign will do real work in Lesson 03, when
momenta moving in opposite directions partly cancel in a total.

Three consequences to notice before any calculation:

1. **At rest, momentum is zero.** Velocity is zero, so `p = 0`
   whatever the mass. A parked truck has no momentum, however heavy
   it is.
2. **Momentum scales with both factors.** Double the mass at the same
   velocity and momentum doubles; double the velocity at the same mass
   and momentum doubles. A heavy slow object can therefore carry more
   momentum than a light fast one — the comparison is decided by the
   product, never by mass or speed alone.
3. **Momentum is not energy and not force.** A later lesson checks
   kinetic energy (`½mv²`) alongside momentum: energy depends on
   speed squared and has no direction, while momentum depends on
   velocity to the first power and does. Keeping the two quantities
   separate is half the unit.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — A rolling ball.** A 0.50 kg ball rolls to the right at
6.0 m/s. What is its momentum?

*Reasoning:* Identify mass and velocity, then multiply, keeping the
sign for direction. Right is positive, so `v = +6.0 m/s`.

`p = mv = (0.50 kg)(+6.0 m/s) = +3.0 kg·m/s`

The ball's momentum is 3.0 kg·m/s to the right. Note the units:
kilograms times metres per second gives kg·m/s, exactly as the
definition requires — a useful check on every momentum problem.

**Example 2 — A car and a truck at the same speed.** A 1,200 kg car
and a 12,000 kg truck both travel to the right at 15 m/s. Compare
their momenta.

*Reasoning:* Same velocity, so the ratio of the momenta is the ratio
of the masses.

- Car: `p = (1,200 kg)(15 m/s) = 18,000 kg·m/s`
- Truck: `p = (12,000 kg)(15 m/s) = 180,000 kg·m/s`

The truck carries ten times the car's momentum — 180,000 against
18,000 kg·m/s — because it has ten times the mass at the same speed.
Stopping the truck is a ten-times-larger momentum-change job, which is
why Lesson 02's impulse and Lesson 05's safety design matter so much
for heavy vehicles. (These are practice values for comparison, not
measured vehicle data.)

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult or a partner; say the sign out loud before
writing the number.

1.3. A 2.5 kg cart rolls right at 4.0 m/s. Calculate its momentum.

1.4. A 0.20 kg ball moves left at 8.0 m/s. Calculate its momentum,
with sign.

1.5. An 80 kg runner moves at 6.0 m/s, and a 0.50 kg ball moves at
40 m/s, both to the right. Calculate both momenta. Which object is
harder to stop, and by what factor?

## Independent practice

1.6. A 3.0 kg object moves left at 2.5 m/s. Calculate its momentum.

1.7. An object has momentum +60 kg·m/s and mass 4.0 kg. Find its
velocity, with direction.

1.8. **Error analysis.** A learner says, "Momentum must be a scalar,
because mass is a scalar and you just multiply." Explain the error:
which factor carries direction, and why do two objects moving at the
same speed in opposite directions not have the same momentum?

## Application

1.9. A cyclist and bicycle together have mass 75 kg and travel at
8.0 m/s. Calculate their momentum, then compare it with the car in
Example 2 (18,000 kg·m/s). Express the comparison as "about 1/__ of
the car's momentum" and say in one sentence what that means for
stopping.

## Exit check

1.10. A 0.80 kg object moves right at 5.0 m/s. State its momentum
with sign and units, and state the sign convention used.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Say "momentum" as "mass times
  velocity, with direction" every time before writing the symbol `p`;
  the phrase is the definition.
- **Alternate response mode:** A learner who struggles with signs may
  answer with an arrow sketch (right/left) beside each number first,
  then convert the arrow to a sign.
- **Extension:** A 0.060 kg tennis ball served at 50 m/s has momentum
  3.0 kg·m/s — the same as Example 1's ball. Find another
  mass–velocity pair with momentum 3.0 kg·m/s, and explain why equal
  momentum does not mean equal kinetic energy (Lesson 04 returns to
  this).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: dropping the sign; writing the unit as N or J;
judging "harder to stop" by mass or speed alone. Answers: see the
answer key, Lesson 01. The sign convention (right = positive) is
re-used in every later lesson; correct it here, not in Lesson 03.
