# Project — A Movement Anthology and Interpretation

## The task

You are the editor of a small anthology: **five movements, three
headnotes, one interpretation**. The anthology argues, by
selection and commentary, that American literature is a
conversation between movements and voices — not a parade of
periods.

## Deliverables

**1. Three headnotes (one per movement cluster, 250–400 words
each).** Choose three of the five movements (Romanticism,
Realism, Naturalism, Modernism, Harlem Renaissance). Each
headnote contains:

- (a) The movement's belief in one paragraph: what it thought
  literature should do, and why that belief mattered in its time.
- (b) One anchor text from this unit that fits the movement, and
  one way that text *strains* the movement's belief (a trait that
  does not fit, a doubt the text raises, a voice the movement
  overlooked).
- (c) Two quotations from the anchor text, each followed by one
  sentence of analysis.

**2. A point-of-view essay (400–600 words).** Choose Gilman or
Chopin. Argue, with evidence, whose perspective centers the text
and what that centering reveals and conceals. Include at least
three quotations and one paragraph acknowledging the strongest
counter-reading.

**3. An interpretation (presented in Week 13).** Choose one
anthology text and present a two-minute spoken interpretation of
one passage: a staged reading, a storyboard, or a described
illustration. Your presentation must name one staging or visual
choice (position, sound, lighting, framing, or omission) and
defend it from the text in two sentences. You may use the
Lesson 05 illustration as a model, not as a substitute: your
interpretation must be your own.

**4. A one-page editor's introduction (150–250 words).** What
does your anthology argue, taken as a whole? What conversation
between movements and voices do your selections stage? End with
one question your anthology leaves open.

## Materials

Your notebook, the anchor texts, your movement field guide, the
practice sets, and library access for one additional text if you
want a fourth headnote (optional, not required). No fees, no
special equipment. If you record your interpretation, the
recording stays in private storage and is never shared outside
the household without the adult's review.

## Steps

1. **S16 — Plan.** Choose your three movements and your
   point-of-view text. Get the adult's approval of the plan:
   movements, texts, quotations, interpretation passage.
2. **S16–S17 — Draft.** Write the headnotes, then the essay,
   then the editor's introduction last (it is easier to say what
   the anthology argues after you have built it).
3. **S17 — Revise.** Trade drafts with the adult: check every
   quotation against its edition, every classification against
   the four-part format, and every interpretation choice against
   the four questions from Lesson 05.
4. **S20 — Present.** Deliver the two-minute interpretation in
   the Week 13 seminar. Answer one question from the adult or a
   listener with textual evidence.

## Rubric (40 points)

| Criterion | 10–9 (strong) | 8–7 (solid) | 6–5 (developing) | 4–0 (needs rework) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Movement understanding (10)** | Each headnote states the belief accurately, fits and strains it with evidence, quotations analyzed (not dropped in) | Beliefs accurate; fit shown; strain attempted; most quotations analyzed | Beliefs vague or partly wrong; fit by label only; quotations unanalyzed | Beliefs wrong or missing; labels without evidence |
| **Point-of-view essay (10)** | Centering argued with 3+ quotations; reveals and conceals both addressed; counter-reading fairly stated | Centering argued with quotations; reveals or conceals addressed; counter-reading present | Centering asserted more than argued; few quotations; counter-reading missing | Summary instead of argument; no quotations |
| **Interpretation (10)** | Choice named, defended from text in 2+ sentences; performance matches the defense; four-question thinking visible | Choice named and defended; performance mostly matches | Choice vague or undefended; performance unrelated to defense | No identifiable choice or defense |
| **Editor's introduction + craft (10)** | Anthology argument clear; open question genuine; prose organized, quoted accurately, standard English | Argument present; prose mostly organized; quotations mostly accurate | Argument unclear; organization weak; quotation errors | No argument; prose blocks understanding |

**Scoring notes.** A project can score highly while disagreeing
with the adult's reading, as long as the disagreement is argued
with evidence. A project that states the adult's reading without
evidence scores lower than a well-argued dissent. Quotations
must match the assigned editions; a misquotation found in
revision costs nothing if corrected, but an uncorrected one
costs accuracy points in the final version.

## What "done" looks like

A finished anthology reads as one argument, not three book
reports stapled together: the introduction's claim should be
visible in each headnote, and the interpretation should feel
like the anthology's last chapter, not an unrelated performance.
If your introduction could fit any anthology, rewrite it until
it could only fit yours.
