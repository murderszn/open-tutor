# Lesson 04 — Modernism and the Harlem Renaissance: Fragmentation, Vernacular, Voice

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson you can analyze how Modernist
fragmentation and Harlem Renaissance vernacular voice work, and
compare how two texts from the same period treat a shared theme —
identity, freedom, or belonging — in different ways.

## Time and materials

About 100 minutes across two sessions. Materials: this lesson,
Langston Hughes's "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" (first published in
*The Crisis*, June 1921) and "I, Too" (1926) via the linked
Poetry Foundation / Academy of American Poets pages in the
Resource Pack, and your movement field guide. Read both poems
before the first session.

## Readiness check (5 min)

1. Hughes's speaker says he has known rivers "ancient as the
   world." What does choosing rivers — rather than cities, or
   books, or battles — let the poem claim about identity?
2. Write a sentence in the voice of someone who has been told to
   wait for a seat at a table. Then write the same sentence as
   that person might say it ten years later, no longer waiting.
   What changed — the facts, or the voice?

## Explicit teaching

### Modernism: form breaks because the world feels broken

Modernism is the hardest movement to define by subject, because
its subject is often **consciousness itself**. After a world war,
rapid industrialization, and mass migration to cities, many
writers felt that inherited forms — the well-made plot, the
smooth narrative, the single steady voice — no longer described
experience honestly. So they broke them:

- **Fragmentation:** scenes, images, and voices set side by side
  without smooth transitions, the way a mind actually jumps.
- **Stream of consciousness:** thought rendered as it flows, not
  as it would be summarized afterward.
- **Allusion and collage:** fragments of other texts, songs,
  headlines, and speech pasted together, leaving the reader to
  build the connections.
- **Broken syntax:** sentences that stop, restart, or refuse to
  finish, because the feeling they carry refuses neat grammar.

The test of a Modernist passage is never "is it difficult?" It
is: **does the break do work?** A fragmented passage about a
fragmented mind earns its form. A fragmented passage about a
peaceful afternoon may just be showing off. Always ask what the
form is *for*.

### The Harlem Renaissance: a movement that is also an argument

The Harlem Renaissance (roughly the 1920s, centered in New York's
Harlem neighborhood) was a flowering of Black art — poetry,
fiction, music, theater, visual art — and it was also a claim:
that Black life, in its own speech, music, humor, and sorrow, is
not a subject for someone else's literature but the center of
American literature itself. Its writers drew on the blues and jazz
— repetition with variation, call and response, a line that
swings — and on the vernacular: the actual spoken English of
Black communities, treated as literary language, not as comic
dialect.

Hughes is the clearest example, and the most useful for this
lesson, because his techniques are audible on first reading.
"The Negro Speaks of Rivers" (1921) builds its argument out of
repetition and scale:

> "I've known rivers: / I've known rivers ancient as the world
> and older than the flow of human blood in human veins. / My
> soul has grown deep like the rivers."

Three moves happen in those lines. First, **repetition with
expansion**: the same phrase returns, longer and deeper each
time, the way a blues line returns with a new shade of meaning.
Second, **scale**: a single speaker claims rivers that span
continents and millennia — the Euphrates, the Congo, the Nile,
the Mississippi — so that one person's identity is anchored in
deep history rather than in the narrow room his society offers
him. Third, **the soul as depth**: identity is not argued, it is
imaged — deep like rivers, not shallow like a puddle. You do not
need a footnote to feel the claim; the poem makes you feel the
depth before you analyze it.

"I, Too" (1926) works by a different technique: **understatement
as prophecy**. Its speaker is sent to eat in the kitchen when
company comes. He does not rage; he laughs, eats well, grows
strong, and states, flatly, that tomorrow he will be at the
table. The poem's power is in what it refuses to do: it does not
beg for the seat, and it does not threaten. It predicts. The
future tense *is* the argument — dignity stated as fact, before
the fact exists.

### Two modernisms, one period, different answers

Here is where the unit's comparison skill (RL.11-12.9) earns its
keep. A white Modernist and a Harlem Renaissance poet in the same
decade might both break forms, use fragments, and distrust smooth
narratives. But they often break form for different reasons. One
might fragment to show a self falling apart; the other might use
repetition and vernacular to show a self being built — a people
claiming a voice that had been denied them. Same decade, same
broken tools, opposite directions: disintegration versus
assembly. If your comparison only says "both writers use
unusual forms," you have described the decade, not the texts.
The comparison must say **what each writer's form is for**.

A useful test sentence: "Both texts treat the theme of identity,
but where Text A uses [technique] to [effect], Text B uses
[technique] to [different effect], and the difference shows that
[your claim about the period]." Fill that sentence with
quotations and you have a comparison; leave it empty and you
have a list.

### Worked example 1: hearing a technique

**Question.** What does repetition do in "The Negro Speaks of
Rivers" that a single statement could not do?

**Model answer.** A single statement — "my identity is ancient"
— would be an assertion the reader could accept or doubt.
Repetition makes it an experience: the phrase "I've known
rivers" returns after each river, so the reader *travels* the
rivers with the speaker, accumulating depth the way the poem
says the soul does. Each return adds a continent and a history
(Euphrates, Congo, Nile, Mississippi), so the repetition is not
decoration but accumulation: the poem's form enacts its claim
that identity deepens over time. When the closing lines repeat
the opening, the repetition feels earned, not circular — the
same words now carry four rivers' worth of history.

### Worked example 2: comparing two treatments of one theme

**Question.** Hughes's "I, Too" and the Modernist idea of the
fragmented self both deal with identity. How do their treatments
differ?

**Model answer.** Both start from a self that society has not
made room for. But where a fragmented Modernist passage might
render that self as broken — voices interrupting, sentences
failing — "I, Too" renders its self as gathering strength: the
speaker laughs, eats well, grows strong, and speaks in complete,
measured sentences that look forward ("Tomorrow, I'll be at the
table"). The difference is direction: fragmentation performs a
self coming apart under pressure, while Hughes's steady voice
performs a self assembling itself despite pressure. The shared
theme is identity under exclusion; the opposed techniques show
two different theories of what exclusion does to a person — and
two different hopes about what comes next.

## Guided practice

1. Mark every repeated word or phrase in "The Negro Speaks of
   Rivers." Beside each repetition, write what is added the
   second time that was not there the first.
2. In "I, Too," find the poem's quietest line — the one that
   says the least while meaning the most. Defend your choice in
   two sentences.
3. **Comparison drill.** Complete this sentence three ways,
   once for Hughes versus a Modernist passage of your choice,
   once for Hughes versus Gilman, and once for Hughes versus
   Hawthorne: "Both texts treat the theme of _____, but where
   Text A uses _____ to _____, Text B uses _____ to _____."

## Independent practice

1. Write a paragraph comparing how Hughes and one other writer
   from this unit treat the theme of freedom or belonging. Use
   at least two quotations and the comparison sentence from the
   teaching above.
2. **Original imitation (labeled).** Write eight lines in a
   Hughes-influenced voice — repetition with variation, concrete
   images, vernacular rhythm — on a subject of your own. Label
   it clearly as your own imitation, not Hughes's work. Then
   write three sentences on which technique you borrowed and
   what it let you say.
3. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "The Harlem
   Renaissance is just Modernism by Black writers." In four
   sentences, explain what that sentence gets right (shared
   period, shared formal experiments) and what it erases (a
   distinct project, audience, and argument about centrality).

## Exit check

Answer without notes: (a) Name two Modernist techniques and what
each is *for*. (b) What does vernacular voice claim about whose
speech counts as literary? (c) In one sentence, how do Hughes's
"I, Too" and a fragmented Modernist passage treat identity
differently?

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** If a poem's rhythm is hard to hear on the page,
  listen to a recorded reading (adult-selected; see the Resource
  Pack) and mark where the reader pauses. Pauses are part of the
  poem's meaning.
- **Language access:** *Vernacular*, *fragmentation*, and
  *call and response* are defined above and in the unit
  vocabulary list; use each in a sentence of your own.
- **Extension:** Research one other Harlem Renaissance writer
  (for example, Zora Neale Hurston, Countee Cullen, or Jean
  Toomer) and write a paragraph placing that writer on the
  unit's map: which movement traits fit, which strain, and why.

Common misconceptions: (1) treating the Harlem Renaissance as a
subset of Modernism rather than a distinct project in dialogue
with it; (2) praising difficulty as such instead of asking what
a formal break does; (3) quoting Hughes from memory rather than
from the linked edition — the adult checks quotations against
