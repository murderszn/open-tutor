# Lesson 05 — Interpretation Across Versions: Print, Performance, Illustration

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson you can analyze multiple interpretations
of one story or poem — in print, in a performed reading, and in
an illustration — evaluating how each version interprets its
source with specific evidence, and you can defend your own
interpretation the same way.

## Time and materials

About 100 minutes across two sessions. Materials: this lesson,
Gilman's "The Yellow Wallpaper" (already read), the generated
illustration below, and, if the adult selects one, a recorded
reading of an excerpt from the story (see the Resource Pack).
No other materials are needed.

## Readiness check (5 min)

1. Imagine two actors reading the same line — "John laughs at
   me, of course" — one warmly, one coldly. How much of the
   line's meaning changes? Write one sentence for each reading.
2. If an illustrator draws a scene from a story, is the drawing
   *in* the story or *about* the story? What is the difference?

## Explicit teaching

### Every version is an interpretation

When a story moves from print to another form — a stage, a
recording, a film, an illustration — nothing neutral happens.
Someone must decide: what does this character look like? How fast
does this line go? What does the camera, or the illustrator's
eye, linger on, and what does it cut? Those decisions are
interpretations, whether or not the person making them calls them
that. A faithful adaptation is not one that changes nothing
(changing nothing is impossible across media); it is one whose
changes serve something true in the source.

This is the skill RL.11-12.7 names: **analyze multiple
interpretations of a story, drama, or poem, evaluating how each
version interprets the source text.** The evaluation part is what
separates this lesson from "which version did you like?" Liking
is not evidence. An evaluation names a choice, traces its effect,
and judges it against the source: does this choice bring out
something the text supports, flatten something the text keeps
complex, or invent something the text never suggests?

### Four questions for any version

Use these four questions for every version you compare in this
lesson, in the practice, and in the assessment. Copy them into
your notebook.

1. **Emphasis.** What does this version linger on, enlarge, or
   repeat — and what does the source linger on instead?
2. **Omission.** What does this version leave out, and does the
   omission change the story's meaning or only its length?
3. **Addition.** What does this version add that the source does
   not contain — a face, a sound, a color, a gesture — and does
   the addition have support in the text?
4. **Judgment.** On balance, does this version's set of choices
   make the source's central tension clearer, muddier, or
   different — and what is your evidence?

A strong version comparison does all four, with quotations from
the source and descriptions of the version. A weak one says "the
movie was different from the book" and stops.

**Text-only alternative (everything needed to answer this
lesson's questions without seeing the image).** The image shows
a late-1800s upstairs room. The walls are covered in a bold,
looping yellow pattern that takes up most of the picture and
seems to lean over the furniture. At the left, a woman in a long
dark dress sits at a small wooden desk beside a tall window,
writing with a pen; loose papers and a closed book lie on the
desk, and an ink bottle stands beside them. At the right, a man
in a dark suit stands in an open doorway, one hand on the
doorframe, looking toward the woman. The room is lit by daylight
from the window. No other people, animals, or objects appear.
The woman's face is turned down toward her writing; the man's
expression is watchful and unreadable.

Now apply the four questions:

- **Emphasis.** The illustration enlarges the wallpaper until
  it dominates the room — a visual argument that the pattern is
  not background but a force in the story, matching the
  narrator's growing obsession. It also gives the husband a
  body and a doorway: he is literally framed, watching, which
  emphasizes surveillance.
- **Omission.** The illustration omits the bed, the barred
  windows, the torn paper, and the creeping woman the narrator
  sees behind the pattern. Omitting the creeping figure keeps
  the image in the story's early, ambiguous phase — before the
  reader knows how far the narrator's vision will go.
- **Addition.** The illustration adds a specific face, posture,
  and room layout the story never describes. The man's
  watchfulness is an interpretation: the story lets John laugh,
  dismiss, and prescribe, but it never stages him silently
  watching her write. That addition makes surveillance visible
  in a way the text only implies.
- **Judgment.** On balance, the illustration clarifies the
  story's central tension — a woman writing under watch, in a
  room whose walls compete with her — but it flattens the
  story's ambiguity by showing the woman calmly writing rather
  than, say, tearing at the paper or staring at it. A reader who
  only saw this image might miss how frightened and how
  absorbed the narrator becomes. That is a fair criticism, and
  it is also the point: every version trades something away.

### Worked example 1: print versus performed reading

**Question.** Suppose a recorded reading performs the line
"John laughs at me, of course, but one expects that in marriage"
in a light, fond tone. Evaluate that choice.

**Model answer.** Emphasis: the fond tone emphasizes the
narrator's performed acceptance — the marriage as she presents
it to the world. Omission: it omits the strain a slower, flatter
reading would give the words "of course" and "one expects," which
can sound like a woman reciting a rule she did not write.
Addition: tone itself is an addition — print has no tone, so the
reader's voice supplies one, and a fond voice supplies a
different narrator (resigned but affectionate) than a flat voice
would (resigned and afraid). Judgment: the fond reading is
defensible — the sentence *can* be read as wry acceptance — but
it risks flattening the understatement Lesson 03 analyzed: if
the audience hears only fondness, it may miss that the sentence
is also evidence of control. A strong evaluation would say so
and name the line's double edge as the reason.

### Worked example 2: your own interpretation

**Question.** Stage, in words, one moment from "The Yellow
Wallpaper" for a live reading: choose the speaker's position,
one sound, and one lighting choice, and justify each from the
text.

**Model answer.** Moment: the narrator's first close description
of the wallpaper. Position: the reader stands, not sits, and
steps closer to the audience with each sentence, so the body
enacts the narrator's absorption. Sound: a faint, repeated
paper-rustling under the reading, growing slightly louder — the
pattern becoming audible the way it becomes inescapable.
Lighting: a single warm light that narrows as the passage goes
on, so the room seems to close in. Justification: the story's
sentences about the pattern grow longer and more detailed as the
narrator looks longer; the staging makes that textual fact
physical. The choices are interpretive — the story never
specifies a rustling sound — but they are supported: they
externalize a movement the text itself performs.

## Guided practice

1. Apply the four questions to the illustration above in a
   full paragraph each (emphasis, omission, addition, judgment).
   Quote at least one line from the story in each paragraph.
2. **Two readings, one line.** Read aloud the sentence
   "Personally, I believe that congenial work, with excitement
   and change, would do me good" twice: once as a confident
   declaration, once as an apology. Write two sentences on how
   the meaning changes, and which reading the story's context
   better supports.
3. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "The illustration is
   wrong because the room in the story doesn't look like that."
   In three sentences, explain why "wrong" is the wrong word
   for an interpretation, and what question should replace it.

## Independent practice

1. Choose any scene from "Young Goodman Brown" or "The Yellow
   Wallpaper" and write a version comparison: the scene in
   print versus the scene as you would stage it (position,
   sound, lighting, one cut). Use all four questions and at
   least two quotations.
2. **Explain your reasoning.** Is there any interpretation of a
   story that is simply incorrect — not just weak, but wrong?
   Defend your answer with an example of an interpretation the
   text rules out, and an example of one the text leaves open.
3. Write a short review (one paragraph) of the Lesson 05
   illustration as if for a classmate who cannot see it: what
   it gets right about the story, what it flattens, and whether
   you would keep it. Use the text-only alternative if you need
   to check a detail.

## Application

Present your project anthology interpretation (project.md) in
the Week 13 seminar: a two-minute spoken interpretation of one
anthology text, with one staging or visual choice defended from
the text. The seminar is assessed in the unit assessment, Part C.

## Exit check

Answer without notes: (a) Name the four questions for evaluating
a version. (b) What is the difference between a version that is
unfaithful and a version that is interpretive? (c) Name one
choice in the Lesson 05 illustration and one judgment about it,
with evidence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** If you cannot see the illustration, the
  text-only alternative above contains every detail the
  questions require. No question in this lesson depends on
  vision alone; the adult may also describe the image aloud.
- **Language access:** *Interpretation*, *adaptation*,
  *emphasis*, and *omission* are defined above and in the unit
  vocabulary list; use each in a sentence of your own.
- **Extension:** Find a recorded reading or stage version of
  any public-domain story from this unit (adult-selected) and
  write a four-question evaluation of a two-minute excerpt.
  Note where the version's choices surprised you.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: (1) judging versions by liking instead of
by evidence; (2) treating any change as unfaithfulness, instead
of asking what the change serves; (3) treating the generated
illustration as historical evidence or as Gilman's own vision —
it is neither, and the caption says so explicitly. Keep that
distinction visible in discussion. Answer-key reference:
answer-key.md, Lesson 05.
