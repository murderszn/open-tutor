# Lesson 02 — Dark Romanticism and Realism: Hawthorne and Poe

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson you can analyze how Hawthorne and Poe
use symbol, setting, and structure, and explain how each choice
contributes to meaning — including where the text deliberately
refuses to settle a question.

## Time and materials

About 100 minutes across two sessions. Materials: this lesson,
Hawthorne's "Young Goodman Brown" (Project Gutenberg, *Mosses from
an Old Manse*, EBook #512), Poe's "The Raven" (1845), and your
movement field guide from Lesson 01. Read "Young Goodman Brown"
before the first session.

## Readiness check (5 min)

1. In one sentence, what happens in "Young Goodman Brown"?
2. In one sentence, what are you *not* sure happened?
3. Why might an author want you to be unsure? Write your best
   guess before reading on.

## Explicit teaching

### Romanticism's shadow: Dark Romanticism

The Romantics trusted the individual soul. The **Dark Romantics**
— Hawthorne, Poe, and Melville among them — kept the Romantics'
tools (symbol, heightened language, nature with moral weight) but
reversed the trust: what if the soul, looked at closely, is guilty,
self-deceived, or haunted? What if the symbol does not clarify but
deepens the mystery?

That reversal is the whole game of "Young Goodman Brown." A young
Puritan leaves his village at sunset for a night journey into the
forest, meets a figure who may be the devil, witnesses what may be
a witches' sabbath attended by the village's most respected
citizens, and returns at dawn unable to trust anyone again —
including his wife. The story never tells you whether the night
journey was real, a dream, or a temptation he half-invented. That
refusal is not a flaw. It is the story's argument: **a mind that
has learned to suspect everyone cannot unlearn it, whether the
evidence was real or not.**

### Symbol: a thing that means more than itself — and refuses to stop

A **symbol** is an object, person, or place that carries meaning
beyond itself. An **allegory** is a story in which many elements
stand for ideas in a systematic way. Hawthorne's stories live
between the two: his symbols invite allegorical reading and then
resist it.

The story's central symbol is Faith — both the wife's name and the
word for trust itself. The story opens:

> "Young Goodman Brown came forth at sunset into the street at
> Salem village; but put his head back, after crossing the
> threshold, to exchange a parting kiss with his young wife. And
> Faith, as the wife was aptly named, thrust her own pretty head
> into the street, letting the wind play with the pink ribbons of
> her cap..."

Read that opening the way a Dark Romantic wants you to: the wife
is a person, and she is also an idea. When Brown later sees — or
dreams he sees — a pink ribbon flutter down from the sky in the
forest, the symbol does double work: it suggests Faith herself has
fallen, and it suggests that *faith itself* has fallen. The story
never tells you which reading is right, and the pink ribbon never
stops meaning both. That is **ambiguity**: not confusion, but a
text deliberately holding two meanings at once.

Contrast this with a simpler symbol. In a fable, a fox *is*
cunning, full stop. In Hawthorne, a ribbon is a wife, a virtue, a
loss, and a suspicion — and the story's horror is that Brown can
no longer tell which.

### Setting and structure: the journey that returns to the same place

Notice the story's structure, because it is doing argumentative
work:

1. **Sunset departure** — Brown leaves home, against Faith's
   pleading, for an errand he will not name.
2. **Forest journey** — he meets a companion, resists, falters,
   rushes on; each stage strips away one more person he trusted.
3. **The sabbath vision** — the whole village, it seems, is there.
4. **Dawn return** — he is back in Salem village, physically
   unchanged, spiritually unrecognizable.

The structure is a circle that is not a circle: Brown returns to
the same street and cannot live in it anymore. The story's
structure argues what its plot only suggests — that suspicion,
once learned, is a place you cannot leave. When you analyze
structure (RL.11-12.5), always ask: **what does the shape of the
story claim that the events alone do not?**

### Poe: sound as meaning, and the narrator you cannot trust

Poe works the same Dark Romantic territory with different tools.
"The Raven" opens:

> "Once upon a midnight dreary, while I pondered, weak and
> weary..."

The poem's power is not only in what the raven says — one word,
"Nevermore" — but in **sound doing the work of meaning**: the
repetition, the internal rhyme, the rhythm that rocks like grief
itself. A narrator who asks a bird questions he knows it can only
answer one way is telling you, through form, that he is
interrogating his own mind, not a bird. In "The Tell-Tale Heart,"
the narrator insists he is not mad while describing, in loving
detail, exactly how mad his act was; the reader's job is to hear
the gap between the narrator's claim and the narrator's evidence.
That gap is the **unreliable narrator**: a first-person voice
whose account the reader has reason to doubt.

Unreliability is not lying. It is a structural choice: the author
gives you a narrator whose perceptions, motives, or judgment are
compromised, and then lets you do the work of separating what
happened from what the narrator believes happened. You will need
this tool again in Lesson 03, where the question gets harder:
what if the narrator is both the victim of the story and the only
person telling it?

### Worked example 1: tracking a symbol through a story

**Question.** Trace the pink ribbons through "Young Goodman
Brown" and explain what they do.

**Model answer.** The ribbons first appear as an emblem of
innocence and domestic love: Faith's cap, the wind playing with
them, the parting kiss at the threshold. They reappear in the
forest as a falling ribbon — something lost, or surrendered, or
imagined lost. After his return, Brown cannot look at Faith
without, the story implies, seeing that ribbon again. The symbol
therefore does three things: it **marks** what Brown leaves
behind, it **measures** what he believes he loses in the forest,
and it **haunts** his return, because a symbol, unlike a fact,
cannot be disproved. The story's ambiguity lives in the ribbons:
if the night was a dream, the ribbon is still lost in Brown's
mind, and that is loss enough.

### Worked example 2: structure as argument

**Question.** Why does Hawthorne end the story after Brown's
return instead of in the forest?

**Model answer.** Ending in the forest would make the story about
a supernatural event: did it happen? Ending after the return
makes it about a human consequence: what does a person become
after he believes it happened? The final movement — Brown
shrinking from his wife, from the minister, from everyone, for
the rest of his life — argues that the damage of suspicion does
not depend on the truth of the vision. The structure's claim is
that **belief has consequences whether or not it is true**, and
that claim could not be made if the story stopped at dawn in the
forest.

## Guided practice

1. Find three moments in "Young Goodman Brown" where the
   narration uses the word "seemed," "as if," or "perhaps."
   For each, write what the hedge lets Hawthorne avoid deciding.
2. In "The Raven," choose one stanza and mark every repeated
   sound (rhyme, alliteration, repeated word). Write two
   sentences on what the sound pattern makes you feel that the
   stanza's plain meaning does not.
3. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "The story is about
   how the devil is real." In three sentences, explain why that
   reading misses the story's central choice, using the word
   *ambiguity* correctly.

## Independent practice

1. Write a symbol-tracking paragraph on one object from Poe or
   Hawthorne (the raven, the forest, the staff, the ribbon).
   Follow the worked example's structure: first appearance,
   return, what it does, what it refuses to settle.
2. Rewrite the opening paragraph of "Young Goodman Brown" as a
   Realist would write it: same events, no symbol, no forest that
   means anything. Then write three sentences on what the
   Realist version loses and what it gains.
3. **Explain your reasoning.** Is Brown an unreliable narrator,
   or is the narration itself unreliable? Defend your answer
   with two quotations. (There is more than one defensible
   answer; the defense is the assignment.)

## Application

Begin your project anthology (project.md): choose which movement
Hawthorne best represents and draft the headnote — one paragraph
stating the movement's belief, one paragraph on how this story
both fits and strains that belief. Bring the draft to the next
session.

## Exit check

Answer without notes: (a) What is the difference between a symbol
and an allegory? (b) What does ambiguity do that confusion does
not? (c) Name one thing "Young Goodman Brown" proves, one thing
it suggests, and one thing it leaves open.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** If the story's old-fashioned diction slows you,
  read it aloud once with the adult, paraphrasing each paragraph
  in your own words before analyzing it. The analysis counts,
  not the speed.
- **Language access:** *Ambiguity*, *allegory*, and *unreliable
  narrator* are defined above and in the unit vocabulary list;
  use each in a sentence of your own.
- **Extension:** Compare Hawthorne's forest with a forest in a
  Realist or Naturalist story you know. Write a paragraph on how
  the same setting changes its meaning when the movement changes.

Common misconceptions: (1) treating ambiguity as authorial failure
("Hawthorne just didn't decide"); (2) reducing every symbol to one
fixed meaning; (3) confusing the narrator's beliefs with the
author's beliefs. The three-column audit in Workshop B
