# Lesson 01 — Mapping Literary Movements

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson you can name five literary movements —
Romanticism, Realism, Naturalism, Modernism, and the Harlem
Renaissance — describe what each believed literature should do, and
classify an unseen passage by movement using at least two named
traits and quoted evidence.

## Time and materials

About 100 minutes across two sessions. Materials: this lesson, a
notebook, the movement-fingerprint table below (copy it into your
notebook), and a pen for annotating.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in your notebook before reading on:

1. Name one book or story you have read that felt "realistic" and
   one that felt "dreamlike." What made each feel that way — the
   events, the language, or something else?
2. If two writers live in the same city in the same decade, must
   they write alike? Why or why not?

There are no wrong answers here; the lesson will give you sharper
words for the instincts you just used.

## Explicit teaching

### What a movement is — and is not

A literary movement is a **shared project**: a group of writers,
working at roughly the same time, who agree — loosely — about what
literature is for and how it should be written. Movements have
magazines, meeting places, arguments, and enemies. They do not have
membership cards. A writer can belong to a movement's conversation
while quarreling with half of it, and a movement's dates are always
approximate: historians draw the boundaries afterward, and the
writers themselves often did not know they were "in" anything.

That is why this unit never asks you to memorize a date and stop.
It asks you to **recognize traits in a passage and argue from them**.
"Romantic because it was published in 1835" is a weak answer.
"Romantic because the passage treats an individual's feeling as a
source of truth, and its forest is described as if it had a moral
character" is a strong one — it could be checked against the text.

### Five fingerprints

Copy this table. You will use it all unit.

| Movement (rough span) | What literature should do | Typical subjects | Typical techniques | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Romanticism** (c. 1800–1860 in America) | Trust feeling, imagination, and the individual conscience over cold reason and social convention | Nature as moral teacher; the solitary soul; the sublime and the strange | Symbol and allegory; heightened, lyrical language; nature reflecting inner states | Not every nature passage is Romantic — ask what the nature *means* |
| **Realism** (c. 1865–1900) | Show ordinary life faithfully, without romance or melodrama | Middle- and working-class daily life; marriage, work, manners | Careful social detail; plausible dialogue; restrained narration | Realism can still select and judge — "faithful" is itself a choice |
| **Naturalism** (c. 1890–1920) | Show human beings shaped — sometimes trapped — by forces larger than themselves | Poverty, heredity, environment, chance, instinct | Detached, almost scientific narration; characters as case studies; fateful irony | Naturalism is not pessimism for its own sake; it is an argument about causes |
| **Modernism** (c. 1910–1945) | Break inherited forms to match a world that feels broken, fast, and uncertain | The city; consciousness itself; fragmentation of self and society | Fragmentation; stream of consciousness; allusion; collage; broken syntax | Difficulty is not the point — the break must *do* something |
| **Harlem Renaissance** (c. 1920s–1930s) | Put Black life, art, music, and speech at the center of American culture | Harlem streets and clubs; migration; music (blues, jazz); dignity and protest | Vernacular speech; blues and jazz rhythms; lyric pride; double consciousness (seeing oneself through others' eyes) | Never reduce it to background color — it is a literary argument, not a setting |

Two cautions before the examples. First, **Dark Romanticism** —
Hawthorne, Poe, Melville — is Romanticism's shadow side: it keeps
the Romantics' interest in the soul and the symbol but doubts that
the soul is good. You will meet it in Lesson 02. Second, movements
overlap: a 1920s poem can be both Modernist in form and Harlem
Renaissance in voice. When that happens, name both and say which
trait belongs to which movement. That is a better answer than
forcing one label.

### Worked example 1: a passage that argues with itself

Read this original passage (written for this lesson, not quoted from
any author):

> The mill had taken her father's lungs the way the river took
> the road every spring — not out of cruelty, but because that is
> what mills and rivers do. She counted the coins for rent, set
> them in a row, and understood, without anyone telling her, that
> no amount of wanting would change the sum.

**Step 1 — list candidate traits.** The passage treats a human fate
as decided by large forces (the mill, the river, the rent). The
narration is detached: it reports rather than pleads. The character
understands her situation but cannot change it.

**Step 2 — match to a fingerprint.** "Forces larger than the person
decide the outcome" is the Naturalist fingerprint. The detached,
case-study tone matches too. A Realist passage might also describe
rent and a mill, but Realism would more likely linger on social
detail and the character's choices; here the point is precisely
that choice does not change the sum.

**Step 3 — write the classification as an argument, not a label:**
"This passage is Naturalist because it presents the mill and the
river as forces that act on the character regardless of her will
('not out of cruelty, but because that is what mills and rivers
do'), and because the narrator reports her understanding with
detached finality ('no amount of wanting would change the sum').
The emphasis is on causes outside the character, not on her moral
growth."

Notice the structure you should copy: **label + trait + quotation +
what the trait does**. Every classification in this unit needs all
four parts.

### Worked example 2: the same subject, two movements

Two original passages about the same walk home. Decide which
movement each belongs to, and why.

**Passage A:**

> She walked home under a sky the color of wet slate, counting
> the cracks in the pavement because counting was the only thing
> that kept her hands from shaking. The streetlights came on one
> by one, each in its own time, as if the evening were being
> assembled from parts that did not quite fit.

**Passage B:**

> She walked home beneath a heaven that seemed to lean down and
> listen. Every tree she passed stood like a witness, and the
> wind moved through the branches the way a choir moves through
> a hymn — not to be heard, but to be felt in the chest.

**Analysis.** Passage A is **Modernist**: the scene is assembled
from fragments ("each in its own time," "parts that did not quite
fit"), perception is broken and anxious, and the sentence structure
itself stutters between observation and feeling. Passage B is
**Romantic**: nature is given moral attention ("a witness"), the
sky "leans down and listens" as if it had a soul, and feeling is
treated as knowledge — the wind is understood through the chest,
not measured. Same walk, opposite theories of what literature is
for. If you can feel that difference, you can classify anything
this unit puts in front of you.

### A warning against the most common error

Do not classify by **subject alone**. A forest is not automatically
Romantic; a factory is not automatically Naturalist; a jazz club is
not automatically Harlem Renaissance. Classify by **what the
passage does with its subject**: does it treat feeling as truth?
Does it report social fact? Does it argue that forces decide fate?
Does it break form? Does it center a voice that other literature
pushed aside? Subject is a hint; technique and belief are evidence.

## Guided practice

Work with your adult or a partner. For each original passage, write
a four-part classification (label + trait + quotation + what the
trait does). One is done for you in the worked examples; do the
rest the same way.

1. "He had been a good man in the way that a kept ledger is good:
   every column added, every debt marked paid, and no one, not
   even his wife, could say what the totals were for."
2. "The train — the train — the train kept saying it until the
   word stopped meaning a machine and started meaning the
   distance between her and everything she had left."
3. "Mama sang while she ironed, and the song had the shape of
   the work: a verse for the collar, a chorus for the sleeves, and
   a long low note for the part nobody thanked her for."
4. "The committee met on Tuesdays. The committee always met on
   Tuesdays. On the Tuesday that mattered, the minutes recorded
   only that the meeting had been adjourned."

## Independent practice

1. Write your own four-part classification of a passage from any
   book you have read this year. Quote at least one sentence and
   name two traits.
2. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "This passage is
   Realist because it is about a poor family." Explain, in three
   sentences, what is missing from that argument and how to fix it.
3. Choose one movement from the table and write a paragraph
   defending it against its harshest critic: what does this
   movement let a writer show that the others cannot?

## Application

Make a one-page "movement field guide" for your notebook: five
rows, one per movement, each with (a) the movement's belief in one
sentence, (b) one technique to listen for, and (c) one trap to
avoid. You will use this guide in every later lesson; the adult
checks it before Lesson 02.

## Exit check

Without looking at the table, write one sentence for each movement
stating what it believed literature should do. Then, for the
movement you found hardest, write one question you still have.
Bring both to the next session.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** If five movements at once feel like too much,
  master two first — Romanticism and Realism — and add one
  movement per session. The fingerprint table stays open during
  all practice; memorization is never the test.
- **Language access:** The words *detached*, *vernacular*, and
  *fragmentation* are defined in the unit vocabulary list; say
  each aloud and use it in a sentence of your own before the
  guided practice.
- **Extension:** Research one writer who rejected their movement's
  label (for example, a Harlem Renaissance writer who disliked the
  term, or a Realist accused of romanticizing). Write a paragraph
  on what the quarrel reveals about movements as labels.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: (1) treating publication date as proof of
movement; (2) classifying by subject instead of technique and
belief; (3) treating the Harlem Renaissance as a setting rather
than a literary project. All three are addressed by insisting on
the four-part classification format. Answer-key reference:
answer-key.md, Lesson 01.
