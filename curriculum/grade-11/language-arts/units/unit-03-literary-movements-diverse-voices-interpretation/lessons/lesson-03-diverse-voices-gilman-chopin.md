# Lesson 03 — Diverse Voices and Point of View: Gilman and Chopin

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson you can analyze point of view where
what is stated and what is meant come apart — irony and
understatement — and explain whose perspective centers a story
and what that centering reveals and conceals.

## Time and materials

About 100 minutes across two sessions. Materials: this lesson,
Gilman's "The Yellow Wallpaper" (Project Gutenberg, EBook #1952)
and Chopin's *The Awakening* (Project Gutenberg, EBook #160,
excerpts: chapter 1 and the closing chapters, per the adult's
edition). Read "The Yellow Wallpaper" before the first session.

## Readiness check (5 min)

1. Gilman's story opens: "It is very seldom that mere ordinary
   people like John and myself secure ancestral halls for the
   summer." What does the narrator's word "ordinary" already
   suggest about how she sees herself — and how others see her?
2. If a story is told by the person it happens to, what can that
   narrator *not* show you? List two things before reading on.

## Explicit teaching

### Point of view is a decision, not a default

Every story chooses a **point of view**: who tells it, how much
that teller knows, and whose consciousness the reader lives
inside. The choice is never neutral. A first-person narrator gives
you intimacy and blindness at the same time: you feel what she
feels, and you cannot see what she cannot see. A third-person
narrator can move between minds, but every move is still a
choice about whose inner life counts.

This lesson's standard (RL.11-12.6) names the hard case: **a case
in which grasping a point of view requires distinguishing what is
directly stated from what is really meant** — satire, sarcasm,
irony, or understatement. Gilman and Chopin both build their
stories on that gap. Their narrators and heroines often say less
than they mean, or mean the opposite of what the people around
them hear. Your job is to hear both layers.

### Gilman: the narrator who is not believed — including by herself

"The Yellow Wallpaper" is told as secret journal entries by a
woman whose physician husband, John, has prescribed a "rest cure"
for what he calls a "temporary nervous depression — a slight
hysterical tendency." She is forbidden to work, to write, and to
think about her condition. She writes anyway, in secret. The
story's form *is* its argument: a woman told not to think, thinking
on paper where no one can stop her.

Watch how understatement works in the opening. The narrator
writes:

> "John laughs at me, of course, but one expects that in
> marriage."

Read literally, that sentence is mild, even fond. Read as
understatement — saying less than the situation warrants — it is
devastating: being laughed at by the person who controls your
treatment is not a small marital habit; it is the mechanism of
her confinement. Later she writes:

> "Personally, I disagree with their ideas. Personally, I
> believe that congenial work, with excitement and change, would
> do me good."

"Personally" does enormous work here. It marks the one space in
the story where her judgment is allowed to exist — a private
sentence, hedged, apologetic, and exactly right. The doctors are
wrong, the reader can see it, and the narrator can only say so in
a word that sounds like an apology. That is **verbal irony** in
its quietest form: the sentence means more than it says, and the
people inside the story hear less than the reader does.

As the story goes on, the wallpaper itself becomes the narrator's
other voice. She studies its pattern, finds a woman creeping
behind it, and finally identifies with that woman. Is she mad, or
is she the only person in the house seeing clearly? The story
refuses a clean answer — and that refusal matters for point of
view: a first-person narrator deteriorating in front of you is
also a narrator whose perceptions you cannot simply trust or
simply dismiss. You must hold both possibilities, and say which
evidence supports each.

### Chopin: the third person that stays close

*The Awakening* opens with a caged parrot and a mockingbird:

> "A green and yellow parrot, which hung in a cage outside the
> door, kept repeating over and over: 'Allez vous-en! Allez
> vous-en! Sapristi! That's all right!' He could speak a little
> Spanish, and also a language which nobody understood, unless
> it was the mocking-bird that hung on the other side of the door,
> whistling his fluty notes out upon the breeze with maddening
> persistence."

Two caged birds, speaking languages nobody around them
understands, at the door of a summer house. The novel never
explains the symbol; it does not need to. Edna Pontellier, wife
and mother in a world that has assigned her exactly two roles,
will spend the novel discovering that she wants a third thing —
herself — and discovering how little room that world has for it.
Chopin's narration stays close to Edna's consciousness without
becoming her voice: you feel her awakening from the inside while
still seeing, from the outside, the society that will not make
room for it. That double vision is the novel's point-of-view
achievement, and it is why the ending — Edna walking into the
sea — can be read as defeat, as liberation, or as both. The
novel, like Gilman's story, declines to choose for you.

### Whose perspective centers the story?

Here is the unit's central question about voice, and it has a
political edge the two stories share. In Gilman's story, the
doctors' perspective is the official one: John and her brother,
both physicians "of high standing," agree she is not really sick.
The story centers the perspective those men dismiss. In Chopin's
novel, the perspective of a wife and mother who wants more than
wifehood and motherhood is centered against a society that calls
that want selfish. In both cases, **centering a dismissed
perspective is itself the literary act**: the story does not
merely describe a woman's situation; it insists that her
consciousness is a valid place from which to see the world.

That is what "diverse voices" means in this unit. It does not
mean "stories about different kinds of people" as decoration. It
means: **whose consciousness gets to be the lens, whose judgment
gets taken seriously, and what does the story look like when the
lens moves?** When you can answer those three questions for any
story, you can analyze point of view.

### Worked example 1: hearing understatement

**Question.** What does the narrator mean — and not mean — when
she writes, "John laughs at me, of course, but one expects that
in marriage"?

**Model answer.** Stated: being laughed at is normal in marriage;
the narrator accepts it. Meant: the laughter is part of a pattern
in which her perceptions are dismissed by the person with medical
and marital authority over her. The words "of course" and "one
expects" perform acceptance — they are the voice of a woman who
has learned that protest will be read as a symptom. The reader
hears the gap because the story has already shown us the stakes:
a woman forbidden to work, write, or think about her condition is
not describing a joke; she is describing control, in the only
language available to her. The sentence is understatement as
survival: it says the least that can be said while still saying
it.

### Worked example 2: the same theme, two lenses

**Question.** Gilman and Chopin both write about confinement.
How does point of view change what confinement means in each?

**Model answer.** In Gilman, confinement is experienced from
inside, in first person, as a shrinking room: the narrator's
world narrows to wallpaper because everything else has been
forbidden, and the reader narrows with her. Confinement is a
consciousness closing in. In Chopin, confinement is observed from
close third person, as a social world: Edna's cage is made of
roles — wife, mother, hostess — enforced by other people's
expectations, and the reader sees both Edna's inner awakening and
the outer world that cannot accommodate it. Same theme, different
knowledge: Gilman gives you the inside of a locked room; Chopin
gives you the inside of a person inside a locked society. The
comparison shows that "confinement" is not one thing — its
meaning depends on whose eyes you borrow.

## Guided practice

1. Find two more sentences in "The Yellow Wallpaper" where the
   narrator understates something serious. For each, write the
   stated meaning and the meant meaning in two columns.
2. **Point-of-view rewrite.** Rewrite the opening of "The
   Yellow Wallpaper" from John's point of view, in three
   sentences, as John would tell it. Then write two sentences on
   what your version hides that Gilman's version shows.
3. In *The Awakening*, chapter 1: list every detail that marks
   Edna's world as ordered, supervised, or performed (clothes,
   routines, other people's remarks). What does the list suggest
   about whose rules Edna lives under?

## Independent practice

1. Write a perspective map for one anchor text: three columns —
   *What the narrator/heroine knows*, *What the reader knows
   that she does not (or not yet)*, *What nobody in the story
   knows*. Fill each column with at least two items and a
   quotation or page reference.
2. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "Gilman's narrator is
   crazy, so nothing she says can be trusted." In four sentences,
   explain why that reading fails: distinguish unreliability
   from worthlessness, and name one true thing the narrator
   perceives that the doctors miss.
3. **Explain your reasoning.** Is Edna's ending a defeat, a
   liberation, or both? Defend your answer with evidence from
   the closing chapters and one sentence acknowledging the
   strongest counter-reading.

## Application

Continue your project anthology: draft the headnote for the
Realism/Naturalism-adjacent voice in your anthology (Gilman or
Chopin — your choice), including one paragraph on point of view
and one paragraph on what that voice's centering reveals about
its period. Bring the draft to the next session.

## Exit check

Answer without notes: (a) Define verbal irony and understatement
in one sentence each. (b) Name one thing a first-person narrator
can show that a third-person narrator cannot, and one thing the
reverse. (c) In one sentence, what does "diverse voices" mean in
this unit — beyond "stories about different people"?

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** If tracking stated-versus-meant feels slippery,
  use the two-column habit for every quotation in this lesson:
  left column, what the words say; right column, what the
  situation suggests. The columns are the analysis.
- **Language access:** *Understatement*, *first-person
  narration*, and *consciousness* are defined above and in the
  unit vocabulary list; use each in a sentence of your own.
- **Extension:** Compare Gilman's narrator with Poe's narrator
  from Lesson 02: two first-person voices, two kinds of
  unreliability. Write a paragraph on how the reader's trust
  works differently in each story.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: (1) equating an unreliable narrator with a
liar, or with a narrator whose every statement is false;
(2) treating irony as "saying the opposite" in every case, when
understatement and dramatic irony work differently; (3) reading
Edna's ending as a settled verdict instead of an ambiguity to be
argued. Sensitive-content framing is in the unit README; keep
discussion literary and historical, without graphic detail.
