# Answer Key — Unit 03: Literary Movements, Diverse Voices, and Interpretation

For the guiding adult only. Sample answers show the reasoning a
full-credit response must contain; wording may differ. Where more
than one answer is defensible, the key says so and names what the
defense must include. Do not hand this file to the learner.

## Lesson 01 — Guided practice (four-part classifications)

1. **Realism (primary).** Trait: faithful social observation
   with restrained narration. Quotation: "every column added,
   every debt marked paid." What it does: goodness is measured
   the way society measures it — by accounts kept — and the
   final clause ("no one... could say what the totals were for")
   quietly questions that measure without melodrama. Accept
   Modernist only if the learner argues the aphoristic break
   (the totals question) as fragmentation of a stable moral
   account, with that quotation analyzed.
2. **Modernism.** Trait: repetition that empties and remakes
   meaning; consciousness rendered as sound. Quotation: "the
   train — the train — the train kept saying it until the word
   stopped meaning a machine." What it does: repetition breaks
   the word from its referent, so language itself becomes the
   experience of distance.
3. **Harlem Renaissance.** Trait: work song structure and
   vernacular voice centering labor. Quotation: "a verse for
   the collar, a chorus for the sleeves, and a long low note for
   the part nobody thanked her for." What it does: song organizes
   work and claims dignity for unthanked labor; the form (verse /
   chorus) is blues-shaped.
4. **Realism.** Trait: plausible institutional detail, flatly
   reported. Quotation: "the minutes recorded only that the
   meeting had been adjourned." What it does: the record's
   omission *is* the event — a restrained narration that lets
   procedure reveal what mattered by leaving it out. Accept
   Naturalism only if argued as institution-as-force, with the
   "Tuesday that mattered" analyzed as fate by bureaucracy.

## Lesson 01 — Independent practice

1. Accept any passage with a label, two traits, and at least one
   quotation analyzed (not merely cited). Deduct if the
   classification rests on subject or date alone.
2. **Error analysis (sample).** The argument is missing a
   trait and a quotation: "poor family" names a subject, and
   Realism is defined by technique and belief, not subject. Fix:
   name a Realist trait (e.g., plausible social detail rendered
   without melodrama), quote a sentence showing it, and say
   what the trait does in that sentence.
3. Accept any movement defended by what it uniquely enables
   (e.g., Romanticism: interior moral knowledge; Naturalism:
   causal argument about forces; Harlem Renaissance: a centered
   voice). Credit the defense, not the movement chosen.

## Lesson 02 — Guided practice

1. Hedges to find include: the forest company "seemed" to include
   familiar faces; the sabbath music came "as if" from a distance;
   Brown "perhaps" dreamed the whole night. For each: the hedge
   lets Hawthorne avoid deciding whether the events were real,
   dreamed, or tempted into being — and that avoidance is the
   story's point, since Brown's later suspicion does not depend
   on the answer.
2. Sample (first stanza of "The Raven"): "dreary / weary,"
   "tapping / rapping," and the repeated "nevermore" pattern to
   come create an incantatory, rocking rhythm; the sound makes
   grief feel inescapable and circular in a way the stanza's
   plain meaning (a man reading late, a knock) does not.
   Credit any stanza with sounds marked and a feeling named
   that the meaning alone does not supply.
3. **Error analysis (sample).** The reading mistakes the
   story's question. Hawthorne never settles whether the devil
   is real; the story's horror is that Brown *believes* the
   vision and cannot un-believe it. "The devil is real" removes
   the ambiguity that is the story's argument about suspicion.

## Lesson 02 — Independent practice

1. Sample (the raven): first appears as a visitor answering one
   word; returns as the only answer to every question; does the
   work of grief that will not be argued with; refuses to settle
   whether the bird is supernatural, a coincidence, or the
   narrator's mind. Structure must follow: first appearance,
   return, what it does, what it refuses to settle.
2. Credit a Realist rewrite that removes symbol and moralized
   nature, and a loss/gain paragraph naming at least one real
   loss (moral ambiguity, symbolic depth) and one real gain
   (social clarity, plausible causation).
3. Accept either answer with two quotations: (a) the *narration*
   is unreliable in the forest sequence (hedges, "seemed," "as
   if," dream-framing), while Brown as a character is
   self-deceived; or (b) Brown is the unreliable center because
   the narration stays close to his perceptions. The defense,
   not the label, earns credit.

## Lesson 03 — Guided practice

1. Acceptable pairs (stated / meant): "I am absolutely
   forbidden to 'work' until I am well again" — stated: a medical
   rule; meant: writing and thinking themselves are forbidden.
   "I did write for a while in spite of them; but it DOES
   exhaust me" — stated: writing tires her; meant: secrecy and
   opposition exhaust her. "John is practical in the extreme. He
   has no patience with faith" — stated: character description;
   meant: he dismisses anything not felt, seen, and put down in
   figures — including her inner life.
2. Sample John's version: "My wife is recovering well. The rest
   treatment requires quiet and freedom from excitement, and I
   have arranged the house accordingly. She imagines patterns
   in the wallpaper, which is a symptom we are treating with
   rest." What it hides: her judgment (that work would help),
   her writing, and the fact that the treatment itself worsens
   her — all visible only from her side of the story.
3. Chapter 1 details include: Mr. Pontellier reading his
   newspaper, the caged parrot and mockingbird, the gallery and
   bridges between cottages, the ordered cottages, Edna's role
   as wife/mother/hostess observed by others. The list suggests
   Edna lives under other people's rules and observations before
   she has any room of her own.

## Lesson 03 — Independent practice

1. Sample map (Gilman): narrator knows — her judgment that work
   would help ("Personally, I believe..."), her secret writing,
   what the pattern looks like to her. Reader knows that she
   does not (yet) — how far the identification with the creeping
   woman will go, whether John will discover the writing.
   Nobody knows — whether the wallpaper's woman is hallucination,
   symbol, or both; whether another treatment would have helped.
   Chopin maps are credited on the same structure.
2. **Error analysis (sample).** Unreliability is not
   worthlessness: a narrator can be wrong about some things and
   right about others. Gilman's narrator correctly perceives
   that she is dismissed, forbidden to work, and deteriorating
   under the cure — the very facts the doctors' diagnosis
   ("nothing the matter... but temporary nervous depression")
   denies. The reader's job is to sort true perceptions from
   distorted ones, not to discard the narrator.
3. Accept defeat, liberation, or both, with closing-chapter
   evidence and a fairly stated counter-reading. A both-answer
   must explain what is lost (life, children, future) and what
   is claimed (self-possession, refusal) without collapsing
   them into one verdict.

## Lesson 04 — Guided practice

1. Repetitions: "I've known rivers" returns after each river;
   each return adds a river and a history (Euphrates, Congo,
   Nile, Mississippi), so repetition accumulates depth rather
   than merely repeating. "My soul has grown deep like the
   rivers" frames the poem and returns at the close, now carrying
   all four rivers.
2. Accept "I laugh" (says least, means refusal to be diminished
   — laughter as resistance) or "Tomorrow, I'll be at the table"
   (a future stated as fact). Credit requires a defense naming
   what the line does not say that it still means.
3. Samples: Hughes vs. a Modernist passage — "Both treat
   identity, but where the Modernist passage uses fragmentation
   to show a self coming apart, Hughes uses steady repetition
   to show a self assembling." Hughes vs. Gilman — "Both treat
   confinement, but where Gilman uses first-person narrowing to
   show a room closing in, Hughes uses expanding scale to show
   a history opening out." Hughes vs. Hawthorne — "Both treat
   standing before a larger power, but where Hawthorne uses
   symbol to show guilt haunting a soul, Hughes uses rivers to
   show history sustaining one."

## Lesson 04 — Independent practice

1. Credit a paragraph with two quotations and the comparison
   sentence used correctly (technique + effect for each text).
2. Imitation: credit only if clearly labeled as the learner's
   own work, and the technique paragraph names the borrowed
   device (repetition with variation, vernacular rhythm, or
   concrete image) and what it enabled. Deduct if presented as
   Hughes's work or unlabeled.
3. **Error analysis (sample).** Right: same decade, shared
   formal experiments (fragmentation, vernacular, broken
   narrative). Erased: a distinct project (centering Black life
   and voice as American literature's center, not margin), a
   distinct audience, and a distinct argument (assembly and
   dignity rather than only disintegration).

## Lesson 05 — Guided practice

1. Credit four paragraphs that follow the lesson's analysis:
   emphasis (wallpaper dominates; husband framed watching);
   omission (bed, bars, torn paper, creeping woman absent —
   keeps the early ambiguity); addition (specific faces, room
   layout, watchful posture — surveillance made visible);
   judgment (clarifies the tension, flattens the later
   deterioration). Each paragraph needs at least one story
   quotation.
2. Sample: a confident reading makes "Personally, I believe..."
   sound like a claim the narrator owns; an apologetic reading
   makes it sound like a claim she must smuggle past authority.
   The story's context (forbidden to work or think about her
   condition) better supports the apologetic reading, because
   the sentence's hedge ("Personally") is itself evidence of
   constraint.
3. **Error analysis (sample).** "Wrong" assumes an illustration
   can be true or false like a statement; an illustration is an
   interpretation, a set of choices. The right question is the
   four-question evaluation: what does it emphasize, omit, add,
   and does its set of choices clarify or flatten the source?

## Lesson 05 — Independent practice

1. Credit a comparison using all four questions, two quotations,
   and a staging (position, sound, lighting, one cut) that is
   the learner's own.
2. Incorrect example: claiming Gilman's narrator is John's
   sister — the text establishes her as his wife. Open example:
   whether Brown's forest night was real, dreamed, or tempted.
   Credit requires one ruled-out claim with textual reason and
   one genuinely open question.
3. Credit a review paragraph naming one strength (e.g., the
   wallpaper's dominance matching the narrator's obsession),
   one flattening (e.g., calm writing vs. later deterioration),
   and a keep/revise verdict with a reason.

## Practice §A — Movement sorting

- **A1. Realism.** Trait: ordinary life rendered without
  inflation. Quotation: "they called it Tuesday." What it
  does: refuses to turn routine into fate — the anti-Naturalist
  move is the point.
- **A2. Modernism.** Trait: fragmentation; perception arriving
  out of order. Quotation: "all arriving at once and none of
  them in order." What it does: the sentence's form enacts a
  consciousness overwhelmed by sense data.
- **A3. Dark Romanticism.** Trait: the soul treated as a
  weighable, corruptible thing; ironic quest. Quotation: "the
  thing he weighed grew lighter every time he touched it."
  What it does: the attempt to measure the soul diminishes it —
  a Dark Romantic doubt about human perfectibility.
- **A4. Realism.** Trait: institutional/social detail, flatly
  reported. Quotation: "the marriage continued exactly as the
  contract required." What it does: procedure reveals the
  marriage's terms more honestly than romance would.
- **A5. Harlem Renaissance.** Trait: work song structure,
  vernacular voice, labor centered. Quotation: "a verse for
  lifting, a chorus for carrying." What it does: song makes
  work dignified and collective.
- **A6. Naturalism.** Trait: heredity, environment, and economic
  position deciding fate; detached narration. Quotation: "a
  cough he had inherited along with his father's name." What
  it does: inheritance (name and cough together) makes fate
  feel inescapable rather than chosen.

## Practice §B — Ambiguity audit (sample rows)

| Proved | Suggested | Left open |
|---|---|---|
| Brown leaves Salem at sunset ("came forth at sunset") | The forest company includes respected villagers (figures "seemed" familiar) | Whether the night was real, dreamed, or tempted |
| Faith pleads with him to stay (parting kiss scene) | Faith herself attended the sabbath (falling ribbon) | Whether Faith fell, or Brown only believes she did |
| Brown returns to Salem at dawn | Brown's suspicion is justified by what he saw | Whether any villager actually did what he saw |
| Brown lives the rest of his life distrusting everyone | The village's piety was a performance all along | Whether Brown could ever have returned to trust |

**B1.** The audit shows the forest events are mostly *suggested*,
while the aftermath is *proved*: the story's certainty is about
consequence, not occurrence. **B2.** Most learners find "Left
open" hardest, because it requires resisting the urge to decide
what the story deliberately does not decide.

## Practice §C — Perspective maps

**C1 (Gilman, sample).** Narrator knows: her judgment that work
would help; her secret writing; the pattern's changes. Reader
knows she does not (yet): how far identification with the
creeping woman will go; that John will find her at the end.
Nobody knows: whether another treatment would have helped;
whether the creeping woman is hallucination, symbol, or both.
**C2 (Chopin, sample).** Edna knows: her growing desire for
selfhood; her dissatisfaction with wife/mother roles. Reader
knows she does not (yet): how the novel will end; how fully
society will refuse her. Nobody knows: whether a different life
was possible for Edna in that world. **C3.** John's version
loses her judgment, her writing, and the evidence that the cure
harms her. A husband's version of *The Awakening* loses Edna's
inner awakening and keeps only the outer scandal — the very
inversion Chopin's close third person refuses.

## Practice §D — Theme comparison (samples)

**D1.** Both treat confinement, but where Gilman uses
first-person narrowing to show a room closing in ("I am
absolutely forbidden to 'work'"), Chopin uses close third person
to show roles closing in (Edna observed as wife, mother,
hostess). The comparison shows confinement is not one thing:
it can be a locked room or a locked society. **D2.** Both treat
standing before a larger power, but where Hawthorne uses symbol
to show guilt haunting a soul, Hughes uses rivers to show
history sustaining one — opposite directions for the same
question. **D3.** Credit a summary paragraph with no judgment
words (no "tragic," "brave," "selfish"), followed by a labeled
analysis paragraph that does judge. Keeping them separate
matters because summary must be checkable against the text
before interpretation begins; mixing them lets judgment smuggle
itself in as fact.

## Practice §E — Version comparison

**E1.** Credit four paragraphs following the Lesson 05 analysis
with one quotation each. **E2.** Credit two readings with
tone/pace/pause described and a bring-out/flatten evaluation for
each. **E3 (sample).** The error is treating an interpretation
as a factual claim: the illustration shows an artist's choice,
not a scene the story describes. Proof about the story comes
from the story's text — quotations, not pictures.

## Practice §F — Vocabulary in context (sample sentences)

- *Movement:* "Naturalism is the movement that treats fate as
  a force rather than a choice." *Symbol:* "Faith's pink ribbons
  are a symbol of innocence, trust, and loss at once."
  *Ambiguity:* "The story's ambiguity is that the forest night
  may be real, dreamed, or tempted." *Unreliable narrator:* "A
  narrator who insists he is sane while describing his crime in
  loving detail is unreliable." *Point of view:* "Gilman's
  point of view is first person, so the reader knows only what
  the narrator knows." *Dramatic irony:* "The reader sees the
  cure harming the narrator before she names it herself."
  *Understatement:* "'John laughs at me, of course' is
  understatement for dismissal." *Vernacular:* "Hughes treats
  vernacular speech as literary language." *Fragmentation:*
  "The Modernist passage fragments the scene into sense data
  arriving out of order." *Interpretation:* "Every staging is
  an interpretation, not a copy." Accept any sentence that uses
  the word correctly about a unit text.

## Formative quiz (20 pts, 2 pts each)

1. (a) Romanticism. (b) Naturalism. One phrase each; half
   credit for a correct movement with a wrong or missing
   belief.
2. *Ambiguity:* a text deliberately holding two meanings at
   once (the ribbons as wife and as faith). *Symbol:* an object
   carrying meaning beyond itself (Faith's ribbons). One point
   per correct definition with a correct example.
3. **Naturalism.** Trait: impersonal forces deciding a human
   outcome; detached narration. Quotation: "a future that no
   longer asked their opinion" (or "the family had learned to
   live with..."). What it does: the family's fate is reported
   as weather — decided elsewhere, not negotiated. Full credit
   requires all four parts.
4. Stated: being laughed at is normal in marriage. Meant: her
   perceptions are dismissed by the person with authority over
   her treatment. One point per layer.
5. First person: interior consciousness and its blindness
   (Gilman). Third person: movement between minds and the outer
   social world (Chopin). One point per side, with a text each.
6. The future tense argues a prediction as a fact: dignity
   claimed before it exists. A present-tense claim would assert
   a seat the speaker does not yet have, and would be false;
   the future tense makes hope itself the argument.
7. "Wrong" is the wrong word because an illustration is an
   interpretation — a set of choices — not a factual claim.
   The replacing question: how do its choices (emphasis,
   omission, addition) clarify or flatten the source?
8. Sample: Gilman's confinement is a room (physical, imposed
   by prescription); Chopin's is roles (social, imposed by
   expectation). The comparison shows "confinement" names both
   a place and a condition — the word's meaning depends on
   whose perspective describes it.
9. Any two of emphasis, omission, addition, judgment, with one
   applied in two sentences to a version studied (illustration,
   performed reading, or staging).
10. Two movements with correct beliefs (one point each), plus
    one sentence naming a real disagreement (e.g., Romanticism
    trusts feeling as truth; Realism distrusts feeling as
    evidence).

## Culminating assessment (50 pts)

**Part A (6 pts each).** A1 — **Romanticism.** Trait: nature
simile and individual imagination remaking circumstance.
Quotation: "the sea keeps a shore... by returning every morning
to what the night had rearranged." What it does: daily labor is
re-imagined through nature into moral agency — the Romantic
move of feeling-as-knowledge. Accept Realism only if the learner
argues ordinary shopkeeping detail as the dominant trait with
that quotation analyzed; do not accept Naturalism (the passage
stresses agency, not fate). A2 — **Naturalism.** Trait:
impersonal forces (machine failure, company form, hunger) deciding
a family's fate; detached narration. Quotation: "hunger, like
weather, does not negotiate." What it does: hunger is placed
with weather — outside human bargaining — which is the
Naturalist argument in one simile. Deduct for label-only answers
(missing trait, quotation, or effect).

**Part B1 (6 pts).** Stated: the husband speaks kindly; the
narrator feels cold and apologizes. Meant: kindness functions
as dismissal — apology is the price of being heard. Terms used
correctly: *understatement* ("apologizing for feeling cold" —
a small phrase for a pattern of dismissal); *irony* (kindness
that silences). Reveals: her perception, her resistance (the
temperature log, the small hand). Conceals: whether the draft
is real, what the husband intends, what the full situation is.
Require at least two quotations.

**Part B2 (6 pts).** Accept either verdict with a definition
and two pieces of evidence. Strong answer: reliable about her
own sensations and her record (the log, the self-observed
apology pattern) but not verifiable about objective facts
(temperature, intent). Cannot determine from this passage
alone: the actual temperature, the husband's motives, or any
medical fact. Credit the distinction between sensation-truth
and fact-truth.

**Part C1 (7 pts).** One paragraph per question (emphasis,
omission, addition, judgment), one story quotation per
paragraph, and a defended verdict (clearer / muddier /
different). Full credit requires the verdict to follow from the
preceding three paragraphs, not merely assert taste.

**Part C2 (7 pts).** Credit a staging that is the learner's own:
passage named, position, sound, lighting each present, each
justified from the text in one sentence. Deduct for copying a
worked example's staging or for choices without textual
justification.

**Part D (12 pts).** Score: theme + texts stated up front (2);
two quotations per text (3); comparison sentence used correctly
(2); objective summary paragraph kept judgment-free before
analysis (2); closing sentence stating what the comparison adds
(2); organization and standard English (1). An argued dissent
from the adult's reading with quotations scores full marks; an
unevidenced repetition of the adult's reading does not. Deduct
for misquotations not corrected before submission.

## Project rubric application

Apply the project.md rubric as written. Notes: (1) A headnote
that fits a text to a movement without naming a strain scores at
most 8 in movement understanding. (2) A point-of-view essay
without a counter-reading paragraph scores at most 8 in that
criterion. (3) An interpretation whose staging choice is not
defended from the text scores at most 6 in that criterion,
however polished the performance. (4) An editor's introduction
that could fit any anthology scores at most 6 in craft.
Record per-criterion scores (not just a total) so the learner
can see what to revise.
