# Unit 03 — Literary Movements, Diverse Voices, and Interpretation

**Grade 11 · Language Arts · American literature, rhetoric, and research pathway · Weeks 10–13 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-27 for issue #52; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Units 01 and 02 taught the learner to read closely and to take a
persuasive text apart. This unit asks a different kind of question:
**what does it mean to read a text as belonging to a movement, to a
voice, and to an interpretation — and who gets to decide?**

A literary movement is not a box a book is born into. It is a shared
set of beliefs about what literature should do, held by writers working
at roughly the same time, in conversation and in argument with one
another. Romanticism trusted feeling and the individual imagination;
Realism insisted on ordinary life rendered faithfully; Naturalism
argued that forces larger than any person — heredity, environment,
chance — shape human fate; Modernism broke forms apart to match a
broken-feeling world; the Harlem Renaissance made Black life, music,
and speech the center of American art rather than its margin. The same
American decades produced writers who fit none of these labels neatly,
and writers who used a movement's tools against its own assumptions.
That tension is the unit's subject.

The unit runs in five moves:

1. **Mapping movements** — a fingerprint for each of five movements
   (Romanticism, Realism, Naturalism, Modernism, the Harlem
   Renaissance) and practice classifying unseen passages by traits,
   not by author name.
2. **Dark Romanticism and Realism** — Hawthorne's "Young Goodman
   Brown" (1835) and Poe: how symbol, setting, and ambiguity make
   meaning that a summary cannot carry.
3. **Diverse voices and point of view** — Gilman's "The Yellow
   Wallpaper" (1892) and Chopin's *The Awakening* (1899): whose
   perspective centers the story, what irony and understatement let
   a narrator say without saying, and what a first-person voice
   hides as well as reveals.
4. **Modernism and the Harlem Renaissance** — fragmentation and
   vernacular voice: how Hughes and his contemporaries made rhythm,
   speech, and image do the work that plot used to do, and how two
   texts from the same period can treat a shared theme — identity,
   freedom, belonging — in opposite ways.
5. **Interpretation across versions** — one story in print, in a
   performed reading, and in an illustration: how every version
   interprets its source, what each version adds and loses, and how
   to evaluate an interpretation with evidence instead of taste.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **when
several good readers disagree about a text, what counts as evidence
for one reading over another — and when is the disagreement itself
the most honest answer?**

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–02 and the week-1 diagnostic (insecure skills are
re-taught in use):

- Cite strong, thorough textual evidence for explicit and inferential
  claims, including where the text leaves matters uncertain
  (RL.11-12.1).
- Determine themes/central ideas, analyze their development and
  interaction, and write an objective summary (RL.11-12.2).
- Use the close-reading routine: annotate, question, connect,
  summarize.
- Name and apply the rhetoric toolkit (claim, evidence, reasoning;
  ethos, pathos, logos; parallelism, antithesis, allusion) from
  Unit 02.

The unit does **not** assume fluency with: analyzing author choices
in developing story elements and structure (RL.11-12.3, RL.11-12.5);
analyzing point of view where stated and meant must be distinguished
— satire, sarcasm, irony, understatement (RL.11-12.6); analyzing
multiple interpretations of a story, drama, or poem across versions
(RL.11-12.7); or demonstrating knowledge of 18th-, 19th-, and
early-20th-century foundational American works, including how two or
more texts from the same period treat similar themes (RL.11-12.9) —
those are this unit's new content.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Map five literary movements — Romanticism, Realism, Naturalism,
   Modernism, the Harlem Renaissance — by their characteristic
   beliefs, subjects, and techniques, and classify an unseen passage
   by movement, justifying the classification with at least two
   named traits and quoted evidence. (RL.11-12.3, RL.11-12.9; track
   objectives 1, 3)
2. Analyze author choices in Hawthorne and Poe — symbol, setting,
   plot order, character introduction, and structure — and explain
   how each choice contributes to meaning and aesthetic impact,
   including where the text deliberately leaves a question
   unresolved. (RL.11-12.3, RL.11-12.5; track objectives 1, 3)
3. Analyze point of view in Gilman and Chopin, distinguishing what
   is directly stated from what is really meant (irony,
   understatement), and explain whose perspective centers the story
   and what that centering reveals and conceals. (RL.11-12.6;
   track objectives 3, 6)
4. Compare how two or more texts from the same period treat a
   similar theme (confinement and autonomy in Gilman and Chopin;
   identity and voice in Hughes and his modernist contemporaries),
   write an objective summary of each text, and state what the
   comparison adds that either text alone does not show.
   (RL.11-12.2, RL.11-12.9; track objectives 1, 2)
5. Analyze multiple interpretations of one story or poem across
   versions (print, performed reading, illustration), evaluating
   how each version interprets the source with specific evidence,
   and present an original interpretation supported by the text.
   (RL.11-12.7, SL.11-12.1, SL.11-12.4; track objectives 7, 12)

**A note on RL.11-12.7's full wording.** The standard's parenthetical
asks for at least one play by Shakespeare and one play by an American
dramatist across the year's interpretation work. This unit teaches
the interpretation-analysis skill on stories, poems, and an
illustration; the Shakespeare and American-drama requirement is met
in Unit 08 (poetry, drama, seminar), which is built for plays. Do not
claim this unit alone satisfies every clause of RL.11-12.7.

## Anchor texts and editions

All anchor texts are public domain. Short quotations in lessons are
checked against the editions below; learners read the full texts
there. The adult previews every text before assigning it.

- Nathaniel Hawthorne, "Young Goodman Brown" (first published 1835;
  collected in *Mosses from an Old Manse*, 1846). Text via Project
  Gutenberg, *Mosses from an Old Manse and Other Stories* (EBook
  #512), <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/512> — the collection
  contents include "Young Goodman Brown." Checked 2026-10-27.
- Edgar Allan Poe, "The Raven" (1845) and "The Tell-Tale Heart"
  (1843). Public-domain texts; the adult selects the edition (the
  unit quotes only the opening line of "The Raven" and paraphrases
  "The Tell-Tale Heart"; learners read full texts from Project
  Gutenberg or the learner's library edition). Checked 2026-10-27.
- Charlotte Perkins Gilman, "The Yellow Wallpaper" (first published
  1892). Text via Project Gutenberg (EBook #1952),
  <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1952>. Checked 2026-10-27.
- Kate Chopin, *The Awakening* (1899) and "Désirée's Baby" (1893).
  Text via Project Gutenberg, *The Awakening and Selected Short
  Stories* (EBook #160), <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/160>.
  Checked 2026-10-27.
- Langston Hughes, "The Negro Speaks of Rivers" (first published in
  *The Crisis*, June 1921) and "I, Too" (1926). Early Hughes poems
  from the 1920s are treated as public domain in the open educational
  edition cited in the Resource Pack; the adult uses the linked
  Poetry Foundation / Academy of American Poets pages for reading
  and confirms the edition before quoting. The unit quotes only
  brief lines and otherwise paraphrases. Checked 2026-10-27.

**A note on sensitive content.** Gilman's story portrays a woman's
mental deterioration under a prescribed "rest cure"; Chopin's novel
ends ambiguously with its heroine walking into the sea. The unit
discusses these as literary and historical facts, without graphic
detail, and the adult frames them as 19th-century portrayals, not as
models or advice. If the material distresses the learner, the adult
pauses the reading and substitutes the practice passages — no
assignment requires dwelling on a distressing scene.

**A note on language.** The texts use 19th-century language about
race, gender, and mental illness that is dated and sometimes
offensive. The unit names that language as historical evidence and
teaches the learner to quote it accurately in analysis while not
adopting it as their own vocabulary.

## 20-session pacing (~50 minutes each)

| Sessions | Work | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| S1–S2 | **Lesson 01** — mapping movements; five fingerprints; classification drill | Learner classifies 2 unseen passages with 2 traits + quotations each |
| S3 | Workshop A — movement sorting (practice.md §A) | Sorting table complete with a reason for every placement |
| S4–S5 | **Lesson 02** — Hawthorne and Poe: symbol, setting, ambiguity | Learner writes a symbol-tracking paragraph naming what a symbol does and what it refuses to settle |
| S6 | Workshop B — ambiguity audit: what "Young Goodman Brown" proves, suggests, leaves open | Three-column audit complete; no claim in the "proved" column without a quotation |
| S7–S8 | **Lesson 03** — Gilman and Chopin: point of view, irony, understatement | Learner rewrites one passage from another character's point of view and explains what changes |
| S9 | Workshop C — whose perspective centers? (practice.md §C) | Perspective map complete for both anchor texts |
| S10–S11 | **Lesson 04** — Modernism and the Harlem Renaissance: fragmentation, vernacular, voice | Learner compares two poems' treatment of identity with quoted evidence |
| S12 | Seminar — "Do movements help us read, or get in the way?" | Every participant speaks at least twice with textual evidence |
| S13–S14 | **Lesson 05** — interpretation across versions; the wallpaper-room illustration study (S14) | Learner writes a version comparison: 2 agreements, 2 differences, 1 evaluated judgment |
| S15 | Drafting studio — unit assessment essays (complete drafts) | Complete draft with 3+ quoted passages |
| S16 | Project workshop — Movement Anthology and Interpretation (project.md) | Anthology plan approved: 3 headnotes + interpretation plan |
| S17 | Review — objectives 1–5 retrieval and re-teaching | Learner can fingerprint all five movements and define irony/understatement without notes |
| S18 | Formative quiz (assessments/week-04_quiz.md) | Scored; misses feed S19 preparation, not punishment |
| S19 | Unit assessment (assessments/unit-assessment.md) | Submitted essays + short answers |
| S20 | Reflection + interpretation presentations; preview U04 | Written reflection; adult records project notes |

Reading load: "Young Goodman Brown" and "The Yellow Wallpaper" are
each short enough to re-read closely several times; *The Awakening*
is assigned in excerpts (chapters 1, 7, and the closing chapters,
per the adult's edition) with the full novel available through the
library; Hughes poems are short lyrics read in full. The adult
assigns first readings the session before each lesson and checks
that the learner arrives with the text annotated, not merely
skimmed.
