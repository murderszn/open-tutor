# Practice — Unit 04: Language, Rhetoric, Syntax, and Stylistic Analysis

Work in the notebook unless the section says otherwise. Every item
asks for both halves of the analysis: **name + effect** (or, for
usage, **routine + decision**). A label without an effect, or a verdict
without a reference, is incomplete. Keys are in `answer-key.md`.

## §A — Syntax labeling and combining (S3, Lesson 01)

1. Label each sentence as periodic, loose, or parallel, and state its
   effect in one sentence:
   - (a) "After the rain stopped, after the road crews arrived, and
     after the first bus crossed the bridge, the town reopened the
     market."
   - (b) "The town reopened the market, after the rain stopped, after
     the road crews arrived, and after the first bus crossed the
     bridge."
   - (c) "We need bread, we need medicine, and we need news."
2. Combine these three short sentences into one periodic sentence
   without changing any fact: "The bell rang." / "The students stood."
   / "The assembly began." Then write one sentence stating what the
   periodic shape does that the three short sentences did not.
3. Combine the same three sentences into one loose sentence. Then
   write one sentence stating which version (your periodic version
   from item 2 or this loose version) better suits a spoken
   announcement, and why.
4. Rewrite this sentence twice — once so it emphasizes the *wait*,
   once so it emphasizes the *arrival* — by changing only the
   sentence structure, not the facts: "The train arrived after a
   delay of four hours." Label each rewrite (periodic/loose) and
   state its effect.
5. A classmate labeled "The committee voted, the mayor signed, and
   the law took effect" as periodic. Correct or confirm the label,
   with a reason, and state the sentence's effect.
6. Write one original fragment-effect sentence pair: a longer
   sentence followed by a deliberate fragment (2–5 words) that creates
   emphasis. Underline the fragment and write one sentence explaining
   what the break does.

## §B — Connotation sorting and the "tabled" drill (S6, Lesson 02)

1. Sort these words into three columns — positive, neutral,
   negative — and write a one-line reason for each placement:
   *persistent, obstinate, determined, pushy, tenacious, headstrong*.
2. For each pair, state the shared denotation and the connotative
   difference in one sentence each: (a) *odor* / *fragrance*;
   (b) *cheap* / *economical*; (c) *childish* / *childlike*.
3. "The committee tabled the motion." State both readings (American
   legislative / British parliamentary), and state what single piece
   of extra information would settle which reading is correct.
4. Rewrite this sentence twice by changing only the adjective — once
   so the speaker sounds sympathetic to the workers, once so the
   speaker sounds hostile to them: "The workers were **unhappy** with
   the new schedule." State the connotative shift in each version.
5. Track the term *reform* across these three invented headlines
   (written for this unit): "City Announces Reform of Bus Routes"
   / "Reform Stalls as Council Argues Over Cuts" / "Critics Call
   Changes 'Reform in Name Only'." In 3–4 sentences, explain how the
   word's connotation shifts across the three headlines and what the
   cumulative effect is.
6. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "The word *slim* is positive
   and the word *skinny* is negative, so *slim* is always the better
   word." Explain why the "always" is wrong, with one context where
   *skinny* is the better choice.

## §C — Tone revision (S9, Lesson 03)

1. Name the figure and state whether it builds or undercuts the
   claim, with a reason, for each:
   - (a) "The budget is a **lifeboat** for the library."
   - (b) "The report was, shall we say, **thoroughly researched**."
     (Context: the report is two pages long and cites nothing.)
   - (c) "The mountain **watched** over the valley."
2. Rewrite this paragraph twice — once toward a hopeful tone, once
   toward a bitter tone — changing only figures and connotative
   words, not the facts: "The factory closed in March. Workers found
   other jobs slowly. The building stands empty." After each version,
   list the words you changed.
3. Read this original sentence (written for this unit): "The
   principal called the protest 'a lively discussion.'" Is the quoted
   phrase understatement, irony, or both? Defend your answer in 2–3
   sentences, naming what the reader must already know for the effect
   to land.
4. A writer argues that a new park is a good idea, then describes it
   as "a **gamble** with taxpayer money." Does the figure build or
   undercut the writer's own claim? Explain in 2–3 sentences.
5. Write one sentence using personification to assign responsibility
   for a problem (for example, "The river **took** the road"). Then
   write one sentence explaining who gains or loses agency because
   of the personification.
6. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "This poem uses a metaphor, so
   it is persuasive." Explain what is missing from that claim, in
   2–3 sentences, using the name → mapping → judgment steps from
   Lesson 03.

## §D — Usage resolution (S11, Lesson 04)

Run the four-step routine (dispute → reference → audience/guide →
decision + trade-off) for each item. Name the reference you consulted.

1. "Each voter should bring **their** ID." Resolve for (a) a formal
   school essay under a traditional guide, and (b) a school
   newsletter. Give both decisions.
2. "There are **less** people in line than yesterday." Is *less*
   defensible here? Resolve with a reference, and state the exception
   that does or does not apply.
3. "This is the rule I told you **about**." A classmate says ending
   with a preposition is always wrong. Resolve the dispute and
   explain the hypercorrection behind the classmate's belief.
4. Edit this paragraph to the traditional school guide from Lesson
   04 (avoid singular *they*; *fewer* for countable nouns; avoid
   split infinitives where a clean alternative exists). List every
   change with its rule:

   > "Every applicant should submit their form, there will be less
   > delays if everyone cooperates, and to really help the office,
   > applicants should arrive early."

5. A dictionary entry for *irregardless* says "nonstandard." A
   classmate argues, "It is in the dictionary, so it is a real word
   and fine to use in an essay." Explain what "nonstandard" means in
   a dictionary entry and why the classmate's conclusion does not
   follow.
6. Choose one: *who* vs. *whom* in "Give the award to **whoever /
   whomever** arrives first." Resolve with the four-step routine,
   stating the case rule that decides it.

## §E — Thesis drills (S14, Lesson 05)

Use the anchor passage "A Letter from Mill Town" (Lesson 05).

1. Write a thesis sentence making a claim about the passage's
   **parallelism** ("our clock, our calendar, our weather vane").
   It must pass the thesis test: effect + means + location.
2. Write a counter-reading to your thesis from item 1 that a
   reasonable reader could actually hold, in one sentence.
3. Write one body paragraph (quote → effect → link-back) for the
   thesis in item 1.
4. Rewrite this weak thesis to pass the thesis test: "The letter uses
   good descriptions to show the town is sad." State what you changed
   and why, in two sentences.
5. Name the tone of the anchor passage in one precise word (not
   "sad" — be more specific: grieving, bitter, resigned, accusatory?).
   Defend your word with one quotation and one effect statement.
6. The passage's final sentence is: "no report written since has
   closed it." Explain, in 2–3 sentences, what "it" refers to at the
   level of the sentence's grammar and at the level of the passage's
   argument — and why the double reference works.
