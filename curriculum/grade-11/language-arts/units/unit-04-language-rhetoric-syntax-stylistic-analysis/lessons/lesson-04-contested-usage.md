# Lesson 04 — Contested Usage: Convention, Change, and the Style Guide

**Unit 04 · Sessions S10–S11 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** L.11-12.1.a–b, L.11-12.2

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can explain why a usage question can have
more than one defensible answer, resolve a usage dispute by consulting
a reference (not by taste), and edit a paragraph to match a named
style guide.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, a dictionary with usage notes (book or free
online dictionary — see the Resource Pack), and the disputes below.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. Is it "wrong" to end a sentence with a preposition ("the rule I
   told you about")? Where did you learn your answer — from a rule
   someone stated, or from observing how careful writers actually
   write?
2. A friend says, "I literally died laughing." Is *literally* used
   correctly? What would you need to check before answering — your
   own feeling, or something else?

(Adult: question 1 surfaces the prescriptive/descriptive split that
drives the whole lesson. Question 2 is the lesson's test case: the
answer depends on what current dictionaries record, not on what the
learner wishes were true.)

## Explicit teaching

### Usage is a convention — and conventions change

Standard English is not a law of nature. It is a **convention**: an
agreement among writers, editors, teachers, and publishers about which
forms count as careful in formal writing. Conventions have three
properties the lesson keeps returning to:

1. **They are real.** Ignoring them has consequences — a résumé with
   "their" for "there" will be judged, fairly or not. Conventions
   coordinate readers' expectations, and coordination is valuable.
2. **They change over time.** *Awful* once meant "inspiring awe";
   *nice* once meant "foolish." The singular *they* was standard in
   Chaucer, Shakespeare, and Austen, fell out of formal favor in the
   19th century when grammarians pushed generic *he*, and has returned
   in 21st-century style guides. A usage rule that ignores history
   will misdescribe the language it claims to protect.
3. **They are sometimes contested.** When careful writers disagree —
   and style guides disagree with each other — the honest answer is
   not "X is wrong" but "X is contested; here is what each side says,
   and here is what the guide for *this* assignment requires."

Two words for two attitudes toward rules: **prescriptive** (stating
how language *should* be used) and **descriptive** (recording how
language *is* used by careful speakers and writers). Good editors are
both: they describe first, then prescribe for a specific audience and
purpose.

### Resolving a dispute: the four-step routine

When a usage question arises, do not answer from taste. Run the
routine:

1. **Name the dispute precisely.** Not "is this wrong?" but "does
   this guide accept singular *they* with a singular antecedent in
   formal prose?"
2. **Consult a reference.** A current dictionary's usage note, a
   style guide (the assignment's guide first), or a usage manual.
   Record what the reference actually says — including its qualifiers
   ("widely accepted," "still contested," "avoid in formal prose").
3. **Name the audience and the guide.** A text message, a school
   essay, and a legal brief have different conventions. The right
   answer for one is the wrong answer for another.
4. **Decide and note the trade-off.** Choose the form the guide
   requires, and be able to say what you gave up (for example,
   "singular *they* avoids the clunky 'he or she' but may still draw
   a correction from a traditional reader").

### Three disputes, resolved honestly

**Dispute 1 — Split infinitives ("to boldly go").** The rule against
splitting infinitives was invented in the 19th century by writers who
wanted English to behave like Latin, where an infinitive is one word
and cannot be split. English infinitives are two words, so the rule
never fit the language. Modern guides (and the Oxford English
Dictionary's usage guidance) accept split infinitives, especially when
splitting avoids awkwardness ("to really understand" is clearer than
"really to understand" or "to understand really"). Verdict: acceptable
in most formal prose; avoid only if the assignment's guide forbids it.

**Dispute 2 — "Less" vs. "fewer."** The traditional rule: *fewer* for
countable things ("fewer chairs"), *less* for uncountable amounts
("less water"). The complication: careful writers have used *less*
with countable nouns for centuries ("less than ten miles," "less than
twenty people" in measurements of time, money, and distance — all
treated as amounts, not counts). Verdict: follow the traditional rule
as the default, but do not "correct" *less* in time/money/distance
expressions; a dictionary usage note will confirm the exception.

**Dispute 3 — Singular "they."** "Each student should bring their
book." Traditional 19th–20th-century school grammar demanded "his or
her book." Current dictionaries record singular *they* for a person
whose gender is unknown or irrelevant as standard, and major style
guides (APA, Chicago, MLA in recent editions) accept it. Verdict:
acceptable in most current formal prose, including school essays,
unless the assignment's guide says otherwise — and the learner should
be able to cite the guide, not merely assert the verdict. (This
dispute is the S12 seminar question.)

### Editing to a guide

A style guide is a house's set of conventions. Editing to a guide
means: (1) read the guide's rule, (2) find every instance in the
draft, (3) change each instance consistently, (4) leave a note where
you deliberately departed and why. Consistency matters more than any
single choice: a paper that uses the Oxford comma in paragraph one
and drops it in paragraph four looks careless even if both choices are
defensible alone.

## Worked example 1 — resolving "literally" with a reference

Dispute: "I literally died laughing." Is *literally* misused?

- **Step 1 (precise dispute):** Does current standard usage accept
  *literally* as an intensifier meaning "virtually / in effect" for
  something figurative?
- **Step 2 (reference):** Current dictionaries (for example,
  Merriam-Webster) record two senses: (a) "in a literal sense," and
  (b) "in effect; virtually — used in an exaggerated way to emphasize
  a statement or description that is not literally true." The entry
  notes that sense (b) is common but criticized. The usage note, not
  the learner's annoyance, is the evidence.
- **Step 3 (audience/guide):** In a text message or a novel's dialogue,
  sense (b) is unremarkable. In a formal essay, most style guides
  still advise against it — the intensifier adds emphasis but also
  invites a correction.
- **Step 4 (decision + trade-off):** In this unit's formal writing,
  avoid intensifier *literally*; the emphasis it adds is smaller than
  the distraction it risks. In informal writing, it is standard
  contemporary usage, and calling it "wrong" without qualification
  misdescribes the language.

Modeled reasoning: notice what the routine did — it replaced a taste
judgment ("that sounds wrong") with a sourced, audience-specific
answer. That replacement is the lesson's transferable skill.

## Worked example 2 — editing a paragraph to a guide

Draft (written for this unit):

> "Each student should hand in their essay by Friday, there should be
> less than 20 pages, and the teacher will read them over the weekend.
> To really understand the feedback, students should revise quickly."

Guide for this assignment: traditional school style — avoid singular
*they*; use *fewer* for countable nouns; avoid intensifier splits
where a clean alternative exists.

Edited version:

> "Each student should hand in his or her essay by Friday. The essay
> should be fewer than 20 pages, and the teacher will read the essays
> over the weekend. To understand the feedback fully, students should
> revise quickly."

- *their essay* → *his or her essay*: follows the assigned guide,
  even though singular *they* is standard elsewhere. Trade-off noted:
  slightly clunkier, but guide-conformant.
- *less than 20 pages* → *fewer than 20 pages*: countable noun
  (pages) takes *fewer* under the traditional rule.
- *to really understand* → *to understand… fully*: the split is
  avoided not because splits are wrong (they are not — see Dispute 1)
  but because this guide prefers the unsplit form and a clean
  alternative exists. The note matters: the edit is about the guide,
  not about the language being broken.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work in pairs. For each dispute, run the four-step routine in your
notebook (dispute → reference → audience/guide → decision + trade-off).
Hints are in parentheses.

1. "The data **is** convincing." vs. "The data **are** convincing."
   (Hint: *data* is historically the plural of *datum* — what do
   current dictionaries record for general prose?)
2. "I could care **less**." (Hint: the idiom is "couldn't care less"
   — is the shortened form an error, or an established variant? Check
   a dictionary's idiom note before deciding.)
3. "Between you and **I**, the plan is risky." (Hint: what case does
   a preposition require, and why does the hypercorrection happen?)

## Independent practice

1. Resolve this dispute with the four-step routine, citing a
   reference by name: may a formal essay end a sentence with a
   preposition? Write the full routine (all four steps) in your
   notebook.
2. Edit this paragraph to the traditional school guide named in
   Worked example 2, and list every change with its rule:

   > "Every writer should check their sources, there should be less
   > mistakes than in the last draft, and to fully trust the argument,
   > readers need to see the evidence."

3. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "Dictionaries just record
   mistakes until they become correct, so usage rules are pointless."
   Explain, in 3–4 sentences, what is right and what is wrong in that
   claim — using the convention/change/contest distinction from the
   teaching.

## Applied task

Choose one usage dispute not covered in this lesson (for example:
*who* vs. *whom*; *that* vs. *which*; *affect* vs. *effect*; *nauseous*
vs. *nauseated*; the Oxford comma). Research it with a dictionary and
one style guide, then write a half-page "usage brief" containing:

1. The dispute stated precisely.
2. What each reference says, quoted or closely paraphrased, with the
   reference named.
3. Your recommendation for formal school prose, and the trade-off of
   following it.
4. One sentence you wrote yourself that follows your recommendation.

Bring the brief to S12; the seminar will test briefs against each
other.

## Exit check

In your notebook: name one usage rule you previously believed was
absolute, state what a reference actually says about it, and write
one sentence about whether your belief changed. If the reference did
not change your mind, say why — "the dictionary is wrong" is not an
acceptable reason; name the audience or guide that would justify your
position.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** the four-step routine is a scaffold,
  not a test — post it visibly during practice, and allow the learner
  to run it aloud with the adult before writing it.
- **Alternate response mode:** instead of writing the usage brief,
  present it orally (2–3 minutes) with the reference open, or dictate
  it to the adult.
- **For learners who treat every guide as gospel:** remind them that
  guides disagree with each other (Chicago vs. AP on the Oxford comma,
  for example). The skill is choosing *and justifying* a guide for an
  audience, not obeying one forever.

## Extension

Compare how two dictionaries handle the same contested word (for
example, *irregardless* or intensifier *literally*). Quote both usage
notes, note where they agree and differ, and write a paragraph on
what the difference reveals about descriptive vs. prescriptive
approaches. Bring it to S12.

## Teacher note

The most common misconception is that "correct" is a property of a
sentence rather than a judgment relative to a guide and an audience.
Learners will keep asking "but which is *really* right?" — answer by
returning to the routine: the question is incomplete until it names a
guide and an audience. The second misconception is that descriptivism
means "anything goes": it does not — it means the standard is what
careful writers actually do, which is a demanding standard, not an
absent one. Answer-key reference: `../answer-key.md`, Lesson 04.
