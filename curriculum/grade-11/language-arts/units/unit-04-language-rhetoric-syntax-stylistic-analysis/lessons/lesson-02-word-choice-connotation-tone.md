# Lesson 02 — Word Choice: Denotation, Connotation, and Multiple Meanings

**Unit 04 · Sessions S4–S5 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RI.11-12.4, L.11-12.4, L.11-12.6

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can separate what a word means
(denotation) from what it suggests (connotation), explain how a word
with multiple meanings creates ambiguity, and revise a sentence by
swapping one word and explaining the shift that swap causes.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, a dictionary (book or free online dictionary —
see the Resource Pack), and the sentences below.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. A news report calls a group "protesters"; another calls the same
   group "rioters." Do the two reports disagree about facts, about
   word choice, or both? Explain in two sentences.
2. List three words for "a person who will not change their mind":
   one neutral, one admiring, one critical. (If you can only find
   one, that is itself the lesson — keep it in mind.)

(Adult: question 1 previews the lesson's central move — connotation
does argumentative work before any claim is stated. Question 2 is the
classic *stubborn / resolute / obstinate* set; accept any defensible
trio and return to it in the teaching.)

## Explicit teaching

### Denotation and connotation

**Denotation** is a word's dictionary definition — what it points to.
**Connotation** is the feeling, attitude, or association the word
carries with it — what it suggests beyond the definition. Two words
can share a denotation and differ entirely in connotation:

| Denotation (shared) | Neutral | Admiring | Critical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Will not change their mind | firm | resolute | stubborn |
| Inexpensive | low-cost | a bargain | cheap |
| Asks many questions | inquisitive | curious | nosy |
| Large group of people | crowd | gathering | mob |

A writer who chooses *resolute* instead of *stubborn* has not changed
the facts. They have changed the reader's attitude toward the facts —
and attitude, once set, colors every claim that follows. That is why
word choice is a rhetorical act, not a decorative one.

### Multiple meanings (polysemy)

Many words carry several related meanings, and context decides which
one is live. Usually context decides silently and correctly. Sometimes
it does not — and the ambiguity itself becomes the argument, or the
trap:

- "The committee **tabled** the motion." In American legislative
  usage, *to table* usually means to set aside (postpone). In British
  parliamentary usage, *to table* means to bring forward for
  discussion. Same word, opposite actions — and a reader who does not
  know which room they are in will misunderstand the outcome entirely.
- "The **fine** print." *Fine* as "of high quality"? As "small and
  hard to read"? As "a penalty"? The phrase survives because all three
  hover at once — and because the smallness is the point.
- "She gave a **cool** response." Temperature? Composure? Hostility?
  The sentence refuses to say, and the refusal is itself a tone.

The analyst's question is always: *which meaning does this context
activate, and what work does the ambiguity do?* Sometimes ambiguity is
a flaw to be edited out; sometimes it is the writer's instrument.
Telling those apart is the skill.

### Cumulative effect

One connotative word is a nudge. Ten of them, all leaning the same
way, are a slope. RI.11-12.4 asks for the **cumulative** impact of
word choice: track a key term across a passage and watch how the
author "uses and refines" it. A writer who calls a plan "bold" in
paragraph one, "daring" in paragraph two, and "reckless" in paragraph
four has not merely described the plan three ways — they have told you
how to feel about it, in stages.

## Worked example 1 — one swap, three attitudes

Original: "The mayor **insisted** that the budget be cut."

- *Insisted* (denotation: stated firmly and repeatedly). Connotation:
  mildly critical — the mayor is pushing, perhaps against resistance.

Revision A: "The mayor **argued** that the budget be cut."

- Connotation shifts toward neutral and reasonable: arguing is what
  people do with reasons. The reader is invited to hear the case
  rather than judge the pushiness.

Revision B: "The mayor **demanded** that the budget be cut."

- Connotation shifts toward authoritarian: demanding is what people
  with power do when they do not expect to be refused. The same budget
  cut now feels imposed rather than proposed.

Modeled reasoning: none of the three sentences lies. Each selects a
verb whose connotation pre-judges the mayor. A careful reader notices
the verb *before* deciding whether the budget cut is good — because
the verb is already arguing.

## Worked example 2 — tracking a term across a passage

Read this original paragraph (written for this unit):

> "The new schedule was called **flexible** when it was announced,
> **adaptable** when the first complaints arrived, and **unpredictable**
> by the third week, when nobody knew which shift they were working.
> The word changed because the experience changed — or the experience
> changed because nobody would hold the word still long enough to
> check it."

- *Flexible* (paragraph position: announcement): positive connotation
  — freedom, responsiveness. It sells the schedule.
- *Adaptable* (complaints arrive): still positive, but defensive —
  the word retreats from promise to excuse.
- *Unpredictable* (third week): negative connotation — the same
  variability, now experienced as chaos rather than freedom.
- Cumulative effect: the passage does not argue "the schedule is bad"
  in a single claim. It lets the vocabulary slide from praise to
  blame, so the reader *feels* the deterioration in the word choices
  themselves. That slide is the argument.

Modeled reasoning: ask not only "what does this word mean here?" but
"what did this word mean two paragraphs ago, and what changed?"
Authors refine key terms across a text (RI.11-12.4); tracking the
refinement is tracking the argument.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work in pairs. For each item, name the connotation (positive, neutral,
negative) and write one sentence explaining the shift or the ambiguity.
Hints are in parentheses.

1. "The proposal was **ambitious**." vs. "The proposal was
   **grandiose**." (Hint: both mean large in scope.)
2. "The crowd **gathered** outside the hall." vs. "The mob **gathered**
   outside the hall." (Hint: which noun pre-judges the crowd?)
3. "The committee tabled the motion." (Hint: two rooms, two opposite
   meanings — name both and say what extra information would settle
   it.)
4. "Her answer was **curt**." (Hint: denotation — brief. What does
   *curt* add that *brief* does not?)

## Independent practice

1. For each pair, state the shared denotation, then explain the
   connotative difference in one sentence each:
   - (a) *slim* / *skinny*
   - (b) *confident* / *arrogant*
   - (c) *thrifty* / *stingy*
2. Rewrite this sentence three ways — once admiring, once neutral,
   once critical — by changing only the verb: "The student
   **questioned** the teacher's grading." Then write one sentence for
   each version explaining what the verb choice does to the reader's
   attitude toward the student.
3. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "The words *house* and *home*
   have the same meaning, so they are interchangeable in writing."
   Explain why the classmate is wrong, using denotation and connotation
   and one original example sentence for each word.

## Applied task

Choose one paragraph from a text you are reading in another subject
(or from Unit 02's Declaration passage). Underline every word with a
strong connotation (positive or negative). Then:

1. List the words in two columns (positive / negative) and count
   each column. Which way does the paragraph lean, and by how much?
2. Swap two words for near-synonyms with the opposite connotation.
   Read the paragraph aloud with the swaps. What changed — the facts,
   the attitude, or both?
3. Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences) arguing whether the original
   paragraph's word choices are fair persuasion or loaded language,
   with at least two quoted words as evidence.

## Exit check

In your notebook: write one sentence containing a word with a strong
connotation, then rewrite the same sentence with a neutral near-synonym,
and state in one sentence what the swap changed. If you cannot name
the change, you have described the words but not analyzed them.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** keep a running connotation chart
  (word / denotation / connotation / example) in the notebook; add to
  it every session of this unit rather than memorizing lists.
- **Alternate response mode:** instead of writing the applied-task
  paragraph, present the two-column sort orally with the swapped
  paragraph read aloud, and dictate the fairness judgment.
- **For learners who call every strong word "biased":** distinguish
  *loaded* (connotation doing hidden work) from *precise* (connotation
  matching the evidence). A word is not unfair merely because it has
  feeling in it.

## Extension

Research one word whose connotation has changed over time (for
example, *awful* once meant "inspiring awe"; *nice* once meant
"foolish"). Write a half-page history of the shift with a dictionary
as your source, and connect it to Lesson 04's claim that usage changes
over time. Bring it to S6.

## Teacher note

The most common misconception is that connotation is a property of the
word alone. It is not — it is a property of the word *in a context, for
an audience*. *Cheap* is praise at a discount store and an insult
applied to a gift. Always ask "cheap to whom, about what?" before
labeling. Answer-key reference: `../answer-key.md`, Lesson 02.
