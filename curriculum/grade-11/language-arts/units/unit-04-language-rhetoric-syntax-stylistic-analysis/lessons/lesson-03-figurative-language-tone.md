# Lesson 03 — Figurative Language and Tone in Argument

**Unit 04 · Sessions S7–S8 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RI.11-12.4, RI.11-12.6, L.11-12.5.a

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can identify a figure of speech in context,
explain what it does to an argument (builds it up or undercuts it),
and describe the tone it creates — with quoted evidence, not just a
label.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, and the passages below.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. "The new policy is a **bandage on a broken leg**." What does the
   comparison claim about the policy? Is the claim stated directly, or
   does the image carry it?
2. Write one sentence that is polite on the surface but critical
   underneath (for example, about a long meeting). What makes the
   criticism readable even though the words are polite?

(Adult: question 1 previews metaphor-as-argument — the image does the
reasoning. Question 2 previews irony and understatement: tone is the
gap between the words and what they mean. Both answers will be reused
in the teaching.)

## Explicit teaching

### Figures of speech are arguments, not decorations

A figure of speech says one thing in terms of another. In literary
analysis, figures are often treated as beauty. In rhetorical analysis,
they are **moves**: a metaphor does not decorate a claim, it *makes*
one — by mapping the qualities of one thing onto another, it tells
the reader how to judge the second thing before any evidence arrives.

- **Metaphor** (direct comparison): "The budget is a straitjacket."
  The claim — the budget prevents movement — is carried entirely by
  the image. Refute the image ("a straitjacket is imposed; a budget
  is voted") and you have refuted the argument.
- **Simile** (comparison with *like* or *as*): "The committee moved
  like a glacier." Slow, massive, unstoppable — or slow, cold, and
  indifferent? The simile's evaluation depends on which qualities of
  glaciers the reader activates. That openness is a feature: similes
  recruit the reader's own associations.
- **Personification** (human qualities given to a non-human thing):
  "The town **refused** to forget." Towns do not refuse; people do.
  Personification assigns agency — and therefore responsibility — to
  something abstract. Watch who gains agency when a writer
  personifies: the town, the market, the law, history itself.
- **Understatement** (saying less than the case warrants): "The flood
  was **an inconvenience**." Said about a disaster, understatement
  creates irony by the gap between the word and the fact. It can
  signal composure, or contempt, or a refusal to panic — tone decides
  which, and tone is read from context.
- **Irony** (meaning the opposite of, or something sharply different
  from, what the words say): "What a **fine** mess the committee made
  of the schedule." Irony outsources the criticism to the reader: you
  must notice the gap yourself, which makes the criticism feel like
  your own discovery rather than the writer's assertion.

### How diction builds or undercuts an argument

Diction — the writer's total word choice — sets **tone**, the
attitude the writing takes toward its subject. Tone is the argument's
emotional weather, and figures are its instruments:

- A passage that builds an argument uses figures that **reinforce**
  its claim: a reformer calls a law "a bridge" and then argues that
  bridges must be maintained — the metaphor and the claim walk in
  step.
- A passage that undercuts an argument uses figures that **clash**
  with its claim: a speaker praises a plan as "bold" and then
  describes it with images of gambling and luck — the diction quietly
  withdraws the praise it just offered.
- The analyst's test: **does the figure's evaluation match the
  claim's evaluation?** When they match, the figure builds. When they
  diverge, the figure undercuts — sometimes deliberately (irony, a
  deliberate jab), sometimes accidentally (a careless metaphor that
  argues against its author).

### Tone is cumulative

Tone is rarely set by one word. It accumulates across a paragraph the
way connotation accumulated in Lesson 02: a funeral described with
"solemn," "quiet," and "still" is one tone; the same funeral described
with "efficient," "brief," and "handled" is another — and the second
tone is itself an argument about what funerals are for.

## Worked example 1 — a metaphor that builds, then a metaphor that undercuts

Read this original paragraph (written for this unit):

> "The apprenticeship program is a **bridge** between school and
> work. Students cross it carrying what they learned, and employers
> meet them on the other side with real jobs. But a bridge no one
> maintains becomes a **ruin** — and this program's funding has been
> cut three years running. We are not debating whether to build the
> bridge. We are debating whether to let it fall."

- **Building figure:** "bridge" maps school-to-work transition onto a
  structure that connects two banks. The mapping supplies the
  argument's logic: connections need building, and both sides must
  meet. The metaphor *is* the case for the program.
- **Undercutting figure (deliberate):** "ruin" reuses the same
  metaphor against the funding cuts — the bridge image, once
  positive, turns into an accusation. The writer does not change
  images; they let one image change its charge. That is why the
  paragraph is persuasive: the opponent's position (cut funding) is
  reframed *inside the writer's own metaphor* as letting something
  fall.
- **Tone:** urgent but constructive — the paragraph argues for repair,
  not for blame. Note how the final sentence's parallelism ("We are
  not debating… We are debating…") sharpens the reframe.

Modeled reasoning: to analyze a figure, do three things — (1) name it
and quote it, (2) state the mapping (what is compared to what, and
which qualities transfer), (3) judge whether the figure's evaluation
matches or fights the claim. Step 3 is the analysis; steps 1–2 are
only preparation.

## Worked example 2 — understatement and irony as tone

Read this original paragraph (written for this unit):

> "The committee's report was **brief**. It was also **late**,
> **unsigned**, and **missing** the appendix it had promised. The
> chair called the result '**a good first step**.' Those of us who had
> waited six months for it called it something else, though not, in
> the end, in public."

- **Understatement:** "brief" is technically true (the report is
  short) and wildly insufficient (it is also late, unsigned, and
  incomplete). The understatement is the criticism: by saying less
  than the case warrants, the writer makes the reader supply the
  missing judgment — *this is not good enough*.
- **Irony:** "'a good first step'" is quoted precisely so the reader
  can hear it against the facts. The chair's phrase is not endorsed;
  it is exhibited. The quotation marks do argumentative work.
- **Tone:** controlled anger. The paragraph never raises its voice —
  no "outrageous," no "disgraceful." The restraint *is* the tone, and
  the tone argues that the writer is the reasonable party and the
  committee is not.
- **What the figure cannot do:** understatement persuades only a
  reader who shares the context. A reader who does not know the report
  was six months late will read "brief" as praise. Figures depend on
  shared knowledge — which is why the analyst always asks: *what must
  the reader already know for this to land?*

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work in pairs. For each item, name the figure, quote it, state the
mapping or gap, and judge: does it build or undercut the claim?
Hints are in parentheses.

1. "The new tax is a **leash** on small businesses." (Hint: what
   qualities of a leash transfer? Who holds it?)
2. "The mayor's plan was, to put it mildly, **ambitious**."
   (Hint: "to put it mildly" is a signal — of what?)
3. "History **will judge** this council." (Hint: who is given the
   power to judge, and what does that do to present responsibility?)
4. "Oh, **wonderful** — another meeting about the meeting."
   (Hint: the evaluation word fights the situation it describes.)

## Independent practice

1. Read this original paragraph (written for this unit) and answer
   (a)–(c):

   > "The river festival was the town's **heartbeat** for fifty
   > years: it pumped money into shops, it carried news between
   > neighborhoods, it kept the old songs alive. When the festival
   > was cancelled, the town did not die. It **held its breath**.
   > Three years later, it is still holding it."

   - (a) Name the two figures (one in sentence 1, one in sentence 3)
     and quote each.
   - (b) For each, state the mapping: what is compared to what, and
     which qualities transfer?
   - (c) Does the second figure build on or undercut the first?
     Explain in 2–3 sentences, naming the tone the pair creates.
2. Rewrite the festival paragraph's last sentence twice: once so the
   tone becomes hopeful, once so it becomes bitter — changing only
   the figure (not the facts). Write one sentence after each version
   explaining what the new figure does.
3. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "The sentence 'The wind
   whispered through the pines' is personification, so it proves the
   author believes trees can talk." Explain the error (two sentences
   minimum): what personification actually does, and what evidence
   would be needed for the classmate's conclusion.

## Applied task

Write a paragraph (6–8 sentences) arguing for or against a school or
town policy you care about, using exactly two figures: one that builds
your claim and one that undercuts the opposing position. Then:

1. Underline both figures and label each (build / undercut).
2. Trade paragraphs with a partner. Each partner identifies the two
   figures without being told, and states whether each one builds or
   undercuts. Compare: did your partner read them the way you
   intended? Where the reading differs, the figure — not the reader —
   is usually the problem; revise it.
3. Write a 3-sentence reflection: which figure was harder to control,
   and why?

## Exit check

In your notebook: quote one figure from today's lesson, name it, and
write one sentence stating whether it builds or undercuts its claim
and why. A label without the build/undercut judgment is incomplete.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** figures are easier to hear than to
  see — read every practice passage aloud twice, once straight and
  once exaggerating the figure, and note what changes.
- **Alternate response mode:** instead of writing the applied-task
  paragraph, record it as a short speech (1–2 minutes), marking the
  two figures by pausing before each.
- **For learners who label every comparison "metaphor":** use the
  *like/as* test — if the comparison uses *like* or *as*, it is a
  simile; if it does not, it may be a metaphor. Then ask what the
  figure *does* — the label is the least important part.

## Extension

Find one figure in a song lyric, speech, or advertisement. Write a
half-page analysis: quote the figure, state the mapping, judge
build/undercut, and name the shared knowledge the figure depends on.
Bring it to S9.

## Teacher note

The most common misconception is that identifying a figure is the
analysis. It is the first step of three (name → mapping → judgment),
and learners will stop at step one unless every task demands step
three. The second misconception is that irony always means "the
opposite": understatement, overstatement, and sarcasm all live under
irony's roof, and the lesson's examples show understatement and
sarcasm specifically. Answer-key reference: `../answer-key.md`,
Lesson 03.
