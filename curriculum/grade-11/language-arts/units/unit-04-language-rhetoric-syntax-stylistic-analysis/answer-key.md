# Answer Key — Unit 04: Language, Rhetoric, Syntax, and Stylistic Analysis

**For the guiding adult.** Worked solutions for every lesson item,
practice item, quiz question, assessment task, and project rubric
application. Open responses give sample reasoning and acceptable
variation: accept any response that names the required structure or
term, quotes or describes the evidence, and states a defensible
effect; reject responses that label without explaining, or explain
without evidence.

## Lesson 01 — Syntax as style

**Readiness check.** (1) Any pair is acceptable; the short sentence
typically feels more urgent because it lands at once, while the long
sentence delays and explains. (2) Main clause: "Rain fell." (stands
alone). Subordinate clause: "when the rain started" (leans on a main
clause).

**Guided practice.**

1. Periodic — the main clause ("Mara had already finished the problem
   set") comes last, so the delay makes her speed feel remarkable:
   three "before" phrases pile up before the payoff.
2. Parallelism — three clauses share the shape "we will + verb +
   object," creating rhythm, balance, and a sense of a complete plan.
3. Short-sentence series — the staccato pace makes each fact land
   separately; the sequence feels urgent and stark, like a news ticker.
4. Repetition broken — "She did not argue. She did not plead. She
   left." The repeated negation sets a pattern that the short final
   sentence breaks; the break signals quiet resolve: action replaces
   words.

**Independent practice.**

1. (a) Periodic — the delay makes the meeting feel long-awaited and
   the calling of it feel inevitable after three accumulated causes.
   (b) Loose — the news lands first and the causes follow as
   justification; this suits a report, where the meeting itself is the
   news. (c) Fragment-effect ("Not yet.") — the two-word fragment
   interrupts and emphasizes refusal or delay; the break is the point.
   (d) Parallelism — the three infinitive phrases balance against
   each other and the closing clause ("that is the work") gathers them
   into a definition; the form argues completeness.
2. Loose rewrite: "The meeting was finally called, when the letter
   arrived, when the whole street had read it, and when the mayor
   could no longer ignore it." For a speech to an angry crowd, the
   periodic version is better: the crowd hears its own experience
   accumulate (letter, street, mayor) before the verdict, so the
   verdict lands as a climax rather than a bulletin. (Accept the
   reverse judgment if defended on clarity grounds, but the speech
   context favors the periodic build.)
3. The classmate is wrong. "The storm passed, the lights returned, and
   the town exhaled" is a compound sentence of three independent
   clauses, not a periodic sentence: the first clause is already a
   complete main clause, so nothing is withheld. Correct label:
   compound/parallel. Effect: three equal actions in sequence make
   recovery feel like a chain of events, each enabling the next.

**Applied task (sentence strips).** Accept any paragraph whose five
sentences grow from shortest to longest about a door opening, with a
note on where rhythm speeds or slows. A short sentence after a long
one should be identified as the emphasis point. Text-only alternative:
strips 1–5 measure about 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 cm in rising order.

**Exit check (sample).** "Anaphora: 'They asked for a hearing. They
asked for records. They asked for a vote.' The repetition builds a
drumbeat of demands so the refusal that follows feels like a pattern
broken."

## Lesson 02 — Word choice

**Readiness check.** (1) Both: the reports may agree on the facts
(a group gathered and clashed with police, say) and still differ,
because *protesters* vs. *rioters* pre-judges the group. (2) Sample
trio: neutral *firm*, admiring *resolute*, critical *stubborn*.

**Guided practice.**

1. *Ambitious* (positive: large in scope, admirable) vs. *grandiose*
   (negative: large in scope, excessive and showy). Same scope, opposite
   judgment.
2. *Gathered* + *crowd* is neutral; *mob* pre-judges the group as
   disorderly and threatening before any action is described.
3. American legislative usage: *tabled* = set aside, postponed.
   British parliamentary usage: *tabled* = brought forward for
   discussion. Settling information: which country's procedure (or
   whether the motion was debated afterward or shelved).
4. *Curt* denotes brief; it adds rudeness and abruptness that *brief*
   does not. A curt answer is short *and* dismissive.

**Independent practice.**

1. (a) *Slim* (positive/neutral: attractively thin) vs. *skinny*
   (negative: too thin, unhealthy). (b) *Confident* (positive:
   self-assured) vs. *arrogant* (negative: overbearing, dismissive of
   others). (c) *Thrifty* (positive: wisely careful with money) vs.
   *stingy* (negative: unwilling to spend even when appropriate).
2. Sample: admiring — "The student **examined** the teacher's
   grading." (Diligent, careful; the student is a serious learner.)
   Neutral — "The student **asked about** the teacher's grading."
   (No attitude either way.) Critical — "The student
   **second-guessed** the teacher's grading." (Distrustful; the student
   doubts the teacher's competence.) Each verb pre-judges the student
   before the facts are heard.
3. The classmate is wrong. *House* denotes a dwelling; *home* adds
   connotations of belonging, warmth, and family. Example: "They
   bought a house on Elm Street" (the building, neutral) vs. "After
   twenty years abroad, she finally came home" (belonging, not just
   arrival). Interchangeability fails wherever the connotation, not
   the denotation, is the point.

**Applied task.** Accept any two-column sort with counts and a
lean judgment, two opposite-connotation swaps with a read-aloud
note, and a fairness paragraph quoting at least two words. "Loaded"
requires connotation doing hidden work; "precise" requires connotation
matching the evidence — either verdict is acceptable if argued from
the quoted words.

**Exit check (sample).** "The plan was *bold*." → "The plan was
*large-scale*." The swap removes the admiring daredevil coloring, so
the reader judges the plan's size without being nudged to admire it.

## Lesson 03 — Figurative language and tone

**Readiness check.** (1) The comparison claims the policy treats a
symptom while ignoring a much larger injury — the image carries the
claim, not a stated argument. (2) Any polite-but-critical sentence is
acceptable; the criticism is readable from the gap between the polite
words and the situation (for example, "What a *productive* use of an
afternoon" about a pointless meeting).

**Guided practice.**

1. Metaphor ("leash"). Mapping: a leash restrains and is held by
   someone — the tax restrains small businesses and the government
   holds it. It builds the critical claim: the evaluation (control,
   restriction) matches the criticism.
2. Understatement used ironically ("to put it mildly"). The phrase
   signals that the real judgment is stronger than *ambitious* — the
   plan is overambitious or reckless. The surface word is polite; the
   signal withdraws the politeness.
3. Personification ("History will judge"). History gains the agency
   of a judge, which builds moral gravity — but displaces present
   responsibility onto the future. Accept "builds" (gravity) or
   "undercuts" (defers accountability) if the mechanism is named;
   the strongest answers name both.
4. Sarcasm/irony ("wonderful"). The evaluation word fights the
   situation (another pointless meeting); the gap is the criticism.

**Independent practice.**

1. (a) Sentence 1: metaphor — "the town's **heartbeat**." Sentence 3:
   metaphor — "It **held its breath**." (b) Heartbeat maps the
   festival onto a heart pumping money, news, and songs through the
   town like blood; holding breath maps the town onto a person
   suspended, waiting, not dead but paused. (c) The second figure
   builds on the first: a heart that holds its breath is still alive
   but suspended. Together they create a tone of anxious hope — life
   interrupted, not ended.
2. Hopeful: "Three years later, it is still waiting for its next
   beat." (The heart image continues; waiting implies a beat to come.)
   Bitter: "Three years later, it is still holding it, and the air is
   running out." (The suspension becomes suffocation.) Each version
   must explain what its new figure does — the hopeful one extends
   life, the bitter one converts waiting into harm.
3. The classmate confuses a figure with a belief. Personification
   attributes human qualities for effect (to make the wind's sound
   vivid); it does not assert that trees can talk. The conclusion
   would need evidence outside the figure — for example, the author
   stating as fact that trees speak, or treating them as literal
   agents elsewhere.

**Applied task.** Accept any paragraph with two labeled figures (one
build, one undercut), a partner check noting agreement or mismatch,
and a 3-sentence reflection. Where partner readings differ, the
revision (not the defense) is the expected next step.

**Exit check (sample).** "The budget is a straitjacket." Metaphor. It
builds the critical claim because a straitjacket restrains by force —
the figure's evaluation matches the criticism.

## Lesson 04 — Contested usage

**Readiness check.** (1) Any answer is acceptable as a starting
point; the lesson's answer is that no modern guide forbids terminal
prepositions as a rule — the "rule" is a Latin-based myth. (2) The
answer requires a reference, not a feeling: current dictionaries
record intensifier *literally* as a real but criticized sense.

**Guided practice** (four-step routine in each answer; reference =
current dictionary usage note or named style guide).

1. *Data*: historically the plural of *datum*, but current
   dictionaries record singular mass-noun use ("the data is") as
   standard in general prose. Decision: general prose → "is" is
   acceptable; scientific or formal writing → "are" is safer.
   Trade-off: "are" sounds more precise to traditional readers; "is"
   sounds more natural to general readers.
2. "Could care less": dictionaries record it as an established
   American idiom, often criticized as illogical (the logical form is
   "couldn't care less"). Decision: acceptable in informal speech;
   avoid in formal writing, where the expected form is "couldn't care
   less." Trade-off: the idiom is natural but invites a correction.
3. "Between you and I" is wrong: a preposition requires the object
   case ("me"). The hypercorrection comes from over-applying the
   subject rule ("you and I" as subjects) to object position.
   Correct: "Between you and me."

**Independent practice.**

1. Terminal prepositions: dispute — does formal prose forbid ending
   a sentence with a preposition? Reference — modern style guides and
   dictionary usage notes: no such rule exists; the ban was derived
   from Latin. Audience/guide — formal school essay: acceptable,
   especially where rewording ("the rule about which I told you") is
   stiffer. Decision: allow terminal prepositions; trade-off: a
   traditional reader may still flag them, so prefer the natural
   phrasing and reword only when it reads better.
2. Edited paragraph: "Every writer should check his or her sources,
   there should be fewer mistakes than in the last draft, and to
   trust the argument fully, readers need to see the evidence."
   Changes: *their* → *his or her* (singular-*they* avoidance per the
   assigned guide); *less* → *fewer* (mistakes are countable);
   *to fully trust* → *to trust… fully* (split infinitive avoided
   where a clean alternative exists per the assigned guide).
3. The classmate is half right and half wrong. Right: dictionaries
   are descriptive — they record forms people actually use, including
   nonstandard ones. Wrong: recording is not endorsing, and rules are
   not pointless — conventions coordinate readers' expectations, and
   a guide's job is to choose among recorded forms for a specific
   audience. "Nonstandard" in an entry means "recorded but outside
   standard educated use," which is exactly the information a formal
   writer needs.
4. Applied-task briefs vary by dispute chosen. The brief must contain
   the dispute stated precisely, two named references with their
   positions, a recommendation for formal school prose, a trade-off,
   and one original sentence following the recommendation.

**Exit check.** Accept any rule reconsidered against a reference
(for example, split infinitives, terminal prepositions, singular
*they*), with the reference's actual position stated. "The dictionary
is wrong" without an audience or guide justification is incomplete.

## Lesson 05 — Stylistic analysis essay

**Readiness check.** (1) A theme claim states what a passage is
about; a style claim states what a sentence shape or word choice
*does* to create that meaning. This unit's essay requires the style
claim. (2) Any sentence naming structure/word choice, quoting or
describing it, and stating its effect passes; missing parts must be
added.

**Guided practice.**

1. Sample thesis: "The triple series 'our clock, our calendar, our
   weather vane' makes the whistle the town's entire ordering system —
   not a sound but the structure of daily life — so that its stopping
   reads as the collapse of time itself."
2. Sample counter-reading: "A reader might argue the triple series is
   merely lyrical decoration, listing jobs the whistle happened to do,
   rather than an argument about how the town experienced time."
3. Sample body paragraph: "The series builds by accumulation: clock
   (the day), calendar (the year), weather vane (the seasons) — three
   scales of time, each governed by the same whistle. The effect is
   totalizing: no part of the town's routine escapes the whistle's
   authority. That totality is what makes the later silence so
   complete — the passage has established that losing the whistle
   means losing the town's routine itself, which is the thesis's
   claim about time."

**Independent practice.**

1. Sample thesis paragraph (tone = grieving, held under control):
   "The passage's tone is grief held under control, created by short,
   flat declarations that refuse ornament — 'The town did not mourn
   the mill. The town mourned the rhythm.' The flatness is the grief:
   a town performing sorrow would wail, but this town states. A
   reader might counter that the flatness signals resignation rather
   than grief; but the passage names mourning twice and chooses
   'ending' over the council's 'transition,' so the flat sentences
   read as grief disciplined, not grief absent."
2. Sample outline: thesis (sudden grief via syntactic break) → chain
   1 (periodic opening → fragment break) → chain 2 (connotative
   slide: 'transition' vs. 'ending') → chain 3 (parallelism of the
   whistle series: clock/calendar/weather vane) → conclusion (the
   passage performs the stop it describes; a theme-only reading would
   call it "about loss" and miss the performed suddenness).
3. Rewritten thesis: "The letter makes grief feel sudden by breaking
   its long periodic opening into short, flat declarations, so that
   the town's loss arrives as a stop rather than a fade." Changes:
   replaced vague "good descriptions" with a named means (periodic →
   fragment break) and vague "sad"/"emotional" with a precise effect
   (sudden grief), and located the means (the opening). The vague
   version failed the thesis test on all three questions; the rewrite
   answers all three.

**Applied task (reread).** Accept any one-page reread with the three
paragraphs (earlier reading; one structure + one word choice, quoted,
with effects; what the stylistic reading changes or deepens). The
third paragraph may conclude "confirms and deepens" if it says how.

**Exit check.** The thesis paragraph must contain an underlined
effect, means, and counter-reading; any missing element must be
revised before S15.

## Practice §A — Syntax labeling and combining

1. (a) Periodic — the delay makes the reopening feel earned after
   three accumulated causes. (b) Loose — the news lands first; the
   causes follow as justification. (c) Parallelism — three
   same-shape clauses create rhythm and a sense of a complete,
   indivisible set of needs.
2. Periodic combine: "When the bell rang, when the students stood,
   the assembly began." (Main clause last.) Effect: the sequence
   builds and the beginning feels inevitable rather than merely next.
   Accept "When the bell rang and the students stood, the assembly
   began" as periodic with the same effect statement.
3. Loose combine: "The assembly began when the bell rang and the
   students stood." The loose version better suits a spoken
   announcement: the news (assembly began) comes first, which is what
   a listener waiting for information needs.
4. Wait-emphasis (periodic): "After four hours of waiting on the
   platform, in the rain, with no announcement, the train arrived."
   The delay makes the wait the experience. Arrival-emphasis (loose):
   "The train arrived, after a delay of four hours." The arrival lands
   first; the delay is an afterthought. Labels must match the
   rewrites; accept other rewrites with correct labels and effects.
5. The classmate is wrong: the sentence is compound/parallel (three
   independent clauses, main clause first), not periodic — nothing is
   withheld. Effect: three equal actions in sequence give the law's
   passage momentum, each step enabling the next.
6. Sample: "The letter had taken three weeks to reach her, through
   rain, through two wrong addresses, through a strike. Too late."
   The fragment "Too late." breaks the long sentence's accumulation
   and lands as finality — the journey's length makes the lateness
   irreparable.

## Practice §B — Connotation sorting

1. Positive: *determined* (firm with purpose), *tenacious* (holding
   on admirably). Neutral: *persistent* (continuing, no attitude
   stated). Negative: *obstinate* (unreasonably unyielding), *pushy*
   (aggressively insistent), *headstrong* (willful, ignoring advice).
   One-line reasons must match the column; accept *persistent* as
   positive if defended as praise for endurance, but the neutral
   placement is the key's default.
2. (a) *Odor* (negative: unpleasant smell) vs. *fragrance* (positive:
   pleasant smell); shared denotation: a smell. (b) *Cheap* (negative:
   low quality, or stingy) vs. *economical* (positive/neutral: good
   value); shared denotation: low cost. (c) *Childish* (negative:
   immature) vs. *childlike* (positive: innocent, wondering); shared
   denotation: resembling a child.
3. American legislative: set aside, postponed. British parliamentary:
   bring forward for discussion. Settling information: which
   country's procedure applies (or whether the motion was debated
   afterward or shelved).
4. Sympathetic: "The workers were **disheartened** by the new
   schedule." (Disheartened invites sympathy — they suffered a loss.)
   Hostile: "The workers were **ungrateful** for the new schedule."
   (Ungrateful blames them — they complain despite benefits.) Each
   version's shift must be stated: the adjective pre-judges whether
   the workers deserve sympathy or blame.
5. Headline 1: *reform* is a promise (positive, announced change).
   Headline 2: *reform* stalls — the word becomes contested (the
   change is now an argument, not a fact). Headline 3: scare quotes
   make *reform* a mere label the critics reject (negative — the word
   no longer describes, it disguises). Cumulative effect: the word
   drains from promise to disputed label, so the reader's trust in
   the word itself erodes across the three headlines.
6. "Always" is wrong because connotation depends on context and
   audience. *Skinny* is better where thinness is a neutral fact
   needing care, not praise: "The rescued dog was skinny" states a
   condition to be treated; "slim" would falsely praise a starving
   animal. (Also acceptable: established terms such as "skinny latte,"
   where the word is the product's name.)

## Practice §C — Tone revision

1. (a) Metaphor ("lifeboat") — builds: the library is in danger and
   the budget saves it; the evaluation matches the claim for funding.
   (b) Irony/understatement ("thoroughly researched," of a two-page,
   citation-free report) — undercuts: the praise is hollow, and the
   context exposes it; the sentence criticizes by quoting praise it
   does not endorse. (c) Personification ("watched") — builds a
   protective, guardian tone; the mountain gains a caring agency.
2. Hopeful version (sample): "The factory closed in March. Workers
   found new footing slowly. The building stands empty, ready for its
   next chapter." Changed: "other jobs" → "new footing" (progress,
   not mere replacement); added "ready for its next chapter"
   (potential). Bitter version (sample): "The factory closed in
   March. Workers scraped together other jobs slowly. The building
   stands empty, a husk." Changed: "found" → "scraped together"
   (struggle); added "a husk" (dead remains). Accept any versions
   whose changed words genuinely move the tone without changing facts.
3. Both. "A lively discussion" understates a protest (understatement:
   saying less than the case warrants) and is used ironically (the
   speaker means something sharper than a discussion). The reader
   must already know a protest occurred and was contentious;
   otherwise the phrase reads as praise.
4. Undercuts. *Gamble* connotes risk and chance, which fights the
   claim that the park is a good idea; the writer's own diction argues
   against the writer. (If the writer intended irony, that must be
   stated — but as written, the figure undercuts.)
5. Sample: "The river **took** the road." The river gains deliberate
   agency (taking is an act); the road and its builders lose agency
   (they become victims). The personification naturalizes blame: a
   flood becomes an act of the river rather than a consequence of
   where the road was built.
6. The claim is missing steps 2 and 3. Naming the metaphor is step 1;
   the analysis must state the mapping (what is compared to what, and
   which qualities transfer) and judge whether the figure builds or
   undercuts a claim. "Persuasive" is an effect on a reader, and no
   effect has been shown.

## Practice §D — Usage resolution

1. (a) Formal essay under a traditional guide: avoid singular *they* —
   "Each voter should bring his or her ID," or recast plural: "All
   voters should bring their IDs." Trade-off: slightly clunkier, but
   guide-conformant. (b) Newsletter: "their" is acceptable — current
   standard usage, inclusive and clear. Trade-off: a traditional
   reader might still flag it, but the audience and register support
   it.
2. *Less* is not defensible here under the traditional rule: people
   are countable, so *fewer* is required ("fewer people in line").
   The time/money/distance exception does not apply — people are
   counted individuals, not an amount. Decision: use "fewer."
3. Terminal prepositions are acceptable in modern formal prose; no
   current guide bans them as a rule. The classmate's belief comes
   from a Latin-derived myth (Latin infinitives and prepositions
   behave differently) and from hypercorrection — over-applying a
   supposed rule where natural English puts the preposition last.
   Rewording ("the rule about which I was told") is stiffer, not more
   correct.
4. Edited paragraph: "Every applicant should submit his or her form,
   there will be fewer delays if everyone cooperates, and to help the
   office fully, applicants should arrive early." Changes: *their* →
   *his or her* (singular-*they* avoidance); *less* → *fewer*
   (delays are countable); *to really help* → *to help… fully*
   (split infinitive avoided where a clean alternative exists).
5. "Nonstandard" in a dictionary entry means the form is recorded in
   use but lies outside standard educated usage. Inclusion records
   existence; it does not endorse the form for formal writing. The
   classmate's conclusion confuses description (what people say) with
   prescription (what a formal essay should use).
6. *Whoever* is correct. The pronoun is the subject of "arrives"
   inside the clause "whoever arrives first"; the whole clause is the
   object of "to," but case is set by the pronoun's role inside its
   own clause, which is subject → nominative *whoever*. (Routine:
   dispute — who/whom after "to"; reference — grammar rule for
   case inside subordinate clauses; audience — formal prose; decision
   — *whoever*, with no trade-off.)

## Practice §E — Thesis drills

1. Sample thesis: "The triple parallelism 'our clock, our calendar,
   our weather vane' makes the whistle the town's complete ordering
   system, so that its silence reads as the loss of time itself, not
   just of a sound." (Effect: loss of time; means: triple parallelism;
   location: the whistle series.)
2. Sample counter-reading: "A reader might argue the triple series is
   decorative rhythm, listing the whistle's functions without claiming
   it ordered time."
3. Sample body paragraph: "The series accumulates across scales —
   clock (the day), calendar (the year), weather vane (the seasons) —
   so that no part of the town's routine escapes the whistle. The
   effect is totality: the whistle does not mark time, it *is* the
   town's time. That totality is why the later silence is so complete
   — the passage has shown that losing the whistle means losing the
   routine itself, which is exactly the thesis's claim about time."
4. Rewritten thesis: "The letter makes grief feel sudden by breaking
   its long periodic opening into short, flat declarations, so that
   the town's loss arrives as a stop rather than a fade." Changes:
   replaced vague "good descriptions" with a named means (periodic →
   fragment break) and vague "sad" with a precise effect (sudden
   grief), and located the means in the opening. The original failed
   the thesis test on all three questions.
5. Tone word: **grieving** (best answer). Defense: "The town did not
   mourn the mill. The town mourned the rhythm." — the verb *mourned*
   names grief, and the flat, unadorned delivery shows grief held
   under control rather than performed. Accept "resigned" only if
   defended from the final sentence's acceptance; reject "bitter"
   (the passage blames no target) and "sad" (too vague).
6. Grammatically, "it" refers to the difference between the two words
   — the gap between "transition" and "ending." At the argument's
   level, "it" is the dispute itself: whether the closure was change
   or loss. The double reference works because the sentence claims the
   dispute is unclosed just as the mill is closed — the unclosed gap
   mirrors the unresolved grief, and the final word of the passage
   refuses the council's vocabulary of closure.

## Quiz (S18)

1. Periodic — the main clause ("the chair announced the result") is
   withheld until the end, so the announcement feels long-awaited and
   the wait becomes part of the meaning.
2. Loose — the main clause comes first; the announcement is the news
   and the causes follow as explanation.
3. Denotation is a word's dictionary meaning; connotation is the
   feeling or attitude it carries. Sample pair: *slim* (positive:
   attractively thin) vs. *skinny* (negative: too thin) — same
   denotation (thin), different connotation.
4. American legislative: set aside, postponed. British parliamentary:
   bring forward for discussion. Settling information: which
   country's procedure applies (or whether the motion was debated
   afterward or shelved).
5. The figure ("crown jewel") builds praise, but the sentence as a
   whole undercuts it: the funding clause ("a tax nobody voted for")
   reframes the jewel as an imposed cost. Overall judgment: undercut —
   the second clause withdraws the first clause's praise.
6. Undercuts — *gamble* connotes risk and chance, which fights the
   claim that the policy is wise.
7. Routine in brief: dispute — singular *they* with a singular
   antecedent in formal prose; reference — a current dictionary usage
   note or the assignment's style guide; audience/guide — formal
   school essay under a traditional guide; decision — avoid singular
   *they* ("his or her book," or recast plural "All students should
   bring their books"). Trade-off: slightly clunkier, but
   guide-conformant.
8. No. The ban on split infinitives was invented in the 19th century
   from Latin, where an infinitive is one word and cannot be split;
   English infinitives are two words, so the rule never fit. Modern
   guides accept splits, especially when they avoid awkwardness.
9. Sample rewrite: "The passage creates dread through short, flat
   sentences that arrive without warning in the final paragraph."
   (Effect: dread; means: short, flat sentences; location: final
   paragraph.) Accept any rewrite answering all three test questions.
10. *Transition* connotes movement with a destination — manageable
    change; *ending* connotes finality — a stop with nothing after it.
    Both denote the same event, but placing them in parallel sentences
    makes the naming dispute *be* the political dispute: whoever names
    the event controls what response seems reasonable, and the passage
    ends on the town's word, not the council's.

## Unit assessment (S19)

**Part B — sample reasoning (accept defensible variation).**

Sample thesis: "The passage makes bureaucratic indifference feel
violent through compression and flat diction: a nine-minute hearing
set against nine months of waiting, a voice like a weather report, and
a final 'resolution' that resolves nothing."

- Chain 1 (syntax/repetition): "The hearing lasted nine minutes. Nine
  minutes, after nine months of letters, petitions, and waiting
  rooms." The repeated number with opposite scales (minutes vs.
  months) compresses the families' endurance into a single contrast;
  the fragment-like second sentence hammers the imbalance.
- Chain 2 (figure): "a voice like a weather report — flat, practiced,
  already moving on." The simile maps a life-altering decision onto
  routine forecasting; the flatness is the indifference, performed
  rather than described.
- Chain 3 (syntax/parallelism): "They did not protest. They did not
  applaud." The parallel negations show dignity and restraint, not
  agreement — the families refuse both expected responses.
- Chain 4 (word choice): "The report called the outcome 'a
  resolution.' The families called it nothing at all." *Resolution*
  connotes settlement; the families' "nothing" empties it. The passage
  ends on the families' word, as the Mill Town letter ends on the
  town's word.
- Counter-reading: the quiet exit could read as acceptance or
  resignation. Answer: the side-door simile ("the way people leave a
  room where someone has just died") and "run out of words long before
  they ran out of patience" show exhaustion, not consent — the
  families did not agree, they were depleted.
- Conclusion: the style reveals the hearing as a ritual that performed
  listening without listening. A theme-only reading ("families lost")
  misses the indictment of procedure — that the violence here is
  administrative, measured in minutes against months.

/4; conclusion /4; conventions and quotation accuracy /4; total /28.

## Project rubric application

- **Style claim:** check the thesis against the Lesson 05 test
  (effect + means + location). A theme thesis ("this speech is about
  freedom") scores at most 2 on this criterion however well written.
- **Evidence chains:** each chain must show quote → effect →
  link-back. A chain missing the link-back scores at most 2 for that
  chain's share; count the best three chains overall.
- **Counter-reading:** a straw man ("some might disagree") scores at
  most 1; a specific alternative reading of the same evidence,
  answered with evidence, scores 3–4.
- **Revision control:** verify at least three marked changes with
  both types present (syntax + word choice) and a complete change log
  (original, revision, technique, intended effect). Unmarked changes
  do not count.
- **Reflection:** must name the most-improved change, the hardest
  change, and a next revision, each with a reason. A summary of the
  task without these three elements scores at most 1.
