# Unit 04 — Language, Rhetoric, Syntax, and Stylistic Analysis

**Grade 11 · Language Arts · American literature, rhetoric, and research pathway · Weeks 16–19 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-11-03 for issue #52; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Units 01–03 taught the learner to read closely, to delineate arguments,
and to interpret literature through movements and voices. This unit
asks the question underneath all three: **how do the sentences
themselves do the persuading?** A claim is never just a claim — it
arrives in a sentence of a certain length, built a certain way, from
words chosen among near-synonyms that feel different. Change the
sentence, and the argument changes with it.

The unit runs in five moves:

1. **Syntax as style** — periodic and loose sentences, parallelism,
   repetition, and fragment effects; how sentence shape directs
   emphasis and pace.
2. **Word choice and connotation** — denotation, connotation, and words
   with multiple meanings; how a single word choice tilts a claim.
3. **Figurative language and tone in argument** — metaphor, simile,
   personification, understatement, and irony read in context; how
   diction builds an argument up or quietly undercuts it.
4. **Contested usage** — usage as a convention that changes over time;
   resolving usage questions with references instead of by taste; and
   editing one's own prose to match a stated style guide.
5. **The stylistic analysis essay** — rereading a passage through this
   unit's lens and writing a claim about *how* style creates meaning,
   defended with quoted evidence and a fairly handled counter-reading.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **when a
writer chooses this sentence and these words rather than others, what
does the choice do to the reader — and how can you prove it from the
text?**

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–03 and the week-1 diagnostic (insecure skills are
re-taught in use):

- Cite strong, thorough textual evidence for explicit and inferential
  claims (RL.11-12.1, RI.11-12.1).
- Determine themes/central ideas and analyze their development
  (RL.11-12.2, RI.11-12.2).
- Analyze word choice for cumulative impact on meaning and tone
  (RL.11-12.4, RI.11-12.4) at the level of identifying the effect.
- Analyze an author's structural choices and the development of an
  argument (RI.11-12.5, RI.11-12.8).
- Write organized analytical paragraphs with claims and evidence
  (W.11-12.1, W.11-12.2).

The unit does **not** assume fluency with: deliberate syntactic
variation for effect (L.11-12.3.a); resolving contested usage with
references (L.11-12.1.a–b); interpreting figures of speech in context
and analyzing their role in a text (L.11-12.5.a); or determining point
of view or purpose in a highly rhetorical text through style and
content (RI.11-12.6) — those are this unit's new content.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Analyze sentence structure — periodic and loose construction,
   parallelism, repetition, and fragment effects — and explain, with
   quoted evidence, what each structure does to emphasis, pace, or
   tone. (L.11-12.3.a, RI.11-12.6; track objectives 4, 5)
2. Vary syntax deliberately: combine, reorder, shorten, and lengthen
   sentences to achieve a stated effect, and justify each change in
   terms of that effect. (L.11-12.3.a, W.11-12.5; track objective 7)
3. Analyze word choice — denotation, connotation, and words with
   multiple meanings — and explain its cumulative effect on meaning
   and tone in a passage. (RI.11-12.4, L.11-12.4, L.11-12.6; track
   objectives 4, 5)
4. Resolve contested-usage questions by consulting references, explain
   usage as a convention that changes over time, and edit one's own
   prose to conform to a stated style guide. (L.11-12.1.a–b, L.11-12.2;
   track objective 7)
5. Write a stylistic analysis essay that states a precise claim about
   how style creates meaning in a passage, develops it with quoted
   evidence, addresses a counter-reading fairly, and concludes from
   its evidence. (RI.11-12.6, W.11-12.1, W.11-12.9; track objectives
   5, 8, 11)

## Anchor passages and editions

All extended passages in this unit are **original**, written for the
unit so that every word choice and sentence shape can be discussed
without a copyright question and without depending on an edition the
adult does not own. The central passage, "A Letter from Mill Town," is
printed in full in Lesson 05 and quoted throughout the unit. Short
public-domain quotations are used only where the unit names the source:

- Declaration of Independence (adopted July 4, 1776), preamble — text
  verified at the National Archives transcription,
  <https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration-transcript>
  (the unit's one quoted sentence matches this text).
- Frederick Douglass, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?"
  (1852) — referenced in Lesson 05's optional reread; the adult
  selects the edition and confirms any quotation against it before
  assigning. No Douglass passage is reproduced in this unit.

**A note on language and scope.** Contested-usage discussions in
Lesson 04 describe what style guides and dictionaries actually say;
they do not declare a single "correct" English. Where a usage is
genuinely unsettled (for example, singular *they* in formal prose),
the lesson teaches the learner to name the audience, the guide, and
the trade-off — and to follow the guide the assignment names.

## 20-session pacing (~50 minutes each)

| Sessions | Work | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| S1–S2 | **Lesson 01** — syntax as style: periodic/loose sentences, parallelism, repetition, fragment effects; sentence-strip image task | Learner labels the structure of 4 unseen sentences and states each structure's effect in one sentence |
| S3 | Workshop A — syntax labeling drill and sentence combining (practice.md §A) | Combining table complete: 6 original sentences rewritten with a stated effect for each |
| S4–S5 | **Lesson 02** — word choice: denotation, connotation, multiple meanings | Learner revises 3 sentences by swapping one word and explains the connotative shift in each case |
| S6 | Workshop B — connotation sorting and the "tabled" ambiguity drill (practice.md §B) | Every word in the set sorted by connotation with a one-line reason; both readings of "tabled" stated |
| S7–S8 | **Lesson 03** — figurative language and tone in argument | Learner traces one figure across a paragraph and states how it builds or undercuts the argument |
| S9 | Workshop C — tone revision: rewriting one paragraph toward two different tones (practice.md §C) | Two revisions submitted, each with a tone label and a change log naming the altered words |
| S10–S11 | **Lesson 04** — contested usage: convention, change over time, reference-based resolution, style-guide editing | Learner resolves 3 usage disputes with a cited reference and edits a paragraph to a named guide |
| S12 | Seminar — "Is singular *they* correct in formal prose?" (evidence-based discussion) | Every participant speaks at least twice, citing a reference rather than taste |
| S13–S14 | **Lesson 05** — the stylistic analysis essay: rereading "A Letter from Mill Town" through the unit's lens; thesis and counter-reading | Thesis paragraph approved: precise style claim + 2 quoted supports + a named counter-reading |
| S15 | Drafting studio — unit assessment essay (complete draft) | Complete draft with 3+ quoted passages and a counter-reading paragraph |
| S16 | Project workshop — Style Audit and Revision Portfolio (project.md) | Portfolio plan approved: passage chosen, 3 revision targets named |
| S17 | Review — objectives 1–5 retrieval and re-teaching | Learner can define all unit terms and apply each to an unseen sentence without notes |
| S18 | Formative quiz (assessments/week-04_quiz.md) | Scored; misses feed S19 preparation, not punishment |
| S19 | Unit assessment (assessments/unit-assessment.md) | Submitted essay + short answers |
| S20 | Reflection + project presentations; preview U05 | Written reflection; adult records project notes |

Reading load: the unit's passages are short and original, designed to
be re-read closely several times. The adult assigns first readings the
session before each lesson and checks that the learner arrives with the
passage annotated, not merely skimmed.
