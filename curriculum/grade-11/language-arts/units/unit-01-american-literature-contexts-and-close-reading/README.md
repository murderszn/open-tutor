# Unit 01 — American Literature Contexts, Themes, and Close Reading

**Grade 11 · Language Arts · American literature, rhetoric, and research pathway · Weeks 2–5 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-14 for issue #52; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Before a reader can argue about American literature, the reader needs two
things: a map of the territory and a method for walking it. This unit builds
both. The **map** is the historical frame — what "American literature" names
as a field, the major periods from the colonial era to the Harlem Renaissance,
and what each period believed literature should do. The **method** is the
close-reading routine the whole year will use: annotate, question, connect,
summarize — then argue from the text, with evidence, about what it means.

The unit runs in four moves:

1. **The field and the periods** — what counts as American literature, six
   major periods at a glance, and the close-reading routine practiced on
   Frederick Douglass's 1852 speech "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of
   July?"
2. **Theme development across a novel** — tracking two or more themes in
   F. Scott Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby* (1925), how they interact, and
   the difference between an objective summary and an analysis.
3. **Same period, similar themes** — comparing how Douglass, Walt Whitman,
   and Emily Dickinson — all writing in mid-19th-century America — treat
   freedom and identity; building comparative claims on quoted evidence;
   reading figurative language (extended metaphor) in context.
4. **Close-reading seminar** — a modeled full close reading, an
   evidence-based seminar on "Whose America?", and transfer: applying the
   routine to a new text and its illustration.

All anchor texts are public domain in the USA. The novel is read in full
during the unit (reading volume rises toward independent full-length works
across the year); the poems and the Douglass excerpt are short enough to
re-read closely several times — which is the point.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **how does the
time a text comes from shape what it says, and how do we prove what it
says from the words on the page?**

## Prerequisites

From the grade-10 track's end-of-year objectives (verified in the week-1
diagnostic; insecure skills are re-taught in use):

- Cite strong, thorough textual evidence for explicit and inferential
  claims about literature (RL.9-10.1).
- Determine a theme, analyze its development, and write an objective
  summary (RL.9-10.2).
- Analyze word choice — figurative, connotative, technical — and its effect
  (RL.9-10.4, L.9-10.5.a).
- Write organized analytical paragraphs with claims and evidence
  (W.9-10.1, W.9-10.2, W.9-10.9).

The unit does **not** assume fluency with: reading at the high end of the
11–CCR complexity band (RL.11-12.10); knowledge of 18th–20th-century
foundational American works as a body (RL.11-12.9); or comparing multiple
texts from the same period on similar themes — those are this unit's new
content.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Explain what "American literature" names as a field and place six major
   periods (colonial, revolutionary, Romanticism/Transcendentalism, Realism,
   Modernism, Harlem Renaissance) on a timeline, describing each period's
   core belief about what literature should do in one to two sentences.
   (RL.11-12.9; track objective 1)
2. Cite strong, thorough textual evidence to support analysis of what a
   literary text states explicitly and what it implies, and identify at
   least one place in an analysis where the text leaves a matter uncertain.
   (RL.11-12.1; track objective 1)
3. Determine two or more themes in *The Great Gatsby*, analyze how they
   develop and interact across the novel into a complex account, and write
   an objective summary that is clearly distinct from analysis.
   (RL.11-12.2; track objective 2)
4. Compare how two or more foundational mid-19th-century American texts
   treat the similar themes of freedom and identity, grounding the
   comparison in at least three quoted passages and explaining what is
   shared and what differs. (RL.11-12.9; track objectives 1, 3)
5. Interpret figurative language in context (extended metaphor, apostrophe,
   paradox) and analyze its role in meaning and tone; participate in an
   evidence-based seminar discussion that builds on others' ideas and
   probes reasoning with questions. (RL.11-12.4, L.11-12.5.a, SL.11-12.1;
   track objectives 4, 12)

## Vocabulary

**Unit terms** (used in lessons and assessed): *canon, period/movement,
close reading, annotation, theme vs. topic, motif, objective summary,
analysis, textual evidence, inference, ambiguity/uncertainty, extended
metaphor, apostrophe, paradox, connotation, tone, seminar, claim,
counterclaim.*

**Period terms** (recognition level): colonial, revolutionary,
Romanticism, Transcendentalism, Realism/Naturalism, Modernism, Harlem
Renaissance.

## 20-session pacing (~50 minutes each)

| Sessions | Work | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| S1–S2 | **Lesson 01** — the field, the periods, the close-reading routine; Douglass excerpt reading workshop | Learner completes an annotated Douglass excerpt using all four routine moves |
| S3 | Close-reading workshop A — evidence citation with the Douglass excerpt | Learner writes 2 claim–evidence–explanation sets; adult checks quotation accuracy |
| S4–S5 | **Lesson 02** — theme in *Gatsby*; summary vs. analysis | Learner states 2 themes with a chapter-1 example for each |
| S6 | Reading workshop B — *Gatsby* theme tracking (green light; "careless people") | Theme-tracking chart has 6+ dated entries across chapters |
| S7–S8 | **Lesson 03** — comparing Douglass, Whitman, Dickinson on freedom and identity; extended metaphor | Learner drafts a comparative claim with one quote per text |
| S9 | Evidence workshop C — comparative paragraphs | One comparative paragraph with 3 quotes, correctly punctuated |
| S10–S11 | **Lesson 04** — modeled close reading; seminar preparation | Learner completes seminar prep sheet with 3 questions and 5 evidence notes |
| S12 | Seminar 1 — "Whose America?" | Every participant speaks at least twice with textual evidence |
| S13 | Drafting studio — unit assessment essay | Complete draft with 3+ quoted passages |
| S14 | **Applied task** — "Harbor at Dawn" vignette + generated illustration: text-vs-image close reading | Written comparison: 2 agreements, 2 differences, 1 inference |
| S15 | Revision studio — peer-style review with the adult | Revised draft addressing all adult margin notes |
| S16 | Project workshop — American Themes Anthology (project.md) | Anthology plan: 3 texts chosen, theme statement drafted |
| S17 | Review — objectives 1–5 retrieval and re-teaching | Learner can name all six periods and both *Gatsby* themes without notes |
| S18 | Formative quiz (assessments/week-04_quiz.md) | Scored; misses feed S19 review time |
| S19 | Unit assessment (assessments/unit-assessment.md) | Submitted essay + short answers |
| S20 | Reflection + reading-log conference; preview U02 | Written reflection; adult records reading-log notes |

*Gatsby* reading load is distributed across the four weeks (about 2–3
chapters per week); the pacing assumes the learner reads at home or in
workshop time and arrives with chapters read. The adult adjusts if the
reading pace slips — the unit's analysis work, not the calendar, is the
non-negotiable.
