# Project — An American Themes Anthology

**Unit 01 · project workshop S16; final submission with the unit assessment
(S19) · Standards: RL.11-12.9, RL.11-12.2, RL.11-12.1, W.11-12.9.a, W.11-12.10**

## The task

You are the editor of a tiny anthology: **three short American texts,
one connecting theme, and your editorial voice throughout.** The
anthology argues — by selection and by commentary — that your theme runs
through American writing across time.

## Requirements

**1. Three texts (public domain or licensed; the adult approves all
selections):**

- One text from the 19th century (1800s). It may be one of the unit's
  anchor texts.
- One text from the early 20th century (1900–1945). It may be *Gatsby*
  (represented by two chosen passages totaling under 500 words, with full
  citation — do not reprint chapters).
- One free choice: any American text from any period, including a poem,
  speech excerpt, song lyric in the public domain, or a short original
  piece of your own writing.

No text may be longer than 1,500 words in the anthology. For long works,
excerpt with a one-sentence note on where the excerpt sits in the whole.

**2. An editor's introduction (300–400 words)** that:

- Names the theme as a full sentence (the theme test from Lesson 02:
  it must be disagreeable-with-able).
- Explains why these three texts, in this order.
- Previews the comparison the reader will find — what is shared, what
  differs.

**3. Close-reading notes for each text (150–200 words each):** one
specific passage per text, closely read — annotate-style observations,
one genuine question, and what the passage shows about the theme.
Quote briefly and accurately.

**4. A reflection (200–300 words):** What did selecting and comparing
teach you about the theme that reading one text alone didn't? Name one
decision you reversed while building the anthology and why.

## Materials

Notebook, access to the unit's texts, the adult's approval on selections,
and the Resource Pack's text-finding references for the free-choice text.

## Steps

1. **S16 workshop:** Propose three texts and a theme sentence. The adult
   checks: is the theme a sentence? Are the texts genuinely American and
   genuinely from different periods? Is everything public domain or
   properly licensed?
2. **Draft** the introduction and one set of close-reading notes; bring
   to the adult for a 10-minute check (evidence accuracy, quotation
   punctuation).
3. **Complete** the remaining notes and the reflection.
4. **Assemble** the anthology as one document: title page, introduction,
   the three texts (or excerpts) with notes, reflection. Title it
   yourself — an anthology's title is an editorial argument too.

## Deliverables

One assembled anthology document containing all four required parts.

## Rubric

| Criterion | Exemplary (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Beginning (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Text selection & range** | Three texts from three genuinely different periods/modes; each choice clearly serves the theme; order is purposeful | Three texts meet the period requirements; choices serve the theme | Period requirements met but one choice is weakly connected to the theme | Missing a period requirement or texts don't connect to the theme |
| **Evidence quality** | Every note quotes briefly and accurately; each quotation is followed by explanation of what it proves; no unsupported claims | Quotations accurate and mostly explained; at most one unsupported claim | Quotations present but sometimes unexplained or slightly inaccurate | Few or no quotations; claims unsupported |
| **Thematic connection** | Introduction states the theme as an arguable sentence and previews a real comparison (shared + different); notes build the comparison across all three texts | Theme is a sentence; comparison addresses shared and different across texts | Theme is a topic phrase, or comparison only lists similarities | No clear theme; texts treated in isolation |
| **Introduction & reflection** | Introduction orients a new reader and argues for the selection; reflection names a reversed decision with reasons and states what comparison taught | Both present and complete; reflection is specific | One part thin or generic; reflection restates the introduction | Missing a part, or both generic |

**Scoring:** 14–16 = exceeds; 11–13 = meets; 8–10 = approaching (revise
one criterion with the adult); below 8 = re-do with a narrower theme.

## Adult notes

- The "free choice" text is where learners go wrong: steer toward
  public-domain texts the learner can actually obtain (Gutenberg,
  Poetry Foundation, AmericanLiterature.com). Original student writing is
  allowed and often the strongest choice.
- Watch quotation accuracy — learners retyping from memory introduce
  errors. Require checking against the source for every quote.
- If the theme sentence keeps collapsing into a topic ("freedom"),
  narrow it with a *when/where/for whom*: "In 1850s American writing,
  freedom is imagined as..."
