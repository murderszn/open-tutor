# Lesson 04 — Close-Reading Workshop and Seminar Preparation

**Unit 01 · Sessions S10–S11 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RL.11-12.1, RL.11-12.4, SL.11-12.1

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can perform a complete close reading
independently (annotate, question, connect, summarize, then argue), prepare
for an evidence-based seminar with written questions and evidence notes,
and apply the close-reading routine to a new text paired with an
illustration.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, all three comparison texts from Lesson 03, the
seminar prep sheet (below), the "Harbor at Dawn" vignette and illustration
(below; `../assets/harbor-at-dawn-illustration.png`).

## Readiness check (5 min)

1. Write the four moves of the close-reading routine from memory, with one
   sentence on what each move produces.
2. Which move do you find hardest, and why? (One honest sentence — the
   adult uses this to pair seminar preparation.)

## Explicit teaching

### A full close reading, modeled start to finish

Text: Whitman, "Song of Myself" §1 (1855) — the poem's opening three
lines: "I CELEBRATE myself, and sing myself, / And what I assume you shall
assume, / For every atom belonging to me as good belongs to you."

**1. Annotate.** Circle "CELEBRATE" (all caps — the poem's first word is
shouted); underline "sing myself" (a song about the self, not a story);
star "you shall assume" (he drafts the reader into the poem — *you* must
do something); margin note: "every atom" — the smallest unit of matter,
so the claim is total: nothing of him is excluded.

**2. Question.** Why "celebrate" rather than "describe" or "introduce"?
Who is "you" — and what does it mean that the reader is commanded to
"assume" something? Why does a poem about the self begin by giving the
self away ("belongs to you")?

**3. Connect.** 1855: no American poet had opened a book this way — the
Romantic period's belief was that literature should express the
individual soul, and Whitman takes it literally: the soul *is* the
subject. The line also answers Douglass's decade: where Douglass's
"distance" divides Americans, Whitman's "every atom... belongs to you"
refuses division at the level of matter itself.

**4. Summarize.** The speaker announces a song celebrating himself and
immediately extends the celebration to the reader, claiming that
everything belonging to him equally belongs to the reader.

**Then argue (the fifth move the unit has been building toward):** The
opening performs the poem's whole project in three lines — the self is
not private property but shared substance. Evidence: "celebrate" (public,
joyful, not analytical) + "you shall assume" (the reader is conscripted)
+ "every atom" (total, physical). That is a claim *about* the text,
proved *by* the text. Summary was the floor; this is the house.

### Seminar: how evidence-based discussion works

A seminar is not a quiz with talking. The norms (SL.11-12.1):

- **Come prepared** — no one speaks well about a text they haven't
  re-read. The prep sheet below is required, not optional.
- **Build, don't just add** — "Building on what [name] said about the
  grass..." beats "I also think..."
- **Probe with questions** — "What in the text made you read it that
  way?" is the most powerful sentence in the room.
- **Cite as you speak** — "In section 6, when he says..." keeps the
  discussion anchored.
- **Synthesize** — the last ten minutes: what did we collectively figure
  out that no one person brought in?

### Seminar 1 (S12) — "Whose America?"

The guiding question: **Douglass, Whitman, and Dickinson all wrote about
American freedom within a single decade. Whose version is most honest —
and can more than one be true at once?** There is no correct answer; there
are only better- and worse-defended ones.

**Seminar prep sheet** (complete before S12):

1. My position in one sentence: _______________
2. Evidence note 1 — quotation + what it proves: _______________
3. Evidence note 2 — quotation + what it proves: _______________
4. Evidence note 3 — quotation + what it proves: _______________
5. A question I genuinely want the group to answer: _______________
6. A second question, for a claim I disagree with: _______________
7. One thing a classmate might say that would change my mind:
   _______________

## Guided practice (S10, with the adult)

1. Together, perform the five-move close reading on Dickinson's first
   stanza (Lesson 03). The adult annotates aloud; the learner writes the
   questions; together you draft the connect and the argument.
2. Draft two seminar questions together and test them: could each question
   be answered *without* the text? If yes, sharpen it until the text is
   required.
3. Rehearse one probe: the adult states a weak claim ("Whitman and
   Douglass basically agree"), and the learner practices the response:
   "What in the text made you read it that way?" followed by a
   counter-quotation.

## Independent practice (S11)

1. Complete the seminar prep sheet (all seven items). Bring it to S12;
   it is collected.
2. Re-read all three comparison texts once more, slowly. Add at least
   three new annotations total, dated.
3. **Exit check:** In 5–6 sentences, state your current answer to "Whose
   America?" and name the single strongest piece of evidence for it.

## Applied task — "Harbor at Dawn": text and illustration (S14)

Close reading isn't only for famous texts. Below is an original short
vignette written for this unit, paired with an AI-generated illustration.
Your job: read both the way this unit taught you — and notice where they
agree, where they differ, and what each one lets you infer.

**"Harbor at Dawn"** (original prose vignette, written for this unit)

> Before the sun cleared the warehouse roofs, Mara was already on the
> dock, coiling the lines the night boats had left tangled. Gulls argued
> over nothing. Across the water, the far shore was still dark except for
> the bakery windows, lit gold, and she could smell the bread from half a
> mile away — proof, she thought, that somebody's morning started earlier
> than hers.
>
> Her grandfather had worked this same dock when the ferries still ran,
> and he used to say a harbor keeps two kinds of promises: the ones
> painted on the signs, and the ones kept in the dark. She had never been
> sure which kind she was supposed to believe.
>
> The flag at the end of the dock hung limp. She climbed the short ladder
> and freed the knotted halyard with her teeth, and the small flag lifted,
> caught the first real wind of the day, and opened like something that
> had been holding its breath.

**Text-only alternative** (everything needed to answer without seeing the
image): The illustration shows a wide wooden dock at dawn in soft
watercolor tones. A lone figure in dark early-1900s-style work clothes and
a cap stands at the dock's edge, one arm raised to a short wooden
flagpole, hoisting a small light-colored flag. Gulls circle in a
peach-and-gray cloudy sky. Behind, across misty water, stand the masts of
several sailing boats and a distant shoreline of low brick factory
buildings with tall chimneys, some trailing smoke. Golden sunlight breaks
through fog on the right; the water reflects the light. The mood is quiet
and expectant.

**Your task (written, ~1 page):**

1. Name **two details** that appear in *both* the vignette and the
   illustration. Quote or cite the vignette line for each.
2. Name **two details** that appear in *only one* of them. For each, say
   which one — and suggest *why* the other might have left it out.
3. Write **one inference** the illustration supports that the vignette
   never states outright (or vice versa). Defend it with one specific
   visual detail and one line of text.
4. Final question: the vignette's Mara is a young woman working a
   present-day dock; the illustration's figure reads as an older era.
   Does that difference change the vignette's meaning, or just its
   costume? Argue your answer in 4–5 sentences.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** The vignette may be read aloud before the written
  task; the text-only alternative above is the primary route for any
  learner who can't use the image.
- **Alternate response mode:** The four-part task may be answered as a
  labeled chart (Both / Text only / Image only / Inference) with
  sentences, then expanded.
- **Extension:** Write a 150-word companion vignette from the gulls'
  point of view — same harbor, same dawn — and note in a paragraph what
  changes when the observer changes. (Point of view arrives formally in
  U03; this is a preview.)

## Teacher note (misconceptions; answer-key references)

- In seminars, learners often perform agreement ("I agree with...") as a
  substitute for building. Require the building move explicitly: "What did
  their point make you notice that you hadn't?"
- The applied task's question 4 has no single right answer — score it on
  whether the argument uses both text and image evidence, not on which
  side it takes.
- The illustration is AI-generated and labeled as such; it illustrates
  an *original* vignette, so there is no historical-accuracy question to
  get wrong. The `../assets/README.md` records this.
- Answer-key references: guided practice §L04-G; prep sheet and exit
  check §L04-I; applied task §L04-A.
