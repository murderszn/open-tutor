# Lesson 01 — What "American Literature" Means, and How to Read It Closely

**Unit 01 · Sessions S1–S2 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RL.11-12.9, RL.11-12.10, RL.11-12.1

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can explain what "American literature" names as
a field of study, place six major periods on a timeline and say what each
period believed literature should do, and use the four-move close-reading
routine (annotate, question, connect, summarize) on a real text.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, a printed or on-screen copy of the Douglass excerpt
(see below), the period timeline the adult and learner build together in
this lesson.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. Name one book, poem, or speech you think of as "American." What makes it
   American to you — where it was written, what it is about, or something
   else?
2. When you read something hard, what do you actually do first — re-read,
   look things up, ask someone, or something else?

(Adult: question 1 has no wrong answer; it surfaces the learner's working
definition, which the lesson will sharpen. Question 2 previews the
close-reading routine.)

## Explicit teaching

### What "American literature" names

"American literature" is not a shelf of books that all sound alike. It is a
**field of study**: the body of writing produced in (and about) the United
States and its predecessor colonies, read with attention to how American
history, places, and conflicts shaped it. That definition has three parts
worth noticing:

- **Produced in or about America** — the boundary is porous. Frederick
  Douglass wrote in America about American slavery; some American writers
  wrote abroad. The field argues about its own edges, and that argument is
  part of the field.
- **Read with attention to history** — a poem from 1861 and a novel from
  1925 do not mean the same things, because the country that made them was
  not the same country. Context is not background decoration; it is part of
  the evidence.
- **Conflicts included** — American literature contains arguments with
  itself. Douglass's 1852 speech uses the Declaration of Independence's
  language to indict the nation that wrote it. That tension *is* the
  literature, not a problem with it.

### Six periods at a glance

Build this timeline together in the notebook. For each period, write the
dates and one sentence on what the period believed literature should do.

| Period | Dates | What it believed literature should do |
|---|---|---|
| Colonial | ~1607–1775 | Record, instruct, and bear witness — writing served God, the colony, and survival (sermons, captivity narratives, journals). |
| Revolutionary / Early National | ~1775–1820 | Persuade and found — writing built the new nation's arguments and identity (the Declaration, the Federalist essays, early American novels). |
| Romanticism & Transcendentalism | ~1820–1865 | Express the individual soul and the American landscape — writing should be original, feeling, and free from European models (Emerson, Thoreau, Whitman, Dickinson, Poe, Hawthorne, Melville). |
| Realism & Naturalism | ~1865–1914 | Show life as it is — writing should depict ordinary people and social forces truthfully, without romantic decoration (Twain, James, Chopin, Crane). |
| Modernism | ~1914–1945 | Break the old forms to say what the old forms couldn't — writing should experiment because the modern world broke the old certainties (Fitzgerald, Hemingway, Faulkner, Eliot). |
| Harlem Renaissance | ~1918–1937 (overlaps Modernism) | Claim Black artistic identity and excellence — writing should celebrate Black life, confront racism, and prove what a free Black literature could be (Hughes, Hurston, Cullen). |

Two honest notes: periods **overlap** (the Harlem Renaissance is part of
the Modernist years, with its own distinct project), and writers never got
a memo saying which period they belonged to — the labels are tools readers
invented later. Use them to ask better questions, not to file writers away.

### The close-reading routine

Close reading means reading a short passage **slowly enough to notice how
it works**, and proving every claim from the words on the page. The
routine has four moves. Use them in order, every time:

1. **Annotate** — mark the text as you read: circle unfamiliar or
   repeated words, underline images and comparisons, put a star by lines
   that surprise you, write margin notes in your own words.
2. **Question** — write at least three genuine questions the passage
   raises: about a word choice, a contradiction, something unexplained.
   "Why does he say X instead of Y?" is a better question than "What does
   this mean?"
3. **Connect** — link the passage to its period and to other texts: what
   about this could only have been written when it was written? What does
   it argue with, echo, or answer?
4. **Summarize** — in two to three sentences, state what the passage
   says — no opinions yet. Summary is the floor your analysis stands on.

**Worked example 1 — Annotate + Question (modeled on Douglass).**
Read the opening of the excerpt: "Fellow citizens, pardon me, and allow me
to ask, why am I called upon to speak here today? What have I or those I
represent to do with your national independence?"

*Annotation (modeled):* Circle "pardon me" (an apology — why apologize?);
underline "those I represent" (he speaks for more than himself — whom?);
star "your national independence" (he says *your*, not *our* — the most
important word in the sentence).

*Questions (modeled):* Why does he open with a question instead of a
statement? Who are "those I represent"? Why "your" independence and not
"our"?

Notice what the model does: every mark and every question points at a
*specific word*, and each question could be answered by looking harder at
the text — not by guessing.

**Worked example 2 — Connect + Summarize (modeled).**
*Connect:* Douglass spoke on July 5, 1852, invited by an antislavery
society to celebrate Independence Day. The Revolutionary period made the
Declaration's language ("all men are created equal") America's founding
argument; Douglass, speaking in the Romantic period, turns that argument
back on the nation: the celebration excludes the enslaved. His "your"
is doing period work — it measures 1852 America against 1776 America's
promises.

*Summarize (2 sentences):* Douglass opens by asking why he, a formerly
enslaved Black man, has been invited to celebrate a national independence
that does not include Black Americans. He questions whether the principles
of the Declaration of Independence actually extend to the people he
represents.

The summary states only what the passage says. It does not yet say the
passage is powerful, right, or well-written — that is analysis, and it
comes after the evidence is on the table.

## The Douglass excerpt (for annotation)

Read the excerpt below twice: once straight through, once with the pen in
your hand using the four moves. (Full speech verified 2026-10-14 at
<https://americanliterature.com/author/frederick-douglass/essay/what-to-the-slave-is-the-fourth-of-july>;
delivered July 5, 1852, at Corinthian Hall, Rochester, New York.)

> Fellow citizens, pardon me, and allow me to ask, why am I called upon to
> speak here today? What have I or those I represent to do with your
> national independence? Are the great principles of political freedom and
> of natural justice, embodied in that Declaration of Independence, extended
> to us? ... I am not included within the pale of this glorious
> anniversary! Your high independence only reveals the immeasurable
> distance between us. The blessings in which you this day rejoice are not
> enjoyed in common. ... This Fourth of July is yours, not mine. You may
> rejoice, I must mourn. ... What to the American slave is your Fourth of
> July? I answer, a day that reveals to him more than all other days of the
> year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim.
> ... At a time like this, scorching irony, not convincing argument, is
> needed.

## Guided practice (S1, with the adult)

Work through the excerpt together, the adult modeling the first pass:

1. Annotate the excerpt using all four mark types (circle, underline,
   star, margin note). Aim for at least 8 marks.
2. Write three genuine questions about specific words or choices. For each,
   name the exact word or phrase the question is about.
3. Together, draft the connect step: what in the passage could only have
   been written in 1852, and what does it argue with?
4. Draft a two-sentence summary. Check: does every clause trace back to
   something the passage actually says?

**Error-analysis task (important):** Below is a *bad* summary. Find two
things wrong with it.

> "Douglass is angry about slavery and says the Fourth of July is stupid.
> He thinks white people are bad."

(Answer key: (1) "stupid" and "white people are bad" are the summarizer's
judgments, not the passage's words — the summary smuggles in opinion;
(2) it misses the passage's actual structure — the *question* about who
the celebration includes, and the *answer* that it reveals injustice. A
summary reports the argument; it does not grade it.)

## Independent practice (S2)

1. Re-read the excerpt and add at least 4 new annotations you missed the
   first time. Date them so the adult can see the second pass.
2. Choose one of your questions from guided practice and try to answer it
   from the text in 3–4 sentences. Quote the words that answer it.
3. Write a one-paragraph connect: place Douglass's speech in the period
   timeline — which period, and what does the speech show about what that
   period's literature was doing?
4. **Application:** Find one sentence in the excerpt where Douglass says
   "your" where you might expect "our" (there are several). In 4–5
   sentences, explain what work that single word is doing. This is your
   first real close-reading claim — it must quote the sentence.
5. **Exit check:** Without looking at the timeline, name all six periods
   in order and write the one-sentence belief for any three.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** If the 1852 diction is heavy going, read the
  excerpt aloud first — Douglass wrote it to be *heard*. Hearing the
  sentences' rhythm makes the grammar clearer.
- **Alternate response mode:** Annotations may be dictated to the adult or
  recorded as voice notes, one per mark, then transcribed.
- **Extension:** Read the full speech (linked above) and find the passage
  where Douglass says "scorching irony, not convincing argument, is
  needed." Write a paragraph: what is the difference between irony and
  argument, and why would irony be the better weapon *here*?

## Teacher note (misconceptions; answer-key references)

- Learners often treat "theme" and "topic" as the same — this lesson keeps
  the word *theme* out of the objectives on purpose; it arrives properly
  in Lesson 02. If the learner volunteers it, accept but don't assess it
  yet.
- Watch for summaries that are really opinions ("Douglass was brave").
  The error-analysis task exists because this is the single most common
  failure mode in the unit; re-teach with the question "which words in the
  passage say that?"
- Answer-key references: guided practice in `answer-key.md` §L01-G;
  independent practice §L01-I; exit check §L01-E.
