# Lesson 03 — Same Period, Similar Themes: Douglass, Whitman, Dickinson

**Unit 01 · Sessions S7–S8 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RL.11-12.9, RL.11-12.1, RL.11-12.4, L.11-12.5.a

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can compare how two or more mid-19th-century
American texts treat the similar themes of freedom and identity, support
the comparison with at least three quoted passages, and explain how an
extended metaphor works in context.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, the Douglass excerpt (Lesson 01), Whitman "Song of
Myself" sections 1 and 6 (Walt Whitman Archive text verified 2026-10-14:
<https://whitmanarchive.org/item/ppp.01663_01690>), Dickinson "'Hope' is
the thing with feathers —" (Poetry Foundation text verified 2026-10-14:
<https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/42889/hope-is-the-thing-with-feathers-314>).

## Readiness check (5 min)

1. In one sentence: what did Douglass mean by "This Fourth of July is
   yours, not mine"?
2. Predict: a poet writing in the same decade as Douglass — also American
   — would most likely write about freedom in a (a) completely different
   way, (b) similar way with differences, or (c) identical way. Why?

(Adult: the honest answer is (b), and the lesson proves it. Same period
does not mean same mind.)

## Explicit teaching

### Why compare texts from the same period?

RL.11-12.9 asks for something specific: knowledge of foundational
American works **including how two or more texts from the same period
treat similar themes**. The period is the control in the experiment. When
Douglass (1852), Whitman (1855), and Dickinson (c. 1861) all write about
freedom and identity within a single decade, the *differences* between
them can't be explained by "different times" — they must come from
different positions, purposes, and imaginations. Comparison reveals what a
single text hides: its choices.

### How to build a comparative claim

A comparative claim has three parts:

1. **The shared ground** — name the theme and the period both texts
   belong to. ("Both Douglass's 1852 speech and Whitman's 1855 poem take
   up American freedom...")
2. **The difference** — state it as a contrast, not a list. ("...but
   Douglass treats freedom as a *possession denied*, while Whitman treats
   it as an *identity claimed*.")
3. **The evidence** — at least one quotation per text, each followed by
   one sentence explaining what the quoted words prove.

Weak comparison lists ("Douglass talks about slavery. Whitman talks about
grass."). Strong comparison argues about the *relationship* between the
texts' treatments of one theme.

### Figurative language in context: the extended metaphor

A metaphor compares two unlike things; an **extended metaphor** sustains
the comparison across a whole passage or poem, so the reader must keep
two meanings active at once. To read one in context: (1) name the two
things being compared; (2) track which details belong to the image and
which to the idea; (3) ask what the comparison *does* — what it lets the
writer say that plain statement couldn't.

**Worked example 1 — A comparative claim (modeled).**
Shared ground: Douglass and Whitman, writing three years apart, both
answer the question "who counts as American?"

Difference: Douglass draws a line — "Your high independence only reveals
the immeasurable distance between us" — measuring American freedom by who
it excludes. Whitman erases the line: the grass in section 6 grows
"Sprouting alike in broad zones and narrow zones, / Growing among black
folks as among white" — one image of radical inclusion.

Evidence work: Douglass's "distance" is a measurement word; it insists the
gap is real and measurable. Whitman's "alike" and "the same" (in "I give
them the same, / I receive them the same") insist the gap is an illusion.
Same decade, same theme, opposite geometries: Douglass maps a divided
country; Whitman imagines an undivided one. Neither is "right" in the
abstract — the comparison shows two different jobs literature was doing
in the 1850s: indictment and imagination.

**Worked example 2 — Reading Dickinson's extended metaphor (modeled).**
The poem's comparison: hope is "the thing with feathers" — a bird — that
"perches in the soul" and "sings the tune without the words."

Tracking it: stanza 1 establishes the bird (small, inward, wordless song);
stanza 2 tests it against the storm — "sweetest... in the Gale," and only
an extreme storm could "abash the little Bird"; stanza 3 reports the
evidence — heard "in the chillest land" and "on the strangest Sea," yet it
never "asked a crumb" of the speaker.

What the comparison does: a bird is small and vulnerable, yet it flies and
sings without payment or permission. By making hope a bird *inside* the
soul, Dickinson locates freedom where no law can reach it — the opposite
of Douglass's public, political freedom, and quieter than Whitman's
expansive self. All three are answers to the same decade's question; this
is the interior answer.

## The three texts (short passages for comparison)

Keep these on one notebook page for the rest of the unit:

- **Douglass (1852):** "Your high independence only reveals the
  immeasurable distance between us... This Fourth of July is yours, not
  mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn."
- **Whitman (1855), "Song of Myself" §1:** "I CELEBRATE myself, and sing
  myself, / And what I assume you shall assume, / For every atom belonging
  to me as good belongs to you."
- **Whitman (1855), §6:** "Or I guess the grass is itself a child...
  Sprouting alike in broad zones and narrow zones, / Growing among black
  folks as among white."
- **Dickinson (c. 1861):** "'Hope' is the thing with feathers — / That
  perches in the soul — / And sings the tune without the words — / And
  never stops — at all —"

## Guided practice (S7, with the adult)

1. Together, build a comparative claim about **identity** in Whitman §1
   and Dickinson's poem: what does each text say the self *is*? Draft the
   shared ground, the difference, and choose one quotation per text.
2. Test the claim against the evidence: does each quotation actually prove
   its sentence? Cut any sentence the quotes don't support.
3. **Figurative language drill:** In Whitman §6, the grass is "the flag of
   my disposition," "the handkerchief of the Lord," and "a uniform
   hieroglyphic." For each of the three: what two things are compared, and
   what does that particular comparison emphasize about the grass?
4. **Error-analysis task:** A learner writes: "Douglass and Dickinson both
   write about freedom, so they agree." Find the two errors: (1) it
   confuses shared *topic* with shared *position*; (2) it offers no
   evidence and no contrast — agreement is asserted, not argued.

## Independent practice (S8)

1. Write a full comparative paragraph (8–10 sentences): how do Douglass
   and Whitman treat **freedom** differently? Use at least two quotations
   and the three-part structure (shared ground, difference, evidence).
2. Write a second comparative paragraph: how do Whitman and Dickinson
   treat **identity** differently? At least two quotations.
3. **Application — paradox:** Dickinson writes that hope "sings the tune
   without the words." A song without words is a paradox (a seeming
   contradiction). In 4–5 sentences, explain what this paradox lets the
   poem say about hope that a plain statement ("hope persists quietly")
   could not. (L.11-12.5.a)
4. **Exit check:** (a) Name the three texts, their authors, and their
   dates from memory. (b) In two sentences, state one similarity and one
   difference in how any two of them treat freedom. (c) Define *extended
   metaphor* in your own words.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** Whitman's long lines are easier aloud — read §6
  slowly, pausing at the commas, before annotating.
- **Alternate response mode:** Comparative paragraphs may be planned as a
  two-column chart (Text A claim + quote | Text B claim + quote) and then
  dictated or written.
- **Extension:** Add a fourth voice: find one stanza of Whitman's "I Hear
  America Singing" (1860) and write a paragraph placing it in the
  comparison — does it side more with Douglass's indictment, Whitman's
  §1 celebration, or Dickinson's interior hope? Defend with a quotation.

## Teacher note (misconceptions; answer-key references)

- Learners often write "compare and contrast" as two separate
  descriptions stapled together. Insist on the *relationship* sentence:
  "X does ___, while Y does ___" — the "while" is where the thinking lives.
- Watch for presentism ("Douglass would agree with modern views on...").
  Redirect to the 1850s: what did *these words* mean to *that audience*?
- Dickinson's dashes are doing real work (pauses, breaks, withheld
  completion) — worth one minute of explicit attention, not a full lesson.
- Answer-key references: guided practice §L03-G; independent practice
  §L03-I; exit check §L03-E.
