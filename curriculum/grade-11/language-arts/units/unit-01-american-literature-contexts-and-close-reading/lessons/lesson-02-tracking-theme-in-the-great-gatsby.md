# Lesson 02 — Tracking Theme Across a Novel: *The Great Gatsby*

**Unit 01 · Sessions S4–S5 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RL.11-12.2, RL.11-12.1, RL.11-12.10

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can state two or more themes of *The Great
Gatsby* in full sentences, show how each theme develops across chapters
with quoted evidence, explain how the themes interact, and tell an
objective summary apart from an analysis.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, the learner's copy of *The Great Gatsby* (Project
Gutenberg text verified 2026-10-14 at
<https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317>; all quotations below were
checked against the full text at
<https://www.gutenberg.org/files/64317/64317-h/64317-h.htm> on the same
date), a theme-tracking chart (practice.md).

## Readiness check (5 min)

1. In one sentence, what is *The Great Gatsby* about? (Plot only — who
   does what.)
2. Now write one sentence about what the novel *says* — a claim about
   life, not about the plot. Notice how much harder the second sentence
   is. That difficulty is what this lesson is for.

(Adult: the first answer is a topic; the second gropes toward a theme.
Keep both visible — the lesson will sharpen the difference.)

## Explicit teaching

### Topic is not theme

A **topic** is a subject the book touches: wealth, love, parties, the
past. A **theme** is a complete sentence stating what the book *claims*
about that topic. "Wealth" is a topic. "Wealth without purpose hollows out
the people who have it" is a theme — it can be argued with, and it needs
evidence.

A **motif** is a recurring image or detail that carries a theme — the
green light, the eyes of Dr. T. J. Eckleburg, the color imagery. Motifs
are how you *track* a theme: follow the image, and the theme's
development appears.

### How to track a theme across a novel

1. **State it as a sentence** you could disagree with.
2. **Collect appearances** — note the chapter and page, quote briefly,
   and record what the passage shows about the theme *at that point*.
3. **Watch it change** — a theme is not a sticker applied once; it
   develops. Ask: does the novel complicate, deepen, or reverse what it
   seemed to say earlier?
4. **Watch themes interact** — novels run two or more themes at once, and
   they collide. The collision is usually the point.

### Summary vs. analysis

An **objective summary** reports what happens, in order, without judgment:
"Gatsby pursues Daisy, wins her briefly, loses her, and is killed; Nick
returns west." An **analysis** argues what it *means* and proves it:
"Gatsby's pursuit of Daisy shows the American Dream curdling into
self-destruction, because..." Summary answers *what*; analysis answers
*so what*, with evidence. Every analysis in this course stands on a
summary — and the reader must be able to see the seam between them.

**Worked example 1 — Tracking a theme through a motif (modeled).**
Theme (sentence): *The American Dream promises that reinvention can erase
the past, but the novel shows the past pulling the dreamer backward.*

- **Ch. 1:** Nick sees Gatsby reach toward "a single green light, minute
  and far away, that might have been the end of a dock." The light is
  Daisy, the dream — distant, possibly imagined ("might have been").
- **Ch. 5:** Reunited with Daisy, Gatsby finds the light diminished: "It
  had seemed as close as a star to the moon. Now it was again a green
  light on a dock. His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one."
  Possessing the dream shrinks it — the theme develops by *disappointment*.
- **Ch. 9 (final line):** "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne
  back ceaselessly into the past." The last sentence universalizes the
  theme: not just Gatsby — *we*. The dream rows forward; the past is the
  current.

Note the method: one motif, three dated sightings, and each sighting
changes what the theme means. That is theme *development*.

**Worked example 2 — Summary vs. analysis (modeled).**
Summary: "Tom and Daisy are wealthy, careless, and leave damage behind
them; after Gatsby's death they leave town." — plot, in order, no judgment
beyond what the text states.

Analysis: "Nick's judgment — 'They were careless people, Tom and
Daisy — they smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into
their money or their vast carelessness' — turns wealth from a setting into
an accusation: money is the mechanism that lets the Buchanans escape
consequences, and Nick, who opened the novel reserving judgment, ends it
passing one."

The analysis quotes, names the technique (judgment), and connects the end
of the novel to its beginning (Nick's father's advice: "just remember that
all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've
had"). That connection — first page to last pages — is the kind of move
that earns full credit.

## Guided practice (S4, with the adult)

1. Together, state a second theme of the novel as a full sentence. A
   strong candidate: *Illusion sustains the characters, and reality
   destroys them.* Test it: can you disagree with it? Good — now it's a
   theme.
2. Find two passages that develop this theme — one from the first half of
   the novel, one from the second. Quote briefly (under 40 words each)
   and write one sentence per passage on what it shows.
3. **Theme interaction:** In 4–5 sentences, explain how the two themes
   (the Dream/past theme and the illusion/reality theme) collide in
   Gatsby himself. Where does one theme need the other to make its point?

## Independent practice (S5)

1. Add three entries to your theme-tracking chart (practice.md): one new
   sighting for each theme, plus one sighting that serves *both*.
2. Write a 5–6 sentence objective summary of the novel's plot. Then
   highlight or mark every word in it that is secretly a judgment
   (examples: "tragically," "deserved," "beautiful"). Rewrite until no
   judgment words remain.
3. **Application:** Choose one motif (the green light, the eyes of
   Dr. T. J. Eckleburg, or color imagery — gold/yellow, white, gray).
   Write a paragraph tracing it through at least three moments in the
   novel and stating what theme it carries. Quote at least twice.
4. **Exit check:** (a) Write both themes as full sentences from memory.
   (b) In one sentence each, define *theme*, *topic*, and *motif*.
   (c) Give one example of a summary sentence and one of an analysis
   sentence about the novel.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** If the novel's length is daunting, the adult may
  assign the LibriVox audiobook (free, no account; find it via a labeled
  search in the Resource Pack) for one or two chapters, with the learner
  following along in the text.
- **Alternate response mode:** The theme-tracking chart may be kept as a
  dictated log or a simple two-column table (chapter | what it shows).
- **Extension:** Read the novel's first page and last page side by side.
  Write a paragraph on how Nick's voice changes — from "I am still a
  little afraid of missing something" to "borne back ceaselessly into the
  past." What does the change suggest about what the novel taught its
  narrator?

## Teacher note (misconceptions; answer-key references)

- The most common error is stating themes as topics ("The theme is
  wealth"). Require the full-sentence test: if it can't be disagreed
  with, it isn't a theme yet.
- Learners often "track" a theme by listing the same observation three
  times. Push for *development*: what does the later passage show that
  the earlier one didn't?
- Moral: Nick is an *unreliable-adjacent* narrator — he tells us he
  reserves judgment, then judges. Learners who notice this are ready for
  Lesson 04's seminar.
- Answer-key references: guided practice §L02-G; independent practice
  §L02-I; exit check §L02-E.
