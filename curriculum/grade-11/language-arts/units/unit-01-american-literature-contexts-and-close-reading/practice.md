## 2. Evidence-citation drills (objectives 2, 4)

For each claim below: (a) judge whether the quotation actually supports
the claim; (b) if not, explain what's wrong; (c) if yes, add the one
explanation sentence the writer still owes.

1. **Claim:** Douglass felt excluded from the Fourth of July celebration.
   **Quote:** "This Fourth of July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I
   must mourn."
2. **Claim:** Whitman believed all people share one nature. **Quote:** "I
   CELEBRATE myself, and sing myself."
3. **Claim:** In *Gatsby*, possessing the dream diminishes it. **Quote:**
   "It had seemed as close as a star to the moon. Now it was again a green
   light on a dock. His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one."
4. **Claim:** Dickinson's hope is fragile and easily destroyed. **Quote:**
   "And sweetest — in the Gale — is heard — / And sore must be the storm —
   / That could abash the little Bird / That kept so many warm —"
5. **Claim:** Nick admires Gatsby without reservation. **Quote:** "Gatsby
   turned out all right at the end; it is what preyed on Gatsby, what foul
   dust floated in the wake of his dreams that temporarily closed out my
   interest in the abortive sorrows and short-winded elations of men."

## 4. Vocabulary in context (objective 5 support)

Each word below appears in the unit's texts or lessons. For each: write
the sentence it appears in (or find it in the text), define it from
context, then check a dictionary and note any difference.

1. **pale** — "I am not included within the pale of this glorious
   anniversary!" (Douglass)
2. **mourn** — "You may rejoice, I must mourn." (Douglass)
3. **irony** — "scorching irony, not convincing argument, is needed."
   (Douglass)
4. **loafe** — "I loafe and invite my soul" (Whitman)
5. **abash** — "That could abash the little Bird" (Dickinson)
6. **parceled** — "a sense of the fundamental decencies is parcelled out
   unequally at birth" (Fitzgerald, Ch. 1)
7. **supercilious** — "a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner"
   (Fitzgerald, Ch. 1, of Tom Buchanan)
8. **orgiastic** — "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgiastic
   future that year by year recedes before us" (Fitzgerald, Ch. 9)

Then: choose any three and write one original sentence per word that shows
you understand it — a sentence that would be *wrong* if the meaning were
different.

## 5. Summary vs. analysis sorting (objective 3 support)

Label each sentence **S** (summary) or **A** (analysis). For each A,
underline the words doing the analytical work; for each S, note what
information is missing that an analysis would need.

1. Gatsby throws large parties hoping Daisy will attend one.
2. The parties' emptiness — guests who don't know their host — shows that
   Gatsby's wealth buys spectacle but not belonging.
3. Dickinson compares hope to a bird that sings without words.
4. By making hope a bird that "never... asked a crumb," Dickinson argues
   that hope sustains without demanding repayment.
5. Douglass gave his speech on July 5, 1852, in Rochester, New York.
6. Douglass's repeated "your" turns a celebration invitation into an
   indictment by marking exactly who the holiday excludes.
7. Whitman's grass grows among all people equally.
8. The grass's equal growth lets Whitman imagine a democracy that the
   1850s had not achieved — the image does political work the laws didn't.
