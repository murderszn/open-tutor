# Answer Key — Unit 01: American Literature Contexts, Themes, and Close Reading

Every question in the unit's lessons, practice file, applied task, quiz,
and unit assessment is solved here with worked reasoning. Open responses
give sample reasoning plus acceptable variation — credit any response that
meets the stated criteria with accurate textual evidence.

## §L01-G — Lesson 01 guided practice

1. **Annotations:** Accept any 8+ marks using all four types. Look for:
   circled "pardon me" / "your"; underlined "those I represent," "gross
   injustice and cruelty"; stars on "yours, not mine" and the "What to the
   American slave...?" question; margin notes in the learner's own words.
2. **Questions:** Accept any three genuine questions tied to specific
   words, e.g.: "Why does he say 'pardon me' — what is he apologizing
   for?" / "Who exactly are 'those I represent'?" / "Why 'scorching irony'
   instead of just 'irony'?"
3. **Connect:** Accept: 1852 — invited by an antislavery society to
   celebrate Independence Day; the passage turns the Revolutionary
   period's founding language (the Declaration) against the nation that
   still enslaved Black people; it argues with the celebration itself.
4. **Summary check:** Every clause must trace to the passage. Accept
   e.g.: "Douglass asks why he has been invited to celebrate a national
   independence that excludes Black Americans, and answers that for the
   enslaved, the Fourth of July exposes injustice rather than celebrating
   freedom."
5. **Error analysis:** (1) "stupid" and "white people are bad" are the
   writer's judgments, not the passage's words — opinion smuggled into a
   summary. (2) It misses the passage's structure: the *question* (why am
   I asked to speak? who is included?) and the *answer* (the day reveals
   injustice). A summary reports the argument; it doesn't grade it.

## §L01-I — Lesson 01 independent practice

1. **New annotations:** Accept any 4+ dated additions.
2. **Answered question:** Credit 3–4 sentences that answer the learner's
   own question *from the text* with at least one quotation. The
   quotation must be accurate.
3. **Connect paragraph:** Must name the Romantic period (c. 1820–1865)
   and say what the speech shows about it — e.g., that Romantic-era
   American writing put the individual conscience against national
   pieties, / that it used founding documents as moral yardsticks.
   Accept reasonable variation grounded in the timeline.
4. **"Your" vs. "our":** Accept 4–5 sentences arguing that "your" draws
   a boundary: the celebration belongs to the audience, not to Douglass
   or the enslaved; the single word carries the speech's whole claim of
   exclusion. Must quote the sentence. Strong answers note the repetition
   ("yours, not mine... You may rejoice, I must mourn") as a pattern, not
   a one-off.
5. **Exit check:** Six periods in order: colonial, revolutionary,
   Romanticism/Transcendentalism, Realism/Naturalism, Modernism, Harlem
   Renaissance. Any three one-sentence beliefs from the timeline table.

## §L02-G — Lesson 02 guided practice

1. **Second theme:** Accept any arguable full sentence, e.g.: "Illusion
   sustains the characters, and reality destroys them." Must pass the
   disagreement test.
2. **Two passages:** Accept any two brief, accurate quotations — one
   from roughly the first half, one from the second — each with one
   sentence on what it shows. Examples: Ch. 3, "In his blue gardens men
   and girls came and went like moths..." (illusion as spectacle); Ch. 7,
   the confrontation in the Plaza Hotel (reality intruding); Ch. 9, the
   green light "diminished by one" (illusion collapsing on contact).
3. **Theme interaction:** Accept 4–5 sentences explaining the collision:
   e.g., Gatsby's Dream *requires* illusion (Daisy as enchanted object);
   the past-theme says the dream can't escape history; together they
   produce the novel's verdict — the dream is beautiful and doomed because
   it is built on illusion about the past. Credit any coherent account of
   mutual dependence.

## §L02-I — Lesson 02 independent practice

1. **Chart:** Three new entries, each with chapter, short quote, and a
   "what it shows at this point" sentence. The both-themes entry must
   explain the double service.
2. **Summary rewrite:** The final version must contain no judgment words.
   Test: read it aloud — if any word praises or blames, it's not done.
3. **Motif paragraph:** Must trace the motif through three moments with
   2+ quotations and state the theme it carries. E.g., green light: Ch. 1
   sighting (distant dream) → Ch. 5 diminishment (possession shrinks it)
   → Ch. 9 closing (universalized past). Accept Eckleburg or color
   motifs with equivalent structure.
4. **Exit check:** (a) Both themes as sentences from memory. (b) theme =
   arguable sentence about what the work claims; topic = subject area;
   motif = recurring image carrying a theme. (c) Accept any clear
   summary/analysis pair, e.g. S: "Gatsby pursues Daisy across the summer
   of 1922 and is killed." A: "Gatsby's pursuit shows the American Dream
   curdling into self-destruction."

## §L03-G — Lesson 03 guided practice

1. **Comparative claim (identity):** Accept e.g.: Shared ground — both
   Whitman §1 and Dickinson's poem ask what the self is. Difference —
   Whitman makes the self *expansive and shared* ("every atom belonging
   to me as good belongs to you"), while Dickinson makes it *inward and
   self-sustaining* (hope "perches in the soul" and "never... asked a
   crumb"). One quotation per text; each sentence must be supported.
2. **Evidence test:** Credit honest cuts — any sentence the quotes don't
   support must go.
3. **Grass figures:** (a) "flag of my disposition" — grass is like a flag:
   it declares the speaker's nature publicly; (b) "handkerchief of the
   Lord" — grass is like a dropped keepsake: intimate, designed, bearing
   its owner's mark; (c) "uniform hieroglyphic" — grass is like a sacred
   writing: one sign, readable by all, meaning equality ("Sprouting alike
   ... Growing among black folks as among white").
4. **Error analysis:** (1) Shared topic ≠ shared position — both writing
   about freedom doesn't mean agreeing about it. (2) No evidence, no
   contrast — "they agree" is asserted, not argued from quotations.

## §L03-I — Lesson 03 independent practice

1. **Comparative paragraph (freedom):** Must use the three-part structure
   with 2+ quotations. Accept e.g.: shared ground — both take up American
   freedom in the 1850s; difference — Douglass treats freedom as *denied
   and demanded* ("This Fourth of July is yours, not mine"), Whitman as
   *claimed and shared* ("I give them the same, / I receive them the
   same"); the contrast shows indictment vs. imagination as the decade's
   two literary jobs.
2. **Comparative paragraph (identity):** 2+ quotations; accept
   expansive-shared (Whitman) vs. inward-sustaining (Dickinson), or
   public self (Whitman) vs. private endurance (Dickinson).
3. **Paradox:** Accept 4–5 sentences: a song without words is hope that
   persists without explanation or argument; the paradox lets Dickinson
   claim hope is *felt* rather than *stated* — it needs no reasons, no
   audience, no payment ("never... asked a crumb"), which a plain
   statement would flatten into a slogan.
4. **Exit check:** (a) Douglass — "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of
   July?", 1852; Whitman — "Song of Myself," 1855; Dickinson — "'Hope'
   is the thing with feathers —," c. 1861. (b) Accept any one similarity
   + one difference with textual grounding. (c) An extended metaphor
   sustains one comparison across a passage/poem so the reader holds two
   meanings at once.

## §L04-G — Lesson 04 guided practice

1. **Dickinson close reading:** Accept a five-move reading of stanza 1:
   annotations on "feathers," "perches," "without the words"; questions
   about why a *bird*, why *wordless*; connect to the Romantic interior
   self; summary; argument (e.g., the stanza makes hope physical, small,
   and unpaid-for).
2. **Seminar questions:** Each question must *require* the text. Weak:
   "What is freedom?" Strong: "Douglass says the Fourth 'reveals'
   injustice — what does Whitman's grass reveal, and to whom?"
3. **Probe rehearsal:** Credit the two-step move: ask "What in the text
   made you read it that way?" then offer a counter-quotation (e.g.,
   Douglass's "immeasurable distance" against Whitman's "the same").

## §L04-I — Lesson 04 independent practice

- **Prep sheet:** All seven items complete; evidence notes must include
  real quotations with what each proves; questions must require the
  text; item 7 must name a genuinely persuadable point.
- **Exit check:** 5–6 sentences with a position and its single strongest
  evidence. No "correct" position — score the defense.

## §L04-A — Applied task ("Harbor at Dawn")

1. **Two shared details** (cite vignette lines): e.g., the dock at dawn
   ("Before the sun cleared the warehouse roofs, Mara was already on the
   dock"); gulls ("Gulls argued over nothing"); the flag being raised
   ("freed the knotted halyard... the small flag lifted"); the harbor
   with boats/masts; the industrial shoreline (warehouse roofs / factory
   chimneys).
2. **Two single-source details + why omitted:** Accept e.g.: the bakery
   windows and smell of bread (text only — smell can't be painted; the
   illustrator chose the visual shoreline instead); the figure's
   old-fashioned work clothes and cap (image only — the vignette never
   describes Mara's clothing; the image supplies era and class visually);
   the heavy mist (image only — atmosphere is easier to show than tell);
   the grandfather's "two kinds of promises" (text only — interior
   memory has no visual equivalent). The "why" must be a real
   medium-based reason, not "they forgot."
3. **One inference:** Accept any defended inference, e.g.: the
   illustration's prominent factory chimneys suggest an industrial,
   working harbor — supporting an inference that Mara's labor belongs to
   a long line of dock work (visual detail: chimneys + old-style figure;
   text line: "Her grandfather had worked this same dock"). Or: the
   image's older-era figure suggests the scene is *timeless*/memory-like,
   which the text never states.
4. **Era difference:** No single right answer. Full credit requires: a
   clear position + 4–5 sentences + evidence from *both* text and image.
   Strong "changes meaning" answers argue the older-era figure turns
   Mara's present-tense labor into a historical tableau, softening the
   vignette's immediacy. Strong "just costume" answers argue the flag,
   dawn, and work are the meaning, and era-dress doesn't touch them.

## §P — Practice file

**§P.2 Evidence-citation drills:**
1. **Supports.** Owed explanation: the "yours/mine" split *is* the
   exclusion — the quote doesn't just describe feeling, it performs the
   boundary.
2. **Does NOT support.** "I CELEBRATE myself, and sing myself" shows
   self-celebration, not shared nature — the *shared* claim needs "every
   atom belonging to me as good belongs to you." Wrong quote for the
   claim.
3. **Supports.** Owed explanation: "diminished by one" marks the exact
   moment possession shrinks the dream — the mechanism of the theme in
   one image.
4. **Does NOT support.** The stanza says the opposite: the storm must be
   "sore" (severe) to abash the bird — hope is *resilient*, not fragile.
   This is a misreading; the quote refutes the claim.
5. **Does NOT support (as stated).** The full sentence is double-edged:
   "Gatsby turned out all right at the end; it is what preyed on Gatsby"
   — Nick exempts the man while condemning what consumed him. "Without
   reservation" ignores the second clause. Partially supports at best;
   the claim overstates.

**§P.4 Vocabulary:** Accept context-based definitions, then dictionary
check. Key senses: 1. *pale* — boundary/enclosure (beyond the pale =
   outside the limits); 2. *mourn* — grieve, lament; 3. *irony* — saying
   the opposite of what is meant to expose folly; here, *scorching* =
   searing; 4. *loafe* — idle, linger at ease (Whitman's spelling);
   5. *abash* — make ashamed, disconcert; 6. *parceled* — divided and
   distributed in portions; 7. *supercilious* — haughtily contemptuous;
   8. *orgiastic* — ecstatic, unrestrained (here: the frenzied promise of
   the future). Original sentences must demonstrate meaning.

**§P.5 Summary/analysis sorting:** 1-S; 2-A (analytical work: "shows,"
"emptiness," the causal claim about wealth); 3-S; 4-A ("argues,"
"without demanding repayment" — interpretive claim); 5-S; 6-A ("turns...
into an indictment" — the interpretive move); 7-S; 8-A ("lets Whitman
imagine," "does political work" — claims about function).

**§P.6 Timeline:** Six periods with ranges and beliefs (see Lesson 01
table). Overlap: the Harlem Renaissance (~1918–1937) falls inside
Modernism (~1914–1945). Why it matters: 1920s writers share a historical
moment but not a project — Fitzgerald's Jazz Age disillusion and
Hughes's Black artistic self-claiming are contemporaneous and different,
which is exactly the "same period, different treatment" skill Lesson 03
teaches.

## §Q — Quiz key

1. Colonial (~1607–1775), Revolutionary (~1775–1820), Romanticism &
   Transcendentalism (~1820–1865), Realism & Naturalism (~1865–1914),
   Modernism (~1914–1945), Harlem Renaissance (~1918–1937). (2 pts;
   1 pt for order, 1 for ranges)
2. Literature should express the individual soul and the American
   landscape — original, feeling, free from European models. (2 pts)
3. Annotate, question, connect, summarize. The question move produces
   genuine, text-specific questions that drive the reading deeper. (2 pts)
4. Accept any arguable theme sentence + one brief accurate quote. (2 pts)
5. Summary reports what happens without judgment; analysis argues what it
   means with evidence. One-sentence example of each about *Gatsby*.
   (2 pts)
6. "Yours" draws the exclusion boundary: the celebration belongs to the
   audience, not to Douglass or enslaved Black Americans — one word
   carries the speech's claim. (2 pts)
7. Similarity: both take up American freedom / who counts. Difference:
   Douglass — freedom denied/demanded; Whitman — freedom claimed/shared.
   One quotation supporting the contrast. (2 pts)
8. A metaphor sustained across a passage/poem. In Dickinson: hope = a
   bird (the thing with feathers) that perches in the soul and sings.
   (2 pts)
9. "Everyone is equal" is a topic-level observation — it names a subject
   but makes no arguable claim about *how the poem* treats it. Theme
   version, e.g.: "Whitman's grass images make equality a law of nature
   rather than a political achievement." (2 pts)
10. The image claims a natural, democratic American identity — the same
    grass, the same worth, regardless of race. In 1855, seven years into
    the decade that would bring civil war, with slavery legal, the image
    is aspirational and confrontational, not descriptive. (2 pts)

## §UA — Unit assessment key

**Part A (essay, 40 pts):** Score with the teacher-guide rubric. What
"excellent" looks like per passage:

- *Passage 1 (Gatsby):* Strong theses connect the green light to the
  past-current: e.g., "The passage argues that the American Dream is not
  defeated by failure but by time itself." Strong essays quote "recedes
  before us," "borne back ceaselessly," and "orgiastic future," explain
  each, and name a real uncertainty — e.g., who "we" includes, or whether
  "one fine morning" is hope or delusion — and why it matters.
- *Passage 2 (Whitman):* Strong theses: "The grass passage makes
  equality a fact of nature, not a goal of politics." Quotes: "uniform
  hieroglyphic," "Sprouting alike," "among black folks as among white."
  Uncertainty: the "Or I guess" repetitions — Whitman *guesses*; the
  poem's certainty is performed, not proven.
- *Passage 3 (Dickinson):* Strong theses: "The stanza argues hope is
  most itself under pressure." Quotes: "sweetest — in the Gale,"
  "sore must be the storm," "kept so many warm." Uncertainty: whether
  the bird is hope itself or the *feeling* of hope — and whether "never
  asked a crumb" is generosity or indifference.

**Part B:**
1. Six periods + ranges (2 pts) and two one-sentence beliefs (1 pt).
2. Accept e.g.: "Douglass asks why he, a formerly enslaved man, is
   invited to celebrate an independence that excludes Black Americans."
   Flag judgment words like "bravely," "stupidly," "heroically." (3 pts)
3. Two theme sentences (1 pt) + 3–4 sentences on interaction at the
   ending — e.g., the closing universalizes both: the Dream rows forward
   ("beat on") while the past pulls back ("borne back"), and the
   illusion (green light) is revealed as the mechanism. (3 pts)
4. Similarity + difference, each with a quotation — e.g., both write
   freedom as central to American identity; Douglass writes it as
   *withheld* ("yours, not mine"), Dickinson as *inward and
   unwithholdable* ("never... asked a crumb"). (3 pts)
5. Extended metaphor in own words (1 pt) + what it enables: hope as a
   bird makes an abstraction physical, small, vulnerable-yet-enduring,
   and unpaid-for — claims a definition can't carry. (2 pts)
6. Illustration shows atmosphere, spatial relations, and era at a glance
   (1 pt); text gives interiority, time, smell, memory — what can't be
   seen (1 pt). Accept equivalent formulations.
7. Probing question, e.g.: "What in Douglass's 'immeasurable distance'
   sounds like agreement with Whitman's 'the same'?" + the
   counter-quotation. (2 pts)
8. Reflection: one objective named strongest + one needing work, each
   with a specific work citation. Score honesty and specificity, not
   which objectives are chosen. (3 pts)

## §PR — Project rubric application

Apply the project.md rubric as written. Notes:

- **Text selection:** Check period claims (19th c. = 1800s; early
  20th c. = 1900–1945). A *Gatsby* excerpt must total under 500 words
  with citation — verify the word count.
- **Evidence:** Spot-check every quotation against its source; one
  inaccurate quote drops the criterion to Developing until fixed.
- **Thematic connection:** The introduction's theme must pass the
  disagreement test. If it doesn't, return it once for revision before
  scoring — the rubric measures the final product.
- **Reflection:** "What comparison taught" must go beyond "I learned a
  lot" — look for a specific insight about the theme that required two
  or more texts.
