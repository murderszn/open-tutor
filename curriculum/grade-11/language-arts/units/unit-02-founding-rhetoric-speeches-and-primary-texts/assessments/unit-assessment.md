# Unit Assessment — Unit 02: Founding Rhetoric

**Grade 11 · Language Arts · Session S19 · ~50 minutes for Part A;
Part B essay drafted S15 and submitted here**
Answer in the notebook or a word-processed document the adult reviews.
The key and scoring guidance are in `../answer-key.md` (§A) — do not
open them until the adult scores the assessment.

## Part A — Short answers (30 minutes)

A1. Delineate the Declaration's argument in a short paragraph: name
its four parts, state the philosophy section's central claim in one
sentence, and say what work the grievance list does that the
philosophy alone cannot do. (Objective 2)
A2. Madison argues that an extended republic controls faction better
than a small one. Reconstruct his reasoning in 6–8 sentences: the
definition, the rejected cures, and the extended-sphere mechanism —
including the qualifier ("less probable") and why it matters.
(Objective 3)
A3. Analyze one rhetorical feature of Lincoln's Second Inaugural
(antithesis, biblical allusion, or the shift from "I" to "let us"):
trace it across the speech's beginning, middle, and end with one
quotation from each, and explain what it contributes to the speech's
purpose. (Objective 4)
A4. Compare the Declaration and the Bill of Rights on one shared theme
of your choice (liberty, consent, or union): one paragraph on what is
shared, one paragraph on what differs (mechanism or audience), each
paragraph grounded in at least one quotation. (Objective 5)

## Part B — Rhetorical analysis essay (drafted S15, submitted S19)

**Prompt:** Choose one founding text studied in this unit (the
Declaration, Federalist No. 10, Lincoln's Second Inaugural, or the Bill
of Rights). Write a rhetorical analysis essay (700–900 words) that:

1. States a precise, arguable claim about *how* the text persuades —
   not merely that it is persuasive, and not a summary of its content.
2. Delineates the text's reasoning in order (premises, evidence,
   structure), with at least three accurately quoted passages.
3. Analyzes at least two rhetorical devices or appeals, explaining
   what work each does in the argument (not merely labeling it).
4. Develops the strongest counterclaim fairly — the best objection to
   the text's reasoning, or to your claim about it — concedes what is
   true in it, and answers what remains.
5. Evaluates the argument: name one strength and one limit or unproved
   premise, each supported by the text.
6. Concludes from the evidence: the final paragraph must follow from
   the analysis, not introduce a new claim.

**Requirements:** formal register; quotations checked character by
character against the source edition; no invented facts; a short
source note naming the edition used. Essays that summarize without
analyzing, or that label devices without explaining their work, do not
meet the standard even if they are long.

**Scoring (40 points):** claim precision 6; delineation 8; device/appeal
analysis 8; counterclaim 6; evaluation 6; conclusion and formal
register 6. See `../answer-key.md` (§A) for level descriptors and a
sample thesis for each text. The adult records the score privately;
scores are never recorded in this repository.
