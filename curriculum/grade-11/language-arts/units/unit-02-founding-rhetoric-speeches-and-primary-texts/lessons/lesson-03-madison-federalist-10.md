# Lesson 03 — Madison's Federalist No. 10: Faction and the Extended Republic

**Unit 02 · Sessions S7–S8 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RI.11-12.3, RI.11-12.8

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can delineate Madison's argument in
Federalist No. 10: his definition of faction, the two cures he rejects
and why, and his extended-republic case for controlling faction's
effects rather than removing its causes.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, the Federalist No. 10 text (Bill of Rights
Institute handout, linked in the unit README), and a blank premise map
(claim at the top, premises below, objections in the margin).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. What is a "faction" in ordinary modern usage? Give an example that
   is *not* a political party.
2. Can a free society exist without disagreements that harden into
   groups? Why or why not, in two sentences?

## Explicit teaching

### Occasion and audience

Federalist No. 10 was published in New York newspapers in November 1787
under the shared pen name **Publius**, as part of the campaign to
persuade New York to ratify the proposed Constitution. Its audience was
not a classroom: it was voters and convention delegates worried that a
stronger national government would crush liberty — or that the new
republic would tear itself apart. Madison's essay answers a specific
fear: *popular government always dies of faction, so why should this
republic be different?* Everything in the essay serves that question.
An argument read without its occasion is easy to misread as abstract
philosophy; it was advocacy, on deadline, for ratification.

### The definition — refined, not just stated

> "By a faction, I understand a number of citizens, whether amounting
> to a majority or minority of the whole, who are united and actuated by
> some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adverse to the rights
> of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the
> community."

Read the definition slowly; each phrase does work:

- **"whether amounting to a majority or minority"** — the danger is not
  only majorities. A minority faction can also capture institutions.
  Madison refuses the comforting idea that only majorities threaten.
- **"united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of
  interest"** — faction is not mere disagreement. It is *organized*
  passion or interest: people acting together.
- **"adverse to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and
  aggregate interests of the community"** — the test is not "do I
  disagree with them?" but "does their program harm others' rights or
  the community's long-term interest?" That qualifier is what keeps
  "faction" from meaning "any group I dislike."

Madison then traces faction's causes to human nature itself: fallible
reason, self-love, differing opinions about religion and government,
and — most durably — the unequal distribution of property and the
different interests it creates. Causes rooted in human nature and
economic life cannot simply be legislated away. That premise drives the
whole essay.

### Two methods, four options, two rejections

> "There are two methods of curing the mischiefs of faction: The one,
> by removing its causes; the other, by controlling its effects."

Removing the causes has two sub-options, and Madison rejects both:

1. **Destroy liberty.** "Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, an
   aliment without which it instantly expires." The analogy is exact
   and brutal: you can end faction by ending freedom, just as you can
   end fire by removing air. But, Madison says, "it could never be more
   truly said than of the first remedy, that it was worse than the
   disease." Liberty is "essential to political life"; destroying it
   to save the republic destroys the thing being saved.
2. **Give every citizen the same opinions, passions, and interests.**
   This is "as impracticable as the first would be unwise." As long as
   reason is fallible and people are free to use it, opinions will
   differ; as long as people have different abilities and property,
   interests will differ. Uniformity cannot be imposed without the
   first remedy's violence.

Since the causes cannot be removed, "relief is only to be sought in the
means of controlling its *effects*." That single sentence is the
essay's hinge: everything after it is about *how* a republic controls
effects.

### Worked example 1: the air-and-fire analogy, weighed

> "Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, an aliment without which
> it instantly expires. But it could not be less folly to abolish
> liberty, which is essential to political life, because it nourishes
> faction, than it would be to wish the annihilation of air, which is
> essential to animal life, because it imparts to fire its destructive
> agency."

Delineation:

- **Comparison:** liberty : faction :: air : fire. Liberty nourishes
  faction the way air nourishes fire — necessarily, not accidentally.
- **Reasoning:** if you accept the comparison, then abolishing liberty
  to end faction is as foolish as abolishing air to end fire — you kill
  the patient to cure the symptom.
- **Evaluation:** the analogy is powerful but not proof. It *assumes*
  liberty is as essential to political life as air is to animal life.
  A critic could accept that faction feeds on liberty and still argue
  that some liberty-restricting measures (laws against bribery, for
  example) are worth it. Madison's analogy rules that middle position
  out by framing the choice as all-or-nothing. Naming that move — the
  analogy smuggles in an all-or-nothing frame — is evaluation, and it
  does not require rejecting Madison's larger point.

### The extended republic: how controlling effects works

Madison's solution has two linked mechanisms, and learners must keep
them distinct:

1. **The republican principle (for minority factions):** "If a faction
   consists of less than a majority, relief is supplied by the
   republican principle, which enables the majority to defeat its
   sinister views by regular vote." A minority faction loses elections.
   Simple — but it only handles minorities.
2. **The extended sphere (for majority factions, the hard case):**
   "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and
   interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole
   will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens."
   In a large republic, interests are so numerous and varied that a
   majority faction struggles to form, discover itself, and act in
   concert — and even if it forms, it must negotiate with other groups.
   Small republics, by contrast, let a single passion sweep the whole.

The logic is probabilistic, not guaranteed: "less probable," not
"impossible." A careful reader quotes that qualifier. Madison also
argues that representation *refines* public views through a chosen body
of citizens — a second, distinct claim that the essay states more
briefly than the extended-sphere argument and that critics have
challenged more often. Keep the two claims separate in the premise map.

### Worked example 2: the premise map, filled in

**Claim:** A large republic (the proposed Union) can control faction's
effects better than a small republic can.

**Premises:**

- P1. Faction's causes lie in fallible reason and unequal property —
  they cannot be removed without destroying liberty or imposing
  impossible uniformity.
- P2. Therefore only controlling effects remains.
- P3. Minority factions are defeated by majority vote.
- P4. In an extended sphere, the variety of interests makes a majority
  faction less likely to form and act together.
- P5. Representation refines and enlarges public views.

**Objections to note (not yet answered in the essay):** P4 assumes
interests stay divided; modern communication and national parties can
coordinate a majority faction across distance. P5 assumes
representatives will be wiser than their constituents — an assumption
the essay asserts more than proves. A strong evaluation names one such
objection *and* says whether it defeats the claim or merely limits it.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult, thinking aloud.

1. Quote Madison's definition of faction from memory as closely as you
   can, then check it against the text. Which phrase did you drop or
   change, and does the change matter to the definition's scope?
2. Madison rejects destroying liberty with the air-and-fire analogy.
   Restate his rejection in your own words in two sentences, without
   using the words "air" or "fire."
3. A classmate says: "Madison proved large republics never have
   factions." Use the essay's own qualifier ("less probable") to
   correct that sentence in one sentence.

## Independent practice

1. Complete a premise map for Federalist No. 10 (claim, P1–P5 as
   above, objections). For each premise, add one short quotation.
2. Write a 5–7 sentence paragraph: why does Madison think removing
   faction's causes is either unwise or impracticable? Use both
   rejected cures and the reason each fails.
3. Write a 6–8 sentence evaluation: is the extended-republic argument
   stronger for minority factions or for majority factions? Defend
   your answer with the essay's own mechanisms (vote vs. variety).
4. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "Madison hated political
   parties, so he wanted to ban them." Explain in 4–5 sentences why
   that misreads the essay: Madison's target is faction's *effects*,
   his remedy is structural, and his definition covers more than
   parties.

## Application

Apply Madison's test to a present-day civic question the adult approves
(a school policy, a town decision, a club rule): identify one group
that could count as a faction under Madison's definition, one that
would *not* count (because its program is not adverse to others'
rights or the community's long-term interest), and one structural rule
that would control the first group's effects without removing its
liberty. One paragraph each; no partisan position-taking is required,
and the adult checks the example for fairness before it is shared.

## Exit check

Without notes: write Madison's definition of faction in your own words
(keeping the majority/minority point and the "adverse to" test), and
name the essay's hinge sentence — the move from removing causes to
controlling effects — in one line.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** read the essay in three sittings (definition and
  causes; rejected cures; extended republic), summarizing each sitting
  in two sentences before moving on.
- **Alternate response mode:** the premise map may be spoken and
  scribed by the adult; the learner still supplies every premise and
  quotation.
- **Extension:** research, with the adult, one modern criticism of the
  extended-republic argument (for example, that national parties or
  mass media coordinate factions across distance). Write a 5-sentence
  response: does the criticism defeat Madison's claim, limit it, or
  miss it? Cite the criticism's source in the Resource Pack's format.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners equate faction with "party" and Madison's
remedy with "banning groups." The definition's breadth (majority *or*
minority, passion *or* interest) and the causes/effects hinge are the
correctives; drill them before the assessment. Key: answer-key §L03.
Note the essay's advocacy occasion: Madison is arguing *for*
ratification, so his confidence about representation deserves the same
evaluation as any advocate's claim.
