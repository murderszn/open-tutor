# Teacher Guide — Unit 02: Founding Rhetoric: Speeches and Primary Texts

**Grade 11 · Language Arts · for the guiding adult · companion to the
unit README's 20-session pacing**

This unit is the year's analytical hinge: Unit 01's close reading
becomes, here, *argument* reading. Everything later (criticism,
research, public reasoning) assumes the learner can delineate a
reasoning chain and evaluate it separately from agreeing with it. If
delineation is shaky at S17, slow down — U06's public-reasoning work
will punish gaps.

## Session-by-session notes

- **S1–S2 (Lesson 01):** Do the preamble's first sentence together,
  aloud, before any labeling. Learners who label first ("that's
  pathos") never learn to delineate. The "two appeals" requirement in
  independent practice 2 is deliberate: single-label answers are the
  unit's most common shallow response.
- **S3 (Workshop A):** The library passage is original and deliberately
  small. Check the toolkit table row by row; the most valuable
  correction is usually the reasoning row (learners restate the claim
  instead of naming the link).
- **S4–S5 (Lesson 02):** Teach the four-part map before the grievance
  list, or the list will swallow the lesson. For scope, state the
  three honest sentences plainly and let the learner write them; do not
  turn scope into a debate about the founders' character.
- **S6 (Workshop B):** Sorting is not the point — the one-line reasons
  are. A sorted list without reasons earns no credit in the key.
- **S7–S8 (Lesson 03):** The premise map is the lesson. Build it on
  paper, large, and keep it visible through S9. Drill the qualifier
  ("less probable"): learners who drop it will misstate Madison in
  the assessment.
- **S9 (Workshop C):** The "easiest/hardest premise" question is the
  unit's first real evaluation. Accept any defensible ranking that
  quotes a premise; reject rankings argued from "Madison was smart."
- **S10–S11 (Lesson 04):** Read the speech aloud first (about
  5 minutes). The cause/responsibility distinction needs a full
  session of its own; do not rush it into the close analysis.
- **S12 (Seminar):** Question: "Did Lincoln blame anyone?" Your job is
  traffic control and one or two probes, not content delivery. Track
  who speaks with evidence; ensure everyone speaks at least twice. End
  with 10 minutes of synthesis: "What did we figure out together about
  blame vs. cause?"
- **S13–S14 (Lesson 05 + image task):** Read Amendment I aloud and
  paraphrase it *before* analyzing it; reversed paraphrases ("gives
  us") must be corrected on the spot. For the image task, state the
  boundary in your own words at the start: the illustration is a
  prompt, never evidence. Collect the evidence sentence ("this image
  cannot prove…") — a comparison without it is incomplete.
- **S15 (Drafting):** S15 produces a complete draft (not a plan).
  Require three quotations checked against the source edition before
  the learner leaves the session.
- **S16 (Project workshop):** Approve the rhetorical map before any
  drafting. The most common failure is a counterclaim the learner's
  own side would not recognize — send those back.
- **S17–S20:** Review by objective, not by lesson. The quiz (S18)
  diagnoses; S19 is the assessment, not a re-teach. Project
  presentations (S20) are spoken work: score delivery live with the
  checklist below.

## Misconceptions and reteaching

- **Labeling without analysis** ("it is logos because it is logical"):
  require the follow-up sentence — *what work does it do?* — for every
  label, all unit long.
- **Faction = party:** re-teach the definition's breadth (majority or
  minority, passion or interest, adverse-to test) with a non-party
  example from practice §A's application task.
- **Pattern = proof:** the Declaration's list and Madison's analogy
  both invite this error. Reteach with one checked item (Lesson 02,
  worked example 2) and one weighed analogy (Lesson 03, worked
  example 1).
- **Blame flattening (Lincoln):** re-teach cause vs. responsibility
  with two columns and one quotation per column.
- **Negative rights reversed:** re-teach Amendment I by paraphrase,
  every time, until "shall make no law" survives paraphrase.
- **Image as evidence:** if a learner cites the illustration for a
  historical claim, stop and re-teach the boundary; the application
  requires the evidence sentence for exactly this reason.

## Differentiation

- **Reading access:** all four anchor texts are short; offer
  listen-while-reading for the Declaration and Federalist No. 10, and
  chunked reading (5 grievances at a time; 3 Madison sittings).
- **Alternate response modes:** exit checks and the seminar may be
  oral; the text-vs-image comparison may be a table; the premise map
  may be scribed by the adult with the learner supplying content.
- **Extension:** Lesson 03's modern-criticism extension, Lesson 05's
  amendment-history extension, and the Unit 01 Douglass comparison in
  Lesson 02/04 extensions. Extensions never replace core objectives.
- **Reteaching triggers:** if the S18 quiz shows objective 1 or 3
  below proficiency, re-teach with Workshop A/C before S19 rather than
  letting the assessment carry the gap.

## Speaking checklist (Lesson 04 application; project delivery)

Score each item yes/no; all yes = full credit for delivery:

1. Audible and paced (not rushed through in under 60% of the time).
2. Claim stated within the first minute.
3. At least two quotations, accurate, cited aloud ("Lincoln writes…").
4. Cause-to-peace (or claim-to-program) link stated explicitly.
5. Counterclaim (project) delivered in a fair tone, not mocked.
6. Closing delivered as a program, not trailed off.

## What readiness for U03 looks like

The learner leaves U02 able to: delineate any of the four anchor
arguments from memory in outline; quote accurately; name one strength
and one limit for at least two of them; and write an essay that
analyzes rather than summarizes. U03 (literary movements, diverse
voices) will reuse the delineation habit on literary interpretation —
the transfer to watch for is whether the learner still quotes and
evaluates when the text is a story rather than a speech.
