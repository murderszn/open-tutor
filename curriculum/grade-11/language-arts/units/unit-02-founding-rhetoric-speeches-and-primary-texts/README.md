# Unit 02 — Founding Rhetoric: Speeches and Primary Texts

**Grade 11 · Language Arts · American literature, rhetoric, and research pathway · Weeks 6–9 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-21 for issue #52; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Unit 01 taught the learner to read closely and to track themes. This unit
asks a harder question: **how does a text persuade a people to act?** The
founding generation did not merely write down ideas; it argued, in public,
for independence, for a new Constitution, and — after a civil war — for
reconciliation. Reading those arguments as *rhetoric* means asking, for
every passage: who is speaking, to whom, for what purpose, with what
claim, what evidence and reasoning, and what does the style itself do?

The unit runs in five moves:

1. **The rhetoric toolkit** — claim, evidence, reasoning; ethos, pathos,
   logos; and the devices writers actually use (parallelism, repetition,
   antithesis, rhetorical question, allusion).
2. **The Declaration of Independence (1776)** — its four-part structure,
   its philosophy of natural rights and consent, its catalogue of
   grievances, and the hard question of who its promises included — and
   who they excluded in practice.
3. **Federalist No. 10 (1787)** — James Madison's argument about
   "faction": a definition refined across the essay, two rejected cures,
   and the extended-republic solution.
4. **Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address (1865)** — a short speech that
   refuses triumph, assigns the war a theological cause, and ends in a
   program of charity and reconciliation.
5. **The Bill of Rights (1791) as rhetoric** — rights written as
   restraints on government, and a synthesis: how two or more founding
   texts treat the shared themes of liberty, consent, and union.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **when a text
argues for a political act, what makes its reasoning strong — and where
does it fail, hide a premise, or leave a question unanswered?**

## Prerequisites

From Unit 01 and the week-1 diagnostic (insecure skills are re-taught in
use):

- Cite strong, thorough textual evidence for explicit and inferential
  claims (RL.11-12.1).
- Determine themes/central ideas and write an objective summary
  (RL.11-12.2, RI.11-12.2).
- Use the close-reading routine: annotate, question, connect, summarize.
- Write organized analytical paragraphs with claims and evidence
  (W.11-12.1, W.11-12.2).

The unit does **not** assume fluency with: delineating and evaluating
reasoning in seminal U.S. texts (RI.11-12.8); analyzing 17th–19th-century
foundational U.S. documents for themes, purposes, and rhetorical features
(RI.11-12.9); determining point of view or purpose in highly rhetorical
texts through style and content (RI.11-12.6); or evaluating the
effectiveness of an exposition's structure (RI.11-12.5) — those are this
unit's new content.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Name and apply the rhetoric toolkit — claim, evidence, reasoning,
   ethos, pathos, logos, parallelism, repetition, antithesis, rhetorical
   question, allusion — identifying each in an unseen passage with a
   quoted example. (RI.11-12.3, RI.11-12.4, RI.11-12.6; track objective 5)
2. Delineate the Declaration of Independence's argument (philosophy,
   grievances, conclusion), explain how its word choices ("unalienable,"
   "consent of the governed") do persuasive work, and state accurately
   who its 1776 promises did and did not cover in practice.
   (RI.11-12.2, RI.11-12.5, RI.11-12.8, RI.11-12.9; track objectives 1, 5)
3. Delineate Madison's argument in Federalist No. 10: his definition of
   faction, the two rejected cures and why he rejects them, and the
   extended-republic argument for controlling faction's effects.
   (RI.11-12.3, RI.11-12.8; track objective 5)
4. Analyze Lincoln's Second Inaugural Address for theme, purpose, and
   rhetorical features — including antithesis, biblical allusion, and
   the closing call — and evaluate how its reasoning about the war's
   cause supports its program for peace. (RI.11-12.6, RI.11-12.8,
   RI.11-12.9; track objectives 5, 6)
5. Analyze the Bill of Rights as rhetoric (audience, purpose, negative
   phrasing, declaratory and restrictive clauses), compare how two or
   more founding texts treat a shared theme, and write a rhetorical
   analysis essay that states a precise claim, develops a counterclaim
   fairly, and concludes from its evidence. (RI.11-12.5, RI.11-12.9,
   W.11-12.1, W.11-12.9.b; track objectives 5, 8, 11)

unfinished context, not as a settled narrative).*

## Anchor texts and editions

All anchor texts are public domain. Short quotations in lessons are
checked against the editions below; learners read the full texts there.

- Declaration of Independence (adopted July 4, 1776). Text verified
  2026-10-21 at the National Archives transcription,
  <https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration-transcript>
  (transcription of the Stone Engraving; original spelling and
  punctuation).
- James Madison (as Publius), Federalist No. 10 (published in New York
  newspapers, November 1787). Text verified 2026-10-21 via the Bill of
  Rights Institute handout text:
  <https://billofrightsinstitute.org/activities/handout-a-federalist-no-10-by-james-madison-1787-civil-discourse/>
  (the unit's quotations match this text; the adult may also use the
  Library of Congress Federalist Papers collection, linked in the
  Resource Pack, and should open whichever edition is assigned and
  confirm the quotations against it).
- Abraham Lincoln, Second Inaugural Address (March 4, 1865). Text
  verified 2026-10-21 at the National Park Service,
  <https://www.nps.gov/linc/learn/historyculture/lincoln-second-inaugural.htm>
  and the National Constitution Center,
  <https://constitutioncenter.org/the-constitution/historic-document-library/detail/abraham-lincoln-second-inaugural-address-1865>.
- The Bill of Rights (first ten amendments; proposed 1789, ratified
  December 15, 1791). Text verified 2026-10-21 at the National Archives,
  <https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript>.

**A note on language and scope.** The founding texts use 18th-century
language ("all men," "mankind") and describe a political community that,
in 1776 and 1787, excluded enslaved Black people, most women, and Native
peoples from voting and office. The unit teaches both things at once: what
the texts argue, and whom their promises did not yet reach. Neither
admiration nor dismissal is the assignment — delineation and evaluation
are. Later American writers (Douglass in Unit 01, and others in Unit 03)
take up exactly this gap; the learner should be able to name it without
being told what to conclude about it.

## 20-session pacing (~50 minutes each)

| Sessions | Work | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| S1–S2 | **Lesson 01** — the rhetoric toolkit; worked examples from the Declaration's preamble | Learner labels claim/evidence/reasoning and one appeal + one device in an unseen passage |
| S3 | Workshop A — toolkit drill on an original civic passage (practice.md §A) | Toolkit table complete with a quotation for every row |
| S4–S5 | **Lesson 02** — Declaration: structure, word choice, grievances, scope | Learner maps the Declaration's four parts and writes a scope paragraph (who included/excluded) |
| S6 | Workshop B — grievance sorting: which grievances are about law, courts, armies, trade? | Every grievance in the set sorted with a one-line reason |
| S7–S8 | **Lesson 03** — Madison, Federalist No. 10: faction, cures, extended republic | Learner completes a premise map with the definition, both rejected cures, and the solution |
| S9 | Workshop C — premise-map check and the "is Madison right?" evaluation drill | Evaluation paragraph names one strength and one unproven premise |
| S10–S11 | **Lesson 04** — Lincoln's Second Inaugural: theme, purpose, features, cause and peace | Learner traces one rhetorical feature across the speech's beginning, middle, and end |
| S12 | Seminar — "Did Lincoln blame anyone?" (evidence-based discussion) | Every participant speaks at least twice with textual evidence |
| S13–S14 | **Lesson 05** — Bill of Rights as rhetoric; rhetoric across founding texts; applied image task (S14) | Learner writes a Bill of Rights rhetorical paragraph and a text-vs-image comparison (2 agreements, 2 differences, 1 inference) |
| S15 | Drafting studio — unit assessment essay (complete draft) | Complete draft with 3+ quoted passages and a counterclaim paragraph |
| S16 | Project workshop — A Public Address for Today (project.md) | Speech plan approved: question, claim, rhetorical map |
| S17 | Review — objectives 1–5 retrieval and re-teaching | Learner can delineate all four anchor arguments without notes |
| S18 | Formative quiz (assessments/week-04_quiz.md) | Scored; misses feed S19 preparation, not punishment |
| S19 | Unit assessment (assessments/unit-assessment.md) | Submitted essay + short answers |
| S20 | Reflection + project presentations; preview U03 | Written reflection; adult records project notes |

Reading load: the Declaration, Federalist No. 10, the Second Inaugural,
and the Bill of Rights are each short enough to re-read closely several
times. The adult assigns first readings the session before each lesson
and checks that the learner arrives with the text annotated, not merely
skimmed.
