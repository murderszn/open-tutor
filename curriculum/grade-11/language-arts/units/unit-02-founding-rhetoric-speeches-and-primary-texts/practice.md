## §A — Workshop A (S3): toolkit drill on an original passage

Read this original passage, written for this exercise (it is not a
historical text). A student council speaker argues for keeping the
school library open late during exam weeks:

> "We ask for one thing, and one thing only: that the library stay
> open until nine o'clock during exam weeks. The library is not a luxury;
> it is where many of us do our best work. Some of us have no quiet room
> at home. Some of us have no internet at home. Some of us have both,
> and still choose the library, because studying beside others keeps us
> honest. Will we tell those students that their work matters less
> because their homes are noisier? We are not asking the school to spend
> more; we are asking it to trust us with what it already has."

A1. Complete a toolkit table with one row each for: claim, evidence,
reasoning, ethos, pathos, logos, parallelism, repetition, antithesis
(or state that the passage has none, with a reason), rhetorical
question. Each row needs a quotation (or, for reasoning, the link in
your own words).
A2. Which appeal does the passage lean on most? Defend your choice in
3–4 sentences, and name one appeal it barely uses.
A3. What premise does the passage assume about studying "beside
others"? State it in one sentence and say whether it is proved or
assumed.
A4. Rewrite the passage's claim as a single sentence, then write one
sentence naming what the passage leaves unproved (cost, staffing,
safety, or something else).

## §B — Workshop B (S6): grievance sorting

The Declaration's grievances fall into rough families. Sort each charge
below into exactly one family — **Law**, **Courts**, **Armies**,
**Trade/Tax**, **Representation** — and add a one-line reason. (Charges
are paraphrased from the National Archives text; check wording against
the full text.)

B1. Refusing assent to wholesome and necessary laws.
B2. Dissolving representative houses repeatedly.
B3. Obstructing the administration of justice by refusing assent to
laws establishing judiciary powers.
B4. Keeping standing armies among us in peacetime without legislative
consent.
B5. Imposing taxes on us without our consent.
B6. Depriving us of trial by jury in many cases.
B7. Cutting off our trade with all parts of the world.
B8. Suspending our own legislatures and declaring themselves invested
with power to legislate for us.

## §E — Summary vs. analysis

E1. Read this summary of Federalist No. 10 and mark every sentence as
**S** (summary — reports what the text says) or **A** (analysis —
argues about what it means or how strong it is). Then rewrite one **A**
sentence as a pure **S** sentence.

> "Madison defines faction as citizens united by a passion or interest
> adverse to others' rights. His definition is fair because it does not
> target one party. He rejects destroying liberty because liberty is to
> faction what air is to fire. That analogy is his strongest move. He
> argues that an extended republic makes majority factions less likely.
> This argument still works today."

E2. Write a 4-sentence objective summary of Lincoln's Second Inaugural
(no evaluation words: no "powerful," "brilliant," "weak," "moving").
Then, in a separate paragraph, write a 4-sentence analysis of one
rhetorical feature. Label the paragraphs clearly.
