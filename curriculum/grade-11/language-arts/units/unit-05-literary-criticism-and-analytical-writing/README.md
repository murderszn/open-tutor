# Unit 05 — Literary Criticism and Analytical Writing

**Grade 11 · Language Arts · American literature, rhetoric, and research pathway · Weeks 20–23 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-11-13 for issue #52; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Units 01–04 taught the learner to read closely, to take a persuasive text
apart, to name the movement a text belongs to, and to hear how syntax and
word choice do their work. This unit asks the question those skills have
been building toward: **when several good readers disagree about a text,
what makes one reading stronger than another — and how do I build my own
reading into an essay someone else can test?**

The answer is a **critical lens**: a named set of questions a reader asks
on purpose, with named evidence that counts and named blind spots. A lens
is not a verdict and not a personality. Two honest readers using the same
lens should be able to check each other's work; two honest readers using
different lenses should be able to say exactly where their readings part
company. This unit teaches three lenses at a working level — **formalist**,
**historical/biographical**, and **reader-response** — plus a fourth,
**feminist criticism**, as a worked extension, because the anchor texts
invite it and because it shows how a lens can grow out of the questions
earlier lenses leave unanswered.

The unit runs in five moves:

1. **What a lens is** — a lens as a question-set, not an opinion; the
   three-lenses image; one original passage read three ways.
2. **The formalist lens** — the text itself: pattern, tension, and unity
   in Fitzgerald's closing pages and in Gilman's wallpaper.
3. **The historical/biographical lens** — context as evidence: Douglass
   in Rochester in 1852, and Gilman, Dr. S. Weir Mitchell, and the rest
   cure; what context can explain, and what it cannot decide.
4. **Reader-response and feminist lenses** — the reader's part in meaning
   (Hughes's "I, Too") and the questions a feminist lens asks of Gilman
   (who speaks, who is believed, who decides what counts as work).
5. **The analytical essay** — turning a lens reading into a thesis with
   integrated evidence, a fairly developed counter-reading, and a
   conclusion that follows from the evidence; drafting and revising the
   unit's finished essay.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **what does
this lens let me see that I could not see without it — and what does it
hide?**

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–04 and the week-1 diagnostic (insecure skills are re-taught
in use):

- Cite strong, thorough textual evidence for explicit and inferential
  claims, including where the text leaves matters uncertain (RL.11-12.1).
- Determine two or more themes, analyze their development and interaction,
  and write an objective summary (RL.11-12.2).
- Analyze author choices — setting, plot order, character development,
  structure, point of view — and explain their contribution to meaning and
  aesthetic impact (RL.11-12.3, RL.11-12.5, RL.11-12.6).
- Analyze word choice and syntax for impact on meaning and tone
  (RL.11-12.4, L.11-12.3.a); vary syntax deliberately for effect.
- Delineate and evaluate reasoning in seminal U.S. texts (RI.11-12.8,
  RI.11-12.9); map literary movements and classify passages by movement
  traits (U03).
- Write organized analytical paragraphs and a rhetorical analysis essay
  with a precise claim and a counterclaim (W.11-12.1, W.11-12.9.b).

The unit does **not** assume fluency with: naming and applying schools of
criticism as distinct methods; building a thesis that is *about a lens
reading* rather than about a theme; developing a counter-reading (a rival
interpretation, not merely an opposing opinion) fairly; or revising an
analytical essay against lens-specific criteria — those are this unit's
new content.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Name and apply three critical lenses — formalist, historical/
   biographical, and reader-response — stating each lens's central
   question, the evidence it treats as counting, and one thing it cannot
   see. (RL.11-12.1, RL.11-12.3; track objectives 1, 3)
2. Write a formalist analysis of a passage from *The Great Gatsby* or
   "The Yellow Wallpaper" that traces a pattern, names a tension the
   pattern creates, and judges whether the passage resolves it — each
   step supported by quotation. (RL.11-12.3, RL.11-12.4, RL.11-12.5;
   track objectives 1, 3, 4)
3. Use historical/biographical context as evidence — Douglass's 1852
   Rochester occasion; Gilman's rest-cure experience and her 1913
   account of why she wrote the story — while stating in writing what
   that context cannot decide about the text. (RL.11-12.9, RI.11-12.9;
   track objectives 1, 5)
4. Compare a reader-response reading and a feminist reading of one anchor
   text, showing how each lens changes the thesis, the evidence that
   counts, and the conclusion — and naming one question each lens leaves
   open. (RL.11-12.2, RL.11-12.6; track objectives 2, 3, 6)
5. Write an analytical essay (900–1,200 words) with a precise lens-based
   thesis, integrated quotations, a fairly developed counter-reading, and
   a conclusion that follows from the evidence; revise it against adult
   feedback and a lens-specific checklist. (W.11-12.1, W.11-12.2,
   W.11-12.5, W.11-12.9.a; track objectives 8, 9, 11)

## Vocabulary

**Unit terms** (used in lessons and assessed): *criticism, critical lens,
formalism (New Criticism), close reading, pattern, tension, unity,
historical/biographical criticism, context, anachronism, authorial intent,
reader-response criticism, implied reader, interpretive community,
feminist criticism, agency, counter-reading, analytical thesis, concession,
synthesis, evidence, warrant.*

**Reference terms** (recognition level): *New Historicism, Marxist
criticism, psychoanalytic criticism* — named in Lesson 01 only, as
lenses the learner may meet later; not assessed.

## Standards notes

Codes and short descriptions verified 2026-10-07 against the official
Common Core State Standards for grades 11–12 at
<https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/> (the track audit's
source-backed crosswalk, scope-and-sequence §5). Descriptions here are
paraphrases; this crosswalk is a planning reference, not a claim of state
adoption, accreditation, or complete alignment.

  inferences, including where the text leaves matters uncertain.
- **RL.11-12.2** — determine two or more themes/central ideas; analyze
  their development and interaction; write an objective summary.
- **RL.11-12.3** — analyze a complex set of ideas or sequence of events
  and how specific individuals, ideas, or events interact and develop
  (here: author choices in developing story elements and their
  interaction).
- **RL.11-12.4** — determine word/phrase meaning including figurative,
  connotative, and technical senses; analyze word-choice impact on
  meaning and tone.
- **RL.11-12.5** — analyze and evaluate the effectiveness of the
  structure an author uses in an exposition or argument: whether it
  makes points clear, convincing, and engaging.

  contribute to the text's power, persuasiveness, or beauty.

  century foundational American works, including how two or more texts
  from the same period treat similar themes or topics.
- **RI.11-12.9** — analyze 17th–19th-century foundational U.S. documents
  for themes, purposes, and rhetorical features.
- **W.11-12.1 (a–e)** — write arguments with precise claims distinguished
  from counterclaims, fair development of both, cohesion, formal style,
  and a conclusion following from the argument.
- **W.11-12.2 (a–f)** — write informative/explanatory texts examining
  complex ideas clearly and accurately through effective selection,
  organization, and analysis, with precise domain-specific vocabulary.
- **W.11-12.5** — strengthen writing through planning, revising, editing,
  and rewriting, focusing on what is most significant for purpose and
  audience.

  support analysis; write routinely over extended and short time frames.
- **SL.11-12.1 / SL.11-12.3** — participate in evidence-based discussion;
  evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, evidence, and rhetoric.
- **L.11-12.3.a / L.11-12.5.a** — vary syntax for effect; interpret

## Anchor texts and editions

All anchor texts are public domain and were already read in Units 01–03;
the learner re-reads them through new lenses rather than meeting new long
texts. Short quotations in lessons are checked against the editions below;
learners read the full texts there.

- Charlotte Perkins Gilman, "The Yellow Wallpaper" (written 1890;
  published in *The New England Magazine*, January 1892). Text verified
  2026-11-13 at Project Gutenberg, <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1952>
  (eBook #1952; the unit's quotations were checked against this text).
- F. Scott Fitzgerald, *The Great Gatsby* (1925). Text verified
  2026-10-14 (Unit 01) and re-checked 2026-11-13 at Project Gutenberg,
  <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317> (public domain in the USA);
  the closing line quoted in Lesson 02 was checked against this text.
- Frederick Douglass, "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" (speech,
  July 5, 1852). Full text verified 2026-10-14 (Unit 01) at
  <https://americanliterature.com/author/frederick-douglass/essay/what-to-the-slave-is-the-fourth-of-july>
  (public domain; delivered at Corinthian Hall, Rochester, NY, to the
  Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society).
- Langston Hughes, "I, Too" (1926; first published as "I, Too, Sing
  America"). Text verified 2026-11-13 at the Poetry Foundation,
  <https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poetrymagazine/poems/4753/i-too>;
  the unit's quotations match the poem as printed there. The poem is in
  the public domain.
- Emily Dickinson, "'Hope' is the thing with feathers —" (poem 314,
  c. 1861). Text verified 2026-10-14 (Unit 01) at the Poetry Foundation,
  <https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/42889/hope-is-the-thing-with-feathers-314>
  (page notes the poem is public domain).
- Charlotte Perkins Gilman, "Why I Wrote 'The Yellow Wallpaper'" (*The
  Forerunner*, October 1913). Gilman's own account of her rest-cure
  treatment under Dr. S. Weir Mitchell in 1887 and her stated purpose for
  the story ("to save people from being driven crazy"). Historical facts
  in Lesson 03 were checked 2026-11-13 against Gilman's 1913 account and
  the Penguin Classics biographical note: Mitchell prescribed total rest,
  limited intellectual life ("but two hours' intellectual life a day"),
  and "never touch pen, brush or pencil as long as you live"; Gilman
  followed the prescription for about three months and later wrote that
  she "came so near the border line of utter mental ruin that I could see
  over." Her later claim that Mitchell changed his methods after reading
  the story is disputed by historians (Julie Bates Dock) and is taught
  in the lesson as a contested claim, not a settled fact.

**A note on language and scope.** Gilman's story is narrated by a woman
undergoing a 19th-century "rest cure" for what would now be recognized as
postpartum depression; the lessons name the historical diagnosis
("nervous prostration," "temporary nervous depression") and the modern
understanding side by side, without diagnosing the fictional narrator.
Douglass's speech and Hughes's poem are read as literature and as public
argument; the unit teaches what each text claims and what each lens can
and cannot establish about it. Neither admiration nor dismissal is the
assignment — delineation and evaluation are.

## 20-session pacing (~50 minutes each)

| Sessions | Work | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| S1–S2 | **Lesson 01** — what a critical lens is; the three-lenses image task; one original passage read three ways | Learner states each lens's question, evidence, and blind spot, and applies all three to one passage |
| S3 | Workshop A — lens sorting: match 8 critic's questions to their lens, with a one-line reason each | Lens table complete with a correct lens and reason for every row |
| S4–S5 | **Lesson 02** — the formalist lens: pattern, tension, unity in *Gatsby* and Gilman | Learner traces one pattern across a passage, names its tension, and judges its resolution with quotations |
| S6 | Workshop B — formalist drill: the wallpaper pattern in three passages (practice.md §B) | Pattern–tension–unity table complete for all three passages |
| S7–S8 | **Lesson 03** — the historical/biographical lens: Douglass 1852; Gilman and the rest cure | Learner writes one context paragraph that uses a dated fact as evidence and states what it cannot decide |
| S9 | Workshop C — context check: which claims need a date, and which do not? (practice.md §C) | Every claim sorted correctly with a one-line reason |
| S10–S11 | **Lesson 04** — reader-response and feminist lenses: Hughes's "I, Too"; Gilman reread | Learner writes two thesis sentences on the same text, one per lens, with different evidence for each |
| S12 | Seminar — "Whose reading counts?" (evidence-based discussion) | Every participant speaks at least twice with textual evidence |
| S13–S14 | **Lesson 05** — the analytical essay: thesis, evidence integration, counter-reading, revision | Learner produces a full essay plan: thesis, 3 evidence blocks, counter-reading, conclusion |
| S15 | Drafting studio — unit assessment essay (complete draft) | Complete draft (900–1,200 words) with 3+ integrated quotations and a counter-reading paragraph |
| S16 | Project workshop — Two Lenses, One Text (project.md) | Project plan approved: text, two lenses, thesis per lens |
| S17 | Review — objectives 1–5 retrieval and re-teaching | Learner can state all three lenses and apply each to a new short passage without notes |
| S18 | Formative quiz (assessments/week-04_quiz.md) | Scored; misses feed S19 preparation, not punishment |
| S19 | Unit assessment (assessments/unit-assessment.md) | Submitted essay + short answers |
| S20 | Reflection + project presentations; preview U06 | Written reflection; adult records project notes |

Reading load: no new long text. Re-reading is the work: Gilman's story
(about 6,000 words) twice through different lenses, the closing pages of
*Gatsby*, the Douglass excerpt, and Hughes's "I, Too." The adult assigns
the re-reading the session before each lesson and checks that the learner
arrives with the text annotated for the lens in use, not merely re-skimmed.
