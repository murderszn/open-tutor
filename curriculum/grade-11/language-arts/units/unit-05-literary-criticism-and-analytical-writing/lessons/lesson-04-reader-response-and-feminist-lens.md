# Lesson 04 — Reader-Response and Feminist Lenses

**Unit 05 · Sessions S10–S11 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RL.11-12.2, RL.11-12.6

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can write two different thesis sentences
about the same text — one from a reader-response lens and one from a
feminist lens — with different evidence for each, and name one question
each lens leaves open.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, a copy of Hughes's "I, Too" (Poetry Foundation),
a copy of "The Yellow Wallpaper," and the Lesson 01 lens table (kept as
a bookmark).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. In your own words: what does the reader-response lens look *at*, and
   what does it deliberately *set aside*? What about the feminist lens —
   what question does it add that the first three lenses do not ask?
2. Write one sentence about a poem or story that hit you differently on
   a second reading. What changed — the text, or you?

(Adult: question 2 is the reader-response thesis in miniature — meaning
changes when the reader changes. The lesson's discipline is to keep that
change tied to quotations.)

## Explicit teaching

### Reader-response: the reader helps make the meaning

**Reader-response criticism** holds two beliefs: the reader's role
cannot be left out of our understanding of literature, and readers do
not passively consume a meaning that sits ready-made on the page — they
actively make the meaning they find. The lens's tools are:

- **Gaps** — what the text does not say, which the reader must fill
  (who bought Mara's house? why did the sea stop mattering?).
- **The implied reader** — the reader the text seems written for: the
  person who would catch its references, fill its gaps as intended, and
  feel its effects as designed.
- **Interpretive communities** — groups of readers who share ways of
  reading (a classroom, a generation, a profession) and therefore
  produce similar readings — and different communities produce
  different readings of the same text.

The check that keeps reader-response from becoming "anything goes" is
the same as every other lens: **quote the text**. A reader's response is
evidence about the reading, not a substitute for it. "This line made me
angry" is a report; "this line made me angry because the narrator is
told not to think, and the line before it shows her thinking clearly"
is criticism.

### Feminist criticism: who speaks, who is believed

**Feminist criticism** asks how a text represents gender — who speaks,
who is believed, who does the work, whose experience the text treats as
normal, and what happens to women who step outside the roles assigned
to them. Its evidence is the same material the other lenses use —
word choice, structure, point of view — read with one additional
question: *what does this text assume about women and men, and what
does it do with that assumption?* A feminist reading can admire a text
and criticize it at once; it can also show how a text written long ago
exposes the very assumptions its own time held. The lens's blind spot:
used carelessly, it can reduce every text to a single question and miss
what else the text is doing. Used well, it notices what the other
lenses walk past.

### Two lenses, one text — the method

When two lenses read the same text, three things change: the **thesis**
(what is being claimed), the **evidence** (which passages count), and
the **conclusion** (what follows). The text does not change. That is the
point of the comparison, and it is the skill the unit assessment asks
for: hold the text steady, change the lens, and report exactly what
moves.

## Worked example 1 — Hughes's "I, Too" through a reader-response lens

Read the whole poem (18 lines; Poetry Foundation). It opens and closes
with a claim about belonging:

> "I, too, sing America.
> I am the darker brother.
> They send me to eat in the kitchen
> When company comes,
> But I laugh,
> And eat well,
> And grow strong.
> Tomorrow,
> I'll be at the table
> When company comes."

And it ends:

> "Besides,
> They'll see how beautiful I am
> And be ashamed —
> I, too, am America."

**Gaps the reader fills.** The poem never names who "they" are, never
says where "the table" is, and never explains what "tomorrow" means —
a day, a generation, a future America. The reader must fill those gaps,
and different readers fill them differently: a reader who has been sent
out of a room fills "the kitchen" with a real room; a reader who has
not fills it with an idea. Both are reading the same lines.

**The implied reader.** The poem seems written for a reader who knows
Whitman's "I Hear America Singing" — the "too" in the first line only
makes full sense as an answer to Whitman — and for a reader willing to
be addressed as "company." The poem's turn from "I, too, sing America"
to "I, too, *am* America" asks the reader to move from hearing a song
to recognizing a person. A reader who does not make that move has not
misread the poem's words; they have stopped one line short of its
invitation.

**Thesis (reader-response):** "Hughes's 'I, Too' makes its meaning in
the reader's filling of its gaps: 'tomorrow' means something different
to every reader, and the poem's final claim — 'I, too, am America' —
only lands when the reader accepts the invitation to be the 'company'
that must learn to see."

**What the lens leaves open.** It cannot say whether the poem's hope is
justified by history — that is a historical question — or whether its
form is unified — that is a formalist question. It can only say what
the poem asks its reader to do.

## Worked example 2 — Gilman through a feminist lens

Read the story's opening again, with the feminist question in mind —
*who is believed?*

> "John laughs at me, of course, but one expects that in marriage."
>
> "John is practical in the extreme. He has no patience with faith, an
> intense horror of superstition, and he scoffs openly at any talk of
> things not to be felt and seen and put down in figures."
>
> "You see, he does not believe I am sick!"
>
> "And what can one do?"

**Who speaks.** The narrator speaks — but only to "dead paper," in
secret, because her husband "hates to have me write a word." The story's
form (a secret journal) is itself the evidence: the one person who knows
her condition is forbidden to record it. The feminist lens notices what
the formalist lens also sees — the pattern of secrecy — and asks the
additional question: *who benefits from her silence?*

**Who is believed.** John, "a physician, and also of high standing,"
is believed by everyone: "If a physician of high standing, and one's
own husband, assures friends and relatives that there is really nothing
the matter with one but temporary nervous depression — a slight
hysterical tendency — what is one to do?" Her brother, "also a physician,
and also of high standing… says the same thing." Two men with standing
outweigh one woman with experience. The story's final line is the
reversal of that arrangement:

> "I've got out at last," said I, "in spite of you and Jane!"

**Thesis (feminist):** "Gilman's story shows a woman whose knowledge of
her own condition is dismissed because it is not the knowledge of
physicians, and whose final escape is not a cure but a refusal: she
gets out by ceasing to ask to be believed."

**What the lens leaves open.** It cannot decide whether the story's
power comes from its form or its argument — that needs the formalist
lens — and it cannot settle how much of the story is Gilman's own
experience — that needs the historical lens, with its limits (Lesson
03). It can show what the story assumes about whose voice counts, and
what happens when that assumption breaks.

## Guided practice

Work in the notebook. The adult checks each item before you move on.

1. In Worked Example 1, name one gap the reader must fill in "I, Too"
   and write two different ways two different readers might fill it.
2. In Worked Example 2, find the sentence that best supports the claim
   "the narrator is forbidden to record what she knows," and copy it
   exactly.
3. Write one sentence explaining why "the feminist lens proves Gilman
   hated all men" is a misuse of the lens. (Hint: what does the lens
   ask, and what would it need to prove that claim?)

## Independent practice

4. Write two thesis sentences about "The Yellow Wallpaper" — one
   reader-response, one feminist — on the same aspect of the story
   (choose: the wallpaper, the writing, or the ending). Under each
   thesis, list two quotations that count as evidence for *that*
   thesis. Then write one sentence naming what each thesis leaves open.
5. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Reader-response means my opinion
   is just as good as the teacher's, because meaning is whatever the
   reader thinks." Write a three-sentence correction: (i) what the lens
   actually claims, (ii) what still counts as evidence, (iii) one way a
   reader-response reading can be shown to be weak.

## Application — the seminar preparation

Prepare for the S12 seminar ("Whose reading counts?") by writing a
half-page position (8–10 sentences) that does all three of these things:

1. States which of the four lenses you trust most for "The Yellow
   Wallpaper" and why, with one quotation.
2. States which lens you trust least for that story and why, with one
   quotation.
3. Names one question about the story that *no* lens in this unit
   answers well — and says what kind of evidence that question would
   need.

## Exit check

In the notebook, without looking back:

1. Define *implied reader* and *interpretive community* in one sentence
   each.
2. What question does feminist criticism add that the first three lenses
   do not ask?
3. One sentence: when two lenses read the same text, what three things
   change — and what stays the same?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the two worked-example theses may be kept
  as models; the adult may read "I, Too" aloud twice — once flat, once
  with the turn at "Tomorrow" stressed — and the learner notes what
  changes.
- **Alternate response mode:** the two thesis sentences (Independent
  practice 4) may be built as a two-column table first (lens | thesis |
  evidence) and then written out.
- **Extension:** apply the reader-response lens to the final line of
  *Gatsby* ("So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back
  ceaselessly into the past."): write one paragraph on how the line
  lands differently for a reader who has just finished the novel and a
  reader meeting the line alone, with one quotation from the closing
  paragraph as the check.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) reader-response as "anything goes" — the
correction is always a quotation, and Independent practice 5 exists for
exactly this; (2) feminist criticism as a verdict on the author's
character — redirect to the text's assumptions and arrangements, not
the author's morals; (3) treating the four lenses as four opinions to
choose between, rather than four methods that can be combined — the
project (Two Lenses, One Text) rewards combination. Note: Hughes's poem
is sometimes titled "I, Too, Sing America"; the Poetry Foundation text
used here is titled "I, Too." Answer-key references: guided practice
§1–3, independent practice §4–5, application §1–3.
