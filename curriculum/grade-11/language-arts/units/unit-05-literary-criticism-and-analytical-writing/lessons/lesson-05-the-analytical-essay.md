# Lesson 05 — The Analytical Essay: Thesis, Evidence, Counter-Reading, Revision

**Unit 05 · Sessions S13–S14 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** W.11-12.1, W.11-12.2, W.11-12.5, W.11-12.9.a

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can write a full plan for an analytical
essay — a precise lens-based thesis, three evidence blocks with
integrated quotations, a fairly developed counter-reading, and a
conclusion that follows from the evidence — and revise a draft against a
lens-specific checklist.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, the learner's Lesson 01–04 work (especially the
two thesis sentences from Lesson 04), and the unit assessment prompt
(`../assessments/unit-assessment.md` — read it now, before drafting).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. Copy your best thesis sentence from Lesson 04. In one sentence, say
   what lens it uses and what evidence it needs.
2. What is the difference between a *theme* ("the story is about
   freedom") and a *thesis* ("the story shows freedom as…")? Write one
   example of each about "The Yellow Wallpaper."

(Adult: question 2 is the lesson's hinge — a thesis must be arguable,
specific, and tied to a lens. "About freedom" is a topic; "shows
freedom as a refusal to be believed" is a claim someone could dispute
with evidence.)

## Explicit teaching

### What an analytical thesis is

An **analytical thesis** is one or two sentences that state what the
text does, how it does it, and why it matters — through a named lens.
It has four parts:

1. **The lens** — named, not implied ("Through a feminist lens…").
2. **The claim** — arguable, specific, and falsifiable (a reader could
   disagree with evidence, not merely taste).
3. **The how** — the mechanism: pattern, structure, point of view,
   image, context.
4. **The so-what** — why the claim matters for understanding the text.

Weak: "Gilman's story is about a woman who goes crazy." (No lens, no
mechanism, and "goes crazy" is a summary, not a claim.)
Strong: "Through a feminist lens, 'The Yellow Wallpaper' shows a
woman's knowledge being dismissed because it is not a physician's
knowledge: the story's secret-journal form makes her the only witness
to her own condition, and her final escape is a refusal to ask to be
believed." (Lens, claim, mechanism — the secret-journal form — and
so-what: the ending as refusal, not cure.)

### Integrating quotations: the three jobs of every quotation

A quotation in an analytical essay must do three jobs, and the
learner checks each one:

1. **Introduce** — set the quotation up with a claim sentence, so the
   reader knows what to listen for. Never drop a quotation in cold.
2. **Quote** — copy exactly, punctuate correctly, and keep it short
   (a phrase or a sentence, rarely more).
3. **Explain** — say what the quotation *shows*, in the lens's terms.
   The explanation is the analysis; the quotation alone is evidence,
   not argument.

### The counter-reading: the essay's honesty test

A **counter-reading** is a rival interpretation of the same text,
developed fairly — not a straw reader to knock down. It answers the
question: *what would a good reader using a different lens (or the same
lens differently) say, and where is my reading vulnerable?* The essay
must do three things with it: state it accurately, give it its best
evidence, and answer it — by concession ("that reading is right about
X, and my thesis still holds because…") or by refutation ("that reading
misses this quotation…"). An essay without a counter-reading is a
speech; with one, it is an argument. (This is the skill Unit 02's
rhetorical analysis essay introduced as *counterclaim*; here the
counter is an *interpretation*, not merely an opposing opinion.)

### Revision: what matters most for purpose and audience

Revision is not proofreading. Proofreading fixes commas; revision
re-thinks the argument. The unit's revision checklist (also in the
teacher guide) asks, in order:

1. Does the thesis name a lens and make an arguable claim?
2. Does every body paragraph open with a claim and close with an
   explanation of its quotation?
3. Is the counter-reading stated fairly, with its best evidence?
4. Does the conclusion follow from the evidence — or does it introduce
   a new claim?
5. Only then: syntax, word choice, citation format (Unit 04's
   stylistic tools).

## Worked example 1 — from thesis sentences to an essay plan

Take the feminist thesis from Lesson 04:

> "Gilman's story shows a woman whose knowledge of her own condition is
> dismissed because it is not the knowledge of physicians, and whose
> final escape is not a cure but a refusal: she gets out by ceasing to
> ask to be believed."

Turn it into a plan:

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Thesis | As above — lens named, claim arguable, mechanism (secret-journal form; dismissal by physicians), so-what (escape as refusal). |
| Evidence block 1 — who is believed | "If a physician of high standing, and one's own husband, assures friends and relatives that there is really nothing the matter with one but temporary nervous depression — a slight hysterical tendency — what is one to do?" Explanation: two sources of standing (physician, husband) against one woman's experience; the rhetorical question performs her lack of options. |
| Evidence block 2 — the secret form | "John hates to have me write a word." / "this is dead paper and a great relief to my mind." Explanation: the journal exists because speech is forbidden; the story's form *is* the silencing. |
| Evidence block 3 — the ending as refusal | "I've got out at last," said I, "in spite of you and Jane!" Explanation: "in spite of" replaces asking to be believed with acting without permission; the escape is not a cure (she is not well) but a refusal. |
| Counter-reading | A formalist reader could say the ending is a collapse, not a refusal: the narrator's mind breaks, and the wallpaper wins. Its best evidence: the pattern of pursuit-and-collapse (Lesson 02) ends in the narrator creeping around the room. Answer by concession: the reading is right that her mind breaks — and the feminist claim still holds, because the *form* of the break (getting out, speaking to John directly for the first time) is a refusal even inside the collapse. |
| Conclusion | Restate the thesis in the light of the evidence; do not introduce a new claim (e.g., do not suddenly argue about modern medicine — that belongs in a different essay). |

## Worked example 2 — a weak plan, repaired

A learner's first plan:

- Thesis: "The wallpaper is a symbol of the narrator's mind."
- Evidence: three quotations describing the wallpaper.
- Counter-reading: "Some people might disagree."
- Conclusion: "So the story is very powerful."

Repair, step by step:

1. **Thesis:** add a lens and a mechanism — "Through a formalist lens,
   the wallpaper's pattern of pursuit-and-collapse mirrors the
   narrator's mind: each time she follows a line, it breaks, and the
   story's unity is in that mirroring." Now a reader can disagree with
   evidence.
2. **Evidence:** each quotation needs a claim sentence and an
   explanation — not three descriptions in a row. One block per
   *move* of the pattern (following, breaking, returning), not one
   block per quotation.
3. **Counter-reading:** name the rival lens and its best evidence —
   "A historical reader could say the wallpaper matters because the
   rest cure made the room her whole world" — then answer it.
4. **Conclusion:** follow from the evidence: the pattern's unity is the
   story's achievement, not a restatement that the story is powerful.

In the notebook, without looking back:

2. What three jobs must every quotation do?
3. What is the difference between a counter-reading and a straw reader?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the essay-plan table may be filled in
  before any sentences are written; the adult may model one evidence
  block aloud while the learner fills the next.
- **Alternate response mode:** the full essay plan (Independent
  practice 4) may be dictated and then transcribed; the revision
  (Application) may be done by cutting the paragraph into strips —
  claim, quotation, explanation — and reordering them.
- **Extension:** write a second counter-reading for your own essay plan
  from a *third* lens, and decide in two sentences whether to include
  it. (A strong essay often mentions a second rival briefly and sets it
  aside with a reason; a crowded essay tries to answer everyone.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) treating the counter-reading as a paragraph
to defeat rather than a rival to answer — the test is whether the rival
reader would recognize their own position in the summary; (2) writing
the conclusion as a new argument (the Mitchell example in Independent
practice 5 is the classic case); (3) mistaking quotation for analysis —
a paragraph that is mostly quotation with "this shows" attached has not
explained anything. The S15 drafting studio exists to catch these
before S19. Answer-key references: guided practice §1–3, independent
practice §4–5, application.
