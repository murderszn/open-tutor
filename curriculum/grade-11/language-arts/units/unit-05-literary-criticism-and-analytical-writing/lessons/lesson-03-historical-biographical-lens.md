# Lesson 03 — The Historical / Biographical Lens: Context as Evidence

**Unit 05 · Sessions S7–S8 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RL.11-12.9, RI.11-12.9

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can use a dated fact — a speech's occasion,
a writer's treatment, a stated purpose — as evidence about a text, state
exactly what that fact explains, and state in writing what it cannot
decide.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, a copy of Douglass's "What to the Slave Is the
Fourth of July?" (excerpt, as in Unit 01), a copy of "The Yellow
Wallpaper," and Gilman's "Why I Wrote 'The Yellow Wallpaper'" (1913) as
summarized in the unit README — the adult reads the summary aloud with
the learner and checks it against the lesson's quotations.

## Explicit teaching

### Context is evidence — with rules

**Historical/biographical criticism** asks what a text meant in its own
time and how the author's life and world shaped it. Its evidence is
dated and checkable: publication dates, letters, speeches, laws,
medical practice, a writer's stated purpose. Three rules govern its use:

1. **A date is a claim.** "Douglass spoke in 1852" is checkable; "in
   those days" is not evidence. Always give the date, the place, and
   the source.
2. **Intention is evidence, not a verdict.** What an author said they
   meant is one piece of evidence about a text. It does not override
   what the text actually does — authors misremember, change their
   minds, and write better (or worse) than they intended.
3. **Context explains; it does not excuse or convict.** Knowing the
   rest cure was standard medicine in 1887 explains why Gilman's
   narrator is treated as she is. It does not decide whether the story
   is "about" medicine, or whether the story is good. Those are
   different questions, answered with different evidence.

The lens's blind spot is the mirror image of formalism's: by looking
*around* the text, it can under-read the text itself. A historical
reading that never quotes the story's sentences is a history essay
wearing a literature costume.

### Anachronism: the lens's cardinal error

**Anachronism** is judging a past text by a later standard as if the
later standard had been available to the writer — for example, faulting
Gilman for not using the modern term "postpartum depression," or
faulting Douglass for not citing statistics no one had collected. The
historical lens prevents anachronism by asking what the writer *could*
have known and what their audience *would* have heard. It does not
forbid the modern reader from judging; it forbids the modern reader
from pretending the judgment is the writer's.

## Worked example 1 — Douglass in Rochester, July 5, 1852

The facts (verified in Unit 01): Frederick Douglass delivered "What to
the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" on July 5, 1852, at Corinthian Hall in
Rochester, New York, to the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society — the
day *after* Independence Day, to an audience of white abolitionists who
had invited him to celebrate the Fourth. The speech asks, in its most
famous line:

> "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?"

**What the context explains.** The date explains the speech's central
move. Douglass is not speaking *on* the Fourth but *about* it, the day
after, to people who have just celebrated it. That is why the speech's
opening is courteous and its turn is devastating: he first accepts the
invitation and praises the founders, then asks what their celebration
can possibly mean to an enslaved person. The audience matters too: he
is speaking to allies, not enemies, which is why the speech can accuse
without breaking the room — "your" Fourth of July, not "our" Fourth.

**What the context cannot decide.** The date cannot decide whether the
speech is well argued. That is a formalist and rhetorical question,
answered by the speech's structure and reasoning (Unit 02). The date
also cannot decide what Douglass "really" felt; the speech is a public
performance, and the historical lens treats it as one. A learner who
writes "Douglass was angry, so the speech is angry" has confused a
biographical guess with textual evidence. The correct sentence is:
"Douglass delivered the speech the day after the Fourth to an
abolitionist audience, and the speech's turn from praise to accusation
uses that occasion" — date, audience, and a quotation, each doing its
own work.

## Worked example 2 — Gilman, Dr. Mitchell, and the rest cure

The facts (checked 2026-11-13 against Gilman's 1913 account, "Why I
Wrote 'The Yellow Wallpaper,'" and the Penguin Classics biographical
note): after the birth of her daughter in 1885, Gilman suffered severe
depression. In 1887 she placed herself under the care of Dr. S. Weir
Mitchell, a leading nerve specialist, who prescribed his "rest cure":
total rest, isolation from family and familiar surroundings, limited
intellectual life — "Have but two hours' intellectual life a day. And
never touch pen, brush or pencil as long as you live." Gilman followed
the prescription for about three months and later wrote that she "came
so near the border line of utter mental ruin that I could see over."
She wrote "The Yellow Wallpaper" in 1890; it was published in January
1892. In 1913 she wrote that she devised the story "to save people
from being driven crazy," and sent Mitchell a copy.

Now read the story's opening against that history:

> "John is practical in the extreme. He has no patience with faith, an
> intense horror of superstition, and he scoffs openly at any talk of
> things not to be felt and seen and put down in figures."
>
> "John is a physician, and perhaps — (I would not say it to a living
> soul, of course, but this is dead paper and a great relief to my
> mind) — perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster."
>
> "Personally, I believe that congenial work, with excitement and change,
> would do me good."

**What the context explains.** The rest cure explains the story's
central situation with precision: a woman forbidden to work, forbidden
to write, told that thinking about her condition makes it worse ("John
says the very worst thing I can do is to think about my condition"),
prescribed "phosphates or phosphites… and tonics, and journeys, and air,
and exercise." The story's John is a physician-husband whose authority
rests on medicine, exactly as Mitchell's did. The context turns a
puzzling detail — why is writing forbidden? — into a documented medical
instruction of the period.

**What the context cannot decide.** Three limits, each worth naming:

1. **The story is not a diary.** Gilman wrote in 1913 that she never
   had hallucinations or objections to her wallpaper; the story's
   ending is invented. A historical reading that treats the narrator
   as Gilman mistakes fiction for autobiography.
2. **The author's stated purpose is one witness, not the judge.**
   Gilman said she wrote to save people from being driven crazy. That
   is good evidence about her intention. It does not settle what the
   story *does* — a reader may find the story's power in its form, not
   its purpose (Lesson 02), or in its effect on a reader (Lesson 04).
3. **A later claim needs its own check.** Gilman later claimed Mitchell
   changed his treatment after reading the story. Historians (Julie
   Bates Dock) have questioned that claim, noting Mitchell continued to
   promote rest-cure practice afterward. The lesson: a historical claim
   is only as good as its source, and a writer's later memory is a
   source to check, not a fact to repeat.

## Guided practice

Work in the notebook. The adult checks each item before you move on.

1. Write the three rules of using context as evidence (from Explicit
   teaching) in your own words, one sentence each.
2. For Worked Example 1, write one sentence that uses the date correctly
   ("Douglass spoke on July 5, 1852, the day after the Fourth, to…")
   and one sentence that misuses it ("Douglass was angry because…").
   Label which is which and say why.
3. In Worked Example 2, which of the three limits (diary, intention,
   later claim) applies to this sentence: "Gilman proves the rest cure
   was bad medicine because her narrator goes mad"? Explain in two
   sentences.

## Independent practice

4. Write one context paragraph (6–8 sentences) on *either* Douglass or
   Gilman that does all three of these things: (a) states a dated fact
   with its source, (b) quotes the text, (c) ends with a sentence
   stating what the fact cannot decide. Underline the sentence that
   states the limit.
5. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Gilman wrote 'The Yellow
   Wallpaper' in 1890, so the narrator's wallpaper must be the wallpaper
   in Gilman's own house." Write a three-sentence correction: what is
   wrong with this sentence, what evidence the historical lens would
   actually need for such a claim, and what the 1890 date *does*
   establish.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the three rules may be kept as a bookmark;
   the adult may read the Gilman quotations aloud while the learner
   follows the rest-cure facts in a dated timeline.
- **Alternate response mode:** the context paragraph (Independent
  practice 4) may be built as a three-row table first (fact + source |
  quotation | limit) and then turned into sentences.
- **Extension:** research check — using the Resource Pack, find one
  additional dated fact about the publication of "The Yellow Wallpaper"
  (magazine, month, year) and write two sentences: what it explains
  about the story's first readers, and what it cannot decide.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) treating biography as a skeleton key —
"the story means X because Gilman's life was Y" — correct by requiring
a quotation for every biographical claim; (2) treating the author's
stated purpose as the final word on meaning — Gilman's 1913 account is
evidence about intention, and the lesson's third limit is the corrective;
(3) presentism — judging 1887 medicine by today's standards without
naming the standard being used. Note the spelling: the story's title is
"The Yellow Wallpaper" (American spelling) in the Gutenberg text; the
Penguin volume uses "The Yellow Wall-Paper." Answer-key references:
guided practice §1–3, independent practice §4–5, application §1–5.
