# Lesson 02 — The Formalist Lens: Pattern, Tension, Unity

**Unit 05 · Sessions S4–S5 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** RL.11-12.3, RL.11-12.4, RL.11-12.5

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can trace a pattern through a passage,
name the tension the pattern creates, judge whether the passage resolves
that tension, and write a formalist paragraph that proves each step with
quotation — using nothing outside the text.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, a copy of the closing pages of *The Great Gatsby*
(final chapter; Project Gutenberg eBook #64317), and a copy of "The
Yellow Wallpaper" (Project Gutenberg eBook #1952). Re-read both passages
below before the lesson; the adult checks that the learner arrives with
the passages annotated, not merely skimmed.

## Explicit teaching

### Formalism in one paragraph

**Formalism** (also called New Criticism) treats each work as its own
distinct piece, free from its environment, era, and even its author. The
formalist assumes that the keys to understanding a text exist *within the
text itself*: its language, its structure, its images, its tensions.
Three words carry the whole method:

- **Pattern** — something that repeats with a difference: a word, an
  image, a sentence shape, a color, a contrast. A pattern is not a
  coincidence; it is the text telling you what it cares about.
- **Tension** — the push and pull a pattern creates: two ideas, images,
  or values that do not sit comfortably together (past and future;
  right and going; confinement and freedom).
- **Unity** — whether the text resolves the tension, holds it open, or
  lets it stand unresolved — and whether that resolution (or refusal)
  is earned by the pattern or merely asserted.

A formalist paragraph does three things in order: *names the pattern,
shows the tension, judges the unity* — each with a quotation. If any of
the three steps has no quotation, it is an opinion, not an analysis.

### What formalism refuses — and why that is a strength

A formalist does not ask what Fitzgerald intended, what 1925 was like,
or how the ending makes you feel. That refusal is deliberate: it forces
the reading to be checkable by anyone holding the same book. Its cost is
real — a formalist reading of Douglass's speech cannot use the fact that
it was delivered on July 5, 1852, to an abolitionist audience — and the
honest formalist says so. Lesson 03 will show what the refused material
adds. For now, the discipline is the point: *only the words on the page*.

## Worked example 1 — the last line of *Gatsby*

Read the closing paragraph of *The Great Gatsby* (Chapter 9, final
paragraph). Fitzgerald's narrator, Nick Carraway, has been reflecting on
Gatsby's belief in the green light, "the orgastic future that year by
year recedes before us." The novel's final sentence is:

> "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into
> the past."

**Pattern.** The sentence is built on opposed motions. *Beat on* (rowing
forward, effort, will) sits against *borne back* (being carried
backward, passively, by the current). *Current* pulls one way; *past*
pulls the other. The same opposition runs through the paragraph: the
green light is ahead of us, "tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out
our arms farther," and yet it "recedes before us" — every forward motion
is answered by a backward one. The pattern is *effort against drift*.

**Tension.** The sentence refuses to choose. "We beat on" is active and
present; "borne back" is passive and continuous ("ceaselessly"). The
reader is asked to hold both at once: striving is real, and so is being
carried backward. A weaker ending would resolve this — "but we kept
going" or "and so we failed." Fitzgerald does neither.

**Unity.** The ending is unified precisely because it does not resolve
the tension: the novel's whole design is a man trying to repeat the past
and a narrator learning that the past cannot be repeated, and the final
sentence makes that design into a single image. The unity is in the
*holding* of the tension, not in its solution. A formalist judges that
as earned, because the pattern (effort vs. drift) has been built across
the whole novel — the green light, the boats, the receding future — and
the last sentence gathers it rather than introducing something new.

**What this reading does not do.** It does not say Fitzgerald meant the
American Dream to fail, or that 1925 America was hollow. Those are
historical claims, and this lens has set them aside. The claim here is
narrower and checkable: *the sentence's opposed motions enact the
novel's central tension*.

## Worked example 2 — the wallpaper's pattern in Gilman

Read the narrator's description of the wallpaper early in "The Yellow
Wallpaper" (Project Gutenberg #1952):

> "I never saw a worse paper in my life. One of those sprawling
> flamboyant patterns committing every artistic sin."
>
> "It is dull enough to confuse the eye in following, pronounced enough
> to constantly irritate, and provoke study, and when you follow the
> lame, uncertain curves for a little distance they suddenly commit
> suicide — plunge off at outrageous angles, destroy themselves in
> unheard-of contradictions."
>
> "The color is repellant, almost revolting; a smouldering, unclean
> yellow, strangely faded by the slow-turning sunlight."

**Pattern.** The description repeats one move: *following a line that
breaks*. The eye follows "lame, uncertain curves," and the curves
"plunge off at outrageous angles" and "destroy themselves." The pattern
is pursuit-and-collapse, and it repeats later in the story: the narrator
follows the sub-pattern, the "recurrent spot where the pattern lolls
like a broken neck and two bulbous eyes stare at you upside-down," and
again the looking does not settle.

**Tension.** The wallpaper is described as both *dull* and
*pronounced* — "dull enough to confuse the eye in following, pronounced
enough to constantly irritate." Something cannot be boring and
aggressive at once, yet the narrator insists it is both. The tension is
between a pattern that invites study ("provoke study") and a pattern
that defeats it ("destroy themselves in unheard-of contradictions").

**Unity.** The passage is unified with the story's design: a narrator
forbidden to work, forbidden to write, is given one thing she *may*
study — the paper — and the paper rewards study with collapse. The
wallpaper's pattern and the narrator's situation mirror each other: both
are a mind following a line that will not hold. The formalist does not
need to know anything about the rest cure to see this; the mirror is
inside the text. (Lesson 03 will ask what the rest cure adds — and what
it cannot prove.)

## Guided practice

Work in the notebook. The adult checks each item before you move on.

1. In Worked Example 1, which two words carry the opposed motions of
   the final sentence? Write them down with the direction each one
   moves (forward/backward, active/passive).
2. In Worked Example 2, find one more repetition of the
   *pursuit-and-collapse* pattern in this sentence from later in the
   story: "Up and down and sideways they crawl, and those absurd,
   unblinking eyes are everywhere." Explain in two sentences how it
   fits the pattern.
3. A classmate says the *Gatsby* ending is "about how the American
   Dream is dead." Rewrite that as a formalist claim in one sentence:
   keep the claim about the text, drop the historical judgment, and
   name the pattern that supports it.

## Independent practice

4. Choose **one** of these two passages and write a formalist paragraph
   (6–9 sentences) that names the pattern, shows the tension, and
   judges the unity, with at least two quotations.
   - (a) *Gatsby*: "Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgastic
     future that year by year recedes before us. It eluded us then, but
     that's no matter — tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our
     arms farther. . . . And one fine morning —"
   - (b) Gilman: "The wallpaper, as I said before, is torn off in spots,
     and it sticketh closer than a brother — they must have had
     perseverance as well as hatred."
5. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "The wallpaper is yellow because
   yellow meant sickness in the 1890s, which proves Gilman wanted to
   criticize doctors." Write a three-sentence correction: (i) what the
   formalist lens would accept in this sentence, (ii) what it would
   refuse and why, (iii) what lens the refused part belongs to.

## Application — pattern hunting in a new passage

Re-read the opening of "The Yellow Wallpaper" (Project Gutenberg #1952,
first four paragraphs). Find and write down:

1. One repeated word or image in the opening, with its locations.
2. One tension the opening sets up (two things that do not sit
   comfortably together).
3. One sentence predicting — from the pattern alone, without using
   anything you know about the story's ending — what the pattern might
   do by the story's end. (You may be wrong; the adult checks whether
   the prediction follows from the pattern, not whether it matches the
   ending.)

## Exit check

In the notebook, without looking back:

1. Define *pattern*, *tension*, and *unity* in one sentence each.
2. What three things must a formalist paragraph do, in order?
3. One sentence: what does formalism deliberately refuse to use?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the pattern–tension–unity frame may be
  kept as a three-column table; the adult may read the *Gatsby* closing
  aloud while the learner follows the opposed-motion words.
- **Alternate response mode:** the formalist paragraph (Independent
  practice 4) may be built as a table first (pattern | tension | unity,
  with a quotation in each row) and then turned into sentences.
- **Extension:** apply the formalist method to Dickinson's "'Hope' is
  the thing with feathers —": trace the bird pattern across the poem,
  name one tension it creates, and judge whether the poem resolves it —
  in one paragraph with two quotations.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) naming a repeated word and stopping — a
pattern needs the *difference* (what changes each time it returns), not
just the repetition; (2) importing history into a formalist paragraph
("in 1925…") — redirect to the words on the page and save the history
for Lesson 03; (3) treating "unity" as "everything agrees" — the
*Gatsby* ending shows unity can mean a tension held steadily, not
solved. Note the spelling: Fitzgerald's text reads "orgastic" (not
"orgiastic") in the Gutenberg text; do not "correct" it. Answer-key
references: guided practice §1–3, independent practice §4–5,
application §1–3.
