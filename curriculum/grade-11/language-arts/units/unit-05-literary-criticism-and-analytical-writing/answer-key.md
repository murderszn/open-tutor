# Answer Key — Unit 05: Literary Criticism and Analytical Writing

**For the guiding adult only.** Never place this file beside student
questions. Open responses below are *sample* reasoning with acceptable
variation noted; the adult accepts any answer that meets the stated
criterion with quoted evidence. Quotations were checked against Project
Gutenberg eBook #1952 (Gilman), eBook #64317 (Fitzgerald), and the
Poetry Foundation text of Hughes's "I, Too" (2026-11-13).

## Lesson 01 — What a Critical Lens Is

### Guided practice

1. **Lens table with examples from Worked Example 1.**
   - Formalist — question: what does the text itself do? Evidence:
     the verbs *creep*, *watched*, *going* and the *tide / high water /
     inland* contrast. Blind spot: the author's life and history.
   - Historical/biographical — question: what did the text mean in its
     time, and how did the author's world shape it? Evidence for "The
     Tide Clock": none exists (invented passage, no author or date).
     Blind spot: the text as a made object can be under-read.
   - Reader-response — question: what does the text do to a reader, and
     how does the reader help make meaning? Evidence: the gaps (why the
     sea stopped mattering; who bought the house) and different readers'
     different fillings. Blind spot: drift into "anything goes" without
     quotation.
2. **False.** A reader-response claim still has to point at words on
   the page: "this line made me angry because the narrator is told not
   to think, and the line before it shows her thinking clearly" is
   criticism; "this line made me angry" alone is a report. The quotation
   is the check.
3. "What did *tide clock* mean to a coastal family in 1950?" —
   **historical/biographical**; it needs a dated source about coastal
   households (a date, not a guess). "Why does the word *going* appear
   in the last line and not the first?" — **formalist**; it needs the
   passage itself, tracking where *going* and its contrasts appear.

### Independent practice

4. **Sample paragraph (acceptable variation allowed).** "The formalist
   lens is most useful for 'The Tide Clock,' because the passage is
   built on a pattern only the text itself can show: the clock 'creeps'
   toward a high water that 'would never come,' and the last line
   replaces *right* with *going* — 'She did not need it to be right.
   She needed it to be going.' The historical lens is least useful,
   because the passage is invented: there is no author, date, or
   publication history to consult, so the lens has no evidence and must
   refuse the reading. The reader-response lens is useful but second:
   it explains why the last line lands differently for different
   readers, but it cannot show the pattern that makes the line work.
   The formalist lens's blind spot is that it cannot say anything about
   why a tide clock would matter to a real family — that question needs
   history it has set aside." (Criterion: ranks the lenses with reasons,
   quotes once, names the first lens's blind spot.)
5. **Sample correction.** "The sentence is wrong because the historical
   lens cannot prove anything about an invented author — 'The Tide
   Clock' has no author, so there is no grandmother to be based on.
   The lens would need a dated source (a letter, an interview, a
   publication record) naming a real person and a real connection. The
   feeling of realness proves only that the passage is vividly written
   — a formalist observation about its concrete detail, not a
   historical fact."

### Application — the image task

1. **Magnifying glass → formalist:** it enlarges what is already on
   the page, looking closer at the text itself. **Stack of letters →
   historical/biographical:** letters are dated, personal evidence from
   the writer's world, looked at *around* the text. **Round mirror →
   reader-response:** a mirror shows the viewer, as the lens shows what
   the reader brings and does.
2. A blank book is right because the illustration is about *lenses*,
   not about one story — any printed text would make the image about
   that text. What it cannot show: any real text's words, patterns, or
   history; it shows the tools, not the work they do.
3. The glasses stand for the assumptions every reader already wears —
   the pair of glasses no one chose on purpose. A critic should mention
   them because no reading is neutral: naming what you bring is what
   makes a lens honest rather than hidden.

### Exit check

1. Formalist: what does the text itself do? Historical/biographical:
   what did the text mean in its own time, and how did the author's
   life and world shape it? Reader-response: what does the text do to a
   reader, and how does the reader help make its meaning?
2. Formalist cannot see history, the author's life, or the reader's
   experience. Historical/biographical can under-read the text itself.
   Reader-response can drift into "anything goes" without quotation.
3. Sample: "A lens is a named method with a question, evidence, and a
   blind spot that any reader can check; an opinion is a preference
   that needs no evidence."

## Lesson 02 — The Formalist Lens

### Guided practice

1. The opposed motions are carried by **"beat on"** (forward, active,
   rowing) against **"borne back"** (backward, passive, carried by the
   current).
2. "Up and down and sideways they crawl, and those absurd, unblinking
   eyes are everywhere." The eyes *crawl* in every direction (pursuit —
   the narrator follows them everywhere) and are "unblinking" and
   "everywhere" (collapse — they cannot be escaped or settled; looking
   does not resolve into a single image). It fits the pattern because
   the looking repeats and again fails to settle.
3. Sample formalist claim: "The novel's final sentence enacts its
   central tension through opposed motions — 'beat on' against 'borne
   back' — so that striving and drift occupy the same sentence." (No
   historical judgment about the American Dream; the pattern is the
   support.)

### Independent practice

4. **Sample paragraph, option (a) (*Gatsby*).** "The passage repeats
   the green light's pattern of approach-and-recession: the light is
   'the orgastic future that year by year recedes before us,' and the
   promise that follows — 'tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out
   our arms farther' — is cut off by an ellipsis and a dash: 'And one
   fine morning —'. The tension is between the forward verbs (*run*,
   *stretch*) and the backward verb (*recedes*): the future is always
   arriving and always farther away. The passage holds the tension
   rather than resolving it — the sentence breaks off instead of
   finishing — and that broken-off sentence is the unity: the novel's
   design is a promise that cannot be completed, and the form performs
   it. The pattern is earned because the green light has receded across
   the whole novel, not only here." **Sample paragraph, option (b)
   (Gilman).** "The sentence builds a pattern of clinging: the paper
   'sticketh closer than a brother' — a biblical echo (Proverbs 18:24,
   'a friend that sticketh closer than a brother') turned sinister —
   and the tearing only proves the clinging, since tearing takes
   'perseverance as well as hatred.' The tension is between the attempt
   to remove the paper and the paper's refusal to leave: destruction
   and attachment in the same gesture. The sentence holds the tension:
   the paper is both hated and inescapable, and the story's unity is in
   that holding — the narrator will later tear the paper and still be
   inside the room. The pattern is the meaning: what clings cannot
   simply be torn away." (Criterion: pattern named, tension shown,
   unity judged, two quotations.)
5. **Sample correction.** "(i) The formalist lens would accept the
   observation about the text — that the wallpaper is yellow and that
   the yellow is described as 'smouldering, unclean' — because that is
   in the words on the page. (ii) It would refuse the claim that yellow
   'meant sickness in the 1890s,' because that is a historical claim
   needing a dated source outside the text. (iii) The refused part
   belongs to the historical/biographical lens, which would need
   evidence about 1890s color associations before it could be used."

### Application — pattern hunting

1. Sample: the word *queer*/*strange* repeats — "there is something
   queer about it" and "there is something strange about the house — I
   can feel it" — and the house is called "a haunted house" and "a
   colonial mansion." (Accept any repeated word/image with locations.)
2. Sample tension: the house is "the most beautiful place" and also
   "haunted," cheap, long untenanted — beauty and wrongness in the same
   description; or John "laughs at" the narrator while she insists
   something is queer — dismissal against perception.
3. Sample prediction: "The pattern of noticing something wrong and
   being laughed at suggests the noticing will keep going and the
   laughing will keep dismissing, until one of them breaks." (Criterion:
   the prediction follows from the pattern; it need not match the
   ending.)

### Exit check

1. Pattern: something that repeats with a difference. Tension: the push
   and pull a pattern creates between two ideas, images, or values.
   Unity: whether the text resolves the tension, holds it open, or lets
   it stand — and whether that is earned.
2. Name the pattern, show the tension, judge the unity — each with a
   quotation.
3. It refuses to use anything outside the text: history, the author's
   life and intentions, and the reader's experience.

## Lesson 03 — The Historical / Biographical Lens

### Guided practice

1. Sample restatement of the three rules: (1) give the date, place, and
   source — a date is a claim, not a decoration; (2) what the author
   said they meant is evidence, not the final word on what the text
   does; (3) context explains what a text's situation was — it does
   not excuse the text or convict it, and it does not decide whether
   the text is good.
2. Correct use: "Douglass spoke on July 5, 1852, the day after the
   Fourth, to the Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society, and the
   speech's turn from praise to accusation uses that occasion." Misuse:
   "Douglass was angry because he had to speak the day after the
   Fourth." The first states checkable facts and ties them to the
   text's move; the second guesses an emotion from a date.
3. The **diary limit** (limit 1) applies: the story is fiction, not a
   diary — the narrator's madness is invented (Gilman wrote in 1913
   that she never had hallucinations), so the narrator's fate cannot
   prove anything about the medical treatment. The historical fact
   (the rest cure existed and was prescribed) explains the situation;
   it does not make the fictional outcome evidence about real medicine.

### Independent practice

4. **Sample paragraph (Douglass).** "Frederick Douglass delivered
   'What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?' on July 5, 1852, at
   Corinthian Hall in Rochester, New York, to the Rochester Ladies'
   Anti-Slavery Society (verified in Unit 01 at americanliterature.com).
   The date explains the speech's central question — 'What, to the
   American slave, is your 4th of July?' — because Douglass is speaking
   the day *after* the celebration, to people who have just celebrated
   it, and the word *your* marks the distance between the celebrants
   and the enslaved. The occasion also explains the speech's courtesy:
   he is addressing allies who invited him, so the accusation must
   work inside the room rather than against it. **What the date cannot
   decide is whether the speech is well argued — that is a question for
   its structure and reasoning, not its calendar — or what Douglass
   privately felt, since the speech is a public performance.**"
   (Criterion: dated fact + source, quotation, limit sentence — the
   limit sentence must be present and underlined or marked.)
5. **Sample correction.** "The sentence is wrong because the story is
   fiction: the wallpaper in the narrator's room is invented, and
   Gilman wrote in 1913 that she never had objections to her own
   wallpaper. The historical lens would need a dated source — a letter,
   a household record, a photograph — showing that Gilman's house had
   such wallpaper and that she used it in the story. The 1890 date
   establishes when the story was written, which places it in the years
   after her 1887 rest cure — not whose wallpaper it describes."

### Application — which claims need a date?

1. Needs no date — the passage itself is the evidence (formalist).
2. Needs a date — "abolitionist audience" is a historical claim about a
   real occasion (July 5, 1852, Rochester Ladies' Anti-Slavery Society).
3. Needs no date — the story states the prohibition directly.
4. Needs a date — a claim about a real doctor's real instruction to a
   real patient (1887, Mitchell's rest cure).
5. Needs a date — a publication fact (January 1892, *The New England
   Magazine*).
6. Needs no date — the story states it ("My brother is also a physician").
7. Needs a date — a claim about Gilman's stated purpose, from her 1913
   account ("Why I Wrote 'The Yellow Wallpaper'").
8. Needs no date — the sentence itself is the evidence (formalist).

### Exit check

1. (1) A date is a claim — give date, place, source. (2) Intention is
   evidence, not a verdict. (3) Context explains; it does not excuse
   or convict.
2. Anachronism is judging a past text by a later standard as if the
   later standard had been available — e.g., faulting Gilman for not
   using the modern term "postpartum depression."
3. Sample: "An author's stated purpose is one witness's evidence about
   intention; a text's meaning is what the text does, which must be
   shown from its words and form."

## Lesson 04 — Reader-Response and Feminist Lenses

### Guided practice

1. Sample gap: the poem never says who "they" are. One reader fills
   "they" with a specific household that excluded them; another fills
   it with American society in general. Both quote the same lines;
   the readings differ in what the reader brings.
2. "John hates to have me write a word." (Also acceptable: "There comes
   John, and I must put this away, — he hates to have me write a word.")
3. Sample: "The lens asks what a text assumes about women and men and
   what it does with that assumption; to prove Gilman 'hated all men'
   it would need evidence about the author, not the text — and a
   feminist reading of the story is about the *arrangement* (who is
   believed, who speaks), not the author's feelings about men."

### Independent practice

4. **Sample theses on the wallpaper.** Reader-response: "For a reader
   who has been told not to think about their own condition, the
   wallpaper's pattern reads as a mind given one thing to study and
   then punished for studying it: 'It is dull enough to confuse the
   eye in following, pronounced enough to constantly irritate, and
   provoke study.'" Evidence: "when you follow the lame, uncertain
   curves for a little distance they suddenly commit suicide" and
   "Up and down and sideways they crawl, and those absurd, unblinking
   eyes are everywhere." What it leaves open: whether the pattern's
   power comes from its form or from the reader's experience — that
   needs the formalist lens. Feminist: "The wallpaper is the one thing
   the narrator is allowed to look at, because looking is not working:
   'John says the very worst thing I can do is to think about my
   condition,' so the paper becomes the only place her attention is
   permitted to go." Evidence: "I did write for a while in spite of
   them; but it does exhaust me a good deal — having to be so sly about
   it" and "He said that after the wallpaper was changed it would be
   the heavy bedstead, and then the barred windows." What it leaves
   open: how much of the story's force is Gilman's own history — that
   needs the historical lens, with its limits.
5. **Sample correction.** "(i) Reader-response claims that readers
   actively make meaning by filling gaps — not that every opinion is
   equally good. (ii) A reading still needs evidence: quotations from
   the text that the response is a response *to*. (iii) A
   reader-response reading is weak when it cannot point at any words —
   for example, claiming the poem is 'about the sea' when the poem
   never mentions the sea."

### Application — seminar preparation

Criterion, not a single answer: the position must (1) name the most
trusted lens with a quotation and a reason tied to the text, (2) name
the least trusted lens with a quotation and a reason, (3) name one
question no lens in the unit answers well and the evidence that
question would need (e.g., "Did the story change how doctors treated
postpartum depression?" — needs medical-history sources, not literary
evidence). Accept any lens ranking that meets the criteria.

### Exit check

1. Implied reader: the reader the text seems written for — the person
   who would catch its references and fill its gaps as intended.
   Interpretive community: a group of readers who share ways of reading
   and therefore produce similar readings.
2. Feminist criticism asks who speaks, who is believed, who does the
   work, and whose experience the text treats as normal.
3. The thesis, the evidence, and the conclusion change; the text stays
   the same.

## Lesson 05 — The Analytical Essay

### Guided practice

1. **Sample four-part rewrite** (from a Lesson 04 reader-response
   thesis on "I, Too"): Lens — reader-response. Claim — the poem's
   meaning is made in the reader's filling of its gaps. How — the
   unfilled "they," "the table," and "tomorrow," which the reader must
   supply. So-what — the final claim "I, too, am America" only lands
   when the reader accepts the invitation to be the "company" that
   must learn to see.
2. **Sample introduce sentence for Evidence block 2:** "The story's
   form is the first proof: the narrator can only write because
   writing itself is forbidden, and the prohibition is stated
   plainly." (Criterion: a claim sentence that tells the reader what
   to listen for before the quotation arrives.)
3. **Sample refutation:** "The formalist reading is wrong to call the
   ending a collapse, because the narrator's last act is direct speech
   to John — 'I've got out at last' — which the story has forbidden
   her until this moment; a collapse would be silence, and she speaks."
   **Which is stronger:** the concession is stronger here, because the
   formalist evidence (the mind does break — she creeps around the
   room) is genuinely strong, and conceding it while holding the
   feminist claim makes the essay more credible than denying a real
   pattern. (Accept the refutation as stronger if the learner argues
   that the direct speech outweighs the creeping — the criterion is a
   reasoned choice, not the choice itself.)

### Independent practice

4. **Criterion:** the plan must contain a four-part thesis, three
   evidence blocks each with a quotation and a one-line explanation, a
   counter-reading with its best evidence and an answer, and a
   conclusion sentence that follows from the evidence. The adult checks
   the table before drafting (S15).
5. **Sample correction.** "(i) Two problems: the conclusion introduces
   a new claim (that the story 'changed medicine forever') that the
   essay never proved, and it praises the author's character ('brave
   woman') instead of concluding from the text's evidence. (ii) The
   historical lens establishes that Gilman *claimed* Mitchell changed
   his methods — and that historians question the claim — not that the
   story changed medicine. (iii) Replacement: 'The story's power is in
   its form: a woman forbidden to write writes anyway, and her final
   sentence refuses to ask permission — which is the refusal the essay
   has traced.'" (Accept any replacement conclusion that follows from
   the essay's evidence.)

### Application — revise a paragraph

**Sample revision.** "The wallpaper's pattern defeats the eye that
tries to follow it, and that defeat is the story's central image.
Gilman writes that the paper is 'dull enough to confuse the eye in
following, pronounced enough to constantly irritate, and provoke
study' — dull and pronounced at once, so that looking is both invited
and punished. The contradiction matters because the narrator is
forbidden to do anything *but* look: the paper is the one thing her
attention is allowed to rest on, and it rewards attention with
collapse." (Criterion: claim sentence first, quotation introduced and
punctuated, explanation sentence after it that says what the quotation
shows.)

### Exit check

1. Lens, claim, how (mechanism), so-what.
2. Introduce (set it up with a claim sentence), quote (exactly, briefly,
   punctuated), explain (say what it shows, in the lens's terms).
3. A counter-reading is a rival interpretation developed fairly, with
   its best evidence; a straw reader is a weakened version invented to
   be knocked down.

### D. Thesis workshop

1. **Missing:** lens, claim (it states a topic, "the American Dream"),
   mechanism, so-what. **Rewrite (sample):** "Through a formalist
   lens, *Gatsby*'s closing sentence enacts the novel's central tension
   between striving and drift — 'beat on' against 'borne back' — so
   that the American Dream appears in the novel's form as a promise
   that recedes even as it is pursued."
2. **Missing:** a claim about the *text* (it judges history, not the
   story) and a mechanism. **Rewrite (sample):** "Through a
   historical/biographical lens, 'The Yellow Wallpaper' uses the rest
   cure's documented prohibitions — no work, no writing, no thinking
   about one's condition — as the story's situation, so that the
   narrator's secret journal becomes the one place her knowledge is
   allowed to exist."
3. **Missing:** lens, mechanism, so-what (it reports a feeling). **Rewrite
   (sample):** "Through a reader-response lens, Hughes's 'I, Too' makes
   its power in the gaps it leaves — the unnamed 'they,' the unplaced
   'table,' the undefined 'tomorrow' — so that each reader must supply
   the exclusion from their own experience, and the final 'I, too, am
   America' lands as a claim the reader has helped to make."
4. **Missing:** lens, mechanism, so-what (it labels the story without
   arguing). **Rewrite (sample):** "Through a feminist lens, 'The
   Yellow Wallpaper' shows a woman's knowledge being dismissed because
   it is not a physician's knowledge: the secret-journal form makes her
   the only witness to her own condition, and her final escape is a
   refusal to ask to be believed."

### E. Quotation integration and error analysis

1. **Problem:** cold quotation with no introduction and no explanation
   ("This is important" explains nothing). **Rewrite (sample):** "John's
   authority is stated as a fact of character before it becomes a fact
   of medicine: Gilman writes that 'John is practical in the extreme,'
   and that practicality is what lets him dismiss everything his wife
   reports as superstition."
2. **Problem:** the quotation is dropped in as proof of a claim it does
   not prove ("bad" is asserted, not shown). **Rewrite (sample):** "The
   narrator's judgment of the paper is absolute from the start — 'I
   never saw a worse paper in my life' — and the sentence that follows
   gives the reason: the pattern 'commit[s] every artistic sin,' so
   that the paper offends taste before it ever frightens her."
3. **Problem:** the quotation is made to prove a historical claim (the
   American Dream is dead) that the sentence does not make. **Rewrite
   (sample):** "The novel's final sentence holds two motions at once —
   'So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly
   into the past' — and the tension between rowing forward and being
   carried back is the novel's form for a hope that recedes even as it
   is pursued."
4. **Problem:** quotation with praise instead of explanation ("a good
   ending" is taste). **Rewrite (sample):** "Hughes ends by changing
   one word: the poem opens 'I, too, sing America' and closes 'I, too,
   am America,' so that singing becomes being — the claim moves from
   what the speaker does to what the speaker is."

## Formative quiz (week-04_quiz.md)

**Scoring:** Part A 4 pts each (16); Part B 6 pts each (12); Part C 5 pts
each (10); Part D 8 pts; Part E 8 pts. Total 54. Award partial credit
per the criteria below; the adult notes which objective each miss maps
to (see the quiz header).

1. Formalist — what does the text itself do? Historical/biographical —
   what did the text mean in its own time, and how did the author's
   life and world shape it? Reader-response — what does the text do to
   a reader, and how does the reader help make its meaning? (1 pt per
   lens named, 1 pt per question stated; 4 pts total — award 3 for two
   lenses fully correct.)
2. Formalist. It cannot see history, the author's life, or the reader's
   experience (any one of these earns the point). (2 pts lens, 2 pts
   blind spot.)
3. Historical/biographical. It cannot decide what the story *does* — its
   form, its effect on a reader — or whether the author's stated purpose
   is the final word on meaning (any one earns the point). (2 pts lens,
   2 pts limit.)
4. Reader-response. The two beliefs: (1) the reader's role cannot be
   omitted from our understanding of literature; (2) readers do not
   passively consume meaning — they actively make it. (2 pts lens,
   1 pt per belief.)
5. Pattern: opposed motions (forward/backward); tension: striving
   ("beat on") against being carried back ("borne back"); unity: the
   sentence holds the tension rather than resolving it — the holding
   *is* the unity. Must quote at least one word (e.g., "beat on,"
   "borne back," "ceaselessly"). (2 pts per element.)
6. Tension: the paper is dull *and* pronounced — boring and aggressive
   at once; it invites study and defeats it. The pursuit-and-collapse
   pattern continues in "when you follow the lame, uncertain curves for
   a little distance they suddenly commit suicide — plunge off at
   outrageous angles" (or the "two bulbous eyes" passage). (3 pts
   tension, 3 pts pattern with quotation.)
7. Two dated facts (any two): Gilman under Dr. S. Weir Mitchell's rest
   cure in 1887; Mitchell's instruction "never touch pen, brush or
   pencil as long as you live"; Gilman followed the prescription about
   three months; story written 1890, published January 1892; Gilman's
   1913 account ("Why I Wrote 'The Yellow Wallpaper'") stating she
   wrote "to save people from being driven crazy." Explanation: the
   rest cure explains the story's situation (forbidden work, forbidden
   writing, prescribed rest). Limit (must be stated): it cannot decide
   what the story does, whether the narrator is Gilman, or whether
   Mitchell changed his methods (the last is disputed by historians).
   (1 pt per fact, 2 pts explanation, 1 pt limit.)
8. The occasion shapes the opening: Douglass speaks the day after the
   celebration to people who just celebrated it, so he first accepts
   the invitation and praises the founders, then turns the question on
   them — "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?" The word
   *your* marks the distance. (2 pts occasion, 2 pts the turn, 1 pt
   quotation.)
9. **Sample.** Reader-response thesis: "Hughes's 'I, Too' makes its
   meaning in the reader's filling of its gaps: 'tomorrow' means
   something different to every reader, and the final claim only lands
   when the reader accepts the invitation to be the 'company.'"
   Evidence: "Tomorrow, / I'll be at the table / When company comes."
   Feminist thesis: "The poem shows a man claiming a place at the table
   his society assigns him away from — 'They send me to eat in the
   kitchen' — and the claim is made by laughing and growing strong,
   not by asking permission." Evidence: "But I laugh, / And eat well, /
   And grow strong." Open: the reader-response thesis leaves open
   whether the hope is justified by history; the feminist thesis leaves

## Unit assessment (unit-assessment.md)

### Part 1 — essay

Score with the rubric in the assessment (60 points). **Rubric
application — sample:** an essay earning full marks on the thesis
names its lens in the introduction and states a claim a reader could
dispute with evidence (e.g., the feminist thesis in Lesson 05, Worked
Example 1). A counter-reading earning full marks states the rival
position in a sentence the rival would accept, gives its best
quotation, and answers it — by concession ("that reading is right
about X, and my thesis still holds because…") or by refutation. An
essay that merely asserts "some might disagree" without evidence earns
at most 4 of 12 on the counter-reading. Quotations must be exact;
a single misquotation costs the accuracy points for that block, and
the adult has the learner re-check the quotation against the edition
rather than penalizing the whole essay.

### Part 2 — short answers

1. **Formalist:** question — what does the text itself do? Evidence —
   words, images, patterns, structure, tensions inside the text.
   Blind spot — history, the author's life, the reader's experience.
   **Reader-response:** question — what does the text do to a reader,
   and how does the reader help make meaning? Evidence — the reader's
   responses, gaps the reader fills, different readers' readings.
   Blind spot — drift into "anything goes" without quotation. (Award
   5 pts per lens: 2 for the question, 2 for the evidence, 1 for the
   blind spot; 10 total.)
2. **Sample (Gilman).** Pattern: pursuit-and-collapse — "when you
   follow the lame, uncertain curves for a little distance they
   suddenly commit suicide." Tension: the paper invites study
   ("provoke study") and defeats it ("destroy themselves in unheard-of
   contradictions"). Unity: the passage holds the tension — the
   contradiction is the description, not a problem to solve — and that
   holding matches the story's design, in which the narrator is given
   one thing to study and punished for studying it. Quotations: the two
   above. (Award 3 pts pattern, 3 pts tension, 2 pts unity, 2 pts
   quotations.)
3. **Sample (Gilman).** Fact: in 1887 Gilman underwent Dr. S. Weir
   Mitchell's rest cure, which prescribed "never touch pen, brush or
   pencil as long as you live" (source: Gilman's 1913 account, "Why I
   Wrote 'The Yellow Wallpaper'"). What it explains: why the story's
   narrator is forbidden to write and to think about her condition.
   What it cannot decide: whether the story's ending is a cure, a
   collapse, or a refusal — that is a question for the text's form, not
   its history. (Award 3 pts fact + date + source, 3 pts explanation,
   4 pts limit stated explicitly.)
4. **Sample.** Reader-response: "The line lands differently depending
   on what the reader has been kept from — a reader who has been sent
   out of a room hears 'I, too, am America' as an arrival; the evidence
   is 'Tomorrow, / I'll be at the table.'" What it leaves open: whether
   the hope is justified by history. Feminist: "The line claims
   belonging without asking permission — the speaker does not ask to be
   let in, he announces that he is already America; the evidence is
   'They'll see how beautiful I am / And be ashamed —.'" What it
   leaves open: how the poem's short lines and repetitions do their
   work — that needs the formalist lens. (Award 2 pts per sentence with
   distinct evidence, 1 pt per open question; 10 total — 5 per lens
   with its open question.)

## Project — Two Lenses, One Text

**Rubric application — sample:** a portfolio earning "excellent" on
the comparison names precisely what changed (e.g., "under the
formalist lens my thesis was about the pattern; under the feminist
lens it was about who is believed — and the wallpaper passage I quoted
for pattern became, under the second lens, evidence about forbidden
work"). A portfolio that quotes the same passage in both analyses
without explaining why it counts differently has not done the
comparison — return it with that one question. The "keep only one"
choice must be reasoned from the text, not from preference ("I liked
the feminist lens better" earns at most 2 on that criterion).
