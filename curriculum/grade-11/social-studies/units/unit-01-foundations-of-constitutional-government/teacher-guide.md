# Teacher Guide — Unit 01: Foundations of Constitutional Government

This guide supports the adult teaching the unit. It contains lesson notes,
misconception flags, pacing advice, and nonpartisanship guardrails.
**All answers live in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md), never here.**

## Before the unit

- **Preview every primary source.** Read the Declaration transcript, the
  Federalist No. 10 and Brutus No. 1 excerpts you will assign, and the
  Bill of Rights text. Select excerpt lengths (400–600 words for the
  Lesson 04 essays; shorter for the Declaration grievances). Pre-teach
  the vocabulary list in the unit README before each lesson — never
  assign 18th-century prose cold.
- **Set the nonpartisan frame (W3S1).** Say explicitly: "We study how
  institutions are designed and how arguments work. We do not endorse
  parties, candidates, or causes. When we discuss contested questions,
  every position must be argued with evidence, and I will hold all sides
  to the same standard." Redirect modern examples to institutions and
  principles.
- **Sensitive content.** Lesson 06 names slavery, the three-fifths
  clause, and exclusion directly — factually, without graphic detail.
  Never ask a learner to *defend* slavery or exclusion; the deliberation
  question is about the *system's* democratic character. Offer a written
  reflection alternative for any discussion a learner finds overwhelming.

## Lesson-by-lesson notes

- **L1 (legitimacy theories):** The key move is distinguishing *power*
  from *right*. If a learner says "the strongest should rule," press:
  "That's a claim about who *can* rule. What would make it *right*?" The
  triptych activity works best when the adult withholds the theory names
  until learners have described what they see.
- **L2 (Declaration):** Stress the *argument structure* over the poetry.
  Learners who can map claim → evidence → conclusion can analyze any
  founding document. Flag: the Declaration created no law and no rights —
  correct the "it's the law of the land" error the first time it appears.
- **L3 (Articles):** The emotional core is the founders' *fear* — they
  were not incompetent, they were traumatized by centralized power. Keep
  Shays's Rebellion factual (debt, court shutdowns, private militia) and
  frame it as *evidence the founders cited*, not as heroism or villainy.
- **L4 (ratification debate):** Assign sides for the deliberation
  *against* learners' inclinations when possible — arguing the unfamiliar
  side builds the mechanism-level thinking the assessment rewards. Do not
  let this become a modern-party proxy: intervene the first time a 1787
  argument is mapped onto a current party.
- **L5 (principles):** The trace-the-power diagram is the unit's hardest
  cognitive task; model one fully before assigning. Emphasize *asymmetry*:
  checks are deliberately uneven (override vs. veto, purse vs. sword).
- **L6 (Bill of Rights / 1788 question):** The two-ledgers table is the
  deliberation's ammunition. Insist on *specific provisions* (Article I,
  §2; Article I, §9; the 13th/14th/19th Amendments), not vibes.

## Misconception watch (across the unit)

1. The social contract as a literal signed document (L1).
2. The Declaration as enforceable law (L2).
3. "The founders were stupid" theory of the Articles (L3).
4. Federalists = kings, Anti-Federalists = democrats (L4).
5. Checks and balances as symmetric/equal branches (L5).
6. The Bill of Rights as original to the Constitution; the Bill of Rights
   as fixing everything (L6).

## Pacing and re-teaching

- **W3S5 check:** if learners cannot map the Declaration's argument,
  re-teach with a non-founding example (the Lesson 02 application
  outline) before W4.
- **W5S5 check:** if trace-the-power diagrams show fewer than four
  checks, re-model with a second power before W6.
- **Quiz signal (W6S4):** below 12/20, or a blank on item 5
  (Federalist/Anti-Federalist mechanisms), re-teach Lesson 04's argument
  map before the assessment. Do not administer the assessment to fill
  time — it measures reasoning that needs the re-teach.
- **Project defense (W6S4):** probe the counterclaim first. A learner
  who revises their thesis under questioning has done *better* — score
  the rubric accordingly and say so.

## What readiness looks like

The learner is ready for U02 (the Constitution's institutions in detail)
when they can: name the three legitimacy theories and the founders'
claimed one; explain the Articles' failure structurally; contrast Madison
and Brutus on mechanisms, not slogans; trace a power through at least
four checks; and defend a qualified position on the 1788 democratic
question with provisions cited. The unit assessment measures exactly this.
