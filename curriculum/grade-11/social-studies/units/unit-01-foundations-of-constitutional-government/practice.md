# Unit 01 — Guided and Extra Practice

Practice sessions in this unit are W3S2, W4S2, and W5S2 (see the unit
[README](README.md) pacing). Each session below runs ~45 minutes. The adult
checks work against [answer-key.md](answer-key.md); misconceptions to watch
are flagged in the [teacher guide](teacher-guide.md).

## Practice session A — W3S2: Legitimacy theories and the Declaration

1. **Retrieval (no notes, 5 min).** From memory, write the three legitimacy
   theories and one sentence for each. Then write Locke's three natural
   rights and the consent claim. Check against Lesson 01 and correct in a
   different color.
2. **Triptych re-analysis.** Re-open the
   [legitimacy-theories triptych](assets/legitimacy-theories-triptych.png).
   This time, argue the *opposite* of your Lesson 01 answer for one panel:
   what is the *strongest* case that a panel you matched to one theory
   could represent a *mixture* of theories? (Example: soldiers enforcing
   order could serve a social contract if the people authorized them.)
   4–5 sentences.
3. **Declaration argument drill.** Without looking: write the Declaration's
   three parts in order and one sentence on what each part does. Then open
   the transcript and verify — fix any error and note what you misremembered.
4. **Vocabulary in context.** Use each of these in a sentence about the
   founding (not a definition): *unalienable*, *usurpation*, *consent of
   the governed*, *tyranny*, *grievance*.
5. **Extension (if time).** Pick one grievance from Lesson 02. Rewrite it
   as a one-line news headline from 1776, then as a one-line headline from
   the king's perspective. What changes between the two?

## Practice session B — W4S2: The Articles and the ratification debate

1. **Retrieval (no notes, 5 min).** List the four powers the Articles
   *withheld* and, for each, the crisis that exposed the gap. Correct
   against Lesson 03.
2. **Articles evidence table — finish and defend.** Complete the
   eight-row power table from Lesson 03 if unfinished. Then add a ninth
   row of your own: a power the Articles *should* have granted (in your
   judgment), with a one-sentence justification from the social-contract
   test.
3. **Federalist/Anti-Federalist speed sort.** The adult reads eight
   claims; you hold up F (Federalist/Madison) or A (Anti-Federalist/Brutus)
   and say the mechanism behind it:
   - "A large republic controls faction by multiplying it."
   - "Representatives in a vast republic cannot know the people's minds."
   - "Power naturally grows; written limits are needed."
   - "Filtering public views through elected representatives refines them."
   - "The necessary-and-proper clause will swallow the states."
   - "Liberty survives only in small republics."
   - "Nine states' ratification legitimizes the new government."
   - "Changing the ratification rule mid-process breaks faith."
   
   (Answer key gives the expected sort with one-line justifications.)
4. **Counterclaim writing.** Choose your *weaker* side from the speed
   sort. Write the strongest 3-sentence argument for that side on the
   large-republic question. Then write one sentence conceding its best
   point before returning to your own view.
5. **Extension (if time).** Draft the opening two sentences of your
   project report's thesis (see [project.md](project.md)); the adult gives
   feedback on whether it is arguable and evidence-bound.

## Practice session C — W5S2: Principles and checks

1. **Retrieval (no notes, 5 min).** Write the five principles and the
   constitutional location of each. Correct against Lesson 05.
2. **Trace-the-power — second round.** Trace a *different* power than you
   chose in Lesson 05, with at least five checks cited to article/section
   or amendment. Then answer: which check in your diagram is the *weakest
   in practice*, and why? (2 sentences.)
3. **Asymmetry hunt.** Find one check that works in only one direction
   (A can block B, but B cannot block A the same way). Explain in 2–3
   sentences why the asymmetry exists — what abuse does it prevent?
4. **Judicial-review reasoning chain.** Without notes, reconstruct the
   three-question logic from the Lesson 05 extension (right? remedy?
   who decides?) in your own words. Then state in one sentence why the
   *third* question was the revolutionary one.
5. **Extension (if time).** Revisit your W3S2 triptych answer: which
   panel best describes the Constitution's *own* legitimacy claim, and
   which details in the image support that? (2–3 sentences.)

## Retrieval bank (use in any review session)

- Define: social contract, natural rights, legitimacy, confederation,
  federalism, faction, ratification, amendment, checks and balances,
  popular sovereignty.
- Name the year and significance: 1776, 1777, 1781, 1786, 1787, 1788, 1791,
  1803, 1808.
- Quote-check: "We hold these truths..." — which part of the Declaration's
  argument is this, and what work does it do?
- Mechanism-check: state Madison's faction mechanism and Brutus's
  representation mechanism in one sentence each.
- Application: name one check on each branch of government.
