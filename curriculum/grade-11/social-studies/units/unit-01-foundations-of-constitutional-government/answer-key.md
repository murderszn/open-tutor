# Answer Key — Unit 01: Foundations of Constitutional Government

**Adult eyes only.** Every question in the lessons, practice sessions,
quiz, and unit assessment is solved here. Sample open responses show
acceptable reasoning; defensible alternatives that use evidence earn full
credit. Reconciled 2026-10-14 against the questions as written.

## Lesson 01

1. **Definitions (sample):** *legitimacy* — a government's claimed right
   to rule and be obeyed. *natural rights* — rights held by being human,
   not granted by government (Locke: life, liberty, property). *social
   contract* — the idea that legitimate authority rests on the people's
   agreement/consent. *consent of the governed* — the requirement that
   the people agree to be ruled. **Sort:** (a) pharaoh as living god →
   **divine right**; (b) "We the people... ordain" → **social contract**;
   (c) "Might makes right" → **force**; (d) "by God's grace" → **divine
   right**; (e) "No taxation without representation" → **social
   contract** (consent claim).
2. **Activity B:** (a) Left panel → divine right (rays of light on the
   crown; courtiers bowing — authority from above). Middle → force
   (soldiers, shields, spears, banners — authority from arms). Right →
   social contract (ordinary people signing together; handshake —
   authority from mutual agreement). (b) No — most real governments never
   had a literal signing. The signature stands for *consent*: expressed
   through elections, acceptance of the system, the ability to leave or
   dissent, and (in the American case) ratification. (c) Same answers from
   the text descriptions.
3. **Rewrite (sample):** "The principal appoints the student council, and
   the council's decisions are final." → social-contract version (≤40
   words): "We, the students, elect a council to represent us and agree
   to follow its rules while they serve our welfare; we may replace the
   council each term." Theory behind the original: **rule by
   appointment/force-from-above** (closest to divine right in structure —
   authority descending, no consent).
4. **Error analysis:** (1) The contract is not between government and
   *each citizen* individually — it is the *people collectively*
   authorizing government. (2) "Contract" is a political *idea*/model
   about the basis of authority, not a literal signed document (see 2b).
5. **Application:** Accept any real preamble/charter with the theory
   correctly identified and words quoted (e.g., a national constitution's
   "We the people" → social contract; a monarchy's constitution invoking
   divine authority → divine right; a party constitution citing
   revolutionary victory → force/conquest mixture).
6. **Exit check (sample):** Locke's theory makes authority conditional on
   protecting rights, so withdrawing consent ends it. Divine right makes
   authority come from God, so the people have no standing to remove
   what God ordained.

## Lesson 02

1. **Close read:** (i) equality — "all men are created equal"; (ii)
   rights — "endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights...
   Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness"; (iii) government purpose
   — "to secure these rights, Governments are instituted among Men";
   (iv) consent — "deriving their just powers from the consent of the
   governed" → social-contract idea: authority is legitimate only with
   the people's agreement (Lesson 01, theory 3); (v) revolution — "it is
   the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it."
2. **Grievance sort (sample):** "He has refused his Assent to Laws..."
   → consent violation: the people's legislatures overruled. "For
   imposing Taxes on us without our Consent" → direct consent violation.
   "He has kept among us, in times of peace, Standing Armies without the
   Consent of our legislatures" → military power without consent. "He has
   dissolved Representative Houses repeatedly" → destroying the very
   channel of consent. Pattern, not accidents: the same "Object" —
   ruling without the people's consent — recurs across grievances.
3. **Modern-English rewrite:** accept accurate paraphrases; the paired
   sentence must name the right violated (e.g., taxes without consent →
   the consent/liberty right from Part 1).
4. **Counterclaim (sample):** "Virtual representation is exactly what the
   Declaration rejects: consent must come from the governed themselves
   through their own legislatures, not from distant lawmakers who never
   face colonial voters. A 'representative' you cannot elect or remove is
   representation in name only — which is why the grievances stress
   *our* legislatures and *our* consent."
5. **Application:** accept any coherent three-part outline (principle →
   documented pattern → verdict) on an appropriate topic.
6. **Exit check:** (a) philosophical claim → evidence (grievances) →
   conclusion (independence). (b) Sample: the document claims universal
   equality while its signers' society enslaved Black people and excluded
   women — the principle is universal but the practice was restricted.

## Lesson 03

1. **Power table:** tax → **withheld** → Congress could only request
   funds; chronic debt and unpaid soldiers. Regulate interstate commerce
   → **withheld** → states taxed each other's goods; no unified trade
   policy. Declare war → **granted** → Congress could authorize war but
   not fund or compel troops. Coin money → **granted** → but competing
   state currencies and collapsed paper money. Enforce laws → **withheld**
   (no executive) → Congress's resolutions depended on state compliance.
   Amend the Articles → unanimous consent required → a single state
   (Rhode Island, 1781) could block repair. Admit new states → **granted**.
   Settle disputes between states → **granted** (Congress as arbiter).
2. **Timeline:** 1776 — independence declared, no national government
   yet; 1777 — Articles adopted by Congress (not yet in force);
   1781 — Articles in force: Congress can make war/peace and treaties but
   cannot tax or regulate trade; 1786 — Annapolis (trade talks fail for
   lack of attendance) and Shays's Rebellion (Congress cannot raise
   troops/money to respond); 1787 — Philadelphia Convention called to
   address the defects.
3. **Scenario (sample):** The statement should *request* cooperation and
   perhaps propose a voluntary compact — while the honest admission
   names the missing tools: no power to compel the states, no power to
   regulate their border taxes, no enforcement arm.
4. **Error analysis (sample):** "The founders had just fought a war
   against centralized British power, so they deliberately built a weak
   center to protect state liberty. The design failed not from stupidity
   but from overcorrection: the fear of tyranny produced a government
   too weak to secure anyone's rights."
5. **Application:** accept any sharp test question (e.g., "Can this
   government raise an army it cannot afford to disband?") with a
   2–3 sentence Articles answer.
6. **Exit check:** Sample triple: no taxation → debt/unpaid army (exposed
   by the postwar debt crisis); no commerce regulation → interstate
   trade wars (exposed at Annapolis); no enforcement/executive → laws
   without teeth (exposed by Shays's Rebellion).

## Lesson 04

1. **Argument map (sample):** Madison — claim: an extended republic
   controls faction; mechanism: multiplying factions across a large
   electorate prevents any one from dominating; authority: observation of
   popular governments' "mortal diseases"; strongest: accepts the
   faction problem rather than wishing it away; weakest: assumes
   representatives will actually "refine" rather than serve factions.
   Brutus — claim: only small republics stay free; mechanism:
   representation requires shared sentiments, impossible at scale;
   authority: Montesquieu; strongest: names power's tendency to grow as
   a real dynamic; weakest: offers no solution to the Articles' proven
   weakness at small scale.
2. **Authorization brief:** Federalist side — the Convention *proposed*;
   ratification by the people's conventions is the legitimizing act, so
   consent cures the procedural irregularity (Declaration's "alter or
   abolish" logic). Anti-Federalist side — the Convention exceeded
   limited instructions and changed the acceptance rule from thirteen to
   nine; a founding that breaks its own rules teaches that rules are
   optional for the powerful.
3. **Dialogue:** each speaker needs ≥2 lines with reasons; check that
   Brutus's lines invoke scale/representation and Madison's invoke
   faction-multiplication.
4. **Error analysis:** (1) Neither side wanted a king; the Federalists
   wanted a stronger *republican* national government, the
   Anti-Federalists wanted power kept close in the states. (2) The real
   axis is *scale and structure*: can a large republic stay free, and by
   what mechanism — not monarchy vs. democracy.
5. **Application:** accept any observed case naming Madison's mechanism
   and reporting whether it held.
6. **Exit check (sample):** Madison: a large republic is *safer* because
   many factions check each other. Brutus: a large republic is *fatal*
   because genuine representation is impossible at scale. They most
   disagree about whether representation can work across a continent.

## Lesson 05

1. **Principle cards (sample):** Popular sovereignty — all power comes
   from the people ("We the People," Preamble). Limited government —
   only granted powers may be exercised (Article I, §§8–10). Separation
   of powers — legislative/executive/judicial divided (Articles I–III).
   Checks and balances — each branch can block the others (e.g., veto,
   Article I, §7). Federalism — power divided between nation and states
   (enumerated vs. reserved; Tenth Amendment).
2. **Trace-the-power:** (a) declare war — Congress declares (Art. I, §8);
   President commands (Art. II, §2); Congress funds in ≤2-year
   appropriations; courts generally defer; people replace lawmakers.
   (b) nominate a justice — President nominates (Art. II, §2); Senate
   confirms (check); justice serves during good behavior (Art. III, §1);
   Congress sets court size/jurisdiction; impeachment available.
   (c) regulate interstate commerce — Congress regulates (Art. I, §8);
   President executes; courts review (judicial review); states retain
   police powers; people elect Congress.
3. **Rebuttal (sample):** "Checks block *abuse*, not all action: the
   veto slows a bill but a two-thirds override still passes it — delay,
   not paralysis. What checks *stop* is one branch acting alone: no law
   without both houses and presentment, no spending without
   appropriation. Slowness is the price of preventing concentrated power."
4. **Horizontal vs. vertical (sample):** "Separation guards against one
   *branch* capturing the whole government (the Brutus fear of
   concentrated power). Federalism guards against one *level* capturing
   everything — even a perfectly checked national government could still
   crush local self-rule, so the states hold reserved powers as a second
   firewall."
5. **Application:** accept any institution with one separation feature
   and one check (or a well-argued missing check) identified.
6. **Exit check:** five principles named; one-sentence problem per
   principle (e.g., popular sovereignty → "who authorizes power?";
   limited government → "what stops power from growing?"; separation →
   "what stops one office holding everything?"; checks → "what stops one
   branch dominating?"; federalism → "what stops the center swallowing
   the parts?").

## Lesson 06

1. **Amendment flowchart:** Propose — 2/3 of both houses OR convention
   called by 2/3 of states. Ratify — 3/4 of state legislatures OR 3/4 of
   state conventions. Supermajorities required because the Constitution
   is a *compact*: the terms should not change on a passing majority's
   whim (the Declaration's "prudence" logic; stability protects the
   consent that authorized it).
2. **Two ledgers (sample):** Democratic — "We the People" as authority;
   ratification by popular conventions; directly elected House every two
   years; Article V as ongoing consent; radical for 1788 (no monarch, no
   aristocratic house). Not democratic — enslaved people (1 in 5) with
   no rights; three-fifths clause (Art. I, §2) inflating slave-state
   power; slave-trade protection to 1808 (Art. I, §9); women voteless;
   Native nations excluded; property qualifications in most states.
3. **Ninth/Tenth (sample):** "The Ninth answers the fear that listing
   rights would imply unlisted rights don't exist; the Tenth answers the
   fear that the national government would swallow the states. Both
   vindicate *Brutus*: they are written limits on concentrated power,
   exactly what he demanded — though Madison drafted them."
4. **Error analysis:** "The 1787 Convention proposed a Constitution with
   no bill of rights; ratification in 1787–88 succeeded only with
   promises that amendments would follow. The Bill of Rights was proposed
   by the First Congress and ratified in 1791 — the gap exists because
   Anti-Federalist pressure forced the addition."
5. **Application:** accept any amendment 11–27 with problem, path, and
   effect correctly stated and a source cited.
6. **Exit check:** either position earns credit with two reasons. Sample
   "strength" position: "It is a strength: the founders knew they were
   fallible and built a repair procedure requiring broad consensus — the
   13th and 19th Amendments prove the mechanism works. A founding that
   claimed perfection would be more dangerous, not less."

## Quiz (20 pts)

1. (2) Divine right — authority from God; force — authority from
   overwhelming power/conquest; social contract — authority from the
   people's agreement/consent.
2. (2) Job: protect natural rights (Locke: life, liberty, property).
   Source: the consent of the governed.
3. (3) Part 1 — philosophy: states the principles (equality, rights,
   consent). Part 2 — grievances: documents the king's violations as
   evidence. Part 3 — conclusion: declares independence as the verdict.
4. (2) Any two: tax → debt/unpaid soldiers; regulate commerce →
   interstate trade barriers; enforce laws (no executive) → resolutions
   ignored; unanimous amendment → Rhode Island blocks the 1781 impost.
5. (3) Madison: a large republic *controls* faction. Brutus: a large
   republic *cannot stay free*. Madison's mechanism: multiplying factions
   so none dominates. Brutus's mechanism: representation requires shared
   sentiments, impossible at continental scale.
6. (2) Five principles named. Sample check: the President vetoes a bill
   passed by Congress (executive checks legislative), overridable by
   two-thirds.
7. (3) Propose: 2/3 of both houses of Congress; convention called by 2/3
   of the states. Ratify: 3/4 of state legislatures; 3/4 of state
   conventions.
8. (1) Sample: it proclaims universal equality while the society it
   speaks for enslaves Black people and excludes women — universal
   principle, restricted practice.

**Scoring:** 2-pt items: 1 pt per correct half. 3-pt items: 1 pt per
correct third. Accept paraphrases that preserve the mechanism or fact.

## Unit assessment (50 pts)

**Task 1 (8):** a. (3) Matches + one visual detail each (see L1 key).
b. (2) Panel C; quote "deriving their just powers from the consent of
the governed." c. (3) Full credit for either position with reasoning:
*criticism accepted* — the signature is metaphorical and tacit consent
is weaker than real agreement; *criticism rejected* — the signature
stands for ratification/elections/acceptance, and the theory never
required a literal signing, only a genuine consent mechanism. Credit
the engagement with what "consent" means, not the side chosen.

**Task 2 (10):** a. (4) Philosophical claim: "all men are created
equal... unalienable Rights"; consent principle: "deriving their just
powers from the consent of the governed"; revolutionary consequence:
"it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it"; grievances
= the evidence section; the conclusion ("Free and Independent States")
= the verdict. b. (3) Any two grievances correctly tied to the consent
principle (e.g., taxes without consent → violates "deriving... from
consent"; dissolving legislatures → destroys the consent channel).
c. (3) Tension stated (universal language vs. slavery/exclusion). Full
credit for "both, with defense": the logic is universal (it condemns
slavery by its own terms) while the authors' credibility is damaged
(they did not live the principle) — or for a well-defended
logic-only/credibility-only answer.

**Task 3 (8):** a. (4) Four withheld powers + consequences (see L3 key).
b. (4) Full credit for the *deliberate-design* answer with evidence
(Shays's Rebellion showing the design's cost; Annapolis showing the
recognition of failure). The "misunderstood government" answer earns
partial credit only if it acknowledges the founders' stated fear of
centralized power.

**Task 4 (10):** a. (4) Problem: faction destroys popular governments;
small republics fail because one faction becomes the majority; extended
republics succeed because many factions check each other (+ refined
representation). b. (3) Step 1: large republics lose the public good
to "a thousand views"; small ones keep it "within the reach of every
citizen" (Excerpt B). Step 2: power-holders "are ever disposed to
increase it," so distant rulers will aggrandize themselves (Excerpt B).
c. (3) Either side with mechanism-level reasoning. Sample Madison:
"Multiplying factions is observable — no single interest dominates a
continental electorate the way one faction captured Rhode Island's
politics." Sample Brutus: "Representation at scale really did thin out
— the House's ratio grew from 1:30,000 toward 1:700,000+, exactly the
distance Brutus predicted."

**Task 5 (8):** a. (4) Sample for (i) controversial tax: enumerated
power exists (Art. I, §8 — limited government: the power must be
granted); bicameral passage + presentment (separation); presidential
veto with 2/3 override (checks); judicial review if it violates rights
(checks); states cannot be commandeered (federalism); voters can
replace lawmakers (popular sovereignty). Any four correct checks with
principles named. b. (4) Sample: "Separation guards against one branch
capturing the whole government; federalism guards against one *level*
capturing everything — a perfectly checked national government could
still extinguish local self-rule, so reserved state powers form a
second firewall."

**Task 6 (6):** Score: 2 pts democratic-radical evidence (popular
sovereignty, ratification conventions, elected House, no monarch); 2 pts
exclusion evidence (two+ specific provisions/facts: three-fifths,
1808 protection, voteless women, Native exclusion, property
qualifications); 1 pt counterclaim presented and answered; 1 pt
judgment on whether Article V changes the verdict (either way, with
reasons). Deduct for partisan framing; redirect, don't zero, on first
