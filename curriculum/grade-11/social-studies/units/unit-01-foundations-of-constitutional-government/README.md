# Unit 01 — Foundations of Constitutional Government and Political Ideas

**Grade:** 11 · **Subject:** Social Studies (U.S. government and civics)
**Track weeks:** 3–6 · **Backlog:** [issue #53](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/53), section U01
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Every government rests on a claim: *we have the right to rule you.* This
unit asks where that claim comes from, tests it against the American
founding, and follows the founders' answer — that legitimate power comes
from the consent of the governed — from the Declaration of Independence
(1776), through the failed Articles of Confederation (1781–1789), through
the ratification fight over the Constitution (1787–1788), to the Bill of
Rights and the amendment mechanism that made the founding an argument rather
than a finished answer.

## Essential question

Where does a government's right to rule come from — and how well does the
American founding's answer hold up?

## Prerequisites

From the track diagnostic weeks (weeks 1–2) and the assumed grade-9/10
social-studies background:

- Classify a source by kind (government document, private letter, opinion
  essay, news report) and infer its maker and audience.
- Write a claim supported by at least two pieces of evidence; identify a
  counterclaim and its evidence.
- Name the Reconstruction Amendments and what they changed on paper;
  explain the New Deal's expansion of the federal role.
- Read 18th-century English with vocabulary support (the adult pre-teaches
  terms before every primary-source reading; see the track
  [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md), §2).

No cross-grade learner links: these are described skills, not references to
other grades' lessons.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain** natural-rights, social-contract, and limited-government ideas,
   and name where each appears in the Declaration of Independence.
   (D2.Civ.4.9-12)
2. **Analyze** the Declaration's argument (claim → evidence → conclusion),
   including whose rights it claims and whose it excludes.
   (D2.Civ.8.9-12)
3. **Evaluate** why the Articles of Confederation failed and what the
   failure revealed about the powers a working government needs.
   (D2.Civ.4.9-12)
4. **Compare** Federalist and Anti-Federalist arguments about the size of a
   republic and the danger the Constitution posed to liberty.
   (D2.Civ.4.9-12)
5. **Explain** the Constitution's core principles — popular sovereignty,
   limited government, separation of powers, checks and balances,
   federalism — and trace one power through the checks that restrain it.
   (D2.Civ.4.9-12)
6. **Assess** the claim that the Constitution was democratic in 1788, using
   the Bill of Rights bargain, the amendment mechanism, and the question of
   who was included and excluded. (D2.Civ.8.9-12; D2.Civ.12.9-12)

In every unit the learner also develops compelling questions (D1.1–D1.5.9-12),
evaluates sources and their limits (D3.1–D3.4.9-12), and communicates
evidence-based conclusions (D4.1–D4.4.9-12).

for Social Studies State Standards**, National Council for the Social

audit (2026-10-08). **Indicator codes are exact; the short descriptions

| Unit objective | Indicator | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|
| 1, 3, 4, 5 | D2.Civ.4.9-12 | How the U.S. Constitution establishes a system of government whose powers, responsibilities, and limits have changed over time and remain contested |

Primary sources are quoted in brief public-domain excerpts with links; all
are in the public domain (18th-century texts and U.S. government
documents). The founding is presented as contested: Federalist and
Anti-Federalist positions, the question of inclusion, and historians'
differing verdicts all appear with evidence. The course is nonpartisan:
institutions and processes are analyzed without endorsing parties,
candidates, or causes.

## 20-session pacing (4 weeks × 5 sessions, ~45–60 minutes each)

Sessions rotate: core lesson → practice → investigation/reading →
argument/deliberation → review. The project launches in Week 3, Session 4
and runs across the investigation sessions; the final argument session
(W6S4) doubles as the project defense.

| Session | Type | Content |
|---|---|---|
| W3S1 | Lesson | [L1 — Where does a government's right to rule come from?](lessons/lesson-01-legitimacy-theories.md): natural rights, the social contract, and rival theories of legitimacy |
| W3S2 | Practice | Triptych activity (image analysis), vocabulary map, retrieval on theories |
| W3S3 | Lesson | [L2 — The Declaration of Independence](lessons/lesson-02-declaration-of-independence.md): argument structure and the consent-of-the-governed claim |
| W3S4 | Investigation | **Project launch** — [Founding Legitimacy Report](project.md): compelling question, source plan, evidence log started |
| W3S5 | Review | Week 3 review + formative check (theories; Declaration argument) |
| W4S1 | Lesson | [L3 — The Articles of Confederation](lessons/lesson-03-articles-of-confederation.md): powers granted, powers missing, crises |
| W4S2 | Practice | Articles evidence table; scenario task — "govern under the Articles" |
| W4S3 | Lesson | [L4 — The Federalist–Anti-Federalist debate](lessons/lesson-04-federalist-anti-federalist-debate.md): Federalist No. 10 vs. Brutus No. 1 on the size of a republic |
| W4S4 | Investigation | Source lab — compare two excerpts on the same question (size of the republic); update project evidence logs |
| W4S5 | Deliberation + review | Structured deliberation: was the 1787 Convention authorized to write a new Constitution? Week 4 review |
| W5S1 | Lesson | [L5 — The Constitution's core principles](lessons/lesson-05-constitutional-principles.md): sovereignty, limits, separation, checks, federalism |
| W5S2 | Practice | Trace-one-power-through-checks diagram task; judicial review arrives later (Marbury preview) |
| W5S3 | Investigation | Project research session — amendment mechanism and Bill-of-Rights bargain evidence |
| W5S4 | Argument | Drafting clinic — paragraph on "which principle matters most for limiting power," with counterclaim |
| W5S5 | Review | Week 5 review + formative check (principles; checks mapping) |
| W6S1 | Lesson | [L6 — The Bill of Rights, the amendment mechanism, and a contested founding](lessons/lesson-06-bill-of-rights-amendment-democratic-founding.md) |
| W6S2 | Investigation | Source lab — two historians' contrasting verdicts on the founding (D2.Civ.8.9-12) |
| W6S3 | Project | Drafting session — Founding Legitimacy Report due at W6S4 |

Success criteria: by W3S5 the learner can name the three legitimacy
theories and map the Declaration's argument; by W4S5 the learner can
explain the Articles' failure and contrast Federalist and Anti-Federalist
claims with evidence; by W5S5 the learner can trace a power through the
checks; by W6S5 the learner can defend an evidence-based position on the
1788 democratic question and has delivered the project report.
