# Lesson 04 — The Ratification Debate: Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists

**Goal:** Compare Federalist and Anti-Federalist arguments on the same
question — can a large republic stay free? — and assess whether the 1787
Convention was authorized to write a new Constitution. **Time:** ~55
minutes. **Materials:** excerpt packets (linked sources); deliberation
protocol in the teacher guide.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** The Articles failed from weakness. What is
> the *opposite* danger — and which fear should matter more?

**Sources (all public domain):**
- James Madison, **Federalist No. 10** (November 23, 1787): full text in
  the open textbook *American Government 2e*,
  [pressbooks.online.ucf.edu/pos2041bw/chapter/federalist-papers-10-and-51/](https://pressbooks.online.ucf.edu/pos2041bw/chapter/federalist-papers-10-and-51/)

  (checked 2026-10-14).

## Teaching

The Philadelphia Convention was authorized only to *revise* the Articles.
Instead, it wrote an entirely new Constitution — and then the country
argued about whether to adopt it. That argument, carried out in newspapers
under pen names, is the richest debate in American political history. Two
voices frame it:

**The Federalist case — James Madison, Federalist No. 10.** Madison starts
from a grim observation: every popular government in history has been torn
apart by **faction** — groups "united and actuated by some common impulse
of passion, or of interest, adverse to the rights of other citizens." You
cannot *remove* the causes of faction (that would require destroying
liberty itself), so you must *control its effects*. Madison's famous
answer: a **large republic**. In a small republic, one faction easily
becomes a majority and oppresses everyone else. In an *extended* republic,
so many factions exist that no single one can dominate; representatives,
filtered through a large electorate, refine public views. Bigness, which
the Anti-Federalists feared, is for Madison the very *cure*.

**The Anti-Federalist case — "Brutus" No. 1.** Brutus answers with the
classical republican tradition (Montesquieu): free government only works
on a *small* scale, where citizens know their representatives and can
watch them. In a vast republic, representatives become distant, the
"necessary and proper" clause and the tax power will let Congress swallow
the states, and "every man, and every body of men, invested with power, are
ever disposed to increase it." Brutus's prediction: consolidation — one
great government — and the slow death of liberty. His remedy: keep power
close to the people, and (in later essays) demand a bill of rights.

**The authorization question.** The Convention's instructions were to
propose *amendments* to the Articles. Writing a new Constitution arguably
exceeded them — and the new document required only **nine** states to take
effect, not the Articles' unanimous thirteen. Federalists replied that the
proposal still needed the people's ratification (in special conventions,
not legislatures), so the *people*, not the Convention, were the true
authors. This is the consent-of-the-governed theory from Lesson 01 doing
real work: legitimacy comes from ratification, not from following the old
rules.

### Worked example 1 — Same question, opposite answers

*Question:* "Can a republic covering thirteen states remain free?"

*Madison's reasoning chain:* Factions are inevitable → a small republic
lets one faction capture the whole → an extended republic multiplies
factions so none dominates → therefore a large republic is *safer* for
liberty. Key move: he *accepts* the Anti-Federalist premise (factions are
dangerous) and flips the conclusion.

*Brutus's reasoning chain:* Liberty requires representatives who share the
people's sentiments → a vast territory makes genuine representation
impossible (too few representatives for too many people, or an unworkable
mob) → distant rulers "will use" power to aggrandize themselves →
therefore a large republic *destroys* liberty. Key move: he attacks
Madison's mechanism — representation across a continent cannot actually
work.

*Evaluation:* Notice neither side argues "bigness is good/bad" as a slogan;
both argue about a *mechanism* (how factions behave at scale; how
representation functions at scale). In your project and deliberation, judge
arguments by their mechanisms, not their conclusions.

### Worked example 2 — Was the Convention authorized?

*Federalist-style argument:* The Convention proposed; it did not enact.
The Constitution takes effect only through ratification by the people in
each state. Under the social-contract theory, *that* popular consent is
what legitimizes the document — the Convention's instructions are
irrelevant because no convention can bind the people's right to re-found
their government (compare the Declaration's "alter or abolish" logic).

*Anti-Federalist-style argument:* The Convention was a legal body with
limited instructions, and it violated them. Worse, it changed the rules of
its own acceptance (nine states instead of thirteen). If those entrusted
with reforming the system break the system's rules to do it, the precedent
is that rules are optional for the powerful — a dangerous founding lesson.

*Evaluation:* Both arguments are internally consistent; they disagree
about *what legitimizes a founding* — procedure under old law, or consent
under new ratification. There is no referee here; your job is to weigh,
not to pick the "right" side before reading.

## Guided practice

1. **Two-column argument map.** For the question "Can a large republic stay
   free?", fill: *Side* | *Core claim* | *Mechanism (how/why)* |
   *Evidence or authority cited* | *Strongest point* | *Weakest point*.
   Use at least two rows per side (Madison: faction theory, extended
   republic; Brutus: representation at scale, power's tendency to grow).
2. **Authorization brief.** Write a 5-sentence legal brief *for one side*
   of the authorization question (assigned by the adult): was the
   Convention authorized to write a new Constitution? Use the
   consent-of-the-governed principle explicitly.

## Independent practice

3. Brutus warns that rulers "disposed to increase" power will subvert state
   authority. Madison says multiplying factions controls that danger. In
   4–5 sentences, stage their disagreement as a dialogue on *one* modern
   example the adult approves (e.g., a federal regulation both sides of a
   community dispute interpret differently). Each speaker gets at least two
   lines, and each line must contain a reason, not just a conclusion.
4. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "The Federalists wanted a king
   and the Anti-Federalists wanted democracy." Identify the two errors:
   what did each side actually want, and what is the real axis of their
   disagreement?

## Application

5. Madison's faction theory is a *mechanism* claim: more factions → no
   faction dominates. Test it: describe one real situation you have observed
   (a club, a team, a class, a community group) where adding more distinct
   viewpoints did or did not prevent one group from dominating. 4–5
   sentences, with the mechanism named.

## Exit check

6. In three sentences: state Madison's answer and Brutus's answer to the
   large-republic question, then name the single assumption they most
   disagree about.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Assign *excerpts*, not full essays — the adult
  selects roughly 400–600 words from each (Federalist No. 10's faction
  definition and extended-republic passage; Brutus No. 1's small-republic
  and power-growth passages), with vocabulary support. Full texts are
  linked for reference.
- **Alternate response mode:** The argument map may be dictated or built
  as a spoken comparison recorded by the adult.
- **Extension:** Read Federalist No. 51 (same UCF Pressbooks link) on
  checks and balances: how does Madison's "ambition must be made to
  counteract ambition" connect to his faction argument in No. 10?

## Teacher note

Guardrail: this debate maps *imperfectly* onto modern parties — do not let
the lesson become a proxy fight about current politics. The nonpartisan
rule: analyze 1787 arguments with 1787 evidence. Misconception: students
often assume the Federalists "won" the argument because the Constitution
was ratified — but the Anti-Federalists won the *Bill of Rights* (Lesson
06), and their warnings shaped two centuries of debate. Answer-key
reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
