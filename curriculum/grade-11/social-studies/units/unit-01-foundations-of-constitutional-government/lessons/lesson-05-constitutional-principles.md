# Lesson 05 — The Constitution's Core Principles: Designing Power That Limits Itself

**Goal:** Explain popular sovereignty, limited government, separation of
powers, checks and balances, and federalism — and trace one power through
the checks that restrain it. **Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:**
Constitution transcript excerpts (Preamble; Article I, §§1–8; Article II,
§1; Article III, §1); checks-diagram template.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** The Articles failed from *too little* power.
> Brutus fears *too much*. What design could thread that needle?

**Source:** [The Constitution of the United States: A
Transcription](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript),
National Archives (public domain; checked 2026-10-14).

## Teaching

The Constitution's answer to both fears is not a *slogan* about liberty —
it is **architecture**: the document organizes power so that power checks
power. Five principles carry the design:

1. **Popular sovereignty** — "We the People... do ordain and establish."
   All authority flows upward from the people, not downward from a crown.
   This is the social contract (Lesson 01) written into the first three
   words.
2. **Limited government** — the federal government has only the powers the
   Constitution *grants* it (the **enumerated powers** of Article I,
   Section 8: tax, borrow, regulate commerce, coin money, declare war, and
   more) — and Article I, Sections 9–10 list things it and the states
   *cannot* do. Power not granted is power not held.
3. **Separation of powers** — the granted powers are *divided* among three
   branches: Congress makes laws (Article I), the President executes them
   (Article II), the courts interpret them (Article III). No one branch
   holds the whole sword.
4. **Checks and balances** — separation alone is not enough, because each
   branch will try to expand (Brutus's warning). So each branch gets tools
   to *block* the others: the President can veto bills, but Congress can
   override with two-thirds; Congress makes laws, but the President
   executes them; both are checked by courts; the Senate confirms
   appointments and treaties. Madison's formula in Federalist No. 51:
   "ambition must be made to counteract ambition."
5. **Federalism** — power is divided *vertically* too: some powers belong
   to the national government (enumerated), some are **reserved** to the
   states (the Tenth Amendment, added with the Bill of Rights), and some
   are **concurrent** (shared, like taxation). Two levels of government,
   each limited, watch each other.

**Judicial review — the check that arrived later.** The Constitution never
says "courts may strike down laws." That power was claimed by the Supreme
Court in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803): if the Constitution is supreme law,
someone must decide when a statute conflicts with it — and that someone,
the Court said, is the judiciary. This is a designed-*change* story the
course returns to: the principles were in the text, but their full meaning
was built over time.

### Worked example 1 — Tracing a power through the checks

*Power:* Congress passes a law taxing a disfavored activity at a ruinous
rate. Trace the checks.

*Reasoning:* (1) **Limited government:** Is taxation an enumerated power?
Yes — Article I, §8 ("lay and collect Taxes"). So the power exists. (2)
**Separation + checks:** The bill must pass *both* House and Senate, then
go to the President, who may veto it — one person's check on the whole
legislature. If vetoed, Congress can override only with two-thirds in each
house — a deliberately high bar. (3) **Judicial check:** If enacted, a
citizen can sue, and courts may strike the law down if it violates a
constitutional limit (e.g., a Bill of Rights protection) — judicial review
in action. (4) **Federalism:** States cannot be commandeered to enforce
it; and the people may replace the lawmakers at the next election —
popular sovereignty as the final check. One power, five restraints.

### Worked example 2 — Where the design bends

*Scenario:* In wartime, Congress declares war (its power), the President
as commander-in-chief directs the campaign (his power), and courts decline
to second-guess battlefield decisions (their restraint).

*Reasoning:* The same architecture behaves differently under pressure:
checks *relax* rather than disappear. Congress still controls funding
("no Appropriation... for a longer Term than two Years" — Article I, §8),
so the legislature's check becomes the purse, not the strategy. The lesson:
checks and balances are not a machine that runs itself; they are tools
whose use depends on the people holding them. This is why the course
treats the principles as *contested* (D2.Civ.4.9-12), not settled.

## Guided practice

1. **Principle cards.** For each of the five principles, write: the name,
   a one-sentence definition in your own words, and the exact constitutional
   location (e.g., "separation of powers — Articles I, II, III").
2. **Trace-the-power diagram.** Choose one: (a) Congress declares war;
   (b) the President nominates a Supreme Court justice; (c) Congress
   regulates interstate commerce. Draw or list every check another branch
   (or the states, or the people) can apply to that power, citing the
   article/section or amendment for each. Minimum five checks.

## Independent practice

3. A classmate claims: "Checks and balances mean nothing can ever get
   done." Using your trace diagram, write a 4-sentence rebuttal:
   distinguish *blocking abuse* from *blocking action*, and name one
   check that slows action and one that stops abuse.
4. **Explain-your-reasoning.** Federalism divides power vertically;
   separation divides it horizontally. In 3 sentences, explain why the
   founders used *both* — what failure mode does each division guard
   against that the other misses?

## Application

5. Pick one current public institution you know well (your school board,
   a city council, a club with bylaws). Identify one separation-of-powers
   feature and one check it has — or one it *lacks* and should add.
   4–5 sentences, nonpartisan framing.

## Exit check

6. Name the five principles. Then, in one sentence: what problem is each
   principle an answer to? (Five short answers.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** The adult assigns short excerpts (Preamble; Article
  I, §8's power list; Article I, §7's veto/override; Article III, §1), not
  the full transcript. Vocabulary ("appropriation," "reprieal," "tribunal")
  is pre-taught.
- **Alternate response mode:** The trace diagram may be built as a spoken
  walk-through recorded by the adult, or as a poster.
- **Extension:** Read the *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) opinion excerpt the
  adult selects (public domain): reconstruct Chief Justice Marshall's
  three-question logic (does Marbury have a right? is there a remedy? can
  the Court order it?) and explain why the third answer created judicial
  review.

## Teacher note

Misconception: "The three branches are equal, so checks are symmetric."
They are not — the design is deliberately *asymmetric* (e.g., Congress can
override a veto, but the President cannot override an override; courts can
void laws, but Congress controls jurisdiction and the purse). Practice 2
surfaces this. Note on judicial review: present *Marbury* as the Court's
*claim* of the power (accepted into the system, but contested then and
debated since), not as textually obvious. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 05.
