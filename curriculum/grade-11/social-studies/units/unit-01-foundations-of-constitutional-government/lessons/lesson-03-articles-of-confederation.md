# Lesson 03 — The Articles of Confederation: A Government Designed to Be Weak

**Goal:** Explain the powers the Articles granted and withheld, and
evaluate why the confederation failed — including Shays's Rebellion and the
Annapolis Convention. **Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** National
Archives Articles page (linked); timeline handout (below).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** From the Declaration — governments exist to
> secure rights and need consent. What *powers* would such a government need
> to actually do the job?

**Sources:**
[Articles of Confederation](https://www.archives.gov/historical-docs/articles-of-confederation),
National Archives (public domain; adopted by Congress November 15, 1777; in
force March 1, 1781 – 1789; checked 2026-10-14).

## Teaching

After declaring independence, the states needed a government — but they
were terrified of creating another distant, powerful ruler. So they built
the **Articles of Confederation** (1781), a "confederation": a league of
sovereign states cooperating, not one nation with a supreme government.
Article II said it plainly: each state kept its "sovereignty, freedom, and
independence."

**What the Confederation Congress could do:** declare war and make peace,
send and receive ambassadors, make treaties and alliances, coin money,
establish post offices, admit new states, settle disputes between states,
and direct the war effort.

**What it could not do** — and this is the lesson's core list:

- **No power to tax.** Congress could *ask* states for money
  ("requisitions") but could not compel payment. States routinely paid
  late or not at all.
- **No power to regulate commerce** between states or with foreign nations.
  States set their own tariffs and trade barriers against each other.
- **No executive branch and no national courts.** Laws Congress passed had
  no enforcement machinery of their own.
- **Amendment required unanimous consent** of all thirteen states — a
  single state could block any fix.

**Why it failed — the evidence.** Three events turned theory into crisis:

1. **The debt and the money.** Congress owed massive Revolutionary War
   debts — to soldiers, to foreign lenders — and could not reliably raise
   revenue. It printed paper money that collapsed in value. A government
   that cannot pay its soldiers or its debts loses legitimacy fast.
2. **Shays's Rebellion (1786–1787).** Debt-crushed Massachusetts farmers,
   many of them war veterans, rose up under Daniel Shays, shutting down
   courts to stop farm foreclosures. The Confederation Congress could not
   raise troops or money to respond; Massachusetts put the rebellion down
   with a privately funded militia. To leaders like George Washington and
   James Madison, the episode proved the confederation could not even keep
   domestic order — the first job of any government.
3. **The Annapolis Convention (1786).** Only five states sent delegates to
   discuss interstate trade problems. Instead of despairing, the delegates
   (led by Alexander Hamilton) called for a broader convention in
   Philadelphia in 1787 to address the Articles' defects. That call became
   the Constitutional Convention.

The conclusion the founders drew: a government *too weak to act* fails the
social contract just as surely as a tyrannical one. The Articles protected
the states from a strong center — and left the center unable to protect
anyone's rights.

### Worked example 1 — The power gap

*Scenario:* In 1785, Spain closes the Mississippi River to American trade,
devastating western farmers. Congress wants to retaliate with a unified
trade policy and, if needed, a show of force.

*Reasoning:* Check the Articles' powers. Congress can negotiate (treaties
are a granted power), but it cannot *regulate commerce* — so no coordinated
trade retaliation. It can declare war, but it cannot *tax* to pay for an
army or *compel* states to supply troops — so no credible force. Result:
Congress protests, Spain ignores it, farmers suffer. The scenario shows the
structural point: granting a *goal* (protect commerce) without granting
the *tools* (taxation, regulation, enforcement) makes the power empty.

### Worked example 2 — Why unanimity was fatal

*Scenario:* In 1781, Congress proposes a 5% import tax to pay war debts.
Twelve states agree. Rhode Island refuses.

*Reasoning:* Under the Articles, amendments need all thirteen states. One
"no" kills the tax — which is historically what happened. Now apply the
social-contract test from Lesson 01: a government that cannot fund itself
cannot secure anyone's rights (soldiers unpaid, creditors unpaid, order
unkept). The unanimity rule meant the Articles could not *learn* — any
single state could veto the system's own repair. A constitution that cannot
be fixed is a constitution that cannot survive its first crisis.

## Guided practice

1. **Power table.** Build a three-column table: *Power* | *Granted to
   Congress under the Articles?* | *Consequence*. Fill in eight rows:
   tax, regulate interstate commerce, declare war, coin money, enforce
   laws, amend the Articles (with the rule), admit new states, settle
   disputes between states.
2. **Timeline reasoning.** Place on a line: 1776 (Declaration), 1777
   (Articles adopted by Congress), 1781 (Articles in force), 1786
   (Annapolis Convention; Shays's Rebellion begins), 1787 (Philadelphia
   Convention). Beneath each date from 1781 onward, write one sentence on
   what the confederation could or could not do at that moment.

## Independent practice

3. **Scenario task — govern under the Articles.** You are a member of the
   Confederation Congress in 1786. Two states are taxing each other's goods
   at their shared border, and merchants demand action. Write the
   Congress's response as a 4–5 sentence official statement — and then, in
   2 sentences, admit honestly what Congress *cannot* do about it.
4. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "The Articles failed because the
   founders were stupid." Rewrite this as a serious historical claim in two
   sentences: what were the founders *afraid of*, and how did that fear
   shape the design?

## Application

5. Design a one-question test for any constitution: "Can this government
   ______?" Your question must expose whether the government can both
   *protect rights* and *limit itself*. Then answer your own question for
   the Articles in 2–3 sentences.

## Exit check

6. List the three missing powers that mattered most, and for one of them,
   name the historical event that exposed the gap.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** The Articles' full text is dense 18th-century legal
  prose; this lesson teaches from the *power list*, not the full text. The
  adult may assign short excerpts (Articles II, III, VIII) with vocabulary
  support.
- **Alternate response mode:** The power table may be built as labeled
  cards sorted into "granted" and "withheld" piles.
- **Extension:** Research the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 (public domain;
  the Confederation Congress's one great achievement): what did it do
  about slavery in the Northwest Territory, and what does that tell you
  about what the Articles' Congress *could* accomplish?

## Teacher note

Misconception: "The Articles failed because Americans were bad at
governing." The design was a *deliberate choice* driven by fear of
centralized power after the British experience — the lesson's practice 4
targets this. Note also: Shays's Rebellion is sometimes mythologized;
present it factually (debt crisis, court shutdowns, privately funded
suppression) without romanticizing armed revolt — the adult frames it as
*evidence the founders cited*, which is its role here. Answer-key
