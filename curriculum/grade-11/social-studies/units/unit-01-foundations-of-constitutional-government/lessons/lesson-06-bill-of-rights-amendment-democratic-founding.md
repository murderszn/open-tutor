# Lesson 06 — The Bill of Rights, the Amendment Mechanism, and a Contested Founding

**Goal:** Explain the Bill of Rights as the price of ratification and the
Article V amendment mechanism, and assess — with evidence — whether the
Constitution was democratic in 1788. **Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:**
Bill of Rights text (first 10 amendments, via the Constitution transcript);
amendment-path worksheet.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Brutus feared concentrated power; Madison
> trusted structure. What *written guarantee* would satisfy Brutus's side?

**Source:** [The Constitution of the United States: A
Transcription](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript)
(amendments included; National Archives; public domain; checked
2026-10-14).

## Teaching

**The Bill of Rights was the price of ratification.** The Constitution's
original text contained few explicit protections for individual liberty —
and the Anti-Federalists made that the center of their campaign. In
several states, ratification passed only with the *understanding* that a
bill of rights would be added immediately. James Madison, who had thought
such a list unnecessary, drafted the amendments in the First Congress; ten
were ratified in 1791. The Anti-Federalists lost the ratification vote but
won the argument that power needs written limits: speech, religion, press,
assembly, arms, due process, trial rights, and the Ninth and Tenth
Amendments' reminders that the people's rights — and the states' powers —
are not limited to what is listed.

**The amendment mechanism (Article V) made the founding revisable.** Two
paths to *propose* (two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or a convention
called by two-thirds of the states) and two paths to *ratify*
(three-fourths of state legislatures, or three-fourths of state
conventions). The design is deliberately hard: change must be broad and
sustained, not a passing majority's whim. Result: 27 amendments in
230+ years. The mechanism matters for this unit's project: the founders
built a system that could *repudiate parts of the founding itself* — the
13th Amendment abolished the slavery the 1788 document protected; the 19th
extended the vote to women; the 14th rewrote the relationship between
citizens and states.

**Was the Constitution democratic in 1788?** Weigh the evidence both ways:

*The democratic case:* "We the People" as the source of authority;
ratification by special conventions rather than by legislatures (an appeal
over the heads of existing governments to the people); a House elected
directly by the people every two years; the amendment mechanism as an
ongoing consent procedure; and, by the standards of 1788 — when no large
country on earth was a republic — an extraordinary gamble on self-rule.

*The exclusion case:* "The People" who counted in 1788 were a fraction of
the population. Enslaved people — about one in five Americans — were
property under the law, and the Constitution protected the slave trade
until 1808 (Article I, §9) and counted enslaved persons at three-fifths
for representation (Article I, §2) — giving slaveholding states *extra*
political power from people denied every right. Women could not vote.
Native nations were dealt with as foreign peoples, not as participants.
Most states limited voting to property-holding or taxpaying men. The
document's universal language and its restricted practice sat side by side.

This unit does not resolve that tension by decree. It asks you to hold
both ledgers and argue from evidence — which is what the final
deliberation and the project require.

### Worked example 1 — Reading the amendment mechanism as consent

*Task:* Explain how Article V embodies the social contract.

*Reasoning:* (1) The contract is *revisable by the parties*: the people,
through supermajorities, can alter the terms — Locke's "alter or abolish"
made procedural. (2) The bar is *high on purpose*: two-thirds to propose,
three-fourths to ratify. This protects the contract from being rewritten
by a temporary majority — the same reason the Declaration counsels
"prudence" before changing long-established governments. (3) The *states*
ratify, preserving the federal bargain: the national people and the state
peoples must both consent. So Article V is the social contract with an
instruction manual.

### Worked example 2 — Evaluating the 1788 democratic claim

*Task:* Assess: "The Constitution was democratic because 'We the People'
ratified it."

*Reasoning:* Test the premise against the exclusion evidence. If "the
People" in 1788 excluded enslaved persons, women, Native peoples, and many
poor men, then the sentence is true only of a *subset* — the claim smuggles
in a definition of "the People" that does the real work. A stronger
formulation: the Constitution was *democratically radical for 1788* (no
monarch, no aristocratic house, popular ratification) and *democratically
narrow by the standards of its own universal language* (Lesson 02's
tension, now in institutional form). The honest answer uses both ledgers —
which is exactly what the deliberation rubric rewards.

## Guided practice

1. **Amendment paths.** Draw both proposal paths and both ratification
   paths as a flowchart with the fractions labeled. Beneath it, answer:
   why would the founders require *supermajorities* rather than simple
   majorities? (2–3 sentences, using the social-contract idea.)
2. **Two ledgers.** Build the table: *Evidence the 1788 system was
   democratic* | *Evidence it was not*. Minimum four entries per side,
   each with a source (article/section, amendment, or historical fact
   from this unit).

## Independent practice

3. The Ninth Amendment says unlisted rights are still retained by the
   people; the Tenth reserves unlisted powers to the states or the people.
   In 3–4 sentences, explain what fear each amendment answers — and whose
   argument from Lesson 04 (Madison's or Brutus's) each one vindicates.
4. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "The Bill of Rights was in the
   original Constitution." Correct the timeline in two sentences: what
   happened in 1787–88, and what happened in 1791 — and *why* the gap
   exists (whose demand created it?).

## Application

5. Choose one amendment from 11–27 (the adult confirms the choice).
   Research its story in 5–6 sentences: what problem prompted it, which
   Article V path it took, and what it changed about the founding bargain.
   Cite your source.

## Exit check

6. One paragraph (5–6 sentences): was the amendment mechanism a
   *strength* of the founding (it lets the people fix mistakes) or an
   *admission of failure* (a good founding wouldn't need fixing)? Take a
   position and give two reasons.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Assign the Bill of Rights text (short) and Article
  V (short); the adult glosses "redress," "quartering," "probable cause,"
  "bail."
- **Alternate response mode:** The two-ledgers table may be built orally
  as a structured debate prep with the adult scribing.
- **Extension:** Trace one failed amendment (e.g., the Equal Rights
  Amendment — proposed 1972, not ratified by the deadline): what does its
  failure show about where the Article V bar actually sits?

## Teacher note

Sensitivity: the exclusion evidence (slavery, the three-fifths clause, the
1808 protection) must be taught factually and directly — the lesson names
them as constitutional *provisions*, not vague "flaws." The adult frames
the deliberation so that no learner is asked to *defend* slavery or
exclusion; the deliberation question is about the *system's democratic
character*, argued from evidence on both sides. Misconception: "The Bill
of Rights fixed everything" — it constrained the *federal* government
only, at first (incorporation against the states comes in U03).
