# Teacher Guide — Unit 04: Elections, Representation, Participation, and Political Parties

**Audience:** the guiding adult. Learner-facing pages link here only
with clear labeling; this guide does not replace the answer key.
**Nonpartisanship rule (read first):** Teach institutions, processes,
and evidence. Do not endorse parties, candidates, or causes; do not
ask learners how they or their families vote in real elections; do
not let the mock election vote on real candidates. When a learner
brings a partisan claim to class, translate it into the unit's
framework: "Which rule, incentive, or data point would decide that
question?" Contested questions (keep or replace the Electoral
College? does money corrupt?) are argued from at least two sides with
evidence, and both sides are graded on reasoning, not on conclusion.

## Lesson notes

### L1 — Elections and the Electoral College

- Spend the most time on the sequence, not the trivia. Learners who
  can order nomination → general election → elector meeting →
  congressional count can place every later fact.
- The 2000 case is emotionally charged for adults, not for learners.
  Keep it mechanical: certified margin, recount order, equal-
  protection holding, 5–4 remedy, 271–266 result. If a learner asks
  "who really won?", answer with the distinction the case itself
  draws: the *certified* winner under the rules as applied, vs. the
  national popular-vote leader — different questions, different
  answers.
- **Misconceptions:** (1) "Voters elect the president directly" —
  correct to slate-of-electors. (2) "The Electoral College is a
  place" — correct to process. (3) "Winner-take-all is in the
  Constitution" — correct to state choice (Maine/Nebraska prove it).
- **Reteach:** If elector arithmetic fails, drill the formula
  (House + 2; DC = 3) with five fictional states before returning to
  the 270 threshold.

### L2 — Apportionment and redistricting

- The fictional maps are the lesson. Have learners recompute every
  total themselves; the "same voters, different winners" moment only
  lands when they do the addition.
- *Rucho* is routinely misreported. Drill the sentence: "The Court
  said federal courts cannot judge partisan gerrymandering — not
  that partisan gerrymandering is good, fair, or constitutional."
  Then name the remaining venues (state courts, legislatures,
  Congress, commissions) every time the case comes up.
- **Misconceptions:** (1) "Gerrymandering = miscounting votes" —
  correct to grouping. (2) "*Shelby County* repealed the Voting
  Rights Act" — correct to §4 formula struck, §2 intact, §5
  inoperative. (3) "Equal population = fair map" — correct: equal
  population is necessary, not sufficient; grouping still decides.
- **Reteach:** If packing/cracking blur, use coins on a table: 20
  coins, 4 rows, move coins between rows and recount "seats."

### L3 — Political parties

- Keep the 1912/1992 contrast quantitative: 27.4% → 88 electors vs.
  18.9% → 0. The mechanism (winning states vs. national share) is
  the whole lesson; do not let it become a debate about the
  candidates' personalities.
- The model platform/brief excerpts are **original simplified
  models**, not real documents. Say so before assigning, and replace
  them with adult-selected real excerpts (dated, with source) for the
  close read in Week 16, Session 4.
- **Misconceptions:** (1) "Parties are in the Constitution" —
  correct to extra-constitutional. (2) "Third parties never matter"
  — correct to agenda-setting and vote-splitting. (3) "Realignment
  happens every election" — correct to durable, generational shifts.
- **Reteach:** If Duverger's law confuses, contrast one
  single-member plurality race with one proportional allocation of
  10 seats by vote share, using the same votes.

### L4 — Interest groups, money, media

- The three-lane table (direct / independent / coordinated) should
  stay visible all week. Every new example gets classified into a
  lane before discussion.
- *Citizens United* vs. *SpeechNow*: drill the pair until learners
  can say which case did what. "Citizens United = independent
  spending protected; SpeechNow = contributions to independent-
  spending-only groups unlimited; together = Super PACs."
- Dark money: present both positions neutrally and grade the
  distinction (disclosed vs. undisclosed), not the verdict.
- **Misconceptions:** (1) "*Citizens United* created Super PACs" —
  correct to *SpeechNow* + FEC advisory opinions. (2) "Super PACs
  give unlimited money to candidates" — correct to no contributions,
  no coordination. (3) "Lobbying = bribery" — correct to information
  and access, which may still be criticized on other grounds.
- **Reteach:** If coordination blurs, use Worked example 1's pair:
  the *same ad* lawful or limited depending on one fact.

### L5 — Participation and theories

- Denominators first, every time: citizen voting-age population vs.
  voting-age population vs. registered voters. Write the denominator
  on the board before any percentage.
- Census figures are survey-based reported voting, not official
  counts. Say so when the table appears; the extension task (survey
  vs. official count) exists for learners who ask why numbers differ.
- Theories are lenses, not teams. A learner who applies all three to
  the same fact and says where each fails has mastered the lesson,
  even if their verdict differs from yours.
- **Misconceptions:** (1) "Turnout = share of all Americans" —
  correct to citizen voting-age population. (2) "Low turnout means
  people don't care" — correct to four explanations. (3) "Elite
  theory says elites are bad" — correct: it says elites decide;
  whether that is bad is a separate judgment.
- **Reteach:** If the theories blur, assign one theory per group and
  have each group explain the same fact (youth turnout gap) from its
  theory, then trade and critique.

## Week 18 synthesis sessions

- **W18S1 (theories revisited):** Re-run the Lesson 05 theories
  against the whole unit: which theory best explains party
  mobilization (pluralist?), map-drawing (elite?), and the mock
  election itself (participatory?). Accept argued answers.
- **W18S2 (contested-election source lab):** Use the National
  Constitution Center account plus one second reputable source
  (adult-selected, dated). The task is framing comparison, not
  verdict: what does each source emphasize, omit, or phrase
  differently? Grade the comparison, not agreement with either
  source.
- **W18S3 (mock election):** Enforce the rules sheet literally:
  neutral question, secret ballots, two counters, stated tie rule.
  If the question proves loaded, stop and rewrite it — that repair
  is a successful debrief, not a failed simulation.
- **W18S4 (defense):** Grade argument structure (claim, evidence,
  counterargument, reply) over conclusion. A well-argued minority
  position outscores a poorly argued majority one.

## Project support

- Part 1 fails most often on sourcing: learners substitute national
  figures for local ones or omit denominators. Require the evidence
  log before the brief is drafted; reject "not found" only after the
  learner shows where they looked.
- If a group cannot find local turnout data, approve a neighboring
  jurisdiction with published data rather than letting the group
  estimate.
- Reteach moves by rubric criterion: audit accuracy → re-do one
  figure with source, date, denominator; mock-election integrity →
  re-run the vote with the rules sheet; argument quality → add one
  counterargument paragraph; proposal → name the explanation and
  the measure; evidence log → source every claim in one section.

## Accessibility and safety

- All lessons: read key texts aloud in short clauses; gloss legal
  vocabulary before assigning; offer oral, drawn, or dictated
  responses wherever writing is not the assessed skill.
- Image activity: the text-only alternative in Lesson 05 carries the
  full question; no learner needs the image to answer.
- Mock election: participation is voluntary; no learner's vote is
  disclosed; results are reported in aggregate only. No real
  candidate or party appears on any ballot.
- No hazards, no fees, no accounts for core learning. Videos are
  optional supplements with search-link alternatives.
