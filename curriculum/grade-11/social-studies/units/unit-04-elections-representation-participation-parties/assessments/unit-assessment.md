# Unit Assessment — Unit 04: Elections, Representation, Participation, and Political Parties

**Time:** ~55 minutes. **Open unit materials** for Tasks 1–3; Task 4
is closed-book except the provided sources. Answers and scoring
guidance are in [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) only.

## Task 1 — Electoral arithmetic (Objectives 1–2; 25 points)

(a) A state has 9 House seats. How many electors does it have?
(b) Under winner-take-all, how many electoral votes does the
statewide winner receive? (c) Under the district method, the winner
takes the state and 6 of its 9 districts — how many electoral votes
does each candidate receive? (d) A second state has 1,800,000 people
and 4 House seats: ideal district population? A proposed district of
495,000 people is how many percent over the ideal, and is it
defensible for a congressional district?

## Task 2 — Document analysis: the Electoral College debate (Objectives 1, 6; 25 points)

Two modeled arguments (written for this assessment; not quotations
from any real person or organization):

> **Argument A (defense):** "The Electoral College forces candidates
> to campaign across regions instead of running up votes in a few
> large cities. It protects smaller states, whose minimum of three
> electors guarantees them a voice, and it confines recounts to
> individual states instead of triggering a national recount."

> **Argument B (criticism):** "The Electoral College lets the loser
> of the national popular vote win the presidency, as in 2000. It
> makes votes in safely one-party states irrelevant to the outcome
> and focuses campaigns on a handful of competitive states, leaving
> most voters ignored."

(a) State the strongest point in each argument in your own words.
(b) For each argument, name one fact from this unit that supports it
and one fact that weakens it. (c) Which argument is stronger, and why?
Your verdict must cite at least two unit facts.

## Task 3 — Data and image analysis (Objectives 2, 4, 5; 25 points)

(a) **Turnout table** (Census Bureau, citizen voting-age population,
reported voting): 2020: 66.8%; 2018: 53.4%; 2022: 52.2%; 2014: 41.9%.
In 2022, voters 18–29 were 11.7% of voters but 19.6% of the citizen
voting-age population; 49.8% of 2022 voters voted early or by mail,
vs. 39.8% in 2018. Answer: (i) the 2020→2022 drop in points; (ii) the
youth underrepresentation gap in points; (iii) whether overall
turnout or voting method changed more between 2018 and 2022, with
numbers; (iv) one explanation (resources, mobilization, rules,
attitudes) for the youth gap, defended in two sentences.
(b) **Image re-analysis:** Re-study the participation-scene
illustration from Lesson 05 (or its text-only alternative). Name the
five participation forms shown, and for each, state whether it
influences government directly or indirectly, with one reason.
(c) **Spending classification:** A corporation pays for an ad
opposing a candidate with no campaign contact; a traditional PAC
gives $5,000 to a candidate; a Super PAC uses a campaign's private
polling to design its ads. Classify each and state the governing
rule.

## Task 4 — Essay (Objectives 3, 6; 25 points)

**Prompt:** "Does the American system give each citizen an equal
voice?" Write 3–4 paragraphs arguing a position, using evidence from
at least three lessons (for example: apportionment and maps, party
and group influence, turnout gaps, democratic theories). Include at
least one counterargument and your reply to it, and end with one
reform or practice — lawful and nonpartisan — that would move the
system closer to your standard of equal voice, stating what standard
you are judging by.
