# Lesson 05 — Participation: Who Takes Part, and What Democracy Expects

**Goal:** Analyze participation patterns from dated Census Bureau data,
explain who participates and why, and compare participatory, pluralist,
and elite theories of democracy. **Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:**
The turnout table below; the generated illustration
[`assets/participation-scene.png`](../assets/participation-scene.png)
with its text-only alternative.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Name three ways to participate in
> politics that are not voting. (Answers include: contacting an
> official, volunteering for a campaign, protesting, donating,
> attending a public meeting, community service, boycotting, or

**Sources:** U.S. Census Bureau, *Voting and Registration in the
Election of November 2022* (Current Population Survey, Voting and
Registration Supplement), via the Census Bureau's release
([govdelivery bulletin](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USCENSUS/bulletins/397ff84),
search-result verified 2026-11-03). All figures below are
**survey-based reported voting among citizen voting-age population**,
not official vote counts; percentages are rounded as reported.

## Teaching

Voting is the most visible form of participation, and the least
common one in most people's lives. This lesson puts voting in its
place: one item on a menu, used unevenly, for reasons we can measure —
and then asks what democracy *expects* of citizens, in three competing
accounts.

### The participation menu

Citizens (and, for some forms, non-citizen residents) can:

- **Vote** in primaries, generals, and local elections.
- **Contact** officials — letters, calls, emails, town-hall questions.
- **Campaign** — volunteer, canvass, donate, run for office.
- **Protest and petition** — marches, boycotts, ballot initiatives
  where states allow them.
- **Associate** — join parties, interest groups, unions, civic
  associations (Lesson 04's actors).
- **Serve the community** — volunteer work, neighborhood associations,
  school boards and local commissions.

Most people do one or two of these; very few do all six. The question
is not "why doesn't everyone do everything?" but "who does what, and
what does the pattern mean for representation?"

### The turnout data: what the Census Bureau found

| Election | Citizens who reported voting | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 (presidential) | 66.8% | Highest presidential-year turnout of this century (Census survey measure) |
| 2018 (midterm) | 53.4% | Highest midterm turnout in four decades at the time |
| 2022 (midterm) | 52.2% | Second-highest midterm turnout in two decades; 1.2 points below 2018 |
| 2014 (midterm) | 41.9% | A low-turnout midterm for comparison |
| 2010 (midterm) | 45.5% | An earlier midterm baseline |

Three patterns matter. **First, the presidential–midterm gap:** turnout
falls by roughly 15 percentage points from a presidential election to
the next midterm (66.8% → 52.2% is a 14.6-point drop). Midterm
electorates are therefore smaller — and different — than presidential
ones, which is why control of Congress can swing while the presidency
does not. **Second, the age gap:** in 2022, voters ages 18–29 were
11.7% of all voters but 19.6% of the citizen voting-age population —
underrepresented by 7.9 points — while voters 65 and older were 30.4%
of voters but 23.8% of the population — overrepresented by 6.7 points.
Elected officials rationally pay more attention to groups that vote.
**Third, the method shift:** 49.8% of 2022 voters cast ballots early
or by mail, up from 39.8% in 2018 — the *method* changed even as the
overall rate barely moved. And registration reached 69.1% of citizens
in 2022, the highest midterm registration rate since at least 2002:
more people were registered, but registration alone does not produce
a vote.

### Why people participate — four explanations

1. **Resources:** Education, income, and free time predict
   participation. People with more schooling vote more, contact
   officials more, and donate more — partly because civic skills are
   taught, partly because politics feels more navigable.
2. **Mobilization:** People participate when asked — by parties,
   groups, friends, and campaigns (Lessons 03–04). A competitive race
   with heavy mobilization draws voters a quiet race does not.
3. **Rules:** Registration deadlines, mail-voting availability, early-
   voting periods, and polling-place access vary by state and affect
   who finds voting easy or hard. Rules do not explain everything —
   high-registration states still have non-voters — but they shape
   the margins.
4. **Attitudes:** **Political efficacy** — the belief that
   participation can matter — plus trust, interest, and a sense of
   civic duty. Someone who believes "my vote doesn't count" is
   describing an attitude, not a fact about arithmetic; the attitude
   still predicts the behavior.

No single explanation wins. A complete account of any turnout gap uses
at least two: resources *and* mobilization, or rules *and* attitudes.

### Three theories of democracy

What is a citizen *supposed* to do? Three classic answers:

- **Participatory democracy** expects broad, direct engagement: citizens
  should vote, deliberate, join, and decide — and a system is healthy
  to the extent ordinary people actually govern. Low turnout is, on
  this view, a democratic failure.
- **Pluralist democracy** expects competition among organized groups:
  citizens participate mainly through groups that bargain, lobby, and
  check one another (Robert Dahl's account). Low individual turnout
  matters less if groups represent real interests and compete openly.
- **Elite democracy** expects a small leadership circle to decide,
  with citizens mainly choosing *which* elites rule (and removing them
  at elections). On this view, low participation is normal — even
  functional — because most citizens rationally leave complex policy
  to specialists. Critics reply that "functional" low participation
  leaves elites unaccountable.

These are theories, not verdicts. The unit assessment asks you to apply
all three to the same evidence and say which fits best — and where
each one fails.

### Worked example 1 — Compute the gaps

*Task:* Using the table: (a) What is the presidential–midterm turnout
gap between 2020 and 2022? (b) By how many points were 18–29-year-olds
underrepresented among 2022 voters? (c) What changed more between
2018 and 2022 — overall turnout, or the share voting early/by mail?

*Reasoning:* (a) 66.8% − 52.2% = **14.6 percentage points** — the
standard midterm drop. (b) 19.6% (share of population) − 11.7%
(share of voters) = **7.9 points** underrepresented. (c) Overall
turnout fell 53.4% − 52.2% = 1.2 points, while early/mail voting rose
49.8% − 39.8% = 10.0 points. The *method* changed far more than the
*rate* — a warning against reading one number as the whole story.

### Worked example 2 — Three theories, one fact

*Task:* Youth turnout (18–29) lags older voters by a wide margin.
What would each theory say — and what would each one miss?

*Reasoning:* A **participatory** theorist says the gap is a failure:
young citizens are not being brought into self-government, and the
system should educate and mobilize them. A **pluralist** theorist says
the gap matters only if young people's *interests* lack organized
representation — youth groups, student associations, and campaigns
targeting young voters can compensate for low individual turnout. An
**elite** theorist says the gap is expected: young people have less
political experience and rationally defer to older, more engaged
voters, while retaining the power to vote elites out later. Each
account fits part of the evidence; none explains why youth turnout
*rose* in 2018 and 2020 — for that, you need Lesson 05's four
explanations (mobilization and competitiveness, above all).

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Using the table: (a) rank the five elections by turnout;
   (b) compute the 2018→2022 change in overall turnout and in
   early/mail share; (c) state in one sentence what the comparison
   proves.
2. **G2.** Do Image Activity A, parts (1) and (2), in writing.
3. **G3.** A town's youth turnout doubles after a registration drive
   at schools. Which explanation — resources, mobilization, rules,
   or attitudes — fits best, and which second explanation would you
   check before claiming victory?

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** A state adopts same-day registration and its turnout rises
   3 points. A critic says "rules don't matter; attitudes do." Write
   a two-sentence reply using the four explanations.
2. **I2.** Apply all three theories to this fact: a city council
   election draws 18% turnout, but a neighborhood association with
   400 members regularly fills council hearings. Which theory is
   most reassured by the hearings, and which is most troubled by the
   18%? Why?
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "52.2% turnout in 2022 means 52.2% of
   Americans voted." Identify the error (who counts in the
   denominator?) and correct it in two sentences.
4. **I4.** Design one lawful, nonpartisan intervention to raise youth
   turnout in your fictional community, and name which explanation it
   targets and how you would measure whether it worked.

## Applied task

**A.** Write a one-page "participation profile" of a fictional
community of 10,000 citizens: invent plausible turnout, registration,
and age-breakdown figures (labeled fictional), then write two
paragraphs — one explaining the pattern with two of the four
explanations, one applying two of the three theories to judge whether
the community's democracy is healthy. End with the one additional
fact you would need before giving a confident verdict.

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) state the presidential–midterm gap
with numbers; (2) name the four explanations for participation;
(3) state one difference between pluralist and elite democracy.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Pre-teach *denominator* (who counts in a
  percentage), *overrepresented/underrepresented*, and *efficacy*
  before the table work; read the table row by row.
- **Alternate response mode:** The image activity may be answered
  orally from the text-only alternative; the profile task may be a
  poster instead of an essay.
- **Extension:** Compare the Census Bureau's survey-based turnout
  with an official-vote-count measure (for example, a state election
  office's turnout report) for one election, and explain in three
  sentences why the two numbers differ (survey over-reporting,
  different denominators: citizens vs. all residents vs. registered
  voters).

> **Teacher note:** The persistent misconception is that "turnout"
  has one denominator. Correct it every time: citizen voting-age
  population, voting-age population (including non-citizens), and
  registered voters give different percentages for the same election.
  Full notes and all answers are in [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md)
  and [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).
