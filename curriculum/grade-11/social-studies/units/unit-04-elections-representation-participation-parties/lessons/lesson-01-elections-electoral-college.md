# Lesson 01 — How American Elections Work: Primaries, Generals, and the Electoral College

**Goal:** Explain the nomination and election sequence and the
Electoral College's mechanics, including the 538 electors, the 270
threshold, winner-take-all allocation, and the Maine and Nebraska
district method. **Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** Constitution
transcript excerpts (Article II, §1; 12th, 23rd Amendments); elector
arithmetic worksheet (see Guided Practice).

## Teaching

An American presidential election is not one election. It is a
sequence, and each step has its own rules, its own voters, and its own
stakes. Confusing the steps is the most common source of confusion
about the whole system, so learn the sequence first.

### Step 1 — Nomination: primaries, caucuses, conventions

Before voters choose between parties, each party chooses its nominee.
States run **primaries** (ballot elections) or **caucuses** (in-person
meetings where participants discuss and vote, sometimes in rounds).
Primary rules vary by state: in a **closed primary**, only voters
registered with that party may vote in it; in an **open primary**, any
registered voter may choose one party's ballot; **semi-open** (or
hybrid) rules sit between, typically letting unaffiliated voters choose
a party ballot while registered partisans vote only in their own
party's primary. Parties also set their own delegate rules, and states
set their own calendars within party and national limits — so the
order, rules, and even the type of contest differ from state to state.
That variation is a feature of federalism, not an accident: the
Constitution leaves election administration largely to the states.

Winning primaries and caucuses earns **delegates** to the party's
national convention, where the nominee is formally chosen. A candidate
who cannot win a majority of delegates cannot win the nomination,
however popular they look in national polls — the nomination is won
state by state, under state rules.

### Step 2 — The general election: 51 contests, not one

The general election for president is held on the **first Tuesday
after the first Monday in November** of every fourth year (federal
offices are on the ballot in even years; the presidency every four).
But voters do not directly elect the president. Article II, §1 says
each state appoints electors "in such Manner as the Legislature
thereof may direct." Today every state chooses its electors by popular
vote — but the vote in each state picks that state's **slate of
electors**, pledged to a candidate, and those electors cast the votes
that formally choose the president. There are 51 separate contests
(50 states plus the District of Columbia), not one national tally.

### Step 3 — The Electoral College: the arithmetic

Each state's electors equal its members of Congress: its House seats
(which depend on population, reapportioned after each census) plus its
two senators. The District of Columbia gets three electors under the
23rd Amendment (1961). So:

- House seats: 435
- Senate seats: 100
- District of Columbia: 3
- **Total electors: 538**
- **Votes needed to win: 270** — a majority of 538.

**Winner-take-all:** In 48 states and the District of Columbia, the
candidate who wins the most votes in the state receives *all* of its
electoral votes, even by a single vote. This is a state-level choice,
not a constitutional requirement. **The exceptions:** Maine and
Nebraska use the **congressional district method** — one electoral
vote to the winner of each congressional district, and the state's two
remaining (Senate-based) votes to the statewide winner. That is why
those two states sometimes split their electoral votes between
candidates.

**The calendar after Election Day:** electors meet in their state
capitals in mid-December and cast separate ballots for president and
vice president (the 12th Amendment, ratified 1804, requires separate
ballots). Congress then counts the votes in joint session in early
January. The Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 clarified that the
Vice President's role in the count is ministerial (ceremonial, not
decisive) and raised the objection threshold to one-fifth of each
chamber. If no candidate reaches 270, the **contingent election**
backup applies: the House chooses the president from the top three
electoral-vote winners, with each state delegation casting one vote,
and the Senate chooses the vice president from the top two. This has
happened twice — 1800 and 1824 — and not since.

### Why the system is contested

Two positions frame the whole debate, and Week 15's investigation
argues both. **Defenders** say the College forces candidates to build
broad geographic coalitions, protects smaller states' weight (every
state gets at least three electors regardless of population), and
keeps the count contained within states. **Critics** say it lets the
electoral-vote winner lose the national popular vote (as in 2000 and
2016), makes votes in safely one-party states matter less for the
outcome, and focuses campaigns on a handful of competitive states.
Neither side is settled by this lesson: the unit's job is to give you
the mechanics accurately enough to judge the arguments yourself.

### Worked example 1 — Compute a state's electors

*Task:* A state has 7 House seats. How many electors does it have?
How many would it have if it used Maine's method and a candidate won
the state but lost one of its 7 districts?

*Reasoning:* Electors = House seats + 2 senators = 7 + 2 = **9**.
Under winner-take-all, the statewide winner takes all 9. Under the
district method, the statewide winner takes the 2 Senate-based votes
plus one vote per district won: 6 districts won = 6 votes, plus 2 =
**8**, while the other candidate takes the remaining district's **1**
vote. Same state, same voters, different allocation rule — which is
exactly why the rule matters.

### Worked example 2 — When 537 votes decided a presidency

*Task:* In 2000, Florida's certified margin was 537 votes out of
nearly 6 million cast. Florida carried 25 electoral votes. Explain
how that margin decided the presidency — and why the national popular
vote told a different story.

*Reasoning:* The Florida Supreme Court ordered a statewide manual
recount of "undervote" ballots. In *Bush v. Gore*, 531 U.S. 98 (2000),
seven justices agreed the recount's varying standards violated equal
protection, and a 5–4 majority held no recount could be completed by
the December 12 "safe harbor" deadline — so the recount stopped and
the certified result stood. Florida's 25 electoral votes gave George
W. Bush 271 electoral votes to Al Gore's 266 — one more than the 270
threshold. Nationally, Gore led the popular vote by roughly 537,000
votes. Under winner-take-all, Florida's 537-vote state margin delivered
all 25 of its electoral votes, and those votes — not the national
total — decided the presidency. (Source: National Constitution
Center, ["On this day, Bush v. Gore settles 2000 presidential
race"](https://constitutioncenter.org/blog/on-this-day-bush-v-gore-anniversary),
search-result verified 2026-11-03; certified margin and 271–266
result corroborated by multiple sources the same day.)

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** A state has 13 House seats. (a) How many electors does it
   have? (b) Under winner-take-all, how many electoral votes does a
   candidate get for winning the state by 100 votes? (c) Under the
   district method, how many would that candidate get if they won the
   state and 9 of its 13 districts?
2. **G2.** In your own words, why is it wrong to say "Americans vote
   directly for the president"? Name the two steps (state popular vote
   → electors → Congress count) in order.
3. **G3.** A candidate has 268 electoral votes with one 4-electoral-vote
   state left to call. What happens if they win it? What happens if
   they lose it and no candidate reaches 270?

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** Compute the electors for: (a) a state with 2 House seats
   (the minimum); (b) a state with 28 House seats; (c) the District of
   Columbia. Show the formula each time.
2. **I2.** Explain the 12th Amendment's separate-ballot rule: what
   problem from the elections of 1796 and 1800 did it fix? (Hint: under
   the original rules, each elector cast two votes without saying
   which was for president.)
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "Maine and Nebraska are the only states
   where the popular-vote loser can win electoral votes." Identify
   the error and correct it in two sentences. (Hint: think about what
   winner-take-all does in *every* state.)
4. **I4.** A learner says the Electoral College is "undemocratic
   because small states get extra weight." Write a two-sentence reply
   that (a) concedes the arithmetic and (b) gives the defenders'
   strongest response — without taking a side yourself.

## Applied task

**A.** Build a 270-path: pick any five states' real electoral-vote
totals from an adult-verified current apportionment table (see the
resource pack's Electoral College references) and show a combination that
reaches exactly 270 or more. Then write one sentence explaining which
step of the sequence — primaries, general election, elector meeting,
congressional count — your path depends on most, and why.

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) state the 538/270 arithmetic; (2)
explain winner-take-all and name its two exceptions; (3) state what
happens if no candidate reaches 270.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Pre-teach *slate*, *pledged*, *contingent*,
  *ministerial* before the Article II reading; read §1 aloud in short
  clauses.
- **Alternate response mode:** Elector arithmetic may be done with a
  calculator, a number line, or orally instead of in writing.
- **Extension:** Research which two elections were decided by
  contingent election (1800, 1824) and why each reached the House.
  What rule change after 1800 made a repeat less likely?
