# Lesson 04 — Interest Groups, Campaign Money, and the Media

**Goal:** Explain how interest groups and the media influence politics,
and the campaign-finance rules after *Citizens United* and
*SpeechNow*: traditional PACs, Super PACs, independent expenditures,
coordination, and disclosure. **Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:**
The spending-classification table in Guided Practice; the iron-
triangle diagram in Worked example 2 (draw it as three boxes with
arrows).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Lesson 03's brief asked a council for
> evening library hours. That is one group's tactic. Name two other
> ways an organized group can try to influence government without
> running a candidate. (Answers include: lobbying officials directly,
> suing in court, mobilizing members to contact officials, funding
> ads, organizing a boycott, or publishing research.)

## Teaching

Parties compete to *win government*. Interest groups compete to
*influence* whoever wins. That difference explains almost everything
in this lesson — including why the same group may lobby both parties,
and why campaign money follows rules that look nothing like ordinary
spending.

### Interest groups: who they are and what they do

An **interest group** is an organized body of people who share a goal
and try to influence public policy: business associations, labor
unions, professional associations, public-interest groups (environment,
civil liberties, consumer protection), single-issue groups, and
governments themselves (a city lobbying its state, a state lobbying
Congress). Their tactics:

- **Lobbying** — meeting officials, testifying, supplying research
  and draft language. Lobbyists' most valuable asset is often not
  persuasion but information: a busy legislator needs a credible
  briefing, and groups provide it.
- **Litigation** — suing to change or block policy (Unit 03's
  strategy, used by movements as well as businesses).
- **Grassroots mobilization** — getting members to contact officials,
  attend hearings, or protest. A thousand letters signal what one
  lobbyist cannot.
- **Electioneering** — endorsing, volunteering for, and spending for
  or against candidates (the money rules below).
- **Going public** — shaping the news agenda so officials feel pressure
  to act (see "the media" below).

Two models describe how groups relate to government. The **iron
triangle** is a stable three-way alliance: a congressional committee,
a government agency, and an interest group, each giving the others what
they need (funding and friendly oversight, favorable rules,
information and political support) — and each resisting outsiders.
The **issue network** is looser and larger: many groups, experts,
journalists, and officials who argue over one policy area without a
stable alliance. Iron triangles explain stability; issue networks
explain why some policy areas never settle.

### Campaign money: three lanes, three rulebooks

Federal campaign finance has three main lanes. Confusing them is the
most common error in this topic, so keep them apart:

1. **Direct contributions to candidates and parties.** Individuals
   may give limited amounts: in the 2025–2026 cycle, $3,500 per
   election to a federal candidate and $5,000 per year to a
   traditional PAC. Corporations and unions may **not** give treasury
   money directly to federal candidates at all. Traditional PACs —
   political committees that pool contributions — may give a
   candidate $5,000 per election if they qualify as multicandidate
   committees. These limits are indexed for inflation in odd-numbered
   years; the adult checks the current FEC chart before any
   dollar-figure exercise.
2. **Independent expenditures.** Money spent *for or against* a
   candidate **without coordinating** with that candidate's campaign
   — ads, mailers, digital campaigns. In *Citizens United v. FEC*
   (2010), the Supreme Court held 5–4 that the First Amendment bars
   the government from restricting independent expenditures by
   corporations and unions: independent spending, the majority said,
   does not create the corruption risk that justifies limiting direct
   contributions. Disclosure and disclaimer rules (ads must say who
   paid for them) were upheld.
3. **Super PACs.** Two months after *Citizens United*, the D.C.
   Circuit held in *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (2010) that if independent
   expenditures cannot corrupt, then contributions *to groups that
   only make independent expenditures* cannot corrupt either. The
   Federal Election Commission therefore recognizes **independent
   expenditure-only committees** — Super PACs — which may accept
   **unlimited** contributions from individuals, corporations,
   unions, and other PACs, spend unlimited amounts on independent
   expenditures, and register with the FEC and disclose their donors.
   What they may **not** do is contribute to candidates or coordinate
   with them: coordinated spending is legally reclassified as an
   in-kind contribution, subject to the ordinary limits. That single
   rule — independence — is the whole legal basis for unlimited Super
   PAC money.

**"Dark money"** is the debate's shorthand for political spending by
nonprofit groups (often organized under section 501(c)(4) of the tax
code) that do not disclose all their donors. Critics argue undisclosed
spending hides who is influencing elections; defenders argue donors
have a privacy interest and that disclosure can invite retaliation.
The unit presents both positions: the facts (some groups disclose,
some do not, under rules courts and the FEC continue to contest) are
settled; the judgment is not.

### The media: agenda-setting, framing, and the horse race

The media influence politics in three ways that are easy to confuse:

- **Agenda-setting:** telling the public *what* to think about — which
  issues lead the news — even without telling them what to think.
  An issue the media ignore rarely becomes a legislative priority.
- **Framing:** presenting an issue in terms that favor one
  interpretation (a "tax relief" frame vs. a "revenue loss" frame for
  the same bill; a protest framed as "unrest" vs. "democracy in
  action").
- **Horse-race coverage:** reporting elections as contests — who is
  ahead in polls, who raised more money, who "won" a debate — rather
  than as policy choices. Horse-race coverage is cheap to produce and
  easy to follow, which is why it dominates; its cost is that voters
  learn more about the race than about the stakes.

Interest groups exploit all three: they stage events to make the
agenda, supply the frame in press releases, and feed the horse race
with polls and fundraising totals. A media-literate citizen asks, for
every story: who supplied this frame, what did the story leave out,
and would the other side describe the same event this way?

### Worked example 1 — Classify the spending

*Task:* Classify each as (a) a direct contribution, (b) an independent
expenditure, or (c) coordinated spending (legally an in-kind
contribution): (1) A voter gives $100 to a candidate's campaign.
(2) A Super PAC airs an ad attacking a candidate, designed without
any contact with the opponent's campaign. (3) A Super PAC airs an ad
using the candidate's own unreleased footage, obtained after a
private strategy meeting with the campaign.

*Reasoning:* (1) **Direct contribution** — money to the campaign
itself, subject to the per-election limit. (2) **Independent
expenditure** — expressly advocates against a candidate, no
coordination, so it is unlimited and lawful for a Super PAC.
(3) **Coordinated spending** — the private meeting and the shared
footage show coordination, so the ad is reclassified as an in-kind
contribution to the campaign and subject to contribution limits.
The *same ad* is lawful or limited depending on one fact:
coordination. That is why the coordination rule is the most litigated
line in campaign finance.

### Worked example 2 — Draw the iron triangle

*Task:* A farm-state congressional committee, the Department of
Agriculture, and a farm association interact every year. Draw the
triangle and label what each side gives the others.

*Reasoning:* The committee gives the agency its budget and friendly
oversight hearings; the agency gives the committee programs to claim
credit for and the association favorable rules; the association gives
the committee campaign support and votes, and the agency technical
information and public backing. Each side benefits, so each side
defends the arrangement — which is why an outsider (say, a consumer
group wanting different farm policy) finds three doors closed at once.
An issue network, by contrast, would show many competing groups and
experts arguing in public, with no stable three-way deal.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Classify: (a) a union's PAC gives $5,000 to a candidate;
   (b) a corporation pays for an ad supporting a candidate, with no
   contact with the campaign; (c) a nonprofit runs ads without
   disclosing its donors. Name the lane and the rule for each.
2. **G2.** A Super PAC claims it "cannot be limited because of
   *Citizens United*." Correct the claim in two sentences: which case
   actually created Super PACs, and what limit still applies to them?
3. **G3.** Identify the media effect: (a) every network leads with
   the same factory closing; (b) one outlet calls a bill "relief,"
   another calls it a "giveaway"; (c) a week of stories about poll
   numbers with no policy comparison.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** A candidate's campaign manager meets privately with a
   Super PAC's director, then the PAC launches ads using the
   campaign's slogan and schedule. (a) What is the legal problem?
   (b) What happens to the spending's classification? (c) Why might
   enforcement still be difficult in practice?
2. **I2.** Explain why a group might prefer lobbying to electioneering,
   and why another group might prefer electioneering to lobbying.
   Give one reason for each, tied to the group's goal.
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "Super PACs can give unlimited money
   directly to candidates." Identify the error and correct it in two
   sentences, citing the coordination rule.
4. **I4.** Find the frame: two headlines describe the same protest —
   "Crowd demands action at city hall" and "Mob disrupts council
   meeting." (a) What does each frame imply? (b) What fact would you
   check before accepting either frame?

## Applied task

**A.** Choose one real policy issue (adult-approved, current, and
nonpartisan in presentation). Map its influence landscape: name one
interest group on each side, one lobbying tactic each would plausibly
use, one media frame each would prefer, and one kind of campaign
spending that could enter the debate. End with one paragraph: which
actor in your map has the most influence, and what evidence would
change your mind?

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) state what a Super PAC may and may not
do; (2) name the two 2010 cases and what each decided; (3) distinguish
agenda-setting from framing.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Pre-teach *treasury funds* (a corporation's own
  money, as opposed to a separate PAC's donations), *in-kind* (a
  non-cash contribution, like free services), and *disclaimer* (the
  "paid for by" statement on an ad).
- **Alternate response mode:** The iron-triangle diagram may be
  drawn, built with labeled cards, or described in a paragraph.
- **Extension:** Look up one current FEC enforcement action or
  advisory opinion (adult-selected) and explain which lane and which
  rule it concerns. Report the rule, not a verdict on the parties.
