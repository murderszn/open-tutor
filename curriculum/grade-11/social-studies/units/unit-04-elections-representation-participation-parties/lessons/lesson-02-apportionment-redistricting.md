# Lesson 02 — Apportionment, Redistricting, and Fair Representation

**Goal:** Compute a district's ideal population and analyze
apportionment and redistricting — including one-person-one-vote,
packing and cracking, and the court limits from *Shelby County* and
*Rucho*. **Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** The fictional-state
maps below (practice data, not real maps); calculator optional.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** A state has 8 House seats and a
> population of 3,200,000. What is its ideal district population?
> (Answer: 3,200,000 ÷ 8 = 400,000 people per district.)

**Sources:** Constitution, Article I, §2 (census and apportionment)
and Article I, §4 (Elections Clause), via the National Archives
transcription (see Lesson 01); case holdings summarized from Oyez
search links in [resource-pack.md](../resource-pack.md) — the adult
confirms each case page before assigning.

## Teaching

Lesson 01 showed that House seats — and therefore electors — depend on
population. This lesson follows the population from the census to the
district map, and asks what "fair representation" means when lines are
drawn by politicians.

### Apportionment: dividing 435 seats

Every ten years the census counts the population. The 435 House seats
are then **apportioned** — divided among the states roughly in
proportion to population, with every state guaranteed at least one
seat. States that grew gain seats; states that grew slowly lose them.
Within a state, the legislature (or, in some states, an independent
commission) then **redistricts**: it draws the boundaries of that
state's House districts, plus state legislative districts, so each
district holds roughly the same number of people.

The **ideal district population** is simple division: state population
÷ number of districts. For congressional districts, the Supreme Court
requires districts to be as nearly equal in population as practicable
— a district 10% over the ideal is not "close enough" for Congress.
The principle has a name: **one person, one vote**, from *Baker v.
Carr* (1962), which opened redistricting to court review, and
*Reynolds v. Sims* (1964), which required state legislative districts
to be substantially equal in population. The idea is that each
person's vote should weigh about the same as any other person's.

### Gerrymandering: packing and cracking

Drawing lines is power. A **gerrymander** is a district map drawn to
favor a party or group. Its two classic techniques use the same voters
to produce different winners:

- **Packing:** Concentrate the other side's voters into a few districts
  they win by huge margins — "wasting" their surplus votes.
- **Cracking:** Spread the other side's voters thinly across many
  districts so they lose narrowly everywhere — "wasting" their votes
  in defeats.

Neither technique changes a single vote. It changes which votes are
grouped together. Worked example 1 proves it with arithmetic.

### What courts will and will not police

Three limits frame the legal debate — and they point in different
directions, which is why the debate continues:

1. **Race:** Drawing districts predominantly because of race violates
   the Equal Protection Clause (*Shaw v. Reno*, 1993). The Voting
   Rights Act's Section 2 also bans voting practices that deny
   minority voters an equal opportunity to elect candidates of their
   choice. But a state may consider race in limited ways (for example,
   to comply with Section 2), and courts struggle to separate racial
   from partisan motives when the two overlap.
2. **Preclearance, ended:** The Voting Rights Act's Section 5 once
   required certain jurisdictions with histories of discrimination to
   get federal approval *before* changing voting rules or maps. In
   *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), the Supreme Court struck down the
   Section 4 coverage formula that chose those jurisdictions, which
   made the preclearance requirement inoperative. Section 2's
   nationwide ban on racial discrimination in voting remains in force
   — but it works through lawsuits *after* a map is used, not approval
   *before*.
3. **Partisanship:** In *Rucho v. Common Cause* (2019), the Supreme
   Court held 5–4 that partisan-gerrymandering claims are
   **nonjusticiable** in federal court — "political questions" with no
   judicially manageable standard for how much partisanship is too
   much. The Court did *not* declare partisan gerrymandering fair or
   constitutional; it said federal courts cannot be the venue. State
   courts (under state constitutions), state legislatures, and
   Congress remain venues — and several states now use independent
   redistricting commissions, compactness and contiguity rules, or
   bans on partisan data in map-drawing.

### Worked example 1 — Same voters, different winners

*Task (fictional practice data — not a real state):* A state has 4
districts of 100 voters each: 220 voters favor Party A (55%) and 180
favor Party B (45%). Map 1: D1 A 90–B 10; D2 A 60–B 40; D3 A 55–B 45;
D4 A 15–B 85. Map 2: D1 A 70–B 30; D2 A 60–B 40; D3 A 45–B 55; D4
A 45–B 55. Who wins each map, and what does the comparison prove?

*Reasoning:* First check the totals: Map 1: A = 90 + 60 + 55 + 15 =
220; B = 10 + 40 + 45 + 85 = 180. ✓ Map 2: A = 70 + 60 + 45 + 45 = 220;
B = 30 + 40 + 55 + 55 = 180. ✓ Same voters. Map 1 seats: A wins D1,
D2, D3 (3 seats); B wins D4 (1 seat) — A takes 75% of seats on 55% of
votes. Map 1 packs B voters into D4 (85 of them, far more than needed
to win) and cracks the rest across districts B loses. Map 2 seats: A
wins D1, D2; B wins D3, D4 — 2 seats each, 50% of seats on 55% of votes.
Neither map is perfectly proportional, but the comparison proves the
point: with identical voters, the map-drawer can choose between a 3–1
and a 2–2 result. Representation depends on the lines, not just the
votes.

### Worked example 2 — Is a district "equal enough"?

*Task:* A state of 3,200,000 people has 8 House seats (ideal: 400,000
each). One proposed district holds 440,000 people; another holds
360,000. Evaluate both under the congressional standard.

*Reasoning:* 440,000 is 40,000 over the ideal — 10% over. 360,000 is
40,000 under — 10% under. A vote in the 360,000-person district
weighs more than a vote in the 440,000-person district, because fewer
voters share one representative. For congressional districts the
standard is "as nearly equal as practicable," so a 10% deviation
needs a justification the map-drawer can rarely supply — small,
unavoidable differences (a whole county that cannot be split cleanly)
are tolerated; 40,000 people are not a rounding error. (State
legislative districts are judged more leniently than congressional
ones — a distinction the adult flags before any state-map exercise.)

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** A state has 6 House seats and 3,000,000 people. (a) Ideal
   district population? (b) A proposed district has 550,000 people.
   How many percent over the ideal is it, and does it look defensible
   for a congressional district?
2. **G2.** Label each technique: (a) putting 90% of the other party's
   voters in one district; (b) splitting a city's voters across four
   suburban districts so they lose each one narrowly. Which is
   packing, which is cracking?
3. **G3.** After *Rucho*, a voter challenges a map as a partisan
   gerrymander in federal court. What does the court do, and where
   *can* the voter take the challenge instead? Name two venues.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** Recompute Worked example 1 with one change: move 10 Party A
   voters from D2 to D4, and 10 Party B voters from D4 to D2. New D2:
   A 50–B 50; new D4: A 25–B 75. (a) Does the seat count change?
   (b) What does a 50–50 district show about "wasted" votes?
   (Note: a tie is decided by state tie-break rules, not by this
   lesson — the point is the knife-edge, not the winner.)
2. **I2.** Explain in your own words why *Shelby County* did not
   repeal the Voting Rights Act: what did it strike, and what
   survived?
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "After *Rucho*, partisan gerrymandering is
   legal, so nothing can be done about any map." Identify two errors
   in that sentence and correct each.
4. **I4.** A state adopts an independent commission and bans the use
   of partisan voting data in map-drawing. Which problem from this
   lesson does each reform target, and what problem does neither
   reform solve?

## Applied task

**A.** Draw two maps of a fictional 4-district state (100 voters per
district, Party A 55% statewide — use the totals from Worked example
1). Draw one map that gives Party A 3 seats and one that gives the
parties 2 seats each. Label each district's vote split, show your
totals, and write two sentences: which map is "fairer," and by what
standard are you judging — proportionality, competitiveness, or
something else? Defend your standard.

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) define apportionment and redistricting;
(2) explain packing and cracking in one sentence each; (3) state
what *Rucho* decided and what it did not decide.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Pre-teach *apportion*, *contiguity*
  (districts connected, not split into pieces), *compactness*, and
  *justiciable* before the case summaries.
- **Alternate response mode:** Map tasks may be drawn on paper, built
  with counters, or described orally district by district.
- **Extension:** Look up (adult-verified, dated) whether your own
  state draws maps by legislature, commission, or court — and whether
  its state constitution has been used to challenge a map since
  *Rucho*. Report the process, not your opinion of the current map.

> **Teacher note:** The persistent misconception is that
> gerrymandering means "cheating by counting votes wrong." Correct it:
> the votes are counted correctly; the *grouping* of voters is what is
> contested. Full notes and all answers are in
> [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
> [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).
