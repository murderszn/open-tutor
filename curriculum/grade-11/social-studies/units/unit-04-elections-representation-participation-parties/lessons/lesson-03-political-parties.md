# Lesson 03 — Political Parties: Functions, Realignment, and Third Parties

**Goal:** Explain parties' functions, why the United States has a
durable two-party system, what realignment means, and why third-party
and independent candidates face structural barriers. **Time:** ~55
minutes. **Materials:** The two model excerpts below (original,
simplified models — not real party or group texts); the 1912/1992
comparison table in Guided Practice.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** The Constitution never mentions
> political parties. So where did they come from — and why did they
> appear within a decade of ratification? (Answer: leaders who
> disagreed about the new government's powers organized voters and
> lawmakers to win elections and govern; parties are extra-
> constitutional, not constitutional, institutions.)

**Sources:** Case and election figures verified 2026-11-03 (1912:
Roosevelt 27.4% of the popular vote, 88 electoral votes; 1992: Perot
18.9%, 0 electoral votes); party-function and realignment framing
from standard civics references via the
[resource-pack.md](../resource-pack.md) search links — the adult
confirms the reference before assigning.

## Teaching

Parties are the most criticized and most durable institutions in
American politics. Criticized, because they simplify complex choices
into team loyalty; durable, because they solve problems no individual
voter or lawmaker can solve alone. Judge them by what they *do*.

### What parties do: five functions

1. **Nominate.** Parties recruit candidates, run primaries, and put a
   name on the ballot. Without parties, every election would be a
   free-for-all of hundreds of names.
2. **Structure choice.** A party label is a shortcut: it tells a busy
   voter, roughly, what a candidate stands for and which coalition
   they will join in government. Shortcuts can mislead — that is a
   cost — but without them, most voters could not evaluate hundreds
   of races.
3. **Organize government.** In legislatures, parties choose leaders,
   assign committees, and schedule votes. A majority party can pass a
   program; a minority party can block, amend, or publicize an
   alternative.
4. **Mobilize.** Parties register voters, knock on doors, raise money,
   and turn supporters out on Election Day. Much of turnout (Lesson
   05) is party mobilization, not spontaneous civic virtue.
5. **Provide accountability.** A party's record — what it did in
   power — gives voters something to reward or punish at the next
   election, even when individual candidates are unfamiliar.

A **platform** is the party's written statement of positions, adopted
at its convention. Platforms matter less as promises (parties break
them) than as evidence: they show what coalition the party is trying
to hold together, and which issues it would rather avoid.

### Why two parties? Structure, not just habit

The United States has had two dominant parties for most of its
history, and the reason is structural. Elections for most offices use
**single-member districts with plurality winners**: one seat, and the
candidate with the most votes wins it — even without a majority. That
rule punishes small parties twice: a third party can win 20% of votes
nationwide and 0% of seats (Lesson 02's logic, applied to parties).
Political scientists call this tendency **Duverger's law**: plurality
rules tend to produce two parties; proportional rules tend to produce
many. Add ballot-access laws (signature thresholds that vary by
state), debate thresholds, the Electoral College's winner-take-all
rule, and campaign-finance realities, and a new party faces a wall
before it faces a voter.

### Realignment: when the coalitions break

Parties are not permanent. A **realignment** is a durable shift in
which groups support which party, usually around a crisis or a
transformative issue. The classic cases: 1860 (slavery and the
Republican Party's rise), 1896 (industrialization and the
urban-rural, gold-silver divides), and 1932 (the Depression and the
New Deal coalition). More recently, the South's shift from solidly
Democratic to mostly Republican across the 1960s–1990s — driven by
civil rights, among other issues — realigned a whole region's party
identity over a generation, not a single election. Realignment is
slow, contested, and visible only in hindsight: in the moment it
looks like ordinary politics.

### Third parties and independents: influence without winning

Third parties rarely win, but they are not irrelevant. They raise
issues the major parties ignore, force major parties to absorb their
ideas (and sometimes their voters), and occasionally decide an
election by splitting a vote. Two cases make the point:

| Election | Candidate | Popular vote | Electoral votes | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1912 | Theodore Roosevelt (Progressive/"Bull Moose") | 27.4% | 88 | A former president, splitting from his old party, finished *second* — ahead of the incumbent — and still lost. Even a famous candidate cannot beat plurality rules. |
| 1992 | Ross Perot (independent) | 18.9% | 0 | The strongest non-major-party popular-vote showing since Roosevelt — and zero electoral votes, because winner-take-all rewards winning *states*, not national percentages. |

Notice the contrast: 27.4% won 88 electoral votes because Roosevelt
won *states*; 18.9% won none because Perot won *no* state, finishing
second in two. The Electoral College does not reward national
popularity; it rewards state victories.

### Close read: a platform model and an interest-group brief

The two excerpts below are **original simplified models written for
this lesson** — not quotations from any real party or group. They
show the *form* of each document so you can analyze real ones (adult-
selected, dated) with the same questions: who wrote it, for whom,
what does it promise, what does it avoid saying?

> **Model platform plank (fictional Party Q):** "We believe every
> community deserves reliable public libraries. We support extended
> evening and weekend hours, funded by existing state aid, and we
> oppose closing any branch without a public hearing."

> **Model interest-group brief (fictional Library Readers' League):**
> "Evening hours matter most to shift workers and students, who
> cannot visit during the day. We ask the council to fund evening
> hours in all three branches and to publish branch-by-branch
> attendance so the public can judge the result."

Same issue, different jobs: the platform states a broad position for
a whole coalition (and names a funding source, "existing state aid,"
without saying whose aid gets cut); the brief makes a specific ask to
a specific body, names the affected group, and demands measurable
follow-up. Lesson 04 takes up interest groups in full.

### Worked example 1 — Which function is this?

*Task:* A party's state committee registers 20,000 new voters, runs
ads for its gubernatorial candidate, and — after winning — assigns
its members to chair every major committee. Name the functions.

*Reasoning:* Registering voters and running ads are **mobilization**
(turning supporters into voters) plus **nomination** support (the
candidate is already nominated; the ads now sell the choice).
Assigning committee chairs is **organizing government** — the party
using its majority to control the legislature's work. One party, one
election cycle, three functions: parties are not just labels.

### Worked example 2 — Why Perot's 18.9% bought nothing

*Task:* Perot won 18.9% of the national popular vote in 1992 and 0
electoral votes. Roosevelt won 27.4% in 1912 and 88 electoral votes.
Explain the difference in one mechanism.

*Reasoning:* Winner-take-all. Roosevelt's votes were concentrated
enough to *win states* (six of them, plus second place nationally);
Perot's votes were spread across states he never won — second in two
states, first in none. Under winner-take-all, second place in a state
earns the same electoral votes as last place: zero. National share
without state victories is, electorally, nothing — which is why
third-party strategy focuses on winning somewhere, not polling well
everywhere.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Match each action to a party function: (a) adopting a
   platform at a convention; (b) the majority party electing a
   Speaker; (c) a party's volunteers driving supporters to the
   polls; (d) voters punishing a governing party after a recession.
2. **G2.** Compare 1912 and 1992 in a two-row table: candidate,
   popular-vote share, electoral votes, and one sentence on what
   each case proves about plurality rules.
3. **G3.** Read the two model excerpts above. (a) Which document
   names a specific decision-maker, and which speaks to a general
   coalition? (b) What does the platform *avoid* saying about
   funding? (c) What does the brief demand that the platform does
   not?

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** Explain Duverger's law in your own words, then apply it:
   why would a proportional-representation system (seats awarded by
   national vote share) produce more parties than the American system?
2. **I2.** A new party wins 15% in every state and no electoral
   votes. A critic says "the system is rigged." A defender says "the
   system worked as designed." Write one sentence each side would
   accept as fair — then one sentence of your own judgment.
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "Realignment means voters switch parties
   every election." Identify the error and correct it, using one
   real case (1860, 1896, 1932, or the Southern realignment).
4. **I4.** A party platform promises "lower taxes, better schools,
   and safer streets" with no funding plan. Using the Lesson 03
   framework, explain why a voter should read that plank as coalition
   evidence rather than as a promise — in three sentences.

## Applied task

**A.** Write a five-plank platform for a fictional student-government
party, then write a one-paragraph interest-group brief from a
fictional club asking that party for one specific thing. Label which
sentences are platform language (broad, coalition-wide) and which are
brief language (specific ask, named decision-maker, measurable
follow-up). Trade with a partner (or the adult) and check each
other's labels.

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) name three party functions; (2) state
Duverger's law; (3) explain why Perot's 18.9% won no electoral votes
while Roosevelt's 27.4% won 88.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Pre-teach *coalition*, *plurality* (most votes,
  not necessarily a majority), and *incumbent* before the 1912/1992
  table.
- **Alternate response mode:** The platform/brief task may be
  dictated, drawn as a poster, or written — the labeling, not the
  format, is assessed.
- **Extension:** Choose one realignment case and trace one group's
  party loyalty across it (for example, a region's presidential
  voting before, during, and after the shift) using an adult-verified,
  dated source. Report the pattern, not a verdict on the parties.

> **Teacher note:** The persistent misconception is that parties are
> in the Constitution or that third parties "never matter." Correct
> both: parties are extra-constitutional, and third parties matter by
> agenda-setting and vote-splitting even when they lose. Full notes
> and all answers are in [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
> [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).
