# Unit 04 — Elections, Representation, Participation, and Political Parties

**Grade:** 11 · **Subject:** Social Studies (U.S. government and civics)
**Track weeks:** 15–18 · **Backlog:** [issue #53](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/53), section U04
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Units 01–03 asked where government authority comes from, how the
Constitution organizes it, and which rights limit it. This unit asks
the next question: who gets to choose the people who exercise that
authority, how much does each citizen's voice count, and what else —
besides voting — counts as participating? The answers run through
primaries and the Electoral College, district maps, party
organizations, interest groups and campaign money, and the turnout
data. The unit is nonpartisan throughout: institutions and processes
are analyzed without endorsing parties, candidates, or causes, and
every contested question is argued from at least two sides with
evidence.

## Essential question

Who decides who governs — and how much does each citizen's voice count?

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–03 and the track diagnostic weeks (weeks 1–2):

- Explain federalism and the separation of powers, and trace one power
  through the checks that restrain it.
- Explain the amendment process and the rights in conflict from
  Unit 03, including the 15th, 19th, 24th, and 26th Amendments and the
  Voting Rights Act of 1965.
- Classify a source by kind and infer its maker and audience; write a
  claim supported by at least two pieces of evidence, with a
  counterclaim; read a data table and compute a percentage-point
  difference.
- Read founding-era and legal English with vocabulary support (the
  adult pre-teaches terms before every primary-source reading; see the
  track [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md), §2).

No cross-grade learner links: these are described skills, not references
to other grades' lessons.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain** the nomination and election sequence — primaries and
   caucuses, conventions, the general election — and the Electoral
   College's mechanics: 538 electors, the 270 threshold,
   winner-take-all allocation, the Maine and Nebraska district method,
   and the contingent-election backup. (D2.Civ.1.9-12; D2.Civ.2.9-12)
2. **Compute** a state's electors and a district's ideal population,
   and **analyze** apportionment and redistricting — including
   one-person-one-vote, packing and cracking, and the holdings of
   *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) and *Rucho v. Common Cause*
   (2019). (D2.Civ.1.9-12; D2.Civ.2.9-12)
3. **Explain** political parties' functions, why the United States has
   a durable two-party system, what realignment means, and why
   third-party and independent candidates face structural barriers —
   using 1912 and 1992 as evidence. (D2.Civ.1.9-12)
4. **Explain** how interest groups and the media influence politics,
   and the campaign-finance rules after *Citizens United v. FEC*
   (2010) and *SpeechNow.org v. FEC* (2010): traditional PACs,
   Super PACs, independent expenditures, coordination, and disclosure.
   (D2.Civ.1.9-12; D2.Civ.10.9-12)
5. **Analyze** participation patterns from dated Census Bureau data —
   presidential vs. midterm turnout, age and registration gaps — and
   **evaluate** resource, mobilization, rules, and attitudes
   explanations for who participates. (D2.Civ.2.9-12; D2.Civ.14.9-12)
6. **Compare** participatory, pluralist, and elite theories of
   democracy; **evaluate** a contested election from two reputable
   sources; and **run** a nonpartisan mock election on a policy
   question with rules, a tally, and a debrief.
   (D2.Civ.2.9-12; D2.Civ.7.9-12; D3.1–D3.4.9-12; D4.1–D4.4.9-12)

In every unit the learner also develops compelling questions
(D1.1–D1.5.9-12), evaluates sources and their limits (D3.1–D3.4.9-12),
and communicates evidence-based conclusions (D4.1–D4.4.9-12).

Primary sources are quoted in brief public-domain excerpts with links:
the Constitution (National Archives transcription) and U.S. Supreme
Court opinions (U.S. government works, public domain). Election data
come from the U.S. Census Bureau's Voting and Registration Supplements
(survey-based, reported-voting figures — not official vote counts) and
are dated wherever they appear; the adult re-verifies current rules,
maps, and officeholders at the time of use, because election rules,
district maps, and contribution limits change. Current-data claims in
this unit are dated November 2026 or earlier and are framed around
institutional roles and rules, not partisan commentary. All party
platform and interest-group excerpts in Lesson 03 are original,
clearly labeled simplified models — not quotations from any real
party or group; the adult selects real, dated excerpts before
assigning the close read.

## 20-session pacing (4 weeks × 5 sessions, ~45–60 minutes each)

Sessions rotate: core lesson → practice → investigation/reading →
argument/deliberation → review. The project launches in Week 16,
Session 4 and runs across the investigation sessions; the mock
election runs in Week 18, Session 3, and its debrief doubles as the
project defense.

Success criteria: by W15S5 the learner can sequence an election, compute
electors, and explain the 270 threshold; by W16S5 the learner can
explain party functions and classify campaign spending under the
post-2010 rules; by W17S5 the learner can read turnout data, compute
gaps, and apply all three democratic theories; by W18S5 the learner
can evaluate a contested election from two sources, defend an
evidence-based position on equal voice, and has delivered the project
brief and mock-election tally.
