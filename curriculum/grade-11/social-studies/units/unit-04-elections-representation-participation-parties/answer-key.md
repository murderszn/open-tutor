# Answer Key — Unit 04: Elections, Representation, Participation, and Political Parties

**For the guiding adult only.** Every guided-practice, independent-
practice, exit-check, practice-task, quiz, and assessment item is
solved here; applied tasks carry sample responses because more than
one answer is defensible. If a learner's answer differs from this key
in wording but matches it in reasoning, accept it.

## Lesson 01 — Elections and the Electoral College

**G1.** (a) 13 + 2 = **15 electors**. (b) **All 15** — winner-take-all
gives the statewide winner every electoral vote, by any margin.
(c) District method: 2 Senate-based votes + 9 district votes =
**11** for the statewide winner; **4** for the opponent.
**G2.** Americans vote for a slate of electors pledged to a candidate;
the electors meet in state capitals in mid-December and vote; Congress
counts the votes in joint session in early January. The state popular
vote decides *which slate* becomes electors — it does not itself elect
the president.
**G3.** Winning the 4-vote state: 268 + 4 = **272 — wins outright**.
Losing it: the candidate stays at 268; if no candidate reaches 270,
the **contingent election** applies — the House chooses the president
from the top three electoral-vote winners, one vote per state
delegation; the Senate chooses the vice president from the top two.
**I1.** (a) 2 + 2 = **4**. (b) 28 + 2 = **30**. (c) **3** (23rd
Amendment).
**I2.** Under the original rules each elector cast two undifferentiated
votes; in 1796 the runner-up (Jefferson, the president's opponent)
became vice president, and in 1800 Jefferson and his running mate Burr
tied 73–73, sending the election to the House (36 ballots). The 12th
Amendment requires **separate ballots** for president and vice
president, so a tie between running mates cannot recur that way.
**I3.** Error: Maine and Nebraska are the only states that *split*
electoral votes — not the only states where the national popular-vote
loser can win electoral votes. Correction: in every winner-take-all
state the state winner takes all its votes, so a candidate can lose the
national popular vote yet win the presidency by carrying states — as
in 2000, when Bush won 271 electoral votes to Gore's 266 while trailing
nationally by roughly 537,000 votes.
**I4 (sample).** "The arithmetic is real: every state gets two
Senate-based electors regardless of population, so small states weigh
more per voter. Defenders reply that the Senate-based bonus forces
candidates to build geographically broad coalitions and guarantees
small states a voice rather than letting a few large states decide."
**Applied A (sample).** Five-state path (2020-apportionment totals,
adult-verified): California 54 + Texas 40 + Florida 30 + New York 28 +
Pennsylvania 19 = 171 — not enough; add Ohio 17 + Georgia 16 + Michigan
15 + North Carolina 16 + New Jersey 14 + Virginia 13 + Washington 12 +
Arizona 11 + Tennessee 11 + Indiana 11 = 307 total, which clears 270.
The path depends most on the **general election** step: winning those
states' popular votes is what delivers their winner-take-all slates.
**Exit E.** (1) 538 = 435 House + 100 Senate + 3 for DC; 270 is a
majority. (2) Winner-take-all gives all of a state's electoral votes
to its popular-vote winner; Maine and Nebraska instead award one vote
per congressional district plus two to the statewide winner.
(3) Contingent election: House chooses the president (one vote per
state delegation, top three candidates), Senate chooses the vice
president (top two).

## Lesson 02 — Apportionment and redistricting

**G1.** (a) 3,000,000 ÷ 6 = **500,000**. (b) 550,000 is 50,000 over
500,000 = **10% over**; for a congressional district, which must be
as nearly equal as practicable, 10% is not defensible without an
extraordinary justification.
**G2.** (a) **Packing** — concentrating the other side's voters into
one district they win overwhelmingly. (b) **Cracking** — spreading
them thinly so they lose narrowly in several districts.
**G3.** The federal court dismisses the partisan-gerrymandering claim
as nonjusticiable under *Rucho*. Remaining venues: **state courts**
under state constitutions, **state legislatures**, and **Congress**
(which may legislate for congressional redistricting under the
Elections Clause).
**I1.** (a) Yes — the count changes from 3–1 to **2–1 with D2 tied
50–50** (A loses its D2 win; D4 stays B). (b) A 50–50 district wastes
almost nothing in the usual sense: every vote is pivotal, and a tiny
shift flips the seat — which is why competitive districts are prized
by reformers and feared by map-drawers. (Tie-break by state rule;
the lesson grades the knife-edge, not the winner.)
**I2.** *Shelby County* struck the **Section 4 coverage formula**,
which made the **Section 5 preclearance** requirement inoperative for
the covered jurisdictions. It did not repeal the Act: **Section 2's**
nationwide ban on racial discrimination in voting remains in force,
enforced through lawsuits after a map is used rather than approval
before.
**I3.** Error 1: *Rucho* did not declare partisan gerrymandering legal
or fair — it held federal courts lack a manageable standard, i.e.,
the claims are nonjusticiable. Error 2: "nothing can be done" is
false — state courts, state legislatures, Congress, and independent
commissions remain venues for reform.
**I4.** A commission targets **who draws** the lines (removing
legislators' self-interest); banning partisan data targets **what
information** the drawer uses. Neither reform solves racial-
gerrymandering detection, incumbency protection, or the splitting of
communities of interest — and neither guarantees proportional or
competitive outcomes.
**Applied A (sample).** 3-seat map: D1 A 90–B 10, D2 A 60–B 40,
D3 A 55–B 45, D4 A 15–B 85 (totals A 220, B 180; A 3 seats). 2–2 map:
D1 A 70–B 30, D2 A 60–B 40, D3 A 45–B 55, D4 A 45–B 55 (totals A 220,
B 180; 2 seats each). Sample judgment: "The 2–2 map is fairer by
proportionality — 55% of votes producing 50% of seats is closer to
the vote share than 75% of seats — though proportionality is not the
only defensible standard; competitiveness would judge differently."
**Exit E.** (1) Apportionment divides the 435 House seats among
states by population; redistricting draws district boundaries within
a state. (2) Packing concentrates opponents into few districts they
win by huge margins; cracking spreads them thinly so they lose
narrowly in many. (3) *Rucho* held partisan-gerrymandering claims
nonjusticiable in federal court; it did not declare the practice fair
or constitutional, and state venues remain open.

## Lesson 03 — Political parties

**G1.** (a) **Nominate / structure choice** — the platform states the
party's positions for voters (both labels acceptable if defended).
(b) **Organize government**. (c) **Mobilize**. (d) **Accountability**
(voters punishing a governing party's record).
**G2.** 1912: Roosevelt (Progressive), 27.4%, 88 electoral votes, 6
states won, finished second. 1992: Perot (independent), 18.9%, 0
electoral votes, 0 states won. Lesson: plurality + winner-take-all
reward winning states, not national share.
**G3.** (a) The **brief** names a decision-maker (the council); the
**platform** speaks to a general coalition. (b) The platform says
hours are "funded by existing state aid" without saying what loses
that aid or whether the aid suffices. (c) The brief demands published
branch-by-branch attendance — measurable follow-up the platform does
not require.
**I1 (sample).** Duverger's law: single-member plurality elections
tend to produce two parties, because small parties win votes but no
seats and voters abandon them strategically. Under proportional
representation, a party winning 15% of votes wins roughly 15% of seats,
so small parties survive and multiply.
**I2 (sample).** Critic's fair sentence: "A system where 15% of voters
in every state earn no representation looks unresponsive." Defender's
fair sentence: "A system that rewards winning states, not national
percentages, is doing what it was designed to do." Own judgment must
pick a standard (proportionality, stability, or competitiveness) and
apply it — grade the standard's use, not the verdict.
**I3.** Error: realignment is not per-election switching; it is a
durable, generational shift in group loyalties. Example: the South's
shift from solidly Democratic to mostly Republican across the
1960s–1990s, driven substantially by civil rights and related issues.
**I4 (sample).** "A plank without a funding plan is coalition
evidence: it shows which promises the party thinks will hold its
supporters together. It is not a budget, and a voter who reads it as
one will be disappointed by arithmetic the plank never attempted."
**Exit E.** (1) Any three: nominate, structure choice, organize
government, mobilize, provide accountability. (2) Duverger's law:
plurality single-member elections tend to produce two parties.
(3) Perot's votes were spread across states he never won (second in
two, first in none), earning zero winner-take-all electors; Roosevelt's
votes were concentrated enough to win six states and 88 electors.

## Lesson 04 — Interest groups, money, media

**G1.** (a) **Direct contribution** — a traditional PAC giving to a
candidate, capped ($5,000 per election for a multicandidate PAC).
(b) **Independent expenditure** — unlimited, lawful because there is
no coordination. (c) **Dark money** — political spending by a nonprofit
without full donor disclosure; critics: it hides who influences
elections; defenders: donors have a privacy interest.
**G2.** Correction: *Citizens United* protected independent
expenditures; **SpeechNow.org v. FEC** (2010) created Super PACs by
extending that logic to contributions. The limit that still applies:
Super PACs may not contribute to candidates or coordinate with them.
**G3.** (a) **Agenda-setting**. (b) **Framing**. (c) **Horse-race
coverage**.
**I1.** (a) The meeting + shared slogan/schedule show **coordination**.
(b) The spending is reclassified as an **in-kind contribution** to the
campaign, subject to contribution limits. (c) Enforcement is difficult
because coordination must be proved (not merely inferred from similar
ads), and enforcement often concludes after the election.
**I2 (sample).** Lobbying suits a group wanting a technical rule
changed: it has expertise and access, and a quiet meeting beats a
public fight. Electioneering suits a group that cannot get a hearing
from current officeholders: changing *who* decides is faster than
persuading those in office.
**I3.** Error: Super PACs cannot give money directly to candidates at
all. Correction: Super PAC money is unlimited only for **independent
expenditures**; the coordination ban is what keeps that money from
becoming a contribution.
**I4.** (a) "Demands action" frames the crowd as legitimate democratic
participation; "mob disrupts" frames it as disorder and threat.
(b) Check: size, permits, conduct, whether the meeting continued, and
whether the description comes from an observer or a participant —
before accepting either frame.
**Exit E.** (1) A Super PAC may accept unlimited contributions and
spend unlimited amounts on independent expenditures; it may not
contribute to candidates or coordinate with them. (2) *Citizens
United* (2010): independent expenditures by corporations/unions
protected. *SpeechNow* (2010): contributions to independent-
expenditure-only groups unlimited. (3) Agenda-setting decides *what*
the public thinks about; framing decides *how* an issue is presented.

## Lesson 05 — Participation and theories

**G1.** (a) 2020 (66.8%) > 2018 (53.4%) > 2022 (52.2%) > 2010 (45.5%)
> 2014 (41.9%). (b) Overall turnout: 53.4 − 52.2 = **1.2 points down**;
early/mail share: 49.8 − 39.8 = **10.0 points up**. (c) The *method*
of voting changed far more than the *rate* of voting.
**G2.** Left to right: (1) **voting** — direct (it decides the
outcome); (2) **contacting an official** — direct petition of a
decision-maker (accept "indirect" if the learner argues it does not
itself decide — grade the reason); (3) **community service /
volunteering** — indirect (builds community capacity, not a decision);
(4) **peaceful protest** — indirect (shapes opinion and pressure);
(5) **voter registration** — indirect (enables future voting).
Borderline case (sample): contacting is the best borderline — it
reaches a decision-maker directly but decides nothing by itself.
**G3.** **Mobilization** fits best (the drive asked people, in person,
where they are). Check **rules** second: was registration the actual
barrier, or were these students already registered and merely
unasked? (Resources/attitudes are weaker fits for a sudden doubling.)
**I1 (sample).** "Rules matter at the margin — same-day registration
removes a deadline barrier, and 3 points is a real effect. But rules
alone do not explain why most registered non-voters still stay home;
attitudes and mobilization must explain the rest, or the critic's
point stands for the majority."
**I2.** **Pluralist** theory is most reassured: an organized
association actively represents a real interest and fills hearings,
which is pluralism working. **Participatory** theory is most troubled:
12% turnout means the formal decision is made by a small fraction of
those affected. (Elite theory is comparatively untroubled: a small
active group plus low mass turnout is its normal picture.)
**I3.** Error: the denominator is the **citizen voting-age
population**, not "Americans" — it excludes people under 18 and
non-citizen residents. Correction: 52.2% of *citizens 18 and older*
reported voting in the Census survey; the share of *all residents*
would be lower, and official counts use yet other denominators.
**I4 (sample).** Intervention: a nonpartisan registration + candidate-
forum program at schools and workplaces, targeting **mobilization**
(being asked, with information). Measure: registration and turnout
among 18–29-year-olds in the next comparable election vs. the previous
one, with a comparison community that got no program.
**Exit E.** (1) The presidential–midterm gap 2020→2022 is 66.8% −
52.2% = **14.6 percentage points**. (2) Resources, mobilization,
rules, attitudes. (3) Pluralist democracy: competing organized groups
bargain and check one another. Elite democracy: a small leadership
circle decides, while citizens mainly choose and remove elites.

**Task 8.** (a) Agenda-setting. (b) Framing. (c) Horse-race coverage.

**Task 9.** (a) 2020 > 2018 > 2022 > 2010 > 2014.
(b) 66.8 − 52.2 = **14.6 points**. (c) 49.8 − 39.8 = **+10.0 points**.
(d) "Collapsed" is misleading: 2022 (52.2%) was only 1.2 points below

**7.9 points** underrepresented.
**Task 10.** (a) **Pluralist** theory is most reassured: an organized

(b) **Participatory** theory is most troubled: 12% turnout means
formal decisions rest on a small fraction of the affected public.
(c) **Mobilization** fits best: the same population turns out for
meetings (where the association asks and organizes) but not for the
election (where no comparable mobilization reaches them) — sample
defense: "The gap tracks who was asked, not who cares: the association
asks its members every month, while the election asked once, quietly."

## Unit assessment

**Task 1.** (a) 9 + 2 = **11 electors**. (b) **11** (winner-take-all).
(c) Winner: 2 + 6 = **8**; loser: **3**. (d) Ideal: 1,800,000 ÷ 4 =

**Task 2 (scoring: 25 points).** (a) 8 points: one strong point per
argument, accurately restated — A: geographic breadth / small-state
voice / contained recounts; B: popular-vote reversals / safe-state
irrelevance / competitive-state focus. (b) 10 points: one supporting
and one weakening fact per argument (e.g., A supported by the
three-elector minimum and state-contained recounts, weakened by 2000's
reversal and safe-state campaign neglect; B supported by 2000's
537,000-vote reversal, weakened by the fact that a national popular
vote would bring its own problems — national recounts, regional
concentration — which the learner must name). (c) 7 points: a verdict
citing at least two unit facts with reasoning; either verdict earns
full marks if argued from evidence — grade the argument, not the
conclusion.
**Task 3.** (a) (i) **14.6 points**. (ii) **7.9 points**. (iii) Method
changed more: overall turnout −1.2 points (53.4 → 52.2) vs. early/mail
+10.0 points (39.8 → 49.8). (iv) Sample: **mobilization** — young

**Task 4 (essay scoring: 25 points).** Thesis and standard stated
(5); evidence from at least three lessons used accurately (10);
counterargument stated and answered (5); lawful, nonpartisan reform
or practice tied to the stated standard (5). Sample thesis lines
(neither required): "Equal voice fails where maps, money, and turnout
gaps compound — but the system's correctives (courts, commissions,
mobilization) show the standard is equality of *opportunity to
influence*, not equality of outcome." / "Voice is formally equal —
one citizen, one vote within a district — and the unit's gaps are
failures of practice (turnout, access), not of the formal rule."
Deduct for: asserting a reform's effectiveness without evidence;
treating a contested claim (e.g., "money buys elections") as settled
fact; proposing a partisan or unlawful measure (scores 0 on the
reform criterion, with a note explaining why).
