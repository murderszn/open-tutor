# Teacher Guide — Unit 02: The Constitution, Federalism, and Institutional Powers

This guide supports the adult teaching the unit. It contains lesson
notes, misconception flags, pacing advice, and nonpartisanship
guardrails. **All answers live in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md), never
here.**

## Before the unit

- **Preview every primary source.** Read the Article I, §8 list, the
  Article V text, the Supremacy Clause, and the Tenth Amendment in full,
  and select 200–300-word excerpts from *Marbury*, *McCulloch*, and
  *Gibbons* for Lessons 02–03. Pre-teach the vocabulary list in the
  unit README before each lesson — never assign 18th- and 19th-century
  legal prose cold.
- **Set the nonpartisan frame (W7S1).** Say explicitly: "We study how
  institutions are designed and how arguments work. We do not endorse
  parties, candidates, or causes. When we discuss contested questions —
  does federalism protect liberty or block solutions, is the
  Constitution too hard to change — every position must be argued with
  evidence, and I will hold all sides to the same standard." Redirect
  modern examples to institutions and principles, and date every
  current-law claim in the project evidence log.
- **Current law changes.** The project (W7S4–W10S4) is the only place
  learners use current law. Check every project source yourself before
  the defense: statutes change, programs are renamed, and funding
  figures go stale. A project claim without a dated source is returned
  for revision, not scored down for its conclusion.

## Lesson-by-lesson notes

- **L1 (Article I):** The key move is classifying the *holder*, not
  just the verb: coining money is enumerated for Congress and denied to
  states. If a learner says "federalism means the federal government is
  in charge," correct it immediately — federalism is a division, not a
  hierarchy. The powers-sort (Practice Task 1) is the unit's foundation;
  do not proceed to Lesson 02 until learners sort at least 10 of 12
  correctly.
- **L2 (McCulloch/Gibbons):** Keep returning to Marshall's three gates.
  The child-labor question (L2 I1) is genuinely hard: the broad
  *Gibbons*/*McCulloch* reading supports the ban, the Court first
  struck such a ban down in *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918), and then
  upheld federal labor regulation in *United States v. Darby* (1941).
  Credit any answer that applies the gates *and* notes that the doctrine
  moved; do not credit a bare "constitutional" or "unconstitutional"
  without reasoning.
- **L3 (Marbury):** The hardest idea is that a loss for Marbury was a
  win for the Court. If learners say "Marbury won," re-walk step 2
  (Congress cannot expand original jurisdiction by statute) before
  moving on. Stress the limit: judicial review needs a case, and it
  refuses to enforce a law — it does not write a replacement policy.
- **L4 (federalism in practice):** Learners often treat "the federal
  government pays, so the federal government decides everything."
  Keep pressing the distinction between *conditions on offered money*
  (*Dole*) and *orders* (mandates, commandeering under *Printz*).
  The image activity works from the text-only alternative alone — never
  require the image.
- **L5 (Article V):** Drill the two-stage, two-path table until the
  numbers (290/67/34/38) are automatic, and drill the
  proposed-vs.-ratified distinction: most proposed amendments never
  become law. On the Equal Rights Amendment, present the dispute
  neutrally: proposed 1972 with a deadline, 35 states by the extended
  deadline by most counts, later additional ratifications, and an
  ongoing legal dispute over whether deadlines and rescissions block
  certification. Do not present any side's count as settled law.

## Misconception watch (across the unit)

1. Federalism as hierarchy ("federal government in charge") (L1, L4).
2. "Implied" collapsed into "unlimited" — forgetting Marshall's gate 3
   (L2).
3. "Marbury won" / judicial review as a general policy veto (L3).
4. Grants, mandates, and preemption conflated — especially "money with
   strings" called a mandate (L4).
5. Proposed confused with ratified; convention confused with
   legislature (L5).
6. "The Constitution never changes because amendments are rare" —
   ignoring informal change (L5).
7. Classifying an activity instead of its holder (coining money, taxing)
   (L1, Assessment Task 1).

## Pacing advice

- If Week 7 runs long, cut the L1 extension (surprising enumerated
  power), not the powers-sort: the sort is prerequisite for everything
  after it.
- If Week 9 runs long, cut the L4 extension (state grant-page research)
  and fold it into the project, which already requires that research.
- The W10S4 deliberation doubles as the project defense. Protect its
  full session: a rushed defense teaches learners that argument is
  decoration. If time is short, cut W10S2's second source-lab question,
  not the defense.

## Reteaching triggers

- Powers-sort below 10/12 → re-teach L1 with cards before L2.
- Quiz below 12/20, or a blank on quiz item 8 → re-teach Article V and
  *Marbury* (L3 steps, L5 table) before the assessment.
- Assessment Task 6 (grant vs. coercion) weak → re-teach *Dole* vs.
  *NFIB*: the difference between a small condition on new money and the
  threatened loss of all existing funds.
- Project below 28/40 → re-teach power classification and the
  grant/mandate distinction (Practice Tasks 1, 4, 6) before Unit 03.

## Nonpartisanship guardrails

- Assign sides in deliberations *against* learners' inclinations when
  possible; arguing the unfamiliar side builds mechanism-level thinking.
- Never ask a learner to defend or attack a current party, candidate,
  or cause. Frame every modern example as a question about rules and
  institutions: who decides, under what clause, checked by whom.
- If a learner brings a partisan claim to a deliberation, the adult
  response is: "What clause, case, or document supports that — and what
  is the strongest evidence against it?" Hold every side to that
  standard equally.
