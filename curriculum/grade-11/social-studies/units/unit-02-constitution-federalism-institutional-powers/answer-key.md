# Answer Key — Unit 02: The Constitution, Federalism, and Institutional Powers

**Teacher-only file.** Keep separate from all student questions. Every
item below was solved independently against the lesson text and the
cited sources; open responses give sample reasoning and acceptable
variation. Where a question is genuinely contestable (L2 I1), the key
says so and credits reasoned answers.

## Lesson 01 — Article I

- **G1.** (a) Declaring war — **enumerated**, Art. I, §8. (b) Running
  public schools — **reserved**, Tenth Amendment (not delegated, not
  prohibited). (c) A state passing an ex post facto law — **denied to
  states**, Art. I, §10. (d) Building a highway — **concurrent** (both
  levels build roads).
- **G2 (sample).** "Reserved powers are not leftovers — they are the
  powers the Constitution leaves with the states, and they run most of
  daily life. For example, states run public schools and license
  drivers, which touch citizens every day."
- **G3.** Madison's "double security": power is divided (1) between two
  distinct governments (national and state) and (2) within each
  government among separate departments (branches). Citizens benefit
  twice: each level and each branch checks the others, so no single
  government can easily overreach.
- **I1.** See Practice Task 1 below (same 12 powers).
- **I2.** Forbidden by Art. I, §10 (states may not coin money). If the
  state does it anyway, the currency is unconstitutional and not legal
  tender; a federal court can strike the law down under the Supremacy
  Clause, and federal authorities need not accept it. (The state may
  still borrow or tax — it may not create money.)
- **I3.** The governor has the stronger textual case. General policing
  is a reserved power; no enumerated power gives Congress a general
  police force, and the Necessary and Proper Clause cannot supply a
  *new end* — only means to an enumerated end (see L2's gate 1).
  Federal law enforcement that does exist (for example, the FBI) rests
  on enumerated ends such as interstate commerce and federal crimes,
  not on a general patrol power.
- **I4.** Error: concurrent taxation does not include taxing the other
  sovereign's instrumentalities. Correction: "States and Congress may
  both tax private income, but under *McCulloch* a state may not tax a
  federal institution, because the power to tax involves the power to
  destroy it and federal law is supreme."
- **A (sample).** Enumerated: the mail (post offices, §8). Reserved:
  driver's license (state licensing). Concurrent: income tax; the road
  outside (local/state/federal funding). Denied: a state printing its
  own currency (§10). A good "unsure" item: school funding — mostly
  state/local, but with federal grant conditions — settled by checking
  the funding source's official page and its conditions.
- **E (sample).** "Enumerated powers are expressly granted to Congress
  in Article I, §8 (for example, coining money); reserved powers are
  those left to the states by the Tenth Amendment (for example, running
  schools). Madison's double security is that power is divided between
  two governments and then among branches, so each checks the others."

## Lesson 02 — Necessary, proper, commerce

- **G1.** Three gates: (1) the end is legitimate and within the
  Constitution; (2) the means are appropriate and plainly adapted to
  that end; (3) the means are not prohibited by the Constitution. A
  bank today passes on the same reasoning as 1819: taxing, borrowing,
  and commerce are enumerated ends, and a bank is plainly adapted to
  them.
- **G2.** States can tax private activity, but not a federal
  institution: "the power to tax involves the power to destroy," so a
  state tax aimed at the bank could destroy a federal instrument; the
  Supremacy Clause makes federal law supreme over the conflicting state
  tax.
- **G3.** Under *Gibbons*, the monopoly's problem is the Commerce
  Clause plus a federal coastal license: commerce includes navigation,
  and the federal license, as valid federal law, preempts the state
  monopoly under the Supremacy Clause.
- **I1.** Under the broad *Gibbons*/*McCulloch* reading, the ban
  passes: regulating interstate shipment is commerce itself (not a
  remote effect), and banning shipment is plainly adapted to that end.
  Credit answers that add the historical complication: the Court first
  struck such a ban down in *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918, production is
  local), then upheld federal labor regulation in *United States v.
  Darby* (1941). The doctrine moved; a bare "constitutional" without
  reasoning earns partial credit only.
- **I2.** No. Under *McCulloch*'s second holding, a state may not tax a
  federal institution or its property: the tax could destroy or burden
  a federal instrument, and the Supremacy Clause defeats it.
- **I3.** Error: "useful" is not Marshall's test. Correction: "A means
  must serve a legitimate, enumerated end, be plainly adapted to it,
  and not be prohibited by the Constitution. Usefulness alone, for an
  end Congress does not hold, fails gate 1."
- **I4 (sample).** Legislative check: Congress can defund or rewrite
  the agency's authority. Executive check: the President appoints and
  can remove agency heads (within statutory limits) and directs
  enforcement. Judicial check: courts review whether the agency acted
  within its statute and the Constitution. State check: states may sue
  (as in *NFIB*/*Printz* disputes). People's check: elections replace
  the lawmakers who created the agency.
- **A (sample memo).** "Your state may still regulate purely internal
  river traffic and neutral safety rules (speed, pilotage, dock safety)
  that do not conflict with federal law. It may not grant a monopoly
  over interstate navigation or contradict a federal license: under
  *Gibbons*, Congress's commerce power over interstate movement, once
  exercised, preempts the conflicting state grant."
- **E (sample).** "Necessary and proper, broadly read, lets Congress
  use any means plainly adapted to a legitimate enumerated end.
  *Gibbons* held commerce includes navigation — movement, not just
  buying and selling. One limit: the means must not be prohibited by
  the Constitution."

## Lesson 03 — Presidency, courts, judicial review

- **G1 (sample).** Commander-in-chief — checked by Congress's war and
  funding powers. Treaties — need two-thirds Senate concurrence.
  Veto — Congress may override with two-thirds in each house.
  (Also acceptable: appointments — Senate confirmation; pardons —
  Congress cannot reverse a pardon, but may legislate prospectively.)
- **G2.** "Marshall refused because §13 of the Judiciary Act tried to
  expand the Court's original jurisdiction beyond Article III's list,
  so the Act conflicted with the Constitution. By declaring the Act
  unconstitutional, the Court claimed judicial review: the power to say
  what the law is when statute and Constitution conflict."
- **G3.** Supremacy Clause protects: valid federal law (Constitution,
  laws made in pursuance of it, treaties) prevails over conflicting
  state law. Tenth Amendment protects: powers not delegated nor
  prohibited are reserved to the states or the people.
- **I1.** No. Creating a crime is lawmaking, which Article I gives to
  Congress; "faithfully executed" means executing laws Congress made,
  not making new ones. The order usurps the legislative power.
- **I2.** Invalid, under *Marbury* step 2: Article III lists the
  Court's original jurisdiction (ambassadors, states as parties);
  Congress cannot expand it by statute, and a statute conflicting with
  the Constitution must yield.
- **I3.** Article III's salary protection (compensation "shall not be
  diminished during [a judge's] Continuance in Office"). It exists so
  the legislature cannot punish judges for unpopular rulings and so
  destroy judicial independence.
- **I4.** Two errors: (1) "veto any law it dislikes" — judicial review
  requires a *case* and a *constitutional conflict*, not policy
  disagreement; (2) "any law" without limit — the Court refuses to
  enforce a law only where it conflicts with the Constitution, and its
  judgments bind the parties and guide later cases, not the legislature
  as a free-standing veto.
- **A (sample).** Passage: House and Senate must both pass the bill
  (bicameral check). Signature: the President may sign or veto (veto
  check; override needs two-thirds). Challenge: the state sues; the
  court asks, in *Marbury* terms, whether the federal conditions are
  within an enumerated power and whether any state law to the contrary
  must yield under supremacy — and whether commandeering (*Printz*)
  bars ordering state officers to enforce the federal program.
- **E (sample).** "Judicial review is the courts' power to declare a
  law unconstitutional in a case before them, from *Marbury v. Madison*
  (1803). The Supremacy Clause makes valid federal law supreme over
  conflicting state law. The Tenth Amendment reserves undelegated,
  unprohibited powers to the states or the people. They do not
  contradict each other because supremacy applies only to *validly
  federal* law; everything else stays reserved."

## Lesson 04 — Federalism in practice

- **G1.** (a) Town hall + fire truck — local; school + bus — local/state;
  domed capitol — state; columned courthouse — federal. (b) Services:
  fire service, public schooling, state lawmaking, federal courts.
  (c) Shared service (sample): the road — local streets, state highways,
  federal funding and standards — needs two levels because no one level
  builds, funds, and regulates the whole network alone.
- **G2.** (a) Categorical grant (specific purpose + conditions).
  (b) Block grant (broad area, state discretion). (c) Funded mandate
  (requirement + money). (d) Unfunded mandate (requirement, no money).
  (e) Preemption (federal standard displaces conflicting state standard).
- **G3.** *Printz* anti-commandeering rule: Congress may not order
  state officers to enforce a federal program. Instead, Congress may
  enforce the program with its own federal officers, or offer money
  with conditions states may accept or refuse.
- **I1.** No. Grant conditions are voluntary: a state may refuse the
  money and its strings (see *Dole*). Congress may not *force*
  acceptance; the consequence of refusal is losing the funds, not a
  legal order to comply. (Coercion under *NFIB* is the exception when
  the threatened loss is so large it is no real choice — see I2.)
- **I2.** Under *NFIB*, this leans coercive ("gun to the head"): losing
  *all* existing highway funds is not a genuine choice, unlike *Dole*'s
  5% condition on new funds. The answer changes if only a small
  percentage of *new* funds is at stake, or if the condition is closely
  related to the funds' purpose and the state has a real alternative.
- **I3.** Strongest argument against: under *Lopez*, the item is purely
  local and non-commercial; the "ingredients once crossed a state line"
  chain has no stopping point and would erase the national/local
  distinction. Congress's best reply: the item's ingredients moved in
  interstate commerce, so regulating it regulates commerce itself.
  That reply is weak under *Lopez* because the regulated *activity*
  (possession) is not itself interstate commerce.
- **I4.** Error: cooperative federalism means *shared* work, not state
  extinction. Correction: "States still exercise reserved powers
  independently — for example, they run their own criminal law, schools,
  and elections — even while administering joint federal-state
  programs."
- **A.** Scored on: each level's contribution correctly identified;
  one plausible conflict named (for example, federal standards vs.
  state funding priorities); a reasoned layer/marble judgment with
  evidence. Either judgment can earn full marks with evidence.
- **E (sample).** "A categorical grant funds a specific purpose with
  detailed conditions; a block grant funds a broad area with state
  discretion. A funded mandate comes with federal money; an unfunded
  mandate does not. *Lopez* (or *NFIB*) limits national power by
  holding that the commerce power does not reach non-economic local
  activity (or compel entry into commerce)."

## Lesson 05 — Article V and informal change

- **G1.** Proposal: (A) two-thirds of both houses of Congress;
  (B) a convention called on the application of two-thirds of state
  legislatures (34). Ratification: (A) three-fourths of state
  legislatures (38); (B) conventions in three-fourths of the states
  (38). Every amendment except the 21st used proposal path A and
  ratification path A; the 21st used ratification path B.
- **G2.** "Broken" argument: 203 years shows a supermajority system can
  stall a widely supported idea for generations. "Works" argument: the
  27th *did* eventually pass without a deadline, showing Article V
  allows slow, broad consensus rather than momentary majorities.
  Either argument earns credit with reasoning.
- **G3.** (a) 19th Amendment — formal (Article V). (b) *Marbury* —
  informal, judicial interpretation. (c) Two-term tradition before
  1951 — informal, custom/practice. (d) Federal Reserve (1913) —
  informal, legislation under implied powers (also an exercise of
  enumerated borrowing/commerce ends).
- **I1.** Law. House: 300 ≥ 290 (two-thirds of 435) passes. Senate:
  70 ≥ 67 (two-thirds of 100 = 66.67, rounded up) passes. States:
  39 ≥ 38 (three-fourths of 50 = 37.5, rounded up) passes. No stage
  fails.
- **I2.** Not law under the deadline as written: only 37 ratifications
  arrived within seven years, and the 38th came late. The Equal Rights
  Amendment's status is disputed for this reason and because of
  rescission and certification disputes; this unit treats it as not
  completed under its deadline framework, and the adult presents the
  dispute neutrally (see teacher guide).
- **I3.** It raises the treaties question: Article II requires
  two-thirds Senate concurrence for treaties, so an executive agreement
  bypasses that check. Channel: informal change by political practice
  (presidential practice), not by Article V.
- **I4.** Error: amendments are rare, but constitutional *meaning*
  changes constantly. Correction with two examples: "*Marbury* created
  judicial review without amending a word, and the commerce power's
  expansion and later contraction (*Gibbons* to *Lopez*) changed what
  the government may do without any textual change."
- **A.** Scored on: a clear position, at least one reason with
  evidence, and a genuine (steelmanned) counterargument. A slogan
  without a counterargument earns at most half credit.
- **E (sample).** "Proposal: two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or
  a convention called on the application of two-thirds of state
  legislatures. Ratification: three-fourths of state legislatures, or
  conventions in three-fourths of the states. Informal change example:
  judicial interpretation — *Marbury* created judicial review without
  amending the text."

## Practice tasks

- **Task 1.** 1. Enumerated (§8, coin money). 2. Reserved (Tenth).
  3. Concurrent (§8 + state tax powers). 4. Enumerated (§8, declare
  war). 5. Reserved (Tenth). 6. Denied to states (§10, treaties).
  7. Concurrent (Congress borrows under §8; states borrow under their
  own authority). 8. Reserved (Tenth; federal influence, if any, comes
  through grant conditions, not direct regulation). 9. Enumerated
  (§8, counterfeiting). 10. Denied to states (§10, duties on imports/
  exports without Congress's consent). 11. Concurrent (federal courts
  under Art. III; state courts under state authority). 12. Denied to
  both (§9 denies the United States; §10 denies the states).
- **Task 2.** Five checks (sample): both houses must pass the bill;
  the President may veto; Congress may override only with two-thirds
  in each house; courts may refuse to enforce an unconstitutional law
  (*Marbury*); voters may replace lawmakers at the next election.
  Two stages where a majority-supported bill can legally die:
  a sustained veto (override needs two-thirds, not a majority) and
  judicial invalidation on constitutional grounds. (Also acceptable:
  failure in either house despite national majority support.)
- **Task 3.** (a) *McCulloch* (1819) — implied powers + supremacy over
  state taxation of federal institutions. (b) *Gibbons* (1824) —
  commerce includes navigation; federal license preempts state
  monopoly. (c) *Marbury* (1803) — judicial review. (d) *Lopez*
  (1995) — commerce power does not reach non-economic local activity.
  (e) *NFIB* (2012) — commerce does not compel activity; coercive
  funding threats are unconstitutional.
- **Task 4.** 1. Dual (separate spheres, no interference). 2.
  Cooperative (federal money + rules, state delivery). 3. "New"
  federalism / devolution (broad grant, state design discretion).
- **Task 5.** 1. 290 (435 × 2/3 = 290). 2. 67 (100 × 2/3 = 66.67,
  rounded up — there is no fractional senator). 3. 38 (50 × 3/4 =
  37.5, rounded up — there is no half-state ratification). 4. 13
  states (50 − 38 + 1 = 13 can block; equivalently, fewer than 38
  ratifications fails). 5. The 21st Amendment (1933, repeal of
  Prohibition) — the only amendment ratified by state conventions
  rather than legislatures.
- **Task 6.** 1. Federal standard governs (supremacy/preemption on the
  same point). 2. State law stands (reserved police power; no federal
  occupation of that ground). 3. State religious-test law is invalid:
  Article VI, clause 3 bars religious tests for public trust, and
  *Torcaso v. Watkins* (1961) applied the First and Fourteenth
  Amendments against state religious tests. 4. Invalid as
  commandeering under *Printz*: Congress may use federal officers or
  offer funds, but may not order state officers to run a federal
  program.
- **Task 7.** 1. Error: hierarchy. Correction: "Federalism divides
  power between levels; neither is simply 'in charge' of the other
  within its own sphere." 2. Error: "anything useful." Correction:
  "Implied means must serve a legitimate enumerated end, be plainly
  adapted, and not be prohibited." 3. Error: general veto.
  Correction: "Judicial review requires a case and a constitutional
  conflict; the Court refuses to enforce that law, it does not veto
  policy it dislikes." 4. Error: equating change with amendments.
  Correction: "Meaning changes informally — *Marbury* and the commerce
  cases changed government without textual amendment." 5. Error:
  state extinction. Correction: "Cooperative federalism shares work;
  states retain reserved powers such as criminal law, schooling, and
  elections."
- **Task 8.** The adult checks the *corrections*, not the first draft.
  Accept any ten sentences that are accurate after correction; return
  uncorrected errors for a second pass.

## Quiz (20 points)

1. (a) Coining money — enumerated, Art. I, §8. (b) Licensing drivers —
   reserved, Tenth Amendment. (1 pt classification + 1 pt citation
   across both, or 1 pt each part as scored.)
2. Three gates: legitimate enumerated end; means plainly adapted;
   means not prohibited. (Accept Marshall's wording or paraphrase.)
3. Commerce includes navigation (movement of goods and people). New
   York's steamboat monopoly fell: the federal coastal license, as
   valid federal law, preempted it.
4. The Court refused because §13 of the Judiciary Act tried to expand
   its original jurisdiction beyond Article III's list. By declaring
   §13 unconstitutional, it claimed judicial review.
5. Supremacy: valid federal law prevails over conflicting state law.
   Tenth: undelegated, unprohibited powers are reserved to the states
   or the people.
6. Categorical: specific purpose + detailed conditions (example:
   school-lunch funds with nutrition rules). Block: broad area + state
   discretion (example: community-development grant).
7. Two doctrines (any two, with correct statements): *Printz*
   anti-commandeering (Congress may not order state officers to enforce
   a federal program); unfunded-mandate analysis (a requirement without
   money — contested, and the Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995
   requires cost estimates but does not ban mandates); *Lopez*/*NFIB*
   limits if the order also exceeds an enumerated power. Full credit
   requires *Printz* plus one other correctly stated doctrine.
8. Proposal: two-thirds of both houses; convention on application of
   two-thirds of state legislatures. Ratification: three-fourths of
   state legislatures; conventions in three-fourths of the states.
9. House 290 (435 × 2/3); Senate 67 (100 × 2/3 = 66.67, rounded up);
   states 38 (50 × 3/4 = 37.5, rounded up). (Precision note: the Court
   reads Article V as two-thirds of members present with a quorum;
   290/67 are the full-membership figures this unit's assessment uses.)
10. Any two forms with examples: judicial interpretation (*Marbury*,
    *Lopez*); political practice/custom (executive agreements,
    two-term tradition); legislation/institutional growth (Federal
    Reserve, federal agencies).

## Unit assessment (50 points)

- **Task 1 (6 pts, 1 pt each).** 1. Enumerated (§8, commerce).
  2. Reserved (Tenth). 3. Concurrent. 4. Denied to states (§10).
  5. Enumerated (§8, piracies). 6. Denied to Congress (§9; habeas
  suspension only in rebellion or invasion).
- **Task 2 (6 pts).** (1) Gate 1 passes: regulating interstate commerce
  is enumerated, and meat shipped across state lines *is* interstate
  commerce. Gate 2 passes: inspection is plainly adapted to regulating
  that commerce safely. Gate 3 passes: nothing prohibits federal
  inspection of interstate shipments. (2) Yes, the answer changes:
  gate 1 fails — purely intrastate, non-shipping plants are not within
  the interstate-commerce end as argued (a *Lopez*-style limit), even
  though inspection itself is a sensible means.
- **Task 3 (6 pts).** (1) Right: the taxpayer asserts a genuine tax
  dispute, but the *remedy* sought is original jurisdiction in the
  Supreme Court. Conflict: the statute expands original jurisdiction
  beyond Article III's list (ambassadors, states as parties), so it
  conflicts with the Constitution. (2) The Court must decline the case
  and declare the statute unconstitutional: under *Marbury*, when
  statute and Constitution conflict, the Constitution governs, and it
  is the judiciary's province to say what the law is. A genuine
  complaint does not create jurisdiction the Constitution withholds.
- **Task 4 (5 pts).** (1) Town hall/fire truck — local; school/bus —
  local/state; domed capitol — state; columned courthouse — federal
  (2 pts for correct matching with one service each). (2) Shared
  service (sample, 3 pts): roads — local streets, state highways,
  federal funding/standards; conflict example: federal safety or
  funding conditions vs. state construction priorities or budget
  choices. Text-only alternative answers earn full credit.
- **Task 5 (6 pts).** Proposal A: two-thirds of both houses (290/67).
  Proposal B: convention on application of two-thirds of state
  legislatures (34). Ratification A: three-fourths of state
  legislatures (38). Ratification B: conventions in three-fourths of
  the states (38). (a) 13 states can block. (b) The 21st Amendment
  (1933) — the only amendment ratified by conventions, chosen because
  Congress doubted state legislatures would repeal Prohibition.
  (c) Yes, law: 290 meets the House threshold, 67 meets the Senate
  threshold, 38 meets the ratification threshold.
- **Task 6 (7 pts).** (1) Categorical grant condition (3 pts): new
  money offered with a condition — not a mandate (no direct order),
  not preemption (no conflicting state law displaced), not a block
  grant (the condition is specific, not broad). (2) *NFIB* (2 pts):
  withdrawing *all* existing education funds is coercive — a "gun to
  the head" — unlike *Dole*'s small condition on new funds; the
  condition is likely unconstitutional as coercion. *Printz* (2 pts):
  does not limit this offer, because no state officer is ordered to
  enforce a federal program — the mechanism is funding, not
  commandeering. (Credit answers that reach the same distinction even
  if they phrase the *NFIB* conclusion as "likely unconstitutional"
  rather than certain.)
- **Task 7 (5 pts).** (1) Strongest argument (3 pts): bullying is
  non-economic, local activity in a classic state domain (education);
  the "less learning → less earning" chain has no stopping point and
  would let Congress regulate any local activity that eventually
  affects the economy — exactly what *Lopez* rejected. (2) Best reply
  (2 pts): in the aggregate, education affects the national economy,
  and Congress may regulate activities that substantially affect
  interstate commerce. It remains weak under *Lopez* because
  aggregation applies to *economic* activity, and bullying in a
  classroom is not itself economic.
- **Task 8 (4 pts).** Three forms with examples (see Quiz item 10).
  The "why it counts as change" explanation (2 sentences within the
  4 pts) must show that operative meaning or institutional practice
  changed while the text stayed the same (sample: "*Marbury* gave
  courts a power the text never names, so the judiciary's role in
  government changed even though Article III's words did not.").
- **Task 9 (5 pts).** Score on: position clearly stated (1 pt); at
  least one formal piece of evidence correctly used (Article V
  fractions, a specific amendment — 1 pt); at least one informal piece
  correctly used (*Marbury*, *Lopez*/*NFIB*, custom, legislation —
  1 pt); reasoning linking evidence to position (1 pt); genuine
  counterargument, not a strawman (1 pt). Either position can earn
  full marks.

## Project scoring

Use the rubric in [project.md](project.md) (40 points). Sample
judgments: a project that classifies school funding as purely state
money without mentioning federal grant conditions loses points under
"Grant / mandate / preemption analysis," not under "Argument" — the
conclusion is never scored. A project with undated current-law claims
loses points under "Source quality and dating" and is returned for a
dated revision before the defense score is finalized.
