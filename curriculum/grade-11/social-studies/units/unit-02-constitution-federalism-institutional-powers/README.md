# Unit 02 — The Constitution, Federalism, and Institutional Powers

**Grade:** 11 · **Subject:** Social Studies (U.S. government and civics)
**Track weeks:** 7–10 · **Backlog:** [issue #53](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/53), section U02
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Unit 01 asked where a government's right to rule comes from. This unit
asks how the Constitution *organizes* that rule: which powers belong to
the national government, which are reserved to the states, which are
shared, and how each branch and each level checks the others. The
answer is not a list to memorize — it is a system whose meaning has
been fought over in Congress, in the courts, and in statehouses ever
since 1789, from *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) to the commerce-clause
limits of *United States v. Lopez* (1995) and *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012).

## Essential question

Who gets to decide — the national government, the states, or both —
and what stops either one from deciding everything?

## Prerequisites

From Unit 01 and the track diagnostic weeks (weeks 1–2):

- Name the Constitution's core principles (popular sovereignty, limited
  government, separation of powers, checks and balances, federalism) and
  trace one power through the checks that restrain it.
- Classify a source by kind and infer its maker and audience; write a
  claim supported by at least two pieces of evidence, with a counterclaim.
- Read 18th- and 19th-century legal English with vocabulary support (the
  adult pre-teaches terms before every primary-source reading; see the
  track [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md), §2).

No cross-grade learner links: these are described skills, not references
to other grades' lessons.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Classify** powers as enumerated, reserved, concurrent, or denied,
   and explain why the framers divided power this way.
   (D2.Civ.1.9-12)
2. **Explain** the Necessary and Proper Clause and the Commerce Clause
   through *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) and *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824),
   and trace a federal power through the checks that restrain it.
   (D2.Civ.4.9-12)
3. **Explain** the presidency's and the judiciary's formal powers,
   judicial review from *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), the Supremacy
   Clause, and the Tenth Amendment's reservation to the states.
   (D2.Civ.1.9-12; D2.Civ.4.9-12)
4. **Analyze** federalism in practice — dual, cooperative, and "new"
   federalism; categorical and block grants; mandates; preemption — and
   the limits the courts have set on national power.
   (D2.Civ.1.9-12)
5. **Explain** the Article V amendment process (proposal and
   ratification paths) and three forms of informal constitutional change,
   and evaluate whether the Constitution is hard or easy to change.
   (D2.Civ.12.9-12; D2.Civ.4.9-12)

In every unit the learner also develops compelling questions
(D1.1–D1.5.9-12), evaluates sources and their limits (D3.1–D3.4.9-12),
and communicates evidence-based conclusions (D4.1–D4.4.9-12).

Primary sources are quoted in brief public-domain excerpts with links:
the Constitution (National Archives transcription), *Federalist*
No. 51, and U.S. Supreme Court opinions (U.S. government works, public
domain). The unit is nonpartisan: institutions and processes are
analyzed without endorsing parties, candidates, or causes, and contested
questions (does federalism protect liberty or block solutions? is the
Constitution hard to change?) are argued from both sides with evidence.
Current-law examples in the project are verified by the adult at the
time of use and dated in the learner's evidence log — no current-policy
figure in this unit is presented as permanent.

## 20-session pacing (4 weeks × 5 sessions, ~45–60 minutes each)

Sessions rotate: core lesson → practice → investigation/reading →
argument/deliberation → review. The project launches in Week 8, Session
4 and runs across the investigation sessions; the final argument session
(W10S4) doubles as the project defense.

| W8S3 | Investigation | Source lab — compare the *Marbury* holding with the Constitution's text: where does judicial review come from? Update project evidence logs |

and explain McCulloch's implied-powers reasoning; by W8S5 the learner
can explain judicial review's source and limits and trace a law through
checks; by W9S5 the learner can distinguish grants, mandates, and
preemption and apply Lopez/NFIB limits; by W10S5 the learner can map
both Article V paths with correct fractions and defend an
evidence-based position on constitutional change, and has delivered the
project report.
