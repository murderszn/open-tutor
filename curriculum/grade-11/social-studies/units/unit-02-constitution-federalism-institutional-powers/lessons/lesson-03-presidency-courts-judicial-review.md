# Lesson 03 — Articles II and III: Presidency, Courts, Judicial Review

**Goal:** Explain the presidency's and the judiciary's formal powers,
judicial review from *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), the Supremacy Clause,
and the Tenth Amendment's reservation to the states. **Time:** ~55
minutes. **Materials:** Article II, §§1–4; Article III, §§1–2; Article
VI, clause 2; Tenth Amendment; *Marbury* summary below.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Congress makes a law. The President
> vetoes it. Congress overrides with two-thirds in each house. Who has
> the last word — and what if the law itself violates the Constitution?
> (Answer: no branch has the last word alone; today's lesson adds the
> courts' word.)

**Sources:** Constitution transcript, National Archives (public domain;
checked 2026-10-21); *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. 137 (1803) (public
domain).

## Teaching

**The presidency (Article II).** The President is commander-in-chief
of the armed forces, makes treaties with the advice and consent of the
Senate (two-thirds must concur), nominates ambassadors, judges, and
other officers (Senate confirmation), grants pardons for federal
offenses, delivers a State of the Union, receives ambassadors, and must
"take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." The President may veto
a bill; Congress may override with two-thirds in each house. Note what
is *not* listed: the President cannot make laws, appropriate money, or
declare war — those belong to Congress. Presidential power is therefore
real but bounded, and much of its modern reach comes from the
"faithfully executed" duty plus informal practice (Lesson 05), not from
new text.

**The judiciary (Article III).** "The judicial Power of the United
States, shall be vested in one supreme Court, and in such inferior
Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish."
Judges hold office "during good Behaviour" — effectively life tenure —
with salaries that cannot be diminished while in office: independence
by design. The judicial power extends to cases "arising under this
Constitution, the Laws of the United States, and Treaties made" and to
disputes between states and between citizens of different states.
Article III also divides the Supreme Court's work: in cases affecting
ambassadors and cases where a state is a party, the Court has
*original* jurisdiction (it hears them first); in all other federal
cases it has *appellate* jurisdiction (it reviews lower courts'
decisions), "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the
Congress shall make."

**Judicial review — the power the text never names.** The Constitution
never says courts may strike down laws. *Marbury v. Madison* (1803)
claimed that power, and the claim stuck. The facts are a political
farce turned constitutional landmark: outgoing President John Adams
signed last-minute judicial commissions (the "midnight judges");
incoming President Thomas Jefferson's Secretary of State, James
Madison, refused to deliver William Marbury's commission; Marbury sued
directly in the Supreme Court, asking it to issue a *writ of mandamus*
— a court order commanding delivery — under §13 of the Judiciary Act of
1789, which seemed to give the Court that original-jurisdiction power.

Chief Justice Marshall's opinion performed a three-step maneuver that
is worth following slowly:

1. **Marbury has a right.** His commission was signed and sealed; the
   appointment was complete, and delivery was a formality. Withholding
   it violated a vested legal right.
2. **The law must supply a remedy — but not this one.** The Judiciary
   Act's §13 purported to let the Supreme Court issue mandamus as an
   *original* matter. But Article III lists the Court's original
   jurisdiction exhaustively (ambassadors, states as parties), and
   Marbury's case is neither. Congress cannot *expand* the Court's
   original jurisdiction by statute.
3. **Therefore the Court must refuse — and in refusing, claim the
   greater power.** When a statute conflicts with the Constitution, the
   Constitution, as supreme law, must govern: "It is emphatically the
   province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law
   is." If courts must follow the Constitution over a conflicting
   statute, they must be able to declare the statute void. Section 13
   was unconstitutional, Marbury got no commission, and the Court walked
   away with judicial review.

Note the political genius: Marshall gave Jefferson's administration
the *outcome* it wanted (no commission delivered) while taking a power
no administration wanted the Court to have. Note also the limit:
judicial review is the power to refuse to enforce an unconstitutional
law in a case before the Court — not a general veto over policy, and
not a power the Court can exercise without a case.

**Supremacy and reservation, side by side.** Article VI, clause 2
(the Supremacy Clause): the Constitution, federal laws "made in
Pursuance thereof," and treaties "shall be the supreme Law of the Land;
and the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby, any Thing in the
Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding."
The Tenth Amendment then reserves the remainder: powers not delegated
to the United States, nor prohibited to the states, "are reserved to
the States respectively, or to the people." Read together they draw
the unit's central tension: federal law wins *when it is validly
federal*; everything else stays with the states and the people. The
fight in almost every federalism case is over that word *validly*.

### Worked example 1 — Following Marbury's three steps

*Scenario:* A state passes a law requiring all newspapers to print
only stories approved by the governor. A newspaper sues in federal
court, citing the First Amendment.

*Reasoning:* Step 1 — a right: the First Amendment protects freedom
of the press against government abridgment. Step 2 — a conflict: the
state law directly contradicts that protection. Step 3 — the remedy:
under *Marbury*, the court must follow the Constitution over the
conflicting law and refuse to enforce the state law. (Note the
difference from *Marbury* itself: there the *federal* statute was
struck; here a *state* law falls — the Supremacy Clause plus judicial
review do both jobs.)

### Worked example 2 — Supremacy vs. reservation in one dispute

*Scenario:* Congress passes a valid federal safety standard for
commercial trucks. A state passes a stricter truck-safety rule that
contradicts the federal standard on one point. A state passes another
rule setting the speed limit on its own rural roads, where Congress has
passed no standard at all.

*Reasoning:* The stricter rule on the *same point* loses where it
conflicts with the valid federal standard — supremacy (and preemption,
Lesson 04). The speed-limit rule stands: no federal law occupies that
ground, and setting speed limits is a classic reserved police power.
Same state, same session, opposite results — because the question is
never "state or federal in general" but "which law governs *this*
point?"

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** List three formal presidential powers and, for each, name
   the check another branch holds over it.
2. **G2.** Why did Marshall *refuse* Marbury's request and still
   strengthen the Court? Answer in two sentences using the word
   "jurisdiction."
3. **G3.** Read the Supremacy Clause and the Tenth Amendment side by
   side. In one sentence each, state what each one *protects*.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** A President issues an executive order creating a new
   federal crime. Apply Article II: is this within the "faithfully
   executed" duty? Explain, naming which branch holds the lawmaking
   power the order would usurp.
2. **I2.** Congress passes a law expanding the Supreme Court's
   original jurisdiction to all cases involving federal taxes.
   Apply *Marbury* step 2: is the law valid? Why or why not?
3. **I3.** A federal judge's salary is cut by Congress after an
   unpopular ruling. Which Article III protection does this violate,
   and why does that protection exist?
4. **I4.** Error analysis: "Judicial review means the Supreme Court
   can veto any law it dislikes." Identify two errors in that sentence
   and correct each.

## Applied task

**A.** Trace one power through the checks: Congress passes a bill
funding a federal highway; the President signs it; a state sues,
claiming the highway conditions commandeer state officers (see Lesson
04). For each stage — passage, signature, challenge — name the branch
or level acting and the check available against it. End with the
question the court must answer, in *Marbury* terms: is the federal
action within the Constitution, and does any conflicting law yield?

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) define judicial review and name its
source case; (2) state the Supremacy Clause's rule; (3) state the
Tenth Amendment's rule — and one sentence on why the two do not
contradict each other.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Gloss *mandamus* (a court order commanding a
  government official to perform a duty), *vested* (legally complete
  and owned), and *original vs. appellate jurisdiction* before the
  *Marbury* reading. The adult reads Marshall's "province and duty"
  sentence aloud and paraphrases it before learners tackle the steps.
- **Alternate response mode:** The Marbury three-step chain may be
  answered as a flowchart (Right → Remedy → Conflict → Result).
- **Extension:** Read *Federalist* No. 78 (Hamilton) on the judiciary
  as the "least dangerous" branch — it has "neither force nor will,
  but merely judgment." In one paragraph, argue whether *Marbury*
  made the judiciary more dangerous than Hamilton promised, or
  whether the case requirement keeps it the least dangerous still.
