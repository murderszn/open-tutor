# Lesson 05 — How the Constitution Changes: Article V and Informal Change

**Goal:** Explain the Article V amendment process (proposal and
ratification paths) and three forms of informal constitutional change,
and evaluate whether the Constitution is hard or easy to change.
**Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** Article V text; amendment
timeline below (all dates are historical record, not current policy).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** The Constitution has 27 amendments in
> 235+ years. Is that evidence it is hard to change, or evidence it
> changes constantly in other ways? Hold both thoughts — today's
> lesson argues both are true.

**Source:** [The Constitution of the United States: A
Transcription](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript),
National Archives (public domain; checked 2026-10-21).

## Teaching

**Article V — the formal paths.** Article V provides two ways to
*propose* an amendment and two ways to *ratify* one. Congress chooses
the ratification method for each proposal.

| Step | Path A (used for all 27 amendments) | Path B (never used for proposal; once for ratification) |
|---|---|---|
| **Proposal** | Two-thirds of *both* houses of Congress vote to propose | A national convention, called by Congress on the application of two-thirds of the state legislatures (34 of 50) |
| **Ratification** | Three-fourths of the state legislatures ratify (38 of 50) | Conventions in three-fourths of the states ratify (38 of 50) — used only for the 21st Amendment (repeal of Prohibition, 1933) |

Do the arithmetic, because the fractions are the argument. Two-thirds
of 435 Representatives is 290; two-thirds of 100 Senators is 67 (66.67,
rounded up — you cannot have two-thirds of a senator). Precision note:
the Court reads Article V as two-thirds of the members *present* with a
quorum, not necessarily of the full membership; 290 and 67 are the
full-house figures textbooks use, and this unit's assessment uses those
figures. Three-fourths
of 50 states is 37.5, rounded up to 38. A proposal therefore needs a
supermajority in *two* national bodies *plus* a supermajority of the
states: 13 states, voting no, can block any amendment, no matter how
large the national majority. The framers designed that veto deliberately
— and added one unamendable rule: no state may be deprived of equal
suffrage in the Senate without its own consent.

**Why so hard?** The framers feared two opposite failures: a
constitution that changes with every election (no stability) and one
that cannot change at all (no legitimacy). Article V splits the
difference by making change *possible but slow*, and by requiring
agreement across levels (national *and* state) rather than within one
majority. The Bill of Rights (1791) and the Reconstruction Amendments
(13th, 1865; 14th, 1868; 15th, 1870) show the mechanism working under
extraordinary pressure — war and its aftermath. Long quiet stretches
show it stalling: the Equal Rights Amendment, proposed by Congress in
1972, never reached 38 ratifications within its deadline framework and
remains unratified as a completed amendment (its legal status is still
disputed — a good example of why "ratified" needs a date and a count,
not a slogan).

**The 27th Amendment — the slowest success.** Proposed in 1789 as part
of the original Bill of Rights package (it would have barred Congress
from raising its own pay until after the next election), it was
ratified by only a handful of states, then slept for two centuries.
A university student's 1982 research paper revived the campaign; by
1992, 38 states had ratified, and the amendment became law — 203 years
after proposal. No deadline had been attached to it, so no deadline
expired. The lesson: Article V has no general time limit in its text;
Congress has attached deadlines to some modern proposals, and the
courts have generally allowed a "reasonable time" reading for older
ones. Dates matter; check them.

**Informal change — the Constitution changes without Article V.**
Most of the Constitution's *meaning* has changed without a single word
of its text changing, through three main channels:

1. **Judicial interpretation.** *Marbury* created judicial review
   (Lesson 03); *McCulloch* and *Gibbons* expanded the commerce and
   implied powers (Lesson 02); *Lopez* and *NFIB* contracted them
   again (Lesson 04). Same text, different readings, different
   government — because the Court's reading *is*, for practical
   purposes, the Constitution's operative meaning until the Court or
   an amendment changes it.
2. **Political practice and custom.** Presidents now routinely act
   through executive orders and executive agreements (agreements with
   foreign nations that are not treaties and therefore need no
   two-thirds Senate vote); the Cabinet, political parties, and the
   primary system all organize government without any constitutional
   text creating them; the two-term presidential tradition held from
   Washington until Franklin Roosevelt's four elections, after which
   the 22nd Amendment (1951) wrote the custom into text — practice
   first, amendment second.
3. **Legislation and institutional growth.** Congress creates agencies,
   programs, and procedures under its enumerated and implied powers
   (the federal bureaucracy, the Federal Reserve System, the modern
   committee system) that reshape how constitutional powers are
   exercised, without amending a word. Technology and war accelerate
   this: the commerce power now reaches a national economy the framers
   could not have imagined, not because the words changed but because
   the economy did.

**So: hard or easy?** Both, and the distinction is the point. The
*text* is hard to change — deliberately, by supermajority design.
The *meaning* changes constantly — through interpretation, practice,
and legislation. A citizen who watches only Article V will think the
Constitution is frozen; a citizen who watches only the courts will
think it changes overnight. Both are half right. The W10S4 deliberation
asks which half should worry us more.

### Worked example 1 — Counting a ratification

*Scenario:* Congress proposes an amendment; 40 state legislatures
ratify it, 10 do not. Is it law?

*Reasoning:* Yes. Three-fourths of 50 is 37.5, so 38 ratifications are
required, and 40 clears the bar. The 10 dissenting states are bound
anyway — that is what ratification *means*: once the threshold is met,
the amendment binds all states, including those that voted no. Contrast
a proposal ratified by only 37 states: one short, not law, no matter
how large the popular majority behind it. The unit's arithmetic is
unforgiving by design.

### Worked example 2 — Formal or informal?

*Scenario:* In 1803 the Court claims judicial review; in 1933 the
21st Amendment repeals Prohibition; in the 1930s the federal government
begins regulating a national economy under the commerce power.

*Reasoning:* The 21st Amendment is **formal** change — Article V,
proposal by Congress, ratification by state conventions (the only
amendment ratified that way). *Marbury* is **informal** change by
interpretation: no text was added, but the judiciary's role was
transformed. The New Deal's commerce expansion is **informal** change
by interpretation *plus* legislation: the Court read the old words
more broadly, and Congress built programs on that reading. If a learner
calls all three "amendments," correct the vocabulary: only the first
amended the text.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Fill in the Article V table from memory: both proposal
   paths with fractions and numbers (34/38), both ratification paths,
   and which path every amendment except the 21st used.
2. **G2.** The 27th Amendment took 203 years. Does that prove Article
   V is broken, or that it works? Give one argument each way.
3. **G3.** Classify each as formal or informal change, and name the
   channel: (a) the 19th Amendment (1920); (b) *Marbury*; (c) the
   two-term tradition before 1951; (d) the creation of the Federal
   Reserve (1913) under implied powers.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** A proposed amendment wins 300 votes in the House, 70 in
   the Senate, and ratification by 39 states. Is it law? Show the
   arithmetic for each stage and identify the stage that fails, if any.
2. **I2.** Congress proposes an amendment with a seven-year ratification
   deadline; 37 states ratify within seven years and a 38th ratifies in
   year eight. Is it law under the deadline as written? Explain, and
   note why the Equal Rights Amendment's status is disputed.
3. **I3.** A President signs an executive agreement with another
   country instead of a treaty. Which Article V question does this
   raise, and which informal-change channel does it illustrate?
4. **I4.** Error analysis: "The Constitution never changes because
   amendments are rare." Identify the error and correct it with two
   examples of informal change from this lesson.

## Applied task

**A.** Write a paragraph defending one position: *either* "Article V
is too hard — it lets 13 states veto the will of the nation" *or*
"Article V is rightly hard — it protects minorities and stability."
Then write the strongest one-sentence counterargument to your own
paragraph. (The counterargument counts: a position you cannot
steelman is a slogan, not an argument.)

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) name both proposal paths with their
fractions; (2) name both ratification paths with their fractions;
(3) name one form of informal change and one example of it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Gloss *ratify* (formally approve), *supermajority*
  (more than a simple majority), and *convention* (a specially elected
  body, not the legislature) before the Article V reading. The
  fractions are worked as arithmetic on the board, not memorized.
- **Alternate response mode:** The Article V table may be completed as
  a diagram with arrows (Proposal → Ratification → Law) instead of
  prose.
- **Extension:** Research one failed amendment (for example, the
  Equal Rights Amendment or the District of Columbia Voting Rights
  Amendment, proposed 1978). Report: who proposed it, how far
  ratification got, and which explanation best fits the failure —
  supermajority design, public disagreement, or deadline rules?
  Date every claim.

> **Teacher note:** Learners confuse "proposed" with "ratified" and
> "convention" with "legislature." Drill the two-stage, two-path table
> until the numbers (290/67/34/38) are automatic. Full notes and all
> answers are in [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
