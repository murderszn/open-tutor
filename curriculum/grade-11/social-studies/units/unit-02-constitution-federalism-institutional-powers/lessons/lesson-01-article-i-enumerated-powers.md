# Lesson 01 — Article I: Congress and the Division of Power

**Goal:** Classify powers as enumerated, reserved, concurrent, or denied,
and explain why the framers divided power this way. **Time:** ~55
minutes. **Materials:** Constitution transcript excerpts (Article I,
§§1, 8, 9, 10); powers-sort cards (see Guided Practice).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Unit 01 ended with five principles.
> Which one divides power *vertically* — between levels of government —
> rather than *horizontally* between branches? (Answer: federalism.)

**Source:** [The Constitution of the United States: A
Transcription](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript),
National Archives (public domain; checked 2026-10-21).

## Teaching

Article I, Section 1 gives "all legislative Powers herein granted" to
Congress — a House and a Senate. The word *herein granted* does the
work: Congress does not hold general power. It holds the powers the
Constitution lists, and the list is in Section 8. Read it as an
inventory, not a slogan:

- **Enumerated (delegated) powers** — powers the Constitution expressly
  gives the national government. Article I, §8: lay and collect taxes;
  borrow money; regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the states,
  and with the Indian Tribes; establish uniform naturalization and
  bankruptcy rules; coin money and fix weights and measures; punish
  counterfeiting; establish post offices and post roads; grant copyrights
  and patents; constitute tribunals inferior to the Supreme Court;
  define and punish piracies and offenses against the law of nations;
  declare war; raise and support armies (no appropriation for longer
  than two years); provide and maintain a navy; make rules for the armed
  forces and the militia; govern the federal district; and — the clause
  Lesson 02 takes up — make all laws "necessary and proper" to carrying
  the others into execution.
- **Reserved powers** — powers not given to the national government and
  not denied to the states. The Tenth Amendment states the rule: powers
  "not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited
  by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the
  people." Running schools, licensing drivers and doctors, setting most
  criminal law, and running local police and fire services are the
  classic examples.
- **Concurrent powers** — powers both levels hold at once: taxing,
  borrowing, building roads, creating courts, and enforcing laws. Two
  governments taxing the same income is not a mistake in the design; it
  is the design.
- **Denied powers** — powers the Constitution forbids. Article I, §9
  forbids the national government to suspend habeas corpus except in
  rebellion or invasion, pass bills of attainder or ex post facto laws,
  tax exports from a state, or grant titles of nobility. Article I, §10
  forbids states to enter treaties, coin money, pass bills of attainder
  or ex post facto laws, impair the obligation of contracts, or lay
  duties on imports or exports without Congress's consent.

Why divide power this way? The framers had just lived through two
failures. Under the Articles of Confederation, the center was too weak
to tax or regulate trade. Under a king, the center was too strong to
check. Federalism is their compromise, and *Federalist* No. 51 gives its
theory: "In the compound republic of America, the power surrendered by
the people is first divided between two distinct governments, and then
the portion allotted to each subdivided among distinct and separate
departments." The citizen therefore has, in Madison's words, a "double
security": if one government overreaches, the other — and the citizen's
vote in both — stands in the way. That is a *claim* to test, not a fact
to accept: Lessons 04 and 05 ask whether the double security works.

Two cautions before the practice. First, "reserved" does not mean
"unimportant": most of daily life — schools, roads, policing, licensing
— runs on reserved powers. Second, the lines move. A power can start as
a state practice, become a federal program, and be challenged back again
(Lesson 04). Classification is a snapshot of the Constitution's text
plus the courts' reading of it, not a permanent label.

### Worked example 1 — Sorting four powers

*Task:* Classify: (a) coining money, (b) licensing teachers, (c)
collecting income tax, (d) a state coining its own money.

*Reasoning:* (a) **Enumerated** — Article I, §8 expressly gives Congress
the coinage power. (b) **Reserved** — nothing in §8 gives the national
government teacher licensing, and §10 does not forbid it to states, so
it falls to the states under the Tenth Amendment. (c) **Concurrent** —
§8 gives Congress the tax power and the Sixteenth Amendment confirms an
income tax, while states also tax income; both levels hold it. (d)
**Denied to states** — Article I, §10 forbids states to coin money. So
the same *activity* (money) can be enumerated for one level and denied
to the other. Classify the *holder*, not just the verb.

### Worked example 2 — Why division is not just duplication

*Scenario:* A river floods a town. The town's fire department (local),
the state's National Guard (state), and federal disaster aid (national)
all respond. Is that wasteful duplication?

*Reasoning:* No — it is the design working as intended, with different
jobs at each level. The town responds first because it is closest and
fastest; the state coordinates across towns; the national government
supplies resources no single town could. Federalism's cost is real —
coordination takes time and arguments about who pays are inevitable —
but its benefit is also real: no single government must do everything,
and a failure at one level does not leave the citizen with nowhere to
turn. The project's job (see [project.md](../project.md)) is to test
that claim on one real issue.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Sort these four into enumerated, reserved, concurrent, or
   denied, and cite the clause or amendment for each: (a) declaring war;
   (b) running public schools; (c) a state passing an ex post
   facto law; (d) building a highway.
2. **G2.** A learner says, "Reserved powers are the leftovers nobody
   wanted." Write a two-sentence correction using one everyday example
   (schools, licensing, or policing).
3. **G3.** Explain, in your own words, Madison's "double security" in
   *Federalist* No. 51: what are the two divisions, and who benefits
   from each?

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** Classify all 12 powers in the W7S2 powers-sort (see
   [practice.md](../practice.md), Task 1) and write one sentence of
   justification for each.
2. **I2.** A state legislature votes to print its own paper currency to
   pay state workers. Which clause forbids it, and what happens if the
   state does it anyway? (Hint: think about who enforces §10.)
3. **I3.** Congress passes a law creating a national police force to
   patrol every town's streets. A governor objects. Which side has the
   stronger *textual* case, and why? Name the specific power categories
   in conflict.
4. **I4.** Error analysis: "Because taxation is concurrent, a state can
   tax anything Congress taxes, including federal property." Identify
   the error and correct it in two sentences. (Hint: Lesson 02's
   *McCulloch* rule is coming — but the Supremacy Clause already points
   the way.)

## Applied task

**A.** Draw a four-column table — Enumerated / Reserved / Concurrent /
Denied — and place five powers from your own daily life in it (for
example: your school's funding, the road outside, the mail, your
driver's license, a federal income-tax form). For one item, write a
sentence explaining why you are *unsure* of its column, and what source
you would check to settle it.

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) define enumerated and reserved powers;
(2) give one example of each; (3) state Madison's "double security"
idea in your own words.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Read §8 aloud in short clauses; gloss
  *imposts* (import taxes), *letters of marque* (government licenses for
  private warships — obsolete), and *militia* before assigning the text.
- **Alternate response mode:** The powers-sort may be done with cards,
  by pointing, or orally instead of in writing.
- **Extension:** Read the full Article I, §8 list and find the one
  enumerated power that surprises you most. Why do you think the
  framers listed it explicitly? (Strong answers engage with a power
  whose modern importance is small, such as letters of marque, or whose
  scope is contested, such as the commerce power.)

> **Teacher note:** The persistent misconception is that "federalism"
> means "the federal government is in charge." Correct it the first
> time it appears: federalism is a *division*, not a hierarchy. Full
> notes and all answers are in [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md)
> and [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).
