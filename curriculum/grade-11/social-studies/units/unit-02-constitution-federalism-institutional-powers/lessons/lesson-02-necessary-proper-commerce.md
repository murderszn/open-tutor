# Lesson 02 — Necessary, Proper, and Commercial: McCulloch and Gibbons

**Goal:** Explain the Necessary and Proper Clause and the Commerce
Clause through *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819) and *Gibbons v. Ogden*
(1824), and trace a federal power through the checks that restrain it.
**Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** Article I, §8, final clause;
*McCulloch* and *Gibbons* summaries below (opinion excerpts linked in
[resource-pack.md](../resource-pack.md)).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Congress may "coin money." May it
> therefore punish counterfeiters, even though "punish counterfeiting"
> is listed separately? Why does listing both matter? (Answer: yes —
> and the separate listing shows the framers spelled out means as well
> as ends. Today's question is what happens when a means is *not*
> listed.)

**Sources:** Constitution transcript, National Archives (public domain;
checked 2026-10-21); *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. 316 (1819), and
*Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1 (1824) (U.S. Supreme Court opinions, public
domain).

## Teaching

Article I, §8 ends with a clause that looks like a footnote and behaves
like an engine: Congress may "make all Laws which shall be necessary and
proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers." Two readings
fought over it from the start. The **strict** reading: "necessary" means
indispensable — Congress may use only means without which an enumerated
power cannot be exercised at all. The **broad** reading: "necessary"
means useful, convenient, or plainly adapted to a legitimate end.
*McCulloch* settled the fight in favor of the broad reading, and its
reasoning still governs implied-powers disputes.

**The case.** In 1816 Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United
States. Nothing in §8 says "charter a bank." Maryland, hostile to the
bank, taxed its Baltimore branch, and the bank's cashier, James
McCulloch, refused to pay. Two questions reached the Supreme Court
under Chief Justice John Marshall: (1) May Congress charter a bank at
all? (2) May a state tax a federal institution?

**Marshall's answers.** (1) Yes — implied powers. If the end is
legitimate and within the Constitution, "all means which are
appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not
prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution,
are constitutional." A bank is a fitting means to the enumerated ends
of taxing, borrowing, and regulating commerce; the Constitution does
not demand the *only* possible means. Marshall's famous line: "Let the
end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and
all means which are appropriate… are constitutional." (2) No — a state
may not tax the bank. "The power to tax involves the power to destroy":
if Maryland could tax the bank out of existence, a part could destroy
the whole. The Supremacy Clause (Article VI) makes federal law supreme
over conflicting state law, so Maryland's tax falls.

**The Commerce Clause.** Article I, §8 also gives Congress power "to
regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States,
and with the Indian Tribes." *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) gave that clause
its first great reading. New York had granted Aaron Ogden a monopoly
over steamboat traffic in New York waters; Thomas Gibbons ran a competing
ferry under a federal coastal license. Marshall held that "commerce"
includes navigation — the movement of goods and people, not just buying
and selling — and that Congress's commerce power, exercised through the
federal license, overrides the state monopoly. A state law that conflicts
with a valid federal law must yield. Together, *McCulloch* and *Gibbons*
built the national government's working toolkit: implied means plus a
commerce power that reaches the channels of interstate activity.

**The check that remains.** Implied powers are not unlimited powers.
Marshall's test has three gates: the end must be legitimate and within
the Constitution; the means must be appropriate and plainly adapted;
the means must not be prohibited by the Constitution. Lesson 04 shows
the Court enforcing the outer gate — in *Lopez* (1995) and *NFIB*
(2012) — when it judged that Congress had stretched the commerce power
past its constitutional scope. Implied does not mean infinite.

### Worked example 1 — Applying McCulloch's test

*Scenario:* Congress, exercising its power to "raise and support
armies," creates a military academy. No clause says "create academies."
Is it constitutional?

*Reasoning:* Apply the three gates. (1) End: raising armies is
enumerated, so the end is legitimate. (2) Means: an academy that trains
officers is plainly adapted to raising an effective army — not a
pretext for an unrelated goal. (3) Prohibition: nothing in the
Constitution forbids military academies. All three gates pass, so the
academy is a valid implied power. Contrast a *failed* application: a
law creating a national academy to train *local* police officers would
stumble at gate 1, because general policing is a reserved power, not an
enumerated end.

### Worked example 2 — Applying Gibbons's commerce reading

*Scenario:* A state bans all out-of-state trucks from its highways to
protect local trucking firms. A trucking company holds a federal
interstate operating license. Who wins?

*Reasoning:* The federal license wins. Under *Gibbons*, commerce
"among the several States" includes the *movement* itself — trucks on
highways are navigation's modern equivalent — and a state law that
conflicts with a valid federal regulation of that movement is
preempted by the Supremacy Clause. Note what this does *not* decide:
the state may still set neutral safety rules (speed limits, weight
limits) that do not discriminate against interstate commerce. The
distinction is between regulating the *channel* to favor locals and
regulating *safety* within it.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Restate Marshall's *McCulloch* test in your own words as
   three gates, then apply it to Congress chartering a bank today.
2. **G2.** Why did Maryland's tax fail even though states *can* tax?
   Use the "power to destroy" reasoning and the Supremacy Clause.
3. **G3.** A state grants one company a monopoly on ferry service
   across a river that forms a state border. Under *Gibbons*, what is
   the company's problem? Name the clause and the conflicting federal
   instrument.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** Congress, citing the commerce power, bans the interstate
   shipment of a product made with child labor in one state. Apply
   *Gibbons* and *McCulloch*: is the *end* within an enumerated power,
   and is the *means* plainly adapted? (Your answer should flag that
   later courts wrestled with exactly this kind of law — see the
   teacher guide.)
2. **I2.** A state taxes a federal courthouse's parking lot. Apply
   *McCulloch*'s second holding: may it? Explain in two sentences.
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "McCulloch means Congress can do anything
   useful." Identify the error and correct it using Marshall's three
   gates.
4. **I4.** Trace one power through the checks: Congress uses the
   Necessary and Proper Clause to create an agency that regulates
   interstate rail safety. Name one check each branch (or the states)
   can apply to that agency, and one check the people can apply.

## Applied task

**A.** You are a state attorney general in 1825, one year after
*Gibbons*. Write a three-sentence memo to your governor: what can your
state still regulate about steamboats on its own rivers, and what must
it now leave to Congress? Be specific about the line *Gibbons* drew.

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) state the broad reading of "necessary
and proper"; (2) state what *Gibbons* held "commerce" to include;
(3) name one limit on implied powers.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Gloss *charter* (a government grant of corporate
  existence), *preempt* (federal law displaces state law), and *plainly
  adapted* before assigning opinion excerpts. Excerpts are 200–300
  words; the adult reads Marshall's key sentences aloud first.
- **Alternate response mode:** The three-gates test may be answered as
  a labeled diagram (End / Means / Prohibition) instead of prose.
- **Extension:** Research the *Lopez* limit previewed in this lesson:
  in one paragraph, explain why the Court in 1995 said the Gun-Free
  School Zones Act went beyond the commerce power, and whether you
  think Marshall's test would have saved it. (Lesson 04 returns to this
  case in full.)
