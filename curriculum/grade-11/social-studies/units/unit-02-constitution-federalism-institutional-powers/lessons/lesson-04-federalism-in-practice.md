## Teaching

Textbook federalism divides history into three eras. Treat them as
*models* — lenses that highlight different moments — not as clean
periods with fences between them. All three patterns coexist today.

- **Dual federalism ("layer cake").** National and state governments
  operate in separate spheres with little overlap: the national
  government handles war, foreign affairs, and interstate commerce;
  states handle police, health, education, and morals. This was the
  dominant *idea* for much of the 19th century, even as practice
  (internal improvements, land grants) already blurred it.
- **Cooperative federalism ("marble cake").** From the New Deal
  (1930s) and Great Society (1960s) onward, the levels work jointly on
  shared problems: the national government supplies money and
  standards; states administer programs. Swirled, not layered: in one
  program, funding, rules, and delivery come from different levels at
  once.
- **"New" federalism (devolution).** From the 1970s–1980s onward, a
  push to return discretion to states: fewer strings, more state
  choice — often through **block grants** (see below) and through
  court decisions limiting national power. Devolution is a direction
  of argument, not a settled era: later administrations have pulled
  back toward national standards on other issues.

**The money tools.** The national government influences state policy
most often through money, not commands:

- **Categorical grants** — federal money for a *specific* purpose with
  detailed conditions (for example, federal highway funds tied to
  safety standards, or nutrition assistance with eligibility rules).
  States accept the money and the strings together; refusing the money
  is always an option, but rarely a costless one.
- **Block grants** — federal money for a *broad* area (for example,
  community development or social services) with wider state discretion
  over spending within that area. More state choice, less national
  control — and less national assurance about how the money is used.
- **Revenue sharing** (a historical variant, largely ended in the
  1980s) — federal money with almost no strings. Included here so
  learners recognize it in older sources.

**Mandates and preemption — the non-money tools.**

- A **mandate** is a federal requirement imposed on states or local
  governments. A **funded** mandate comes with federal money to pay for
  it; an **unfunded** mandate does not, and is therefore the most
  contested kind — the state must comply and find the money. The
  Unfunded Mandates Reform Act of 1995 requires Congress to estimate
  the cost of major mandates; it does not ban them.
- **Preemption** is the Supremacy Clause at work: valid federal law
  displaces conflicting state law. *Express* preemption says so in the
  statute ("no state may…"); *implied* preemption follows from a
  conflict (complying with both is impossible) or from Congress having
  occupied a whole field. *Gibbons* (Lesson 02) is the founding
  preemption case.
- **Commandeering** is the line the Court drew in *Printz v. United
  States* (1997): the national government may not *order* state
  officers to enforce a federal program (there, background checks under
  the Brady Act). It may offer money, it may regulate directly with its
  own officers, but it may not conscript the states' officers. That is
  the anti-commandeering rule, and it is a real limit on cooperative
  federalism's coercive side.

**The courts' outer limits.** Two modern cases bound the commerce
power that *Gibbons* built:

- *United States v. Lopez* (1995) struck down the Gun-Free School Zones
  Act, which made possessing a gun near a school a federal crime. The
  Court held that possessing a gun near a school was not, in itself,
  economic activity substantially affecting interstate commerce, and
  that accepting Congress's argument would erase any distinction
  between national and local power — including, the Court noted,
  education and criminal law, the states' classic domains.
- *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) upheld the Affordable Care Act's individual
  mandate as an exercise of the *taxing* power, but held that Congress
  could *not* justify the mandate under the Commerce Clause: commerce
  power regulates existing activity, the Court said, but does not
  compel people to *enter* commerce. And the Act's threat to withdraw
  all existing Medicaid funds from states that refused the expansion
  was unconstitutionally coercive — a "gun to the head," in the
  Court's phrase — rather than a lawful condition on new money.

Together: Congress may regulate interstate commerce broadly, but not
infinitely; it may attach conditions to *new* money, but may not use
existing money as a weapon to compel a state's surrender of a reserved
choice.

### Worked example 1 — Grant, mandate, or preemption?

*Scenario:* Congress offers states highway funds if they set a minimum
drinking age of 21. A state that refuses loses only a small percentage
of its highway funds. A second law orders every state to test all
commercial drivers to a federal standard, with no money attached.

*Reasoning:* The highway offer is a **categorical-grant condition** —
money with strings, accepted voluntarily (this is the real
*South Dakota v. Dole*, 1987 pattern, where the Court upheld a 5%
highway-funds condition). The driver-testing order is an **unfunded
mandate**: a requirement without money. The difference matters
politically and legally: conditions on money are judged by
voluntariness and relatedness; naked mandates are judged by whether
Congress has an enumerated power for the requirement at all — and, if
the mandate orders state *officers* to enforce it, by *Printz*'s
anti-commandeering rule.

### Worked example 2 — Applying Lopez and NFIB to a new law

*Scenario:* Congress passes a federal law setting a national
curriculum for all public high schools, citing the commerce power:
students are future workers, so education affects commerce.

*Reasoning:* Under *Lopez*, this fails the commerce test as argued.
Education is a classic state domain; the activity regulated (what is
taught in a classroom) is not itself economic activity, and the chain
("students become workers") is exactly the kind of limitless chain
*Lopez* rejected — accept it, and Congress could regulate anything
that eventually touches the economy. Congress could instead *offer*
categorical grant money tied to curricular standards (the *Dole*
route), or regulate a genuinely interstate element (for example,
interstate textbook commerce). The lesson: the *tool* must fit the
*power*.

## Guided practice

1. **G1 — Image activity (Activity A).** Using the illustration (or
   its text-only alternative): (a) match each building to a level of
   government; (b) name one service each building represents; (c) name
   one service in the scene that needs *two* levels working together
   (for example, the road: local streets, state highways, federal
   funding) and explain why.
2. **G2.** Sort each into categorical grant, block grant, funded
   mandate, unfunded mandate, or preemption: (a) federal money for
   school lunches with nutrition rules; (b) a broad federal grant for
   community development with state spending discretion; (c) a federal
   order that states inspect all bridges to a federal standard, with
   federal money for the inspections; (d) the same order with no money;
   (e) a federal safety standard that overrides a conflicting state
   standard.
3. **G3.** In two sentences, state the *Printz* anti-commandeering
   rule and give one thing Congress *may* still do instead of
   commandeering.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** A state refuses a new categorical grant because it dislikes
   the conditions. May Congress force it to take the money? Explain
   with the voluntariness idea from Worked Example 1.
2. **I2.** Congress threatens to withdraw *all* existing highway funds
   from any state that does not adopt a federal speed limit. Apply
   *NFIB*'s coercion reasoning: is this a lawful condition or a "gun
   to the head"? What fact would change your answer?
3. **I3.** A federal law bans the possession of a purely local,
   non-commercial item within a state, citing commerce because the
   item's ingredients once crossed a state line. Apply *Lopez*:
   what is the strongest argument against the law, and what is
   Congress's best reply?
4. **I4.** Error analysis: "Under cooperative federalism, states lost
   all their power." Identify the error and correct it with one example
   of a power states still exercise independently.

## Applied task

**A.** Choose one shared service from the illustration (road, school,
mail, courts). Write a paragraph tracing it across levels: which level
funds it, which level runs it, which level sets standards for it, and
where the levels could conflict. End with your judgment: is this
service better described by the layer cake or the marble cake — and
what evidence tips it?

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) distinguish a categorical grant from a
block grant; (2) distinguish a funded from an unfunded mandate;
(3) state one limit *Lopez* or *NFIB* set on national power.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Gloss *categorical* (for a named category),
  *coercive* (forcing by threat), and *devolution* (returning power
  downward) before the cases. The *Lopez*/*NFIB* holdings are given in
  plain language first; opinion excerpts are optional.
- **Alternate response mode:** The sorting task (G2) may be done with
  cards, and Activity A may be answered orally from the text-only
  alternative alone — the image is never required to answer.
- **Extension:** Find your own state's current official page for one
  grant-funded program (adult-supervised, dated in your evidence log).
  Report: is it categorical or block, what conditions attach, and
  what would your state lose by refusing it? (This is the project's
  research core.)

> **Teacher note:** Learners often treat "the federal government
> pays, so the federal government decides everything." Keep pressing
> the distinction between *conditions on offered money* and *orders*.
