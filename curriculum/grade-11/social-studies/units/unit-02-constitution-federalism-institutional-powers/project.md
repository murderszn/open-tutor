# Project — Federalism in My State

**Deliverable (due W10S4):** a written report (1,200–1,500 words) *or* a
10-minute presentation (slides or poster) answering the unit's
essential question on one real, shared-responsibility issue:
*Who gets to decide — the national government, the states, or both —
and what stops either one from deciding everything?*

## Choosing an issue

Pick one issue where both levels are visibly involved. Examples:
highway safety and funding; Medicaid or public-health funding;
disaster response and recovery; public-school funding and standards;
environmental protection of a river or air basin that crosses a state
line; election administration (federal standards, state-run elections).
The adult approves the choice at launch (W7S4) and checks that sources
are official and current at the time of use. Do not pick an issue to
endorse a party, candidate, or cause: the project analyzes institutions
and rules, and argues from evidence on both sides.

## Steps

1. **Compelling question (W7S4).** Frame one compelling question and
   two supporting questions about your issue (for example: "Who sets
   the rules for disaster aid after a flood in our state — and who pays
   when the two levels disagree?").
2. **Source plan and evidence log (W7S4–W9S3).** Gather at least four
   sources: (a) one federal document (a statute, regulation, or agency
   page from a `.gov` source); (b) one state document (a statute, agency
   page, or official state source); (c) one reputable news or
   nonpartisan explainer; (d) one constitutional or court source
   (Article I/VI, the Tenth Amendment, or a case from this unit).
   Log each source with its date accessed — current-law claims without
   a date are not accepted.
3. **Power map (W8S3).** Classify every power your issue touches as
   enumerated, reserved, concurrent, or denied, with a clause citation
   for each. Identify at least one grant, mandate, or preemption in
   play, and classify it correctly (categorical vs. block; funded vs.
   unfunded; express vs. implied).
4. **Conflict analysis (W9S3).** Find one real point of friction —
   a disagreement, lawsuit, funding dispute, or waiver negotiation —
   and explain it in *Marbury* terms: which law governs this point,
   and why? Apply at least one limit from this unit (*Lopez*, *NFIB*,
   *Printz*, or the anti-commandeering rule) and say whether it
   constrains either level on your issue.
5. **Argument (W9S4–W10S3).** Answer the essential question for your
   issue: does federalism here protect liberty, block solutions, or
   both — with evidence for each side and a reasoned judgment. Include
   a one-paragraph counterargument to your own judgment.
6. **Defense (W10S4).** Present your findings and defend your
   judgment in the structured deliberation. The adult (or a peer, if
   available) asks at least two challenge questions; your responses
   count toward the argument score.

## Deliverables

- The report or presentation itself (see word/time limits above).
- A one-page power map (table: power → level → clause/case).
- An evidence log (sources, dates accessed, one-sentence relevance
  note per source).
- A works list with full citations; constitutional and court sources
  cited by article, section, or case name and year.

## Rubric (40 points)

| Criterion (points) | Excellent (full) | Developing (half) | Needs work (low/zero) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Power classification (10)** | Every power correctly classified with a clause citation; denied powers name the level denied | Most powers correct; some citations missing or vague | Frequent misclassification (e.g., reserved called enumerated) or no citations |
| **Grant / mandate / preemption analysis (8)** | At least one correctly classified mechanism, with its real conditions or conflict explained | Mechanism named but misclassified, or conditions not explained | No mechanism identified, or purely asserted |
| **Source quality and dating (8)** | Four required source kinds, all official/reputable, all dated; evidence log complete | Three kinds, or some sources undated or weak | Fewer than three kinds, or undated current-law claims presented as permanent |
| **Conflict analysis and limits (8)** | One real friction explained in supremacy/judicial-review terms; one court limit correctly applied | Friction described but not analyzed, or limit misapplied | No friction identified, or limits asserted without application |
| **Argument and counterargument (6)** | Judgment defended with evidence on both sides; counterargument steelmanned, not strawmanned | One-sided argument, or counterargument weak | Judgment asserted without evidence, or no counterargument |

**Scoring notes for the adult:** 36–40 = the learner can independently
analyze a federalism dispute; 28–35 = solid, with one criterion to
re-teach; below 28 = re-teach power classification and the
grant/mandate distinction before Unit 03, using [practice.md](practice.md)
Tasks 1, 4, and 6. The project is *not* graded on the learner's policy
conclusion — only on classification accuracy, source quality, and
argument structure. A learner who argues "federalism blocks solutions"
and a learner who argues "federalism protects liberty" can both score
full marks with equal evidence.

## Safety and nonpartisanship

- Adult-supervised research only; official `.gov` sources first for
  current law. If a source is partisan, label it as such and balance it
  with a source from another perspective — never present one
  perspective's characterization as fact.
- No contact with officials, no real-world advocacy, no personal
  information: the project studies public institutions with public
  sources.
- If the chosen issue involves a current controversy the learner finds
  distressing, the adult may substitute a historical version of the
  same issue (for example, the 1980s highway-funds condition instead of
  a live funding fight) without penalty.
