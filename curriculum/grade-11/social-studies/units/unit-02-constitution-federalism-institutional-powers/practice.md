# Guided and Extra Practice — Unit 02

Use these tasks in the W7S2, W8S2, and W9S2 practice sessions, or as
retrieval homework. All answers are in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md) —
never beside these questions. Items marked ★ are retrieval checks:
answer from memory first, then check your notes.

## Task 1 — Powers-sort (12 powers) ★

Classify each power as **enumerated (E)**, **reserved (R)**,
**concurrent (C)**, or **denied (D)** — and for denied powers, say
*denied to whom*. Write one sentence of justification per power.

1. Coining money
2. Licensing doctors
3. Collecting taxes
4. Declaring war
5. Running public schools
6. A state entering a treaty with a foreign nation
7. Borrowing money
8. Setting the drinking age
9. Punishing counterfeiting
10. A state taxing imports from another state without Congress's consent
11. Establishing courts
12. Granting titles of nobility

## Task 2 — Checks diagram

Draw the path of a bill that becomes a law and is then challenged in
court. Label at least five checks, naming the actor and the tool at
each stage: (a) House/Senate passage, (b) presidential action,
(c) override possibility, (d) court review, (e) the people's check.
Then answer: at which stage could the bill *legally* die even though a
majority of voters supported it? Give two such stages.

## Task 3 — Case matching

Match each holding to its case, then write one sentence explaining the
principle each case established:

- (a) A state may not tax a federal institution.
- (b) "Commerce" includes navigation, and a federal license overrides a
  state monopoly.
- (c) The Supreme Court may declare a law unconstitutional.
- (d) Possessing a gun near a school is not, by itself, interstate
  commerce Congress may regulate.
- (e) Congress may not compel people to enter commerce, and may not use
  existing funds as a weapon to coerce states.

Cases: *Marbury v. Madison* (1803); *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819);
*Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824); *United States v. Lopez* (1995);
*NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012).

## Task 4 — Federalism models

For each scenario, name the model it best illustrates (dual,
cooperative, or "new" federalism) and defend your choice in one
sentence:

1. The national government handles foreign treaties; states handle
   marriage licenses; neither interferes with the other.
2. The national government funds a nutrition program; states set
   eligibility offices and deliver benefits under federal rules.
3. Congress replaces a detailed federal program with a broad grant and
   tells states to design their own programs within the area.

## Task 5 — Article V arithmetic ★

Answer from memory, showing your arithmetic:

1. How many House votes are needed to propose an amendment? (Show the
   fraction of 435.)
2. How many Senate votes are needed? (Show the fraction of 100, and
   explain the rounding.)
3. How many state legislatures must ratify? (Show the fraction of 50,
   and explain the rounding.)
4. How many states, voting no, can block any amendment?
5. Which amendment was ratified by state conventions, and why is that
   path notable?

## Task 6 — Supremacy vs. reservation

For each pair, state which law governs and why:

1. A valid federal workplace-safety standard vs. a conflicting state
   standard on the same point.
2. A state law setting the speed limit on a road Congress has never
   regulated.
3. A state law requiring a religious test for state office (compare
   Article VI, clause 3: "no religious Test shall ever be required").
4. A federal law ordering state police officers to run federal
   background checks (compare *Printz*).

## Task 7 — Error analysis

Correct each statement in two sentences (name the error, give the
correction):

1. "Federalism means the federal government is in charge of the states."
2. "McCulloch means Congress can do anything useful."
3. "Judicial review means the Supreme Court can veto any law it dislikes."
4. "The Constitution never changes because amendments are rare."
5. "Under cooperative federalism, states lost all their power."
