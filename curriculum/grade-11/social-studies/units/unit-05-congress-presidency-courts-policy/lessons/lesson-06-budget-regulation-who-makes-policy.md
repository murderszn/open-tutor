# Lesson 06 — The Budget, Regulation, and Who Really Makes Policy

**Goal:** Analyze the federal budget process — authorization,

## Teaching

### The budget process: two votes, not one

Federal spending requires two separate congressional decisions.
First, **authorization**: Congress passes a law creating or
continuing a program and setting its terms — what it may do and how
much it may spend. Second, **appropriation**: Congress passes a
separate law actually providing the money, usually through twelve
annual appropriations bills, one per subject area. A program can be
authorized and still receive nothing, because authorization is
permission and appropriation is payment. The fiscal year runs
October 1 to September 30. The president proposes a budget through
the Office of Management and Budget (OMB); the Congressional Budget
Office (CBO) — Congress's nonpartisan scorekeeper — estimates what
proposals would cost. If Congress misses the October 1 deadline, it
usually passes a **continuing resolution**, funding the government
temporarily at prior-year levels; if it passes nothing, the parts of
the government without funding shut down.

### Mandatory, discretionary, deficit, debt

Not all spending goes through the annual appropriations fight.
**Mandatory spending** — Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, and
interest on the debt — is set by permanent law and continues unless
Congress changes the underlying statute. **Discretionary spending**
— defense, education, transportation, agencies' operating budgets —
is what the twelve appropriations bills decide each year. When the
government spends more than it collects, the difference is the
**deficit** for that year; the accumulation of all past deficits,
minus surpluses, is the **national debt**. The two words are not
interchangeable: the deficit is a year's flow, the debt is the
running stock.

### The FY2024 numbers — dated and sourced

For fiscal year 2024 (October 1, 2023 – September 30, 2024), the
Congressional Budget Office reports: receipts **$4.919 trillion**
(17.1% of GDP), outlays **$6.752 trillion** (23.4% of GDP), and a
deficit of **$1.833 trillion** (6.4% of GDP). Net interest on the
public debt cost **$950 billion** — about 14 cents of every dollar
spent. The deficit was $138 billion larger than in FY2023. Gross
federal debt stood above $35 trillion at the end of FY2024 (Treasury
Fiscal Data). Use these numbers as a dated snapshot, not as a
permanent fact: the adult re-checks the current figures before
assigning, and the lesson's arithmetic — the deficit as a share of
outlays, interest as a share of outlays — is the skill, not the
memorization of the totals.

### Regulation: the full chain, once more

The budget is only half of how policy gets made; the other half is
regulation. The chain from Lesson 04, stated once as a whole:
Congress passes a statute (the Clean Air Act); the agency writes a
rule through notice and comment (the EPA's NAAQS); the public
comments; the agency finalizes the rule; a challenger sues; the
court reviews the rule against the statute (*Whitman*, 2001 — costs
barred at standard-setting; *Loper Bright*, 2024 — the court decides
the statute's meaning with independent judgment). Policy, in this
account, is made by all three branches in sequence: Congress sets
the goal, the agency fills in the details, the court polices the
boundaries — and the president's appointees run the agency that
does the filling in.

### Who really makes policy?

Now the essential question, with the unit's evidence on the table.
**Congress's case:** only Congress can write statutes and
appropriate money; every agency rule traces back to a statute; the
purse is the oldest check in the book. **The president's case:** the
president commands the bureaucracy, issues executive orders,
proposes the budget, vetoes bills, and speaks to the whole nation at
once — and in a crisis, the public looks to one person, not 535.
**The bureaucracy's case:** agencies write the rules that touch
daily life — air standards, drug approvals, workplace safety — and
their expertise and permanence outlast any single Congress or
president. **The courts' case:** a single decision can erase a rule
(*Loper Bright*) or a presidential action (*Youngstown*), and the
power to say what the law means is the power to set its boundaries.
No institution wins the argument outright. The honest answer is
that policy is made in the friction between them — and that the
friction is the point: each institution's claim on the others is
what keeps any one of them from deciding everything alone.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 — The arithmetic of a deficit.** Using the FY2024
figures: receipts $4.919 trillion, outlays $6.752 trillion, deficit
$1.833 trillion, net interest $0.950 trillion. (a) What share of
outlays was the deficit? Work: 1.833 ÷ 6.752 = 0.2715, about **27%**
— for every $100 the government spent, about $27 was borrowed.
(b) What share of outlays was interest? Work: 0.950 ÷ 6.752 =
0.1407, about **14%** — about $14 of every $100 went to interest
alone, before a single program was funded. (c) If outlays had stayed
the same and receipts had risen 10%, what would the deficit be?
Work: receipts 4.919 × 1.10 = 5.411; deficit = 6.752 − 5.411 =
1.341 trillion. *Check:* does a 10% revenue rise close a 27% gap?
(No — it cuts the deficit by about a quarter, from $1.83T to
$1.34T.)

**Worked example 2 — One regulation, four institutions.** Trace the
ozone standard through all four institutions: (a) Congress passed
the Clean Air Act and told the EPA to set standards "requisite to
protect the public health." (b) The EPA, led by the president's
appointee, proposed and finalized the stricter 1997 NAAQS after
public comment. (c) Industry sued, and the Supreme Court decided in
*Whitman* (2001) that the Act barred cost consideration at
standard-setting. (d) The budget then funded the EPA's enforcement
of the standard. Work the point: remove any one institution and the
story breaks — without Congress there is no authority, without the
agency there is no standard, without the court there is no boundary,
without the budget there is no enforcement. *Check:* which
institution's role changed most after *Loper Bright* (2024)? (The
court's — it now decides the statute's meaning with independent
judgment instead of deferring to the agency.)

## Guided practice

Work with the adult; hints in parentheses.

1. A program is authorized at $5 billion but appropriated $3
   billion. How much can the agency spend, and why? (Hint: which of
   the two votes is payment?)
2. Using the FY2024 figures, compute receipts as a share of outlays.
   What does the result mean in one sentence? (Hint: 4.919 ÷ 6.752.)
3. A president proposes a budget; Congress passes a different one.
   Whose budget becomes law — and what does the president's proposal
   actually do? (Hint: who holds the power of the purse?)

## Independent practice

4. Explain in two sentences the difference between mandatory and
   discretionary spending, and name one program in each category.
5. A learner says, "The national debt is just the deficit."
   Correct the error in two sentences, using the FY2024 deficit
   figure.
6. **Error analysis:** A learner says, "Agencies make policy because
   Congress writes vague laws, so Congress doesn't matter anymore."
   Identify the error and correct it with two pieces of evidence from
   this unit. (Hint: who creates agencies, and who can abolish them?)

## Application

7. Write a one-page verdict on the essential question — "Who really
   makes policy?" — with one paragraph of best evidence for Congress,
   one for the president, one for the bureaucracy, one for the courts,
   and a final paragraph giving your verdict *with a limitation*
   (one fact your verdict cannot explain, or one case where another
   institution plainly decided). Use at least two dated facts from
   this unit.

## Exit check

8. From memory: FY2024 receipts $___ trillion, outlays $___ trillion,
   deficit $___ trillion. (Approximations to one decimal place are
   acceptable; exact figures are in the lesson.)
9. Name the two votes Congress must take before money is spent, and
   say what happens if the second vote never happens.
