# Lesson 04 — The Bureaucracy: Agencies, Rulemaking, and the Courts

**Goal:** Explain the federal bureaucracy's structure and the
Administrative Procedure Act's notice-and-comment rulemaking process,
and analyze how courts review agency action after *Whitman v.
American Trucking Ass'ns* (2001) and *Loper Bright Enterprises v.
Raimondo* (2024). **Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** the
Administrative Procedure Act summary (5 U.S.C. §553); a Federal
Register entry the adult selects; the Clean Air Act chain in this
lesson.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Lesson 02 ended with a signed law;
> Lesson 03 said the president must "take Care that the Laws be
> faithfully executed." Who actually writes the detailed rules that
> carry a law out — Congress, the president personally, or someone
> else? (Answer: executive-branch agencies — the bureaucracy — acting
> under the statute Congress passed.)

## Teaching

### What the bureaucracy is

The federal bureaucracy is the executive branch's working machinery:
more than 2 million civilian employees (Office of Personnel
Management) carrying out the laws Congress passes. It has four
shapes. **Executive departments** — the 15 cabinet departments such
as State, Treasury, Defense, and Health and Human Services — are
headed by secretaries the president appoints and the Senate confirms.
**Independent agencies** such as the Environmental Protection Agency
(EPA) and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)
sit outside the cabinet but still answer to the president.
**Regulatory commissions** such as the Securities and Exchange
Commission (SEC) and the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) are
led by bipartisan boards whose members serve fixed terms and cannot
easily be removed — designed to be insulated from politics.
**Government corporations** such as the U.S. Postal Service and
Amtrak operate like businesses inside the government. Congress
creates every one of these bodies by statute, funds them, and can
abolish them. The president appoints their leaders (with Senate
confirmation for the senior posts). The courts review their actions.
That triple grip — created by Congress, led by the president's
appointees, checked by the courts — is the separation of powers
applied to the branch that does most of the government's daily work.

### How a rule is made: notice and comment

Congress writes statutes in broad terms — "set air-quality standards
requisite to protect the public health" — and leaves the details to
agencies. The Administrative Procedure Act of 1946 (APA) governs how
agencies fill in those details. The standard path is
**notice-and-comment rulemaking**: the agency publishes a **proposed
rule** in the Federal Register, the government's daily journal;
anyone — citizens, companies, advocacy groups, state governments —
may submit **public comments**, usually for 30 to 60 days; the agency
must consider the comments and explain its reasoning; then it
publishes a **final rule**, which takes effect after a waiting period
(usually 30 days). The public-comment step is the democratic hinge
of the whole process: it is the one point where a rule is made in the
open, before it binds anyone. Agencies must also publish the rule's
legal basis and respond to significant comments; a rule issued
without that explanation can be struck down as "arbitrary and
capricious" under the APA.

### The Clean Air Act chain: statute to rule to court

One chain shows the whole system. The Clean Air Act directs the EPA
to set National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) — maximum
concentrations for air pollutants — at a level "requisite to protect
the public health." In 1997 the EPA revised the ozone and fine
particulate standards, making them stricter. Industry groups led by
the American Trucking Associations sued, arguing that the EPA had to
consider the cost of compliance when setting the standards, and that
Congress had unconstitutionally delegated its lawmaking power to the
agency.

In *Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns* (2001), the Supreme Court
decided unanimously — on February 27, 2001, with Justice Scalia
writing — that the Act bars the EPA from considering implementation
costs when it sets primary health-based standards, and that the
delegation was constitutional. The chain is the lesson: Congress
wrote the statute; the EPA made the rule through notice and comment;
the public commented; the courts reviewed the rule against the
statute. Costs still mattered later, at the implementation stage —
the Act allows cost considerations there — but not at the
standard-setting stage. Congress had made that choice, and the Court
enforced it.

### Who decides what a statute means: from Chevron to Loper Bright

For forty years, courts answered the "what does the statute mean?"
question with **Chevron deference** (*Chevron U.S.A. v. Natural
Resources Defense Council*, 1984): if a statute was ambiguous, a
court deferred to the agency's reasonable interpretation. That ended
on June 28, 2024. In *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 603
U.S. 369 (2024), the Supreme Court held 6–3 that the APA requires
courts to "exercise independent judgment in deciding whether an
agency has acted within its statutory authority," and that
*Chevron* is overruled. A statute now has, in the Court's words, a
single best meaning that the court must find — an agency's view is
still considered for its expertise (the weaker *Skidmore* respect),
but it no longer binds the court. The decision also limited the
**major questions doctrine**, sharpened two years earlier in *West
Virginia v. EPA* (2022): on questions of vast economic and political
significance, a court expects Congress to have spoken clearly rather
than leaving the matter to an agency's implication. Present both
decisions neutrally: supporters say they return law-interpreting to
judges and keep agencies inside Congress's instructions; critics say
they shift technical questions from expert agencies to generalist
courts. The learner's job is to state each position with its evidence,
not to pick a winner.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 — Follow one rule.** The Clean Air Act says the
EPA must set a primary ozone standard "requisite to protect the
public health." The EPA proposes a stricter standard, publishes it in
the Federal Register, receives 10,000 comments, and finalizes it.
A trucking company sues, saying the EPA ignored compliance costs.
Work the reasoning through the chain: (a) the statute is the source
of the EPA's power; (b) the rule went through notice and comment, so
the procedure is satisfied; (c) on the merits, *Whitman* (2001)
already decided that the Act bars cost consideration at the
standard-setting stage — so the company's argument fails at this
stage, though costs may matter at implementation. *Check:* what
would the company have to show to win after *Loper Bright*? (That
the statute's best meaning, as a court independently reads it, does
not support the EPA's standard — not merely that the EPA's reading
was one reasonable reading among several.)

**Worked example 2 — Before and after 2024.** In 2023, a court
reviewing an ambiguous workplace-safety statute deferred to the
Labor Department's reasonable reading under *Chevron*. In 2025, the
same question returns. What changes? Work the reasoning: before
*Loper Bright*, the agency's reasonable interpretation controlled if
the statute was ambiguous. After *Loper Bright*, the court must
decide the statute's meaning itself, using its independent judgment;
the agency's reading is persuasive only to the extent its reasoning
is. The agency's expertise still counts — the court weighs it — but
it no longer decides the case. *Check:* does *Loper Bright* reopen
every old case decided under *Chevron*? (No — the Court said cases
decided in reliance on *Chevron* remain good law under statutory
*stare decisis*.)

## Guided practice

Work with the adult; hints in parentheses.

1. An agency publishes a proposed rule on Monday and a final rule
   the following Monday, with no comment period. What APA step was
   skipped, and what can a challenger argue? (Hint: the democratic
   hinge of the process.)
2. A statute tells an agency to set standards "as appropriate and
   necessary." A court must decide what that phrase means. Under
   *Chevron*, who decided? Under *Loper Bright*, who decides?
   (Hint: 1984 vs. 2024.)
3. A rule affects the entire national energy market and rests on a
   vague phrase in a 1970 statute. Which doctrine will the challenger
   invoke, and what will they argue Congress must have done?
   (Hint: *West Virginia v. EPA*, 2022.)

## Independent practice

4. Explain in three sentences why Congress writes broad statutes and
   leaves details to agencies — and one risk of doing so.
5. A citizen wants to influence a proposed rule on school-bus safety.
   List the exact steps she takes, from finding the proposal to
   seeing the final rule, and name the journal where both appear.
6. **Error analysis:** A learner says, "After *Loper Bright*,
   agencies can no longer interpret statutes at all." Identify the
   error and correct it. (Hint: what do agencies still do every day,
   and what did the Court say about their expertise?)

## Application

7. Choose one federal agency the adult approves (EPA, FDA, FCC, or
   the Department of Education). Find one current proposed rule in
   the Federal Register (dated, adult-selected). Write a half-page
   brief: the statute behind the rule, the rule's stated purpose, one
   comment theme you would expect from supporters, one from
   opponents, and the court that would review a challenge.

## Exit check

8. Name the four shapes of the bureaucracy and one example of each.
9. What is notice-and-comment rulemaking, and what journal publishes
   the notices?

> **Teacher note:** The most common misconception is that agencies
> "make laws" on their own authority. Drill the chain: statute first,
> rule second, court review third — an agency that acts without a
> statute has no power at all. The second misconception is that
> *Loper Bright* ended agency expertise; stress that the Court kept
> agency views as persuasive, and only removed their binding force.
