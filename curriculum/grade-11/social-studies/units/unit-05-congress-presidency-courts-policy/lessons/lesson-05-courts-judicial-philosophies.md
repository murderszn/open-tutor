# Lesson 05 — The Courts: Structure, Access, and Judicial Philosophies

**Goal:** Explain the federal court structure, how cases reach the
Supreme Court, and two judicial philosophies, and argue a moot-court
case — *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer* (1952) — from both
sides using Justice Jackson's three-zone framework. **Time:** ~55
minutes for the lesson; the moot-court simulation runs in Week 22,
S3. **Materials:** Constitution transcript excerpts (Article III);
*Youngstown* facts sheet in this lesson; moot-court role cards (see
the teacher guide).

## Teaching

### The federal court structure

Article III creates "one supreme Court" and lets Congress create
lower courts. The system has three levels. At the bottom are the
**94 district courts** — trial courts, one or more per state, where
cases begin, witnesses testify, and juries sit. In the middle are
the **13 courts of appeals** — 12 regional circuits plus the Court
of Appeals for the Federal Circuit — which review district-court
decisions for legal error; they do not re-try facts. At the top is
the **Supreme Court**: nine justices, including the chief justice,
who sit in Washington and decide the cases they choose to hear. State
court systems run alongside this structure, and most everyday cases
— contracts, crimes, family law — are state cases. Federal courts
hear federal questions (the Constitution, federal statutes, treaties)
and disputes between citizens of different states above a dollar
threshold.

### How a case reaches the Supreme Court

Almost no case starts at the Supreme Court. A case climbs: district
court, court of appeals, then a **petition for a writ of
certiorari** — a request that the Supreme Court review the lower
court's decision. The justices vote on the petition, and it takes
four votes — the **Rule of Four** — to grant review. The Court
receives thousands of petitions each year and grants roughly 60 to
80. It chooses cases that present an important federal question,
cases where the lower courts disagree (a "circuit split"), and cases
where a lower court may have decided an important issue wrongly.
When the Court declines review, the lower court's decision stands —
but that is not a ruling on the merits. When it grants review, the
parties file briefs, outside groups file **amicus curiae** ("friend
of the court") briefs, the justices hear oral argument, and then they
decide — by majority opinion, with concurrences and dissents.

### Two judicial philosophies — and what they are not

How should a judge read the Constitution? Two answers dominate the
debate, and the unit presents both without endorsing either.

**Originalism** (often paired with **textualism** for statutes)
holds that the Constitution should be read according to its original
public meaning — what its words meant to those who ratified them —
and that a judge's job is to apply that meaning, not to update it.
Its supporters say it constrains judges: if the meaning is fixed,
judges cannot smuggle their own preferences into the law. Its
critics say the framers' world is too distant to settle modern
questions, and that original meaning itself is often contested.

**Living constitutionalism** (sometimes called pragmatism) holds that
the Constitution states broad principles — liberty, equality, due
process — whose application must grow with new circumstances, and
that judges should read those principles in light of the present.
Its supporters say it keeps the Constitution workable across
centuries of change. Its critics say it lets judges replace the
people's choices with their own.

Two cautions. First, **judicial restraint** and **judicial
activism** are not philosophies; they are labels people attach to
decisions they dislike or like. A restrained judge defers to the
elected branches; an activist judge strikes laws down — but the same
justice can be restrained in one case and activist in another, and
both philosophies can produce either result. Second, neither
philosophy tells you how a case comes out by itself. The facts, the
statute, and the precedent matter. The moot court below tests that:
both sides can argue *Youngstown* in good faith.

### The moot-court case: Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)

**Facts.** In April 1952, during the Korean War, the United Steelworkers
union threatened a nationwide strike. President Truman, fearing that a
steel stoppage would cripple weapons production, issued Executive
Order 10340 seizing the nation's steel mills and directing the
Secretary of Commerce to run them. The steel companies sued. A
district court blocked the seizure; the government appealed; the
Supreme Court decided the case in June 1952, 6–3 against the
president: the seizure was unconstitutional. Justice Hugo Black wrote
the majority opinion: the president's power "must stem either from an
act of Congress or from the Constitution itself," and neither authorized
the seizure. Justice Robert Jackson's famous concurrence offered the
framework the moot court will use:

- **Zone 1 — maximum authority:** the president acts with Congress's
  express or implied authorization. Presidential power is at its
  strongest, because it combines the president's own power with
  Congress's.
- **Zone 2 — the twilight zone:** the president acts where Congress
  has neither granted nor denied authority. Power is uncertain, and
  the outcome turns on "the imperatives of events and contemporary
  imponderables."
- **Zone 3 — lowest ebb:** the president acts against the express or
  implied will of Congress. Power is at its weakest, and the action
  can survive only if the Constitution grants the president the power
  exclusive of Congress.

Jackson placed the steel seizure in Zone 3: Congress had considered
seizure powers in the Taft-Hartley Act and the Defense Production Act
and had chosen not to grant them — so the president acted against
Congress's will, and no constitutional provision gave him the power
to seize private industry.

**Moot-court roles (Week 22, S3):** two advocates for the steel
companies (the seizure was unlawful), two advocates for the United
States (the seizure was lawful), three justices (one writes the
majority, one writes a concurrence, one writes a dissent), and a
clerk who keeps time and records the questions. Each side gets eight
minutes; justices may interrupt with questions at any time. The
debrief asks: which zone did each side argue, and which zone did the
justices choose — and why?

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 — Which level hears it?** A citizen sues a
federal agency, saying its new rule violates the Clean Air Act. A
neighbor sues another neighbor over a broken fence. Which court
hears each case first? Work the reasoning: the agency suit raises a
federal question (a federal statute), so it begins in a federal
district court. The fence suit is a state-law dispute between
neighbors — a state court, not a federal one. *Check:* if the agency
suit loses in the district court, where does it go next? (The court
of appeals for that circuit — and only then, by petition, to the
Supreme Court.)

**Worked example 2 — Apply Jackson's zones.** A president, facing a
port strike, orders the Navy to unload cargo ships. Congress has
passed a statute saying the president "may" use the armed forces to
keep ports open in emergencies. A second president orders the seizure
of a private railroad; Congress has passed a statute saying the
president "may not" seize railroads. Which zone is each action in,
and what follows? Work the reasoning: the port order is Zone 1 —
Congress authorized it, so the president's power is at its maximum
and a court will almost certainly uphold it. The railroad seizure is
Zone 3 — Congress forbade it, so the president's power is at its
lowest ebb and the action survives only if the Constitution grants
the power exclusively to the president, which it does not for
seizing private property. *Check:* what if Congress had said nothing
about railroads at all? (Zone 2 — the twilight zone — where the
court weighs the emergency, past practice, and the statute's
silence.)

## Guided practice

Work with the adult; hints in parentheses.

1. A case presents a question the courts of appeals have answered
   three different ways. Why is the Supreme Court more likely to
   grant certiorari here than in a case every circuit agrees on?
   (Hint: what does the Court exist to resolve?)
2. An originalist judge and a living-constitutionalist judge both
   read the First Amendment's words "Congress shall make no law …
   abridging the freedom of speech." Name one question on which
   their methods would most likely diverge. (Hint: think about a
   technology the framers never saw.)
3. Place EO 9981 (Lesson 03) in Jackson's zones. Congress had not
   legislated on military segregation. Which zone, and why?
   (Hint: had Congress granted, denied, or stayed silent?)

## Independent practice

4. Explain in two sentences why the Supreme Court grants so few
   petitions — and why "declined to hear" is not the same as "decided."
5. A justice writes that "the Constitution's meaning was fixed at
   ratification and a judge's task is to find it, not improve it."
   Name the philosophy and one argument its supporters make.
6. **Error analysis:** A learner says, "The Supreme Court hears
   every case that is appealed to it." Identify the error and
   correct it, naming the Rule of Four. (Hint: how many petitions,
   how many grants?)

## Application

7. Prepare a one-page moot-court brief for the side the adult
   assigns in *Youngstown*: (a) the zone you argue the seizure falls
   in, (b) two reasons from the facts, (c) the strongest argument for
   the other side and your reply to it. Bring it to the Week 22, S3
   simulation.

## Exit check

8. Name the three levels of the federal courts and the number of
   courts at the first two levels.
9. State Jackson's three zones in one sentence each.
