# Lesson 02 — How a Bill Becomes Law (and Where It Stops)

**Goal:** Trace a bill's path through introduction, committee, floor,
conference, and presidential action; name the friction points
(filibuster and cloture, holds, veto, pocket veto) and compute a
veto-override threshold. **Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** the
bill-path diagram in this unit's `assets/`; Constitution transcript
excerpts (Article I, §7).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Lesson 01 said most bills die in
> committee. Name two other places a bill can stop *after* leaving
> committee. (Answers: the floor — a chamber can vote it down, or
> the Senate can filibuster it; the president's desk — a veto or a
> pocket veto.)

**Sources:** [The Constitution of the United States: A
Transcription](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript),
National Archives (public domain); Congress.gov, Library of Congress,
[Key Historical Court Decisions Shaping EPA's Program Under the Clean
Air Act](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R43699) (CRS report,
opened 2026-11-11 — cited for the Clean Air Act's legislative
history in Lesson 04; the legislative-process steps here follow
Article I, §7).

## Teaching

### The five stages

A bill becomes a law by passing five stages, in order, with both
chambers and the president involved. Memorize the stages first; the
frictions make sense only once the path is clear.

*Figure 2 — The legislative path. Deterministic diagram; the text-only
version below carries the same information.*

**Text-only version of the diagram:** Stage 1, Introduction: a member
introduces the bill; it is numbered and referred to a committee.
Stage 2, Committee: hearings, markup, and a vote to report — or the
bill dies here (the most common fate). Stage 3, Floor: debate and a
vote in one chamber, then the same in the other. Stage 4, Conference:
if the two versions differ, a conference committee writes one text
that both chambers must approve. Stage 5, Presidential action: the
president signs (it becomes law), vetoes (it returns to Congress),
or, if Congress has adjourned, lets it die unsigned — a pocket veto.

### Stage 1 — Introduction and referral

Any member of either chamber may introduce a bill. It is numbered
(H.R. in the House, S. in the Senate) and referred to the committee
with jurisdiction over its subject — the decision Lesson 01
practiced. A bill's sponsor may gather co-sponsors to signal support,
but co-sponsorship does not move a bill; only the committee chair's
schedule does.

### Stage 2 — Committee: hearings, markup, report

The committee may hold hearings, calling witnesses to testify — the
room in Lesson 01's illustration. Then it **marks up** the bill,
amending it line by line, and votes on whether to **report** it to
the floor, usually with a written report explaining the bill. The
committee may also do nothing. Doing nothing is the most common
outcome: of the thousands of bills introduced in a two-year Congress,
only a small fraction are ever reported. A bill that is never reported
is not defeated in a vote; it simply expires when the Congress ends.

### Stage 3 — Floor: debate, amendment, vote

In the House, the Rules Committee sets the debate terms for most
bills — time limits and which amendments are in order. In the Senate,
debate is open-ended unless the Senate votes for **cloture**, which
requires 60 votes and limits further debate to 30 hours. A senator
who wishes to delay may **filibuster** — speak at length to prevent a
vote — and a senator may place a **hold**, asking their party leader
to delay a bill or nomination. Neither a filibuster nor a hold is in
the Constitution; both are Senate rules and customs, which the Senate
can change. After debate, the chamber votes. A simple majority
passes the bill — 218 in the House, 51 in the Senate (or 50 plus the
vice president's tie-breaking vote). The bill then goes to the other
chamber, which repeats the process. The exception that proves the
rule is **budget reconciliation**: a special procedure for
spending, revenue, and debt-limit bills that cannot be filibustered
in the Senate and passes with a simple majority — but only for
provisions that fit strict budget rules (the Byrd rule).

### Stage 4 — Conference: one text, or nothing

If the two chambers pass different versions of the same bill, a
**conference committee** — members from both chambers — negotiates a
single text. Both chambers must then approve that exact text, with no
further amendments. If either chamber refuses, the bill dies. If the
two chambers pass identical texts, this stage is skipped.

### Stage 5 — Presidential action: sign, veto, pocket veto

Article I, §7 gives the president ten days (Sundays excepted) to act.
Three outcomes are possible. The president **signs** the bill, and it
becomes law. The president **vetoes** it, returning it to Congress
with objections; Congress may **override** the veto with a two-thirds
vote in each chamber — 290 of 435 in the House and 67 of 100 in the
Senate. Or the president does nothing while Congress is adjourned:
after ten days the bill dies without a signature — a **pocket veto**,
which Congress cannot override because it is not in session to vote.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 — Follow the fictional School Lunch bill.**
Imagine the *School Lunch Nutrition Standards Act*, a fictional bill
requiring the Department of Agriculture to update school-meal
nutrition standards. Trace it: (1) A House member introduces it;
it is referred to the House Committee on Education and the
Workforce. (2) The committee holds a hearing with a school nutrition
director as witness, marks up the text, and reports it 26–17. (3)
The House passes it 301–129. The Senate refers it to its Health,
Education, Labor, and Pensions Committee, which reports a different
version; the Senate passes that version 58–42. (4) A conference
committee writes one text; both chambers approve it. (5) The
president signs it. Notice what the trace shows: the bill changed at
every stage — committee markup, a different Senate version,
conference — and the final law is none of the earlier drafts. *Check:*
at which stage could this bill most easily have died? (Committee —
in either chamber — or the conference, where a disagreement ends the
bill outright.)

**Worked example 2 — Count the override.** The president vetoes a
bill that passed the House 290–145 and the Senate 68–32. Can
Congress override? Work the arithmetic: an override needs two-thirds
of each chamber. House: two-thirds of 435 is 290 — the bill's 290
votes are exactly the threshold, so the House can override if every
supporter holds. Senate: two-thirds of 100 is 66.67, so 67 votes are
needed — the bill's 68 votes clear it. The override succeeds, but
only just, and only if nobody changes their vote. *Check:* if three
House supporters switch, what happens? (287 < 290 — the override
fails and the veto stands.)

## Guided practice

Work with the adult; hints in parentheses.

1. A bill passes the House 250–185 and the Senate 55–45. The
   president vetoes it. Can the House override? Can the Senate?
   (Hint: compute two-thirds of 435 and of 100 first.)
2. A senator announces she will filibuster a bill with 59
   supporters. How many more votes do the supporters need, and what
   is the vote called? (Hint: Lesson 01, the cloture rule.)
3. A bill passes both chambers, but the House version funds a program
   at $10 million and the Senate version at $12 million. What stage
   comes next, and what must it produce? (Hint: the two texts must
   become one.)

## Independent practice

4. Explain in three sentences why a bill's supporters might prefer
   to attach it to a reconciliation bill rather than move it alone.
5. A president vetoes a bill on the last day of a congressional
   session, and Congress adjourns the next day. Is this a regular
   veto or a pocket veto — and can Congress override it? Explain.
6. **Error analysis:** A learner says, "The filibuster is in the
   Constitution, which is why the Senate needs 60 votes to pass any
   bill." Identify both errors and correct them. (Hint: where does
   the 60-vote rule come from, and what does it end — debate or the
   bill?)

## Application

7. Take the fictional School Lunch bill from Worked example 1 and
   re-run it with one change: the Senate version fails cloture,
   57–43. Write the bill's new ending in four sentences: what
   happens to the bill, what its supporters can still try, and what
   the House-passed version is now worth.

## Exit check

8. List the five stages in order, from memory.
9. A bill needs 290 House votes to override a veto. Show the
   arithmetic that produces 290.

> **Teacher note:** Two misconceptions dominate here. First, learners
> treat "passed the House" as "became law" — drill the five stages
> until the learner names the chamber and the stage every time.
> Second, learners think the filibuster is a constitutional rule.
> Stress that cloture is a Senate rule (Rule XXII) the Senate itself
> can change, and that it ends debate rather than defeating a bill.
