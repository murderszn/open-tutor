# Lesson 03 — The Presidency: Formal Powers and Informal Power

**Goal:** Compare the presidency's formal powers with its informal
powers, and evaluate one executive order — Executive Order 9981
(1948) — for its legal basis, its reach, and its limits.
**Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** Constitution transcript
excerpts (Article II); the text of Executive Order 9981 (public
domain; National Archives).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Unit 02 listed the president's formal
> powers: commander in chief, veto, appointments, treaties. Name one
> thing a president can do that is *not* written in Article II.
> (Answers vary: go on television to pressure Congress; issue a
> signing statement; set the national agenda through speeches. This
> lesson names those informal powers.)

## Teaching

### The formal powers: what Article II actually grants

The presidency's formal powers are the ones written down. Article II
makes the president **commander in chief** of the armed forces,
gives the president the power to make **treaties** with the advice
and consent of the Senate (two-thirds), to **appoint** ambassadors,
judges, and officers (also with Senate confirmation), to **veto**
bills, to grant **pardons**, to receive ambassadors, and to "take
Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." That last phrase — the
Take Care Clause — is the source of the president's duty to enforce
the law, and of much of the argument about how far presidential
power reaches. The formal list is short. Everything else a president
does is built on it, on statutes Congress passed, or on custom.

### The informal powers: persuasion, orders, and the public

A modern president's influence far exceeds the formal list, and it
comes from three informal sources. First, **persuasion**: the
president is the one national elected official, and can use speeches,
press conferences, and the State of the Union to set the agenda and
pressure Congress — what Theodore Roosevelt called the **bully
pulpit** ("bully" in his era meaning excellent). Second,
**executive orders**: written directives to the executive branch
telling agencies how to carry out the law. They have the force of
law while they stand, but a later president may revoke them, and a
court may strike them down. Third, **executive agreements** with
foreign governments, which — unlike treaties — do not need the
Senate's two-thirds consent. Presidents also issue **signing
statements** when signing a bill, stating how they understand it;
these are not law, and their legal weight is contested.

### Close read: Executive Order 9981 (July 26, 1948)

On July 26, 1948, President Harry Truman signed Executive Order
9981, "Establishing the President's Committee on Equality of
Treatment and Opportunity in the Armed Forces." Its key sentence
reads:

> "It is hereby declared to be the policy of the President that
> there shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all
> persons in the armed services without regard to race, color,
> religion or national origin."

The order's legal basis is stated in its opening: "by virtue of the
authority vested in me as President of the United States, by the
Constitution and the statutes of the United States, and as Commander
in Chief of the armed services." Three sources, named in one
sentence: the Constitution, statutes, and the commander-in-chief
power. The order created a seven-member advisory committee to
recommend how to carry the policy out, and directed that the policy
"shall be put into effect as rapidly as possible, having due regard
to the time required to effectuate any necessary changes without
impairing efficiency or morale."

Why did this work as an executive order when Congress had not acted?
Because the armed forces are the one institution the president
directly commands. Truman could order the military's internal policy
without new legislation — the same commander-in-chief power that lets
a president direct troop movements. And what were its limits? The
order declared a policy and created a committee; it did not end
segregation overnight. Full integration took years, driven by field
commanders during the Korean War and by later orders and statutes —
including the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which Congress passed. The
lesson is precise: an executive order can move faster than Congress
inside the president's own chain of command, and it cannot do what
only Congress can do — appropriate money, or write a law that binds
private citizens.

### The war power: the one formal power with a statute around it

The commander-in-chief power has one statutory frame the learner
must know: the **War Powers Resolution of 1973**. Congress may
declare war; the president commands the forces. The Resolution
requires the president to notify Congress within 48 hours of
introducing forces into hostilities and to withdraw them after 60
days (plus a 30-day withdrawal period) unless Congress authorizes
the action. Presidents of both parties have questioned whether the
Resolution binds them, and Congress has never successfully enforced
the 60-day clock against a president's objection. Present it as it
is: a statute that states a rule, whose enforcement is itself
contested — an example of the separation of powers as an argument,
not a settled answer.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 — Classify the power.** A president orders the
armed forces to integrate their units (EO 9981). A president asks
Congress in a speech to pass a civil-rights bill. A president signs
a treaty with another country. Classify each: formal or informal —
and under what authority? Work the reasoning: the order is an
exercise of the formal commander-in-chief power, executed through
an executive order. The speech is informal power — the bully pulpit
— with no legal force at all. The treaty is a formal power, but it
is not complete until two-thirds of the Senate consents. *Check:*
which of the three could a court strike down? (The order — if it
exceeded the president's authority; the speech cannot be struck
down because it has no legal effect; the treaty can be challenged
only in narrow circumstances once ratified.)

**Worked example 2 — Find the limit.** A president issues an
executive order directing a federal agency to spend money Congress
has not appropriated. Is the order valid? Work the reasoning: no.
Article I gives Congress the power of the purse; an executive order
cannot appropriate money. The order would exceed the president's
authority — the same test the Supreme Court applied in *Youngstown*
(Lesson 05), where the president's seizure of steel mills fell in
Justice Jackson's "lowest ebb" because Congress had not authorized
it. *Check:* what could the president lawfully do instead? (Ask
Congress to appropriate the money; direct the agency to spend
already-appropriated money within its lawful discretion.)

## Guided practice

Work with the adult; hints in parentheses.

1. A president issues an executive order on a matter Congress has
   already legislated *against*. Under Jackson's framework (preview
   from Lesson 05), is the president's authority at its maximum or
   its lowest ebb? (Hint: Congress has spoken — against.)
2. A president and another country's leader sign an executive
   agreement on trade. A senator complains that the Senate was
   bypassed. Is the senator right that a treaty was required?
   (Hint: what distinguishes an executive agreement from a treaty?)
3. Name the three sources of authority EO 9981 cites in its opening
   sentence, and say which one made the order enforceable inside the
   military. (Hint: read the quoted opening again.)

## Independent practice

4. Explain in two sentences why a president might prefer an
   executive order to asking Congress for a law — and one reason the
   order is the weaker tool.
5. A president vetoes a bill and then explains the veto in a
   nationally televised address. Which act is formal power and which
   is informal — and what does each one accomplish that the other
   cannot?
6. **Error analysis:** A learner says, "Executive Order 9981 ended
   segregation in the military the day Truman signed it." Identify
   the error and correct it. (Hint: what did the order actually
   create, and what did it direct?)

## Application

7. Choose one current or historical executive order the adult
   approves (for example, an order on federal hiring, land use, or
   disaster response). Write a half-page evaluation: (a) the
   authority it cites, (b) whether Congress had legislated on the
   subject, (c) one thing it could do that Congress could not do as
   fast, and (d) one limit on it. Use the EO 9981 analysis as your
   model.

## Exit check

8. Name two formal powers and two informal powers of the presidency.
9. Quote or paraphrase the key sentence of EO 9981, and name the
   year it was issued.

> **Teacher note:** The common misconception is that the president
> can order anything — that an executive order is a law by another
> name. Drill the limit: an order binds the executive branch, cannot
> appropriate money, cannot contradict a statute, and can be revoked
> by the next president. The second misconception is that EO 9981
> "ended" segregation in one stroke; the order's own text — a policy
> declaration plus a committee — shows the slower reality, and the
> lesson's honest account of that pace is the point. Sensitive
> content: EO 9981 concerns racial segregation; preview the material
> and offer a reflection alternative. Answer-key references: guided
