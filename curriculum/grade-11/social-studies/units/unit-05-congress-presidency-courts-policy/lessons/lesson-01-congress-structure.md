# Lesson 01 — Congress: Two Chambers, One Legislature

**Goal:** Explain Congress's design — bicameralism, two-year House and
six-year Senate terms, leadership, and the committee system — and how
each chamber's rules shape what becomes law. **Time:** ~55 minutes.
**Materials:** Constitution transcript excerpts (Article I, §1–§8);
the committee-hearing illustration in this unit's `assets/`.

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Unit 02 said Congress has 435 House
> seats and 100 Senate seats. Which chamber's members face the voters
> more often, and why would the framers design it that way? (Answer:
> the House — two-year terms — so it stays close to public opinion;
> the Senate's six-year staggered terms were designed for steadiness
> and longer deliberation.)

## Teaching

### Why two chambers?

Article I, §1 vests "all legislative Powers herein granted" in a
Congress "which shall consist of a Senate and House of
Representatives." That single sentence is the whole design: one
legislature, two chambers, and no law without both. The framers built
the split deliberately. The House was meant to be close to the people
— large, elected often, apportioned by population. The Senate was
meant to be steady — small, elected rarely, equal by state. A bill
must survive both temperaments, which is why the same proposal can
sail through one chamber and die in the other. That is not a defect in
the design. It is the design: the two chambers check each other
before either can make law.

### The House: 435 seats, two-year terms

The House has 435 voting seats, apportioned among the states by
population after each census, plus non-voting delegates (including
from the District of Columbia). Every member serves two years and
faces re-election in every even-numbered year, so the whole House is
always one election away from a new majority. The size and the short
term make the House the more responsive chamber — and the more
volatile one. The House is led by the **Speaker of the House**,
elected by the members, who controls the floor schedule, refers bills
to committees, and is second in the line of presidential succession.
Each party also elects a **majority leader** and a **minority
leader** and **whips**, who count votes and keep their party's members
aligned. House debate is tightly controlled: the Rules Committee sets
the terms of debate for most bills, including how long members may
speak and which amendments are allowed.

### The Senate: 100 seats, six-year terms

The Senate has 100 seats — two per state, regardless of population.
Senators serve six-year terms, staggered so that about one-third of
the seats are contested in each even-numbered year. The Senate's
president is the vice president, who votes only to break a tie; the
day-to-day presiding officer is the president pro tempore, a senior
member of the majority party. The majority and minority leaders
organize their parties, and whips count votes, as in the House — but
Senate debate is far less controlled. Any senator may speak at length,
and ending debate normally requires **cloture**: 60 votes under Rule
XXII. That single rule is why the Senate is often called the place
where bills go to slow down — and why a bill with majority support
can still fail there. (Lesson 02 works the arithmetic.)

### Committees: where most lawmaking actually happens

Neither chamber debates 435 or 100 members' worth of bills on the
floor. The real work happens in **committees**. A **standing
committee** is permanent, with jurisdiction over a subject — for
example, the House Committee on Ways and Means (taxes and revenue)
or the Senate Committee on Armed Services. **Select committees** are
temporary, created for one inquiry. **Joint committees** draw members
from both chambers. When the two chambers pass different versions of
the same bill, a **conference committee** — members of both — works
out a single text.

Committees do three things that matter for this unit. They **hold
hearings**, calling witnesses to testify under oath and answer
questions. They **mark up** bills — amending the text line by line
before voting on whether to send it to the floor. And they **kill**
bills: most introduced bills never leave committee, which is the
quietest and most common fate a bill meets. A committee's **chair**
runs it; the senior member of the other party is the **ranking
member**. Chairs and ranking members set the agenda, choose witnesses,
and decide which bills get a hearing at all.

**Activity (5 min):** Using the figure and the text-only alternative,
label the roles: (a) the chair — where does the chair sit, and what
does the chair control? (b) the ranking member — which seat is most
likely theirs, and why? (c) the witness — why does the witness sit
alone, facing the committee? (d) the audience seats in the foreground
— who sits there, and what can they do? Answers: (a) center seat of
the dais; the chair recognizes speakers, sets the order, and can
limit or extend questioning. (b) Usually beside the chair or at the
opposite end of the dais — the senior member of the minority party,
who leads that party's questioning. (c) Because a hearing is an
inquiry, not a debate: the committee asks, the witness answers under
oath. (d) Staff, press, and members of the public; they observe but
do not question witnesses.

## Worked examples

**Worked example 1 — Why the same bill faces two different tests.**
A bill to fund a national bridge-repair program passes the House
412–23. In the Senate it stalls at 55 votes to end debate. Why is
that possible? Work the reasoning: the House needs a simple majority
(218 of 435) and controls debate through the Rules Committee, so a
popular bill moves fast. The Senate needs 60 votes for cloture
before it can even vote on the bill, so 55 supporters are not
enough — the bill is not defeated, but it cannot proceed. The
difference is not popularity; it is chamber rules. *Check:* what
would the Senate need — five more votes for cloture, or a different
procedure? (Five more votes; or the bill's supporters could try a
different vehicle, such as attaching it to a bill that can pass
under reconciliation — see Lesson 02.)

**Worked example 2 — Which committee gets the bill?** A member
introduces a bill to change the tax deduction for home mortgage
interest. Which committee should receive it, and why does the
referral matter? Work the reasoning: tax bills go to the House Ways
and Means Committee (or the Senate Finance Committee), because
standing committees have subject-matter jurisdiction and the
Speaker (or Senate leadership) refers bills accordingly. The
referral matters because the chair decides whether the bill gets a
hearing or a markup at all — a bill referred to a hostile committee
may simply never be scheduled. *Check:* if the bill also created a
new housing program, which second committee might claim part of it?
(The Financial Services Committee, or the relevant authorizing
committee — and a bill can be referred to more than one committee in
sequence or jointly.)

## Guided practice

Work with the adult; hints in parentheses.

1. A state has 14 House seats after the census. It loses population
   and drops to 13 seats after the next census. What happens to its
   Senate seats? (Hint: Senate seats do not depend on population.)
2. A House committee votes 24–18 to report a bill to the floor. What
   does "report" mean, and what happens next? (Hint: reporting is a
   recommendation, not a law.)
3. The Senate majority leader wants a vote on a bill with 58
   supporters. What is the immediate obstacle, and how many more
   votes are needed? (Hint: Lesson 01, the cloture rule.)

## Independent practice

4. Explain in two sentences why the framers gave the Senate
   six-year staggered terms while giving the House two-year terms.
5. A witness is called to a hearing on a proposed food-safety rule.
   Name two things the committee can learn from the hearing that it
   could not learn from reading the bill's text.
6. **Error analysis:** A learner says, "The Speaker of the House is
   the most powerful person in Congress because the Speaker decides
   what becomes law." Identify the error and correct it in one
   paragraph. (Hint: what else must happen after the Speaker acts?)

## Application

7. Your state has a bill to rebuild a flood-damaged highway. Using
   the committee structure in this lesson, write the bill's first
   three steps: who introduces it, which committee receives it, and
   what the committee must do before the floor can vote. Name the
   real committee (House Transportation and Infrastructure, or the
   Senate Environment and Public Works Committee) and say why that
   committee has jurisdiction.

## Exit check

8. Name the two chambers' sizes and terms from memory: House ___ seats,
   ___-year terms; Senate ___ seats, ___-year terms.
9. What is a markup, and who runs it?

> **Teacher note:** The most common misconception is that Congress
> is one body that votes once. Learners who hold it will be confused
> by every later lesson. Watch for it in the exit check: if a learner
> writes "Congress voted 300–135," ask which chamber and what stage.
> The second misconception is that committees are decorative. Stress
> that most bills die in committee and that the chair's scheduling
> power is the first veto point a bill meets. Answer-key references:
