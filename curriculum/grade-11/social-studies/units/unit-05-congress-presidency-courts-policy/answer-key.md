# Unit 05 — Answer Key

**Separate from student materials.** Every practice item, quiz
question, and assessment task is solved here. Open responses accept
any answer that matches the reasoning and facts given; the adult
judges equivalence. All figures are FY2024 and dated unless stated.

## Lesson 01 — Congress structure

**Guided 1.** The state's Senate seats do not change: two per state,
regardless of population. It keeps 2 senators.

**Guided 2.** "Report" means the committee voted to send the bill to
the floor, usually with a written report explaining it. Next: the
bill is scheduled for floor debate — in the House the Rules
Committee sets the debate terms; in the Senate the majority leader
schedules it.

**Guided 3.** The obstacle is cloture: ending debate in the Senate
requires 60 votes under Rule XXII. With 58 supporters, the bill is
2 votes short of cloture and cannot proceed to a vote.

**Independent 4.** Accept: the House's two-year terms keep it close
to public opinion and make it responsive (and volatile); the
Senate's six-year staggered terms insulate it from momentary
passions and give it a longer view, so it deliberates more slowly.

**Independent 5.** Any two reasonable answers, for example: the
committee learns the real-world effects from an expert witness; it
learns what questions and concerns members raise; it tests the
witness's credibility under questioning; it hears what supporters
and opponents emphasize.

**Independent 6.** Error: the Speaker does not decide what becomes
law. Correction: the Speaker controls the House's schedule and
referrals, but a bill must still pass the House floor, then pass the
Senate (possibly in a different version, reconciled in conference),
and then be signed by the president (or passed over a veto by
two-thirds of each chamber).

**Application 7.** Accept a correct three-step trace: a member
introduces the bill; it is referred to the House Committee on
Transportation and Infrastructure (highways) or the Senate Committee
on Environment and Public Works; the committee holds a hearing,
marks up the text, and votes to report it before the floor can vote.
The committee has jurisdiction because highways fall under
transportation/infrastructure (House) or environment and public
works (Senate).

**Exit 8.** House 435 seats, two-year terms; Senate 100 seats,
six-year terms.

**Exit 9.** A markup is the committee's line-by-line amendment of a
bill's text; it is run by the committee chair.

## Lesson 02 — How a bill becomes law

**Guided 1.** Neither chamber can override. House: two-thirds of
435 is 290; 250 is 40 short. Senate: two-thirds of 100 is 66.67,
so 67 votes are needed; 55 is 12 short.

**Guided 2.** The filibuster continues: cloture needs 60 votes, and
59 supporters are 1 vote short. No vote on the bill can occur until
cloture is invoked or the filibuster ends.

**Guided 3.** Conference committee: members of both chambers
negotiate a single identical text that both chambers must then
approve without further amendment.

**Independent 4.** Accept: reconciliation bills cannot be
filibustered in the Senate and pass with a simple majority, so a
bill that cannot reach 60 votes for cloture might still pass — but
only if its provisions fit the strict budget rules (spending,
revenue, or debt-limit provisions under the Byrd rule).

**Independent 5.** Pocket veto. Because Congress adjourned within
the ten days, the president's failure to sign kills the bill, and
Congress cannot override it because it is not in session to vote.
A regular veto requires returning the bill with objections while
Congress is in session.

**Independent 6.** Two errors: (a) the filibuster is not in the
Constitution — it is a Senate rule and custom (Rule XXII governs
cloture); (b) 60 votes is the threshold to end debate (cloture),
not to pass the bill — passage needs a simple majority (51 in the
Senate, 218 in the House).

**Application 7.** Accept: the bill stalls — with 57 votes for
cloture it cannot proceed to a Senate vote. Supporters can try to
win 3 more votes, attach the provisions to another bill (including
a reconciliation bill if the provisions fit), or reintroduce it in
a new Congress. The House-passed version has no legal effect on its
own: a bill must pass both chambers in identical form and be signed
(or passed over a veto) to become law.

**Exit 8.** Introduction, committee, floor, conference,
presidential action.

**Exit 9.** 435 × 2/3 = 290.

## Lesson 03 — Presidency

**Guided 1.** Lowest ebb — Zone 3. The president acts against the
express will of Congress, so presidential power is at its weakest.

**Guided 2.** The senator is wrong that a treaty was required. An
executive agreement with a foreign government does not need the
Senate's two-thirds consent; a treaty does. The two instruments are
different by design.

**Guided 3.** The three sources: the Constitution, the statutes of
the United States, and the president's authority as Commander in
Chief. The commander-in-chief power made the order enforceable
inside the military chain of command.

**Independent 4.** Accept: an order is faster because the president
acts inside the executive branch without waiting for Congress; it
is weaker because it binds only the executive branch, can be
revoked by the next president, can be struck down by a court, and
cannot appropriate money or contradict a statute.

**Independent 5.** The veto is formal power (Article I, §7): it
blocks the bill unless Congress overrides it. The televised address
is informal power (the bully pulpit): it can persuade the public and
pressure Congress, but it has no legal force.

**Independent 6.** Error: the order did not end segregation on July
26, 1948. It declared the policy and created a seven-member advisory
committee to recommend how to carry it out; full integration took
years, driven by field commanders during the Korean War and by later
orders and statutes.

**Application 7.** Accept any well-structured evaluation with: (a)
the authority the order cites; (b) whether Congress had legislated
on the subject; (c) one thing it could do faster than Congress; (d)
one limit (revocability, judicial review, no appropriation, or
conflict with statute).

**Exit 8.** Formal (any two): veto, commander in chief,
appointments, treaties, pardons, receiving ambassadors. Informal
(any two): bully pulpit/persuasion, executive orders, executive
agreements, signing statements.

**Exit 9.** "There shall be equality of treatment and opportunity
for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color,
religion or national origin." Issued July 26, 1948.

## Lesson 04 — Bureaucracy and rulemaking

**Guided 1.** The agency skipped notice-and-comment rulemaking
(APA §553): no public comment period, and no response to comments.
A challenger would allege (a) failure to publish a proposed rule
and take comments and (b) arbitrary-and-capricious action for
issuing a final rule without a reasoned explanation.

**Guided 2.** Under *Chevron* (1984), the court deferred to the
agency's reasonable interpretation when the statute was ambiguous —
the agency decided. Under *Loper Bright* (2024), the court decides
the statute's meaning with its own independent judgment; the
agency's view is persuasive only to the extent its reasoning is.

**Guided 3.** The major questions doctrine (*West Virginia v. EPA*,
2022): on a question of vast economic and political significance,
the challenger argues Congress must have spoken clearly rather than
leaving the matter to an agency's reading of a vague phrase.

**Independent 4.** Accept: Congress writes broad statutes because it
lacks the technical expertise and time to specify every detail, and
because flexibility lets rules adapt to changing circumstances. The
risk: agencies gain discretion that may exceed what Congress
intended, with less direct democratic accountability.

**Independent 5.** (1) Find the proposed rule in the Federal
Register; (2) read it and its stated legal basis; (3) submit a
public comment during the comment period (usually 30–60 days);
(4) the agency considers comments and publishes a final rule — also
in the Federal Register.

**Independent 6.** Error: agencies still interpret statutes every
day when they write rules. *Loper Bright* removed only the
*binding* force of their interpretations: courts now decide the
statute's meaning independently, but still weigh agency expertise
(*Skidmore* respect).

**Application 7.** Accept any brief with: the statute behind the
rule, the rule's stated purpose, one expected supportive comment
theme, one expected opposing theme, and the court that would review
a challenge (the relevant court of appeals, then potentially the
Supreme Court).

**Exit 8.** Executive departments (e.g., State, Treasury);
independent agencies (e.g., EPA, NASA); regulatory commissions
(e.g., SEC, FCC); government corporations (e.g., U.S. Postal
Service, Amtrak).

**Exit 9.** Notice-and-comment rulemaking: the agency publishes a
proposed rule, takes public comments, considers them, and publishes
a final rule. Both notices appear in the Federal Register.

## Lesson 05 — Courts and judicial philosophies

**Guided 1.** A circuit split makes certiorari more likely: the
Supreme Court exists in part to make federal law uniform, so
conflicting answers from the courts of appeals present exactly the
question it is designed to resolve.

**Guided 2.** Accept: the originalist asks what "freedom of speech"
meant to those who ratified the First Amendment; the living
constitutionalist asks how the broad principle of free expression
applies to a technology the framers never saw.

**Guided 3.** Zone 2 — the twilight zone. Congress had neither
granted nor denied authority over military segregation, so the
president acted where Congress was silent, on commander-in-chief
power.

**Independent 4.** Accept: the Court grants few petitions because it
selects only cases presenting an important federal question or a
circuit split — roughly 60–80 grants from thousands of petitions.
"Declined to hear" leaves the lower court's decision standing but
is not a ruling on the merits.

**Independent 5.** Originalism. Supporters' argument (any one):
it constrains judges by fixing the Constitution's meaning, so judges
apply the law rather than imposing their own preferences.

**Independent 6.** Error: the Court does not hear every appeal. It
receives thousands of petitions and grants roughly 60–80; it takes
four votes — the Rule of Four — to grant certiorari.

**Application 7.** Accept any brief with: the zone argued, two
reasons from the *Youngstown* facts (e.g., no statute authorized
seizure; Congress had considered and declined seizure powers; the
emergency and the need to supply the armed forces), the strongest
argument for the other side, and a reply to it.

**Exit 8.** District courts (94), courts of appeals (13), Supreme
Court (nine justices).

**Exit 9.** Zone 1: the president acts with Congress's authorization
— power at its maximum. Zone 2: Congress has said nothing — the
twilight zone, power uncertain. Zone 3: the president acts against
Congress's will — power at its lowest ebb.

## Lesson 06 — Budget and who makes policy

**Guided 1.** The agency can spend $3 billion. Authorization is
permission; appropriation is payment — only the appropriated amount
is actually provided.

**Guided 2.** 4.919 ÷ 6.752 = 0.7286, about 73%. Meaning: receipts
covered about 73% of outlays; the remaining ~27% was borrowed.

**Guided 3.** Congress's budget becomes law. The president's budget
is a proposal (drafted by OMB, scored by CBO); only Congress can
appropriate money, so only its appropriations bills have legal
effect.

**Independent 4.** Mandatory spending is set by permanent law and
continues unless Congress changes the statute (Social Security,
Medicare, interest on the debt). Discretionary spending is decided
each year in the appropriations bills (defense, education,
transportation).

**Independent 5.** Correction: the deficit is one year's gap between
spending and receipts — FY2024: $1.833 trillion. The national debt
is the accumulation of all past deficits minus surpluses — above $35
trillion at the end of FY2024.

**Independent 6.** Error: Congress still matters decisively.
Evidence (any two): Congress creates every agency by statute and can
abolish it; Congress funds agencies through appropriations; agency
rules must trace to a statute and are reviewed by courts (*Whitman*,
*Loper Bright*).

**Application 7.** Accept any verdict essay with one paragraph of
best evidence per institution, a ranked verdict, a genuine stated
limitation, and at least two dated facts from the unit.

**Exit 8.** Receipts $4.9 trillion, outlays $6.8 trillion
($6.752T), deficit $1.8 trillion ($1.833T). One-decimal
approximations accepted.

**Exit 9.** Authorization (permission to run a program) and
appropriation (providing the money). If the second vote never
happens, the program receives nothing — the government runs on a
continuing resolution or the unfunded parts shut down.

## Practice Set A

**A1.** None — 300 already exceeds the 290 needed to override in
the House, by 10 votes.

**A2.** Cloture fails: 59 is 1 vote short of 60. Debate continues
and no vote on the bill can occur.

**A3.** Introduction → committee → floor → conference →
presidential action. Frictions: stage 2 — committee inaction (most
bills never leave committee); stage 3 — filibuster, needing 60
votes for cloture; stage 5 — veto (needs a two-thirds override) or
pocket veto.

**A4.** The bill has died in committee by inaction. No vote defeated
it; it was never scheduled for a hearing or markup, and it expires
when the Congress ends.

**A5.** Accept: all 435 House seats are contested every two years,
so one election can flip the House majority; only about one-third
of Senate seats are contested in any election, with six-year terms,
so one election can change at most ~33–34 seats and rarely flips
the majority.

**A6.** Error: House passage is not enactment. Next: the Senate must
pass it (committee, floor — possibly a different version, then
conference to reconcile), and the president must sign it (or
Congress must override a veto by two-thirds of each chamber).

**A7.** Nothing happens to its senators: two per state regardless
of population. It keeps 2 senators.

**A8.** The bill dies. A conference text must be approved by both
chambers; the Senate's rejection ends it.

## Practice Set B

**B1.** The Defense procurement order is within the president's
authority (commander in chief; directing the executive branch). The
order to a private company exceeds it: the president cannot order
private citizens, and no statute authorizes it.

**B2.** The Constitution, the statutes of the United States, and the
president's authority as Commander in Chief. The order created the
seven-member President's Committee on Equality of Treatment and
Opportunity in the Armed Services.

**B3.** (1) No notice-and-comment period (APA §553) — no proposed
rule published for public comment; (2) no consideration of, or
response to, comments — arbitrary and capricious.

**B4.** In 2023 the court deferred to the agency's reasonable reading
under *Chevron*. In 2025, after *Loper Bright*, the court must
decide the statute's meaning with its own independent judgment; the
agency's view is persuasive only to the extent its reasoning is.

**B5.** A treaty needs the Senate's two-thirds consent; an executive
agreement with a foreign government does not.

**B6.** Error: Congress did not give up its power. The EPA acts
under the Clean Air Act, which Congress wrote; the EPA's rule went
through notice and comment; the courts reviewed it (*Whitman*,
2001); Congress can amend the statute, change the funding, or
abolish the agency.

**B7.** (1) Find the proposed rule in the Federal Register; (2) read
it; (3) submit a public comment during the comment period; (4) the
agency considers comments and publishes a final rule in the Federal
Register.

**B8.** (a) Zone 1 — maximum authority; (b) Zone 3 — lowest ebb;
(c) Zone 2 — the twilight zone.

## Practice Set C

**C1.** No — the case is not heard. The Rule of Four requires four
of the nine justices to vote to grant certiorari; four votes to
grant and five to deny means the petition is denied.

**C2.** Accept: the originalist starts from the Fourth Amendment's
original public meaning; the living constitutionalist starts from
the broad principle (security against unreasonable searches) applied
to present circumstances.

**C3.** (a) 1.833 ÷ 4.919 = 0.3727, about 37%. (b) The deficit
equaled about 37% of what the government collected — for every $100
of receipts, the government spent about $137.

**C4.** Neither program can spend money. A program needs both votes:
authorization without appropriation provides no money, and
appropriation without authorization is not permitted (no
permanent-law exception applies here).

**C5.** Error: declining to hear a case is not a ruling. The lower
court's decision stands, but the Supreme Court expressed no view on
the merits.

**C6.** Mandatory spending is set by permanent law (Social Security,
Medicare, interest on the debt). Discretionary spending is decided
in the annual appropriations bills (defense, education,
transportation).

**C7.** The agency receives $420 billion. Congress's appropriation
controls; the president's proposal is a request, not the law.

**C8.** Accept a strong counter-argument with evidence, for example:
courts cannot initiate policy — they only decide cases brought to
them; Congress can amend the statute to override an interpretation
and controls the money; the president commands the bureaucracy that
implements the law; *Youngstown* shows the Court checking power,
not making policy; and after *Loper Bright* the Court interprets the
statute Congress wrote.
