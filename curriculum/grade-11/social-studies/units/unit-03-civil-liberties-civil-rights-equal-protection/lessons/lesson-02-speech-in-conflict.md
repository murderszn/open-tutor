# Lesson 02 — Speech in Conflict

**Goal:** Apply the *Brandenburg* imminent-lawless-action test and the
*Tinker* substantial-disruption test to new speech scenarios, and name
the categories of speech the Court has left unprotected. **Time:** ~55
minutes. **Materials:** First Amendment text; scenario cards (see
Guided Practice).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** The First Amendment says "Congress
> shall make no law … abridging the freedom of speech." After Lesson
> 01, who else is bound by that sentence, and through what clause?
> (Answer: state and local governments, through the Fourteenth
> Amendment's Due Process Clause — selective incorporation, *Gitlow*
> 1925.)

## Teaching

"Congress shall make no law" sounds absolute. No court has ever read
it that way — and the reason is the collision at the heart of this
lesson: one person's speech is another person's safety, reputation, or
orderly school day. The Court's job has been to draw lines that protect
as much speech as possible while naming the narrow places where
government may act. Two tests do most of the work in this lesson.

### From "clear and present danger" to "imminent lawless action"

In *Schenck v. United States* (1919), Justice Holmes upheld a
conviction for distributing anti-draft leaflets during wartime, writing
that speech creating a "clear and present danger" of evils Congress may
prevent is unprotected — famously adding that the First Amendment
would not protect falsely shouting fire in a crowded theater. The test
was loose: danger could be distant, and judges weighed it case by
case. Over fifty years the Court tightened it, and in *Brandenburg v.
Ohio* (1969) replaced it. Brandenburg, a Ku Klux Klan leader, had been
convicted under Ohio's criminal-syndicalism law for speeches praising
revenge against Black and Jewish people. The Court reversed unanimously
and announced the test still used today: speech advocating lawless
action is protected **unless** it is (1) **directed to inciting or
producing imminent lawless action** and (2) **likely to incite or
produce such action**. Both parts must hold. Abstract praise of
lawbreaking, even hateful praise, fails part one; a fiery speech to a
calm crowd that goes home fails part two. Imminence plus likelihood —
that is the whole test, and its narrowness is the point: the government
may punish the spark next to the powder keg, not the unpopular opinion.

### Speech at school: Tinker

Schools are the one place most learners meet speech law directly, and
*Tinker v. Des Moines* (1969) is the governing case. Students protesting
the Vietnam War wore black armbands to school; administrators banned
armbands in advance and suspended the students. The Court held 7–2
that students do not "shed their constitutional rights to freedom of
speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." A school may restrict
student speech only when it **materially and substantially disrupts**
school work or discipline, or invades the rights of others — the
**substantial-disruption test**. Fear of disruption is not enough; the
school needs facts showing actual or reasonably forecast disruption.
Two later limits matter: in *Bethel v. Fraser* (1986) the Court allowed
punishment of lewd, vulgar speech at a school assembly, and in
*Hazelwood v. East High* (1988) it allowed schools to control
school-sponsored expression (a school newspaper) for legitimate
pedagogical reasons. *Tinker* protects the student's own speech; it
does not give students editorial control over the school's own
publications.

### The unprotected categories

Outside the *Brandenburg* and *Tinker* tests, the Court has named a
short list of speech categories that receive no First Amendment
protection at all. Keep the list short and exact — learners who invent
extra categories ("offensive speech," "misinformation") are the most
common error:

- **Incitement** meeting the *Brandenburg* test (imminent + likely).
- **True threats** — serious expressions of intent to commit unlawful
  violence against a person or group.
- **Obscenity** — under *Miller v. California* (1973), material that
  (1) appeals to the prurient interest by community standards, (2) is
  patently offensive, and (3) lacks serious literary, artistic,
  political, or scientific value. Mere offensiveness or nudity is not
  obscenity.
- **Defamation** — false statements of fact that harm reputation.
  Public officials and public figures must additionally prove
  **actual malice**: knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for
  truth (*New York Times v. Sullivan*, 1964).
- **Fighting words** — face-to-face insults likely to provoke an
  immediate violent reaction (*Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 1942).
  The Court has narrowed this category sharply since; it almost never
  decides a modern case on fighting words alone.
- **Speech integral to criminal conduct** — fraud, conspiracy, child
  exploitation material, and similar crimes committed through words.

Notice what is *not* on the list: hateful speech, offensive speech,
false statements in general, and unpopular political opinions. Those
are protected — which is precisely why *Brandenburg* protected a Klan
leader's speech. A free-speech rule that only protected agreeable
speech would protect nothing the majority had not already approved.

One more distinction that organizes the whole lesson: **content-based**
vs. **content-neutral** restrictions. A law that bans speech because
of its message (no anti-war armbands, but pro-war buttons allowed) is
content-based and faces the strictest review — the government must
show a compelling interest pursued by the narrowest means. A law that
regulates speech without regard to message (no amplified sound after
10 p.m. in a park, whoever you are) is content-neutral and faces a
lighter test. Lesson 03 applies the same distinction to assembly.

### Worked example 1 — Applying Brandenburg

*Task:* At a rally, a speaker tells an angry crowd outside a factory,
"Burn it down tonight — meet at the gate at midnight with torches!"
Half the crowd cheers and moves toward the gate. Protected?

*Reasoning:* Unprotected under *Brandenburg*. Part one: the speech is
directed to producing imminent lawless action — "tonight," "midnight,"
a specific target, a specific act. Part two: it is likely to produce
that action — the crowd is angry, nearby, and already moving. Contrast
with a pamphlet arguing "factories should be burned someday": no
imminence, no likelihood, protected. The difference is not the opinion
(both endorse arson); it is imminence plus likelihood.

### Worked example 2 — Applying Tinker

*Task:* A student wears a T-shirt criticizing the school's dress code.
The principal suspends her, saying the shirt "might upset other
students." Another student shouts the same criticism through a
bullhorn during class, stopping instruction for ten minutes. Decide
both.

*Reasoning:* The T-shirt is protected under *Tinker*: no facts show
material disruption — "might upset" is the forecast of discomfort the
Court rejected. The bullhorn is unprotected: it actually disrupted
class, satisfying the substantial-disruption test. Same message,
opposite results — because *Tinker* turns on disruption, not
agreement with the message.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Apply *Brandenburg* to each: (a) an online post praising a
   historical riot; (b) a speaker urging a crowd blocking a hospital
   entrance to "push through the doors now"; (c) a song lyric about
   revolution. State which prong decides each case.
2. **G2.** A school bans all political T-shirts after one student
   wears an anti-war shirt and classmates argue loudly at lunch for
   five minutes. Apply *Tinker*: is the ban justified? What facts
   would change your answer?
3. **G3.** Sort into protected or unprotected, citing the category or
   test: (a) a false newspaper claim that a mayor took a bribe, printed
   after the reporter learned it was false; (b) a protester shouting a
   racial slur inches from a stranger's face, fists raised; (c) a
   comedian's offensive jokes about a religion.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** A student newspaper editorial criticizes the principal's
   budget decisions. The principal pulls the issue, citing
   *Hazelwood*. Is the citation correct? Distinguish student speech
   (*Tinker*) from school-sponsored speech (*Hazelwood*) in your
   answer.
2. **I2.** A city bans all protests in its downtown park after 8 p.m.,
   applying the ban to every group equally. Content-based or
   content-neutral? Which test applies, and what would the city need to
   show?
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "Because *Brandenburg* protects hateful
   speech, the First Amendment protects all speech." Identify the
   error and correct it with two counterexamples from the unprotected
   categories.
4. **I4.** A governor orders a social-media platform to remove posts
   criticizing the governor. Apply Lesson 01: whose liberty is at
   issue, and does the First Amendment bind the governor's order?
   (Hint: state action matters — the platform is private, the order is
   not.)

## Applied task

**A.** Write a two-paragraph "ruling" as a judge in a new case: a
student is suspended for a silent protest (a black armband, as in
*Tinker*) that prompts a heated but nonviolent classroom debate.
Apply *Tinker* explicitly, address the school's best counterargument
(disruption), and state the remedy (suspension stands or is lifted).

## Exit check

**E.** State the *Brandenburg* test in one sentence (both prongs) and
the *Tinker* test in one sentence. Then name two unprotected speech
categories and one category people often wrongly think is
unprotected.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Gloss *syndicalism* (advocacy of violent
  overthrow as a doctrine), *prurient* (appealing to unhealthy sexual
  interest), and *malice* (in defamation law, knowledge of falsity or
  reckless disregard — not ordinary spite) before assigning case
  summaries.
- **Alternate response mode:** Scenario rulings in G1 may be delivered
  orally as a "bench ruling" instead of in writing.
- **Extension:** Read the *Brandenburg* per curiam opinion's key
  paragraph and the *Schenck* "fire in a theater" passage side by side.
  Why did the Court move from Holmes's test to *Brandenburg*'s? Strong
  answers name the shift from punishing dangerous *tendencies* to
  requiring imminence and likelihood.

> **Teacher note:** The persistent misconception is that "free speech"
> means "no consequences for any words anywhere." Correct it early:
> the First Amendment restrains *government*, not private platforms,
> employers, or classmates — and even against government, the
> unprotected categories and the *Tinker* limits apply. Full notes and
> all answers are in [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
> [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).
