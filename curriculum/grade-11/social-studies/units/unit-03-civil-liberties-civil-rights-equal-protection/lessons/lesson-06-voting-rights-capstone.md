# Lesson 06 — Voting Rights as the Capstone Right

**Goal:** Trace voting rights from the Fifteenth Amendment through the
Voting Rights Act to *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), present the
majority and dissent positions fairly, and defend an evidence-based
answer to "what makes an election fair?" **Time:** ~55 minutes.
**Materials:** Fifteenth Amendment and VRA Section 4(b)/5 summaries;
turnout-data task (see Investigation below).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Lesson 05 ended with preclearance: the
> federal government approving voting changes *before* they took
> effect. Why would Congress design a remedy that acts before harm
> rather than after? (Answer: because after-the-fact lawsuits let
> elections happen under discriminatory rules first — and the
> challenged rule could be replaced by a new device before the next
> case finished.)

**Sources:** National Archives Constitution transcription (Fifteenth,
Nineteenth, Twenty-Fourth Amendments; checked 2026-10-28); U.S.
Department of Justice, Civil Rights Division, "The Shelby County
Decision" (holding summarized; checked 2026-10-28); Congress.gov,
Constitution Annotated, Fifteenth Amendment essays (checked
2026-10-28); U.S. Census Bureau, Current Population Survey voting
supplement (data-task source; adult verifies the current table before
assigning).

## Teaching

Voting is called the capstone right for a reason: it is the right by
which citizens protect every other right. Lose the vote, and the
ability to change the laws that protect speech, equality, and liberty
goes with it. That is why the voting-rights story is also the story of
every device invented to take the vote away without ever saying so —
and of the federal responses that, twice, had to be invented to meet
those devices.

### The amendment and the evasion

The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) provides that the right to vote "shall
not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on
account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude," and gives
Congress power to enforce it. The amendment banned one reason for
denial — race — but left the mechanics of voting to the states. States
that wished to evade it therefore did not mention race. They adopted
devices that were race-neutral on their face and discriminatory in
their operation:

- **Poll taxes** — a fee to vote, excluding poor citizens (and, in
  practice, disproportionately Black citizens). The Twenty-Fourth
  Amendment (1964) banned poll taxes in federal elections; *Harper v.
  Virginia State Board of Elections* (1966) struck them down in state
  elections under the Equal Protection Clause.
- **Literacy tests** — tests administered with wide discretion,
  waived for white applicants and applied strictly to Black ones.
- **White primaries** — party rules excluding Black voters from
  primary elections, where most Southern contests were effectively
  decided. The Court struck the white primary down in *Smith v.
  Allwright* (1944).
- **Grandfather clauses** — exempting from literacy tests anyone whose
  ancestors had voted before the Civil War, a category that by
  definition excluded most Black citizens. Struck down in *Guinn v.
  United States* (1915).

The pattern is the lesson's first thesis: a right defined only by its
forbidden reason invites evasion by other means. Each device, struck
down, was replaced by another — the "whack-a-mole" problem Lesson 05
named. The Voting Rights Act was Congress's answer to that pattern:
stop litigating devices one at a time, and instead require covered
jurisdictions to prove *in advance* that a change was not
discriminatory.

### The Voting Rights Act and Shelby County

The VRA (1965) banned literacy tests nationwide (extended and made
permanent in later reauthorizations) and created preclearance: Section
5 required covered jurisdictions to obtain approval from the Justice
Department or a federal court before changing any voting rule;
Section 4(b) supplied the coverage formula — essentially, jurisdictions
that had used a test or device and had low voter registration or
turnout in the 1964, 1968, or 1972 presidential elections. In *South
Carolina v. Katzenbach* (1966), the Court upheld the scheme as
"an uncommon exercise of congressional power" justified by an
"insidious and pervasive evil."

On June 25, 2013, in *Shelby County v. Holder*, the Court held 5–4
that the Section 4(b) coverage formula was unconstitutional. Present
both sides as the Court itself presented them — this is the unit's
central nonpartisanship exercise:

- **Majority (Chief Justice Roberts):** The formula rested on
  decades-old data — registration and turnout figures from the 1960s
  and 1970s — and on practices (literacy tests) long since outlawed.
  The conditions that justified extraordinary federal intrusion in
  1965 no longer characterized the covered jurisdictions; treating
  states differently on the basis of 40-year-old facts violated the
  principle of equal state sovereignty. The opinion stressed what it
  did *not* decide: it did not strike down Section 5 itself, and
  Congress remained free to enact a new, current formula.
- **Dissent (Justice Ginsburg, joined by three justices):** The
  majority ignored the record Congress compiled when it reauthorized
  the Act in 2006 — thousands of pages documenting continuing
  discrimination in covered jurisdictions — and the logic of
  preclearance itself. Her famous image: "Throwing out preclearance
  when it has worked and is continuing to work to stop discriminatory
  changes is like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because
  you are not getting wet." The dissent argued that the Act's success
  was evidence it was still needed, not evidence it could be retired.

The practical effect, stated neutrally and confirmed by the Justice
Department's own summary: because Section 5 depends on the Section
4(b) formula to identify covered jurisdictions, preclearance no longer
operates unless a jurisdiction is covered by a separate court order
under Section 3(c). Voting changes in formerly covered jurisdictions
are now challenged after the fact, through lawsuits under Section 2
of the VRA (which bans discriminatory voting practices nationwide)
and the Constitution — the slower, device-by-device path the 1965
Congress had tried to escape. Whether that is a proper restoration of
federalism or a dangerous reopening depends on the evidence each side
cites; the learner's job is to weigh it, not to inherit a verdict.

### Investigation: reading turnout data honestly

Turnout figures are the most misused numbers in civic argument: they
are quoted without saying *who* is counted (all residents? citizens?
registered voters? eligible voters?), *which* election (presidential
years draw far more voters than midterms), and *when* the data was
collected. For this investigation the adult obtains the current
U.S. Census Bureau Current Population Survey (CPS) voting supplement
table — the official source for national turnout by demographic group
— and records the table title, election year, and access date in the
evidence log. The learner then answers:

1. What is the denominator? (CPS turnout is usually reported as a
   share of the *citizen voting-age population* — confirm this on the
   table before computing anything.)
2. Compute the percentage-point gap between the highest- and
   lowest-turnout groups shown, for one presidential election year.
   Show the subtraction.
3. Name two things the table cannot show: why nonvoters stayed home,
   and whether any particular voting rule caused the gap.
4. Compare the same groups in a midterm year from the same source.
   Does the gap widen or narrow? What does that suggest about
   presidential vs. midterm participation — and what does it *not*
   prove about any single law?

No turnout figure is printed in this lesson, deliberately: turnout
statistics are time-sensitive, and a baked-in number goes stale. The
adult dates every figure the learner cites, and any undated turnout
claim in project work is returned for sourcing. (A clearly labeled
**fictional practice dataset** for arithmetic appears in
[practice.md](../practice.md), Task 6 — it is for computing
percentage-point change only, never cited as real turnout.)

### Worked example 1 — Device or discrimination?

*Task:* A state adopts a new rule: voters must register 30 days before
an election, in person, at a single county office open weekdays 9–5.
The state says the rule prevents fraud. Critics say it burdens hourly
workers. Analyze under Section 2's framework (does the rule deny or
abridge voting on account of race, considering the totality of
circumstances?) without assuming either side's conclusion.

*Reasoning:* The analysis does not start from the rule's stated
purpose but from its interaction with social and historical
conditions: who can reach the office during those hours, what
transportation exists, whether past discrimination affects the group's
access. A rule that is neutral on its face can still violate Section
2 if, in the totality of circumstances, members of a protected group
have less opportunity to participate. Equally, burden alone is not
enough — the challenger must connect the burden to race, not merely to
inconvenience. The example's point: voting-rights law asks about
*opportunity in context*, not about motives alone or effects alone.

### Worked example 2 — Two readings of Shelby County

*Task:* State the strongest version of each side's argument in two
sentences each, without caricature.

*Reasoning:* Majority's strongest version: a federal regime that
singles out some states for prior restraint on their election laws
needs a current justification; a formula frozen in 1960s data cannot
supply one, and Congress — which reauthorized the old formula without
updating it — can write a new one grounded in present conditions.
Dissent's strongest version: Congress *did* compile a current record
in 2006, and preclearance worked precisely by deterring discriminatory
changes before they took effect; removing the deterrent because
discrimination became less visible confuses the treatment's success
with the disease's cure. A learner who can state both versions fairly
has met this lesson's objective, whatever verdict they then defend.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Match each device to its end: poll tax → (Twenty-Fourth
   Amendment / *Harper*); literacy test → (VRA ban); white primary →
   (*Smith v. Allwright*); grandfather clause → (*Guinn*). Then state
   the evasion pattern in one sentence.
2. **G2.** Explain preclearance to a new learner in three sentences:
   what it required, who it covered, and what *Shelby County* did and
   did not decide. (Check against the lesson: Section 5 was *not*
   struck down.)
3. **G3.** Complete the turnout investigation (above) with the
   adult-obtained CPS table. Record the table title, year, and access
   date; show the percentage-point computation; state two limits of
   the data.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** A state moves its polling places from schools to a single
   location 20 miles from the county's largest town. Apply the Section
   2 totality-of-circumstances framing from Worked example 1: what
   evidence would each side need, and what would neither side's
   evidence alone prove?
2. **I2.** Congress proposes a new coverage formula based on the last
   three election cycles' voting-rights violations. Argue for it from
   the majority's reasoning in *Shelby County* (current data) and
   against it from the dissent's reasoning (deterrence and the record
   problem). Which argument is stronger on the *evidence* — and what
   evidence would settle it?
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "*Shelby County* repealed the Voting Rights
   Act." Identify the error precisely: which section was struck down,
   which section survives, and what enforcement path remains.
4. **I4.** The Nineteenth Amendment (1920) banned denial of the vote
   on account of sex. Explain why its ratification did not, by itself,
   secure voting access for Black women in the South — using the
   evasion pattern from this lesson.

## Applied task

**A.** Deliberation brief (used in W14S4): "What makes an election
fair?" Write a one-page brief that (1) proposes three criteria for a
fair election (for example: equal access, accurate counting, public
confidence), (2) applies each criterion to one real voting rule the
adult approves (a current rule, dated, with a cited source), and
(3) states the strongest objection to your own position and your reply.
Bring the brief to the W14S4 deliberation and project defense.

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) state what the Fifteenth Amendment
banned and one device used to evade it; (2) state what *Shelby County*
decided and what it left intact; (3) give your one-sentence answer to
"what makes an election fair?" with one reason.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Gloss *preclearance*, *coverage formula*,
  *totality of circumstances* (judging a practice by all relevant
  conditions together, not by one factor), and *denominator* (the
  population a percentage is computed from) before assigning the data
  task.
- **Alternate response mode:** The deliberation brief may be delivered
  as a recorded or spoken argument instead of in writing.
- **Extension:** Read Ginsburg's umbrella passage and Roberts's
  equal-sovereignty passage side by side. Which argument is about
  *evidence* (what the record shows) and which is about *structure*
  (how federalism should work regardless of the record)? Strong answers
  separate the two kinds of argument before choosing between them.
