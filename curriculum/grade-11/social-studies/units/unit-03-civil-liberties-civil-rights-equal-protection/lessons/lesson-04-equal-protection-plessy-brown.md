# Lesson 04 — Equal Protection: Plessy to Brown

**Goal:** Trace the equal-protection struggle from *Plessy v.
Ferguson* (1896) to *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) and explain
the three tiers of judicial scrutiny with a correct example of each.
**Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** Fourteenth Amendment text;
*Plessy* and *Brown* excerpts (see Sources).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Lesson 01 distinguished liberties
> (government must not) from rights (government must treat equally).
> Which side of that line does a segregated school fall on, and which
> clause governs it? (Answer: rights — the Fourteenth Amendment's
> Equal Protection Clause.)

## Teaching

The Fourteenth Amendment promised equal protection in 1868. For nearly
a century, the promise and the practice ran in opposite directions —
and the story of how the gap closed is the story of strategy as much
as of law. The same clause, the same words, produced opposite results
in 1896 and 1954. The difference was not the text. It was the cases
brought, the evidence assembled, and the Court's willingness to look
past "equal" facilities to what separation actually did.

### Plessy: "separate but equal"

Louisiana's 1890 Separate Car Act required railway companies to
provide "equal but separate accommodations for the white and colored
races." Homer Plessy, a man of mixed ancestry classified as Black
under Louisiana law, sat in a whites-only car and was arrested. In
*Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896), the Court upheld the law 7–1: separation
did not stamp Black passengers with a "badge of inferiority," the
majority said — if it did, that was only because Black people chose to
put that construction on it. Laws requiring separation, on this
reasoning, were a reasonable exercise of the state's police power, so
long as the separate facilities were equal. In practice they rarely
were, but *Plessy* made equality of facilities the only question a
court would ask, and the question was almost never pressed.

Justice John Marshall Harlan dissented alone. His dissent contains the
sentence that would outlive the decision: "Our Constitution is
color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens."
Harlan also warned, with precision the majority lacked, that the
decision would prove as pernicious as *Dred Scott v. Sandford* (1857).
For fifty-eight years, Harlan's dissent was the losing position. Keep
it in mind: dissents are sometimes the majority opinion of a later
generation, waiting for a case that lets the Court adopt them.

### Brown: separation is inherently unequal

The NAACP's Legal Defense Fund, led by Thurgood Marshall, spent the
1930s–1950s building a litigation ladder toward *Plessy*: first
graduate and professional schools, where "separate" facilities were
plainly unequal (*Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 1938; *Sweatt v.
Painter*, 1950; *McLaurin v. Oklahoma*, 1950); then, only when that
ladder reached the top, public elementary and secondary schools. The
strategy was deliberate — win the small, undeniable cases first, so
that by the time the big case arrived, the doctrine of "separate but
equal" had already been hollowed out.

In *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954), the Court consolidated five
cases from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and the
District of Columbia. Chief Justice Earl Warren delivered a unanimous
opinion on May 17, 1954: "We conclude that, in the field of public
education, the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place. Separate
educational facilities are inherently unequal." The reasoning turned
on what education *is*: "perhaps the most important function of state
and local governments," the foundation of good citizenship. Segregation
in that setting, even with equal buildings and books, deprived Black
children of equal educational opportunity because separation itself
generated a sense of inferiority affecting motivation and learning —
a finding the Court supported with social-science evidence, including
the Clark doll studies. *Brown* overruled *Plessy* in public
education; it did not, by its own terms, desegregate every public
facility at once.

Then came the second half of the story, which textbooks often skip.
In *Brown II* (1955), the Court ordered desegregation "with all
deliberate speed" — language that invited delay. Resistance followed:
Virginia's "Massive Resistance" laws, Arkansas's use of the National
Guard to block nine Black students at Little Rock Central High School
in 1957 (met by President Eisenhower's federalization of the Arkansas
National Guard), and a decade of token compliance. A court victory,
Lesson 05 argues, is a tool, not a finish line: *Brown* changed the
law in a day and the schools over a generation.

### The tiers of scrutiny

*Brown* also forces the question every equal-protection case asks:
when may government classify people at all? Governments classify
constantly — by age for driving, by income for taxes, by residency for
tuition. The Court sorts those classifications into three tiers, and
the tier usually decides the case:

- **Strict scrutiny** — for classifications based on race, national
  origin, and religion, and for burdens on fundamental rights (voting,
  interstate travel). The government must show a **compelling**
  interest pursued by **narrowly tailored** means. Almost always fatal
  — but not always: in *Korematsu v. United States* (1944) the Court
  claimed to apply strict scrutiny and still upheld Japanese internment
  during wartime, a decision later formally repudiated. Strict scrutiny
  is a test, not a guarantee.
- **Intermediate scrutiny** — for classifications based on sex/gender.
  The government must show an **important** interest **substantially
  related** to achieving it (*Craig v. Boren*, 1976, striking down
  different drinking ages for young men and women). The Court has also
  applied a form of heightened review to classifications based on
  nonmarital birth.
- **Rational-basis review** — for everything else (age, wealth,
  disability, most economic regulation). The government need only show
  a **legitimate** interest **rationally related** to the
  classification. The challenger usually loses — but not always: a law
  motivated by bare animus toward a group fails even this lenient test
  (*Romer v. Evans*, 1996).

Two cautions. First, the tiers describe *how hard the government must
work to justify a classification*, not whether the classification is
wise policy — a court can uphold a foolish law under rational-basis
review. Second, de jure segregation (segregation *by law*, as in
*Plessy* and *Brown*) is the unit's subject; de facto segregation
(separation produced by housing patterns, wealth, or private choices
without a law requiring it) raises harder questions the Court has
treated differently. Do not collapse the two: *Brown* struck down the
first, not the second.

### Worked example 1 — Reading Plessy against Brown

*Task:* Louisiana argued its railway cars were equal: same seats,
same fares, same schedules. Under *Plessy*'s rule, why did that
argument win? Under *Brown*'s reasoning, why would it fail?

*Reasoning:* Under *Plessy*, equality meant tangible equality —
facilities, fares, schedules. If those matched, the law stood, because
the Court refused to treat the *stigma* of separation as a legal harm.
Under *Brown*'s reasoning, the argument fails because the harm is not
in the seats: separation itself, imposed by law on the basis of race,
marks one group as inferior and denies equal educational (here,
travel) opportunity regardless of how equal the cars look. The two
cases disagree not about the facts of the facilities but about what
counts as inequality.

### Worked example 2 — Assigning the tier

*Task:* Assign each law to a tier and predict the likely outcome:
(a) a state charges out-of-state college students triple tuition;
(b) a city hires only men as firefighters, citing strength; (c) a
state requires voters to be U.S. citizens.

*Reasoning:* (a) **Rational basis** — residency classifications in
tuition have long been upheld; the state's interest in subsidizing its
own residents is legitimate and the tuition gap is rationally related
to it. (b) **Intermediate scrutiny** — a sex-based classification;
the city must show an important interest substantially related to the
rule, and a blanket sex ban (rather than a strength test applied to
everyone) is unlikely to survive, because it uses sex as a proxy for
strength. (c) **Strict scrutiny in form, upheld in practice** — voting
is a fundamental right, so the classification faces strict scrutiny;
but citizenship as a voting qualification has been treated as a
compelling, narrowly drawn condition of the political community itself.
This is the exception that tests whether the learner applies the tier
mechanically or with attention to precedent.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Summarize *Plessy*'s majority reasoning in two sentences
   and Harlan's dissent in one sentence. Then state, in one sentence,
   which side *Brown* adopted.
2. **G2.** Sort into strict, intermediate, or rational basis, with one
   sentence of justification each: (a) a law setting the driving age
   at 16; (b) a law giving hiring preference to veterans; (c) a law
   banning marriage between people of different races (the *Loving v.
   Virginia*, 1967, fact pattern); (d) a law requiring female, but not
   male, teachers to retire at 60.
3. **G3.** Why did the NAACP litigate graduate schools before
   elementary schools? Explain the ladder strategy in three sentences.

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** A school district assigns students to schools by
   neighborhood. Neighborhoods are racially homogeneous because of
   decades-old housing policy, but no current law mentions race. De
   jure or de facto segregation? What would a *Brown* claim need to
   show beyond the demographic fact?
2. **I2.** A state offers a tax credit to families who send children
   to private schools. A challenger claims an equal-protection
   violation because wealthier families benefit more. Which tier
   applies, and what is the likely outcome? (Hint: wealth alone has
   not triggered heightened scrutiny.)
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "*Brown* ended school segregation in
   America in 1954." Identify the error using *Brown II* and the
   Little Rock example, and rewrite the sentence accurately.
4. **I4.** *Korematsu* is often called "strict scrutiny that failed."
   Explain the paradox in two sentences: what does it show about the
   relationship between a legal test and a court's willingness to apply
   it?

## Applied task

**A.** Write a one-page "brief for the next case": you represent
students challenging a state law that funds two school systems —
separate schools for two racial groups — with provably identical
budgets, buildings, and teacher salaries. Argue from *Brown* (not from
general fairness): quote or paraphrase the "inherently unequal"
holding, explain why identical funding does not save the law, and
anticipate the state's best response ("*Plessy* permitted separate but
equal, and here everything is equal"). Conclude with the remedy you
seek.

## Exit check

**E.** In three sentences: (1) state *Plessy*'s rule and *Brown*'s
holding; (2) name the three tiers of scrutiny with one example
classification each; (3) explain "with all deliberate speed" and one
consequence of that phrase.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Gloss *police power* (the state's authority to
  regulate for health, safety, and welfare), *pernicious* (harmful,
  destructive), and *consolidated* (several cases decided together
  under one opinion) before assigning case summaries.
- **Alternate response mode:** The G2 sort may be done as a card
  activity or orally instead of in writing.
- **Extension:** Read Harlan's full *Plessy* dissent paragraph on
  color-blindness and the *Brown* opinion's education paragraph side
  by side. Which sentences in *Brown* echo Harlan? Strong answers
  trace the move from "the Constitution is color-blind" to "separate
  educational facilities are inherently unequal" — the dissent's
  principle becoming the majority's rule.
