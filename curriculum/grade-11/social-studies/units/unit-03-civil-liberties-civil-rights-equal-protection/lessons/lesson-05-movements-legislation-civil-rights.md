# Lesson 05 — Movements and Legislation: How Rights Expand

**Goal:** Evaluate how litigation, legislation, and movements expanded
civil rights — judging what each strategy changed and what it did not.
**Time:** ~55 minutes. **Materials:** Timeline of 1954–1965 (see
Teaching); campaign cards (see Guided Practice).

> **Readiness check (2 min):** Lesson 04 ended with *Brown* (1954)
> changing the rule but not the schools. Name one institution that had
> to act before the rule became reality. (Answers vary: Congress,
> the president, lower courts, state officials, or the movement itself.)

**Sources:** National Archives, Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting
Rights Act of 1965 records (checked 2026-10-28); Congress.gov,
Constitution Annotated, Fourteenth Amendment enforcement essays
(checked 2026-10-28); National Park Service, Selma to Montgomery march
history (checked 2026-10-28); Oyez case summaries for *Heart of Atlanta
Motel v. United States* (1964) and *South Carolina v. Katzenbach*
(1966) (search-result verified 2026-10-28).

## Teaching

*Brown* is often taught as the climax of the civil-rights story. It
was the middle. A court can declare a right; it cannot staff a
movement, pass a statute, register voters, or desegregate a lunch
counter by itself. Between 1954 and 1965, three strategies worked in
combination — litigation, direct action, and legislation — and each
one's limits explain why the others were necessary.

### Strategy 1: Litigation — the ladder and its limits

Lesson 04 described the NAACP's litigation ladder: graduate schools
first, then public schools. The ladder's logic was sound — build
precedent where the inequality was undeniable — but its limits were
real. Litigation is slow (a case takes years), expensive, and
dependent on courts willing to enforce their own rulings. After
*Brown*, Southern states answered with "Massive Resistance" and with
delay dressed as compliance: a district could claim it was
desegregating "with all deliberate speed" while admitting a handful of
Black students a year. Litigation won the principle; it could not, by
itself, produce the pace.

### Strategy 2: Direct action — making the issue unavoidable

The movement's second strategy was to create facts courts and
Congress could not ignore. The Montgomery bus boycott (1955–1956),
sparked by Rosa Parks's arrest and organized by the Montgomery
Improvement Association under Martin Luther King Jr., sustained a
year-long boycott that ended only when a federal court struck down bus
segregation (*Browder v. Gayle*, 1956). The sit-ins of 1960, begun by
four students in Greensboro, North Carolina, spread to dozens of
cities in weeks. The Freedom Rides of 1961 tested the Supreme Court's
ban on segregated interstate travel (*Boynton v. Virginia*, 1960) by
riding through the South and facing mob violence that forced federal
attention. The Birmingham campaign of 1963 — marches, boycotts, and
children's marches met with fire hoses and police dogs on television —
moved President John F. Kennedy to propose civil-rights legislation
in June 1963. Direct action's power was not that it changed law
directly; it changed what the public saw, and therefore what elected
officials felt they had to do. Its cost was borne by the
demonstrators: arrest, beating, and in some cases death. The adult
should name that cost plainly and never present nonviolent protest as
safe or easy.

### Strategy 3: Legislation — the 1964 and 1965 Acts

Congress acted with two landmark statutes, both grounded in
constitutional powers the Court then upheld:

- **Civil Rights Act of 1964.** Title II banned discrimination in
  public accommodations (hotels, restaurants, theaters) affecting
  interstate commerce; Title VII banned employment discrimination based
  on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin. In *Heart of
  Atlanta Motel v. United States* (1964), the Court upheld Title II
  under the Commerce Clause: a motel serving interstate travelers
  affects interstate commerce, and Congress could regulate the local
  discrimination that burdened it. The Act's significance is structural:
  it moved equal treatment from a constitutional principle enforced
  case by case into a federal statute enforced by agencies and private
  lawsuits.
- **Voting Rights Act of 1965 (VRA).** Passed after the Selma marches
  of March 1965 — including "Bloody Sunday" (March 7), when marchers
  crossing the Edmund Pettus Bridge were beaten by state troopers on
  television — the VRA banned literacy tests and other devices used to
  disenfranchise Black voters, and created **preclearance**: under
  Section 5, jurisdictions with a history of voting discrimination,
  identified by the Section 4(b) coverage formula, had to obtain
  federal approval before changing any voting rule. In *South
  Carolina v. Katzenbach* (1966), the Court upheld preclearance as a
  valid exercise of Congress's power to enforce the Fifteenth
  Amendment. Registration of Black voters in the covered South rose
  sharply in the years after passage — the clearest measurable success
  of any strategy in this lesson.

### What each strategy did not do

A fair evaluation names the limits, because the unit's capstone
argument depends on them:

- Litigation declared rights but depended on enforcement it could not
  supply.
- Direct action created pressure but could not itself write statutes
  or register voters at scale.
- Legislation reached further than either, but statutes can be
  amended, underenforced, or reinterpreted by later courts — Lesson 06
  shows exactly that happening to the VRA's preclearance regime.
- None of the three, alone or together, ended de facto separation in
  housing and schools, closed wealth gaps, or resolved the continuing
  argument — visible again in *Students for Fair Admissions v.
  Harvard* (2023), which held race-conscious college admissions
  programs unlawful under the Equal Protection Clause — over how
  equal protection applies when a policy classifies by race in order to
  remedy past inequality. That argument is presented in the project
  and in Lesson 06's deliberation as a live, two-sided dispute, not a
  settled moral.

The through-line for the project: rights expand when strategies
combine. A court opinion without a movement behind it gathers dust; a
movement without a legal theory dissipates; a statute without
enforcement machinery fades. The strongest evaluation a learner can
write names, for one campaign, which strategy did which job — and
which job went undone.

### Worked example 1 — Which strategy did what?

*Task:* For the Montgomery bus boycott, name the strategy, the legal
hook, and the outcome — and state what the boycott alone could not
achieve.

*Reasoning:* Strategy: direct action (boycott) plus litigation. The
boycott created economic pressure and public attention; the legal hook
was *Browder v. Gayle* (1956), in which a federal court struck down
bus segregation. Outcome: Montgomery's buses desegregated. What the
boycott alone could not achieve: a ruling binding other cities, or a
statute — the boycott changed one city's buses, while the court
decision and later legislation generalized the principle. One campaign,
two strategies, different reach.

### Worked example 2 — Commerce Clause as civil-rights tool

*Task:* Why did Congress ground Title II of the 1964 Act in the
Commerce Clause rather than the Fourteenth Amendment alone?

*Reasoning:* The Fourteenth Amendment binds *states* ("No State
shall…"), and the Court had held in the *Civil Rights Cases* (1883)
that it does not reach purely private discrimination. A motel or
restaurant refusing customers is a private actor. The Commerce Clause,
by contrast, lets Congress regulate activity affecting interstate
commerce — and a motel serving interstate travelers plainly does. The
choice of clause was not technical trivia; it determined whether the
statute could reach private discrimination at all, and *Heart of
Atlanta* confirmed it could.

## Guided practice

1. **G1.** Build the 1954–1965 timeline: *Brown* (1954), Montgomery
   boycott (1955–56), sit-ins (1960), Freedom Rides (1961), Birmingham
   (1963), Civil Rights Act (1964), Selma and the VRA (1965). For each
   event, label its primary strategy (litigation / direct action /
   legislation) and write one sentence on what it made possible that
   the previous event had not.
2. **G2.** A learner says, "The Civil Rights Act fixed discrimination."
   Write a two-sentence correction using one fact about enforcement
   (agencies, lawsuits, later court interpretation) and one fact about
   de facto separation.
3. **G3.** Compare *Heart of Atlanta* (1964) with the *Civil Rights
   Cases* (1883): what changed between the two decisions — the text,
   the clause invoked, or the Court's reading of Congress's power?
   (Answer: the clause and the reading, not the Fourteenth
   Amendment's text.)

## Independent practice

1. **I1.** Choose one campaign from this lesson and write a
   "strategy audit": which of the three strategies did it use, in what
   order, and which strategy was missing? What did the missing strategy
   cost it, if anything?
2. **I2.** The VRA's preclearance regime required covered jurisdictions
   to prove a voting change was *not* discriminatory before adopting
   it. Ordinary lawmaking works the other way: a law stands until
   someone proves it discriminatory. Explain why Congress reversed the
   burden for covered jurisdictions, using the history of literacy
   tests and the "whack-a-mole" problem (each struck-down device
   replaced by a new one).
3. **I3.** Error analysis: "Direct action changed the law." Correct
   the sentence in two sentences, distinguishing what movements change
   (public attention, political pressure) from what only courts and
   legislatures can change (legal rules).
4. **I4.** A modern commentator argues the 1964 Act overreached because
   private businesses should be free to choose customers. A second
   argues it underreached because it left housing largely untouched
   until 1968. Summarize each position fairly in two sentences each,
   without endorsing either. (Answer in [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).)

**E.** Name the three strategies and one campaign or statute for each.
Then state, in one sentence, why *Brown* alone did not desegregate
American schools.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Gloss *preclearance* (federal approval required
  before a voting change takes effect), *boycott* (organized refusal
  to buy or use a service), and *disenfranchise* (deprive of the vote)
  before assigning the timeline task.
- **Alternate response mode:** The G1 timeline may be built as a
  physical or digital timeline with images (public-domain photographs
  from the Library of Congress, identified as such) instead of a
  written list.
- **Extension:** Research one figure the adult pre-selects from the
  repository's `resources/black_excellence_figures.md` and trace that
  figure's role in one campaign from this lesson. (The adult checks
  grade fit and verifies every biographical fact before the learner
  cites it; the guide is a research prompt, not learner reading.)
