# Answer Key — Unit 03: Civil Liberties, Civil Rights, and Equal Protection

**Audience:** the guiding adult only. Kept separate from all student
questions. Every lesson item, practice task, quiz question, and
assessment task is solved here; open responses give sample reasoning
and acceptable variation. Where a question has no single correct
verdict, the key models both sides and states what earns full marks.

## Lesson 01 — Liberties, rights, incorporation

- **G1.** (a) Liberty — assembly burdened by a fee that, if applied
  only to one group, also raises equal-protection concerns; full marks
  for either classification with reasoning that names both dimensions.
  (b) Right — equal protection: parks closed to one racial group.
  (c) Liberty — press/speech: Congress banning a book.
- **G2.** Press: *Near v. Minnesota*, 1931. Assembly: *De Jonge v.
  Oregon*, 1937. Free exercise: *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 1940.
  Speech (*Gitlow*, 1925) came first. The order surprises because the
  Bill of Rights bound only the federal government until
  incorporation began — states were free to restrict speech, press,
  and assembly under their own constitutions before these cases.
- **G3.** Barron, a wharf owner, sued Baltimore after city paving
  diverted water and ruined his wharf's value, claiming a Fifth
  Amendment taking. The Court held the Bill of Rights restrained only
  the federal government, so it did not help against a city.
- **I1.** Liberty: freedom of the press, incorporated in *Near*
  (1931). Compelling a newspaper to print a reply burdens press
  freedom (the government dictating content); the case to cite for
  incorporation is *Near*.
- **I2.** Right — Equal Protection Clause. Incorporation is the wrong
  frame because the complaint is not that the state failed to respect
  a listed liberty, but that it applied school funding unequally to
  similarly situated districts.
- **I3.** Both errors reversed: the Bill of Rights originally bound
  only the *federal* government, and the Fourteenth Amendment extended
  protections *to the states* (not to the federal government).
- **I4.** For selective incorporation: case-by-case development lets
  doctrine mature, respects federalism, and avoids importing provisions
  unsuited to state contexts. Against: rights depend on litigation
  luck and timing, leaving gaps (Third Amendment quartering, grand-jury
  indictment) and decades of unequal protection. Full marks for one
  argument each side, stated fairly.
- **Applied (A).** Accept any realistic pair with distinct remedies:
  liberty file seeks to *stop* the government action (injunction
  against the ban); right file seeks to *equalize* treatment (uniform
  administration or funding). Deduct if the two remedies are identical.
- **Exit (E).** Liberties = freedoms government must not take (e.g.,
  speech); rights = equal treatment government must guarantee (e.g.,
  equal school funding). *Barron*: Bill of Rights bound only the
  federal government; the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) supplied the
  mechanism to apply most of it to the states. Any two incorporation
  cases with correct years (e.g., *Gitlow* 1925, *Near* 1931).

## Lesson 02 — Speech in conflict

- **G1.** (a) Protected — abstract praise of a past riot; no
  imminence, no likelihood (fails both *Brandenburg* prongs). (b)
  Unprotected — directed to imminent action ("push through now"),
  likely (crowd present, blocking entrance). (c) Protected — a song
  lyric advocates revolution abstractly; no imminence or likelihood.
- **G2.** The ban is not justified under *Tinker*: a five-minute loud
  lunch argument is not material, substantial disruption of school
  work. Facts that would change the answer: classes unable to proceed,
  violence or threats, sustained disruption documented by the school.
- **G3.** (a) Unprotected — defamation with actual malice (mayor is a
  public official; reporter knew the claim was false). (b)
  Unprotected — fighting words / true threat (face-to-face, fists
  raised, targeted individual). (c) Protected — offensive speech is
  not an unprotected category.
- **I1.** The citation is correct only if the paper is
  school-sponsored (curricular, faculty-advised): *Hazelwood* permits
  school control for legitimate pedagogical reasons. If the paper is
  an independent student publication, *Tinker* governs and criticism
  of lunch contracts, without disruption, is protected. Full marks for
  the distinction, whichever conclusion the learner reaches on the
  facts as stated.
- **I2.** Content-neutral (applies to every group). Time/place/manner
  test: significant government interest (public safety, order),
  narrowly tailored to that interest, ample alternative channels
  (other times, places, unamplified speech). The city must show all
  three; on these facts it likely can.
- **I3.** Error: the First Amendment protects hateful and offensive
  speech but not *all* speech. Counterexamples: incitement meeting
  *Brandenburg*, and defamation with actual malice (or true threats,
  obscenity under *Miller*, fighting words).
- **I4.** The governor's order is government action restraining the
  press: the First Amendment (incorporated against the states) binds
  the governor. The platform is private, but the *order* is state
  action — and as a ban on publication before it happens it is prior
  restraint, bearing a heavy presumption against validity (*Near*;
  *New York Times v. United States*).
- **Applied (A).** Model ruling: suspension lifted. The armband is
  student speech under *Tinker*; a heated but nonviolent debate is not
  material, substantial disruption. The school's best counterargument
  (disruption) fails on the facts: no class stopped, no violence, no
  rights of others invaded. Remedy: lift the suspension and expunge
  the record.
- **Exit (E).** *Brandenburg*: speech advocating lawless action is
  protected unless directed to inciting imminent lawless action and
  likely to incite it. *Tinker*: schools may restrict student speech
  only on facts showing material, substantial disruption or invasion
  of others' rights. Unprotected categories: any two of incitement,
  true threats, obscenity, defamation, fighting words, speech integral
  to crime. Wrongly believed unprotected: hateful or offensive speech.

## Lesson 03 — Religion, press, assembly, petition

- **G1 (illustration questions).** (1) Speech (the speaker at the
  microphone) and assembly (the audience gathered); petition is
  implicated if the forum addresses government, but speech +
  assembly suffice for full marks. (2) Yes, if content-neutral: the
  9 p.m. end and clear aisles serve significant interests (safety,
  building hours), are narrowly tailored, and leave ample alternatives
  (other times, venues). (3) Viewpoint discrimination: charging
  opponents while letting supporters use the hall free violates the
  rule that government may not favor one viewpoint even in a limited
  forum.
- **G2.** Free Exercise (and free speech): the neutral exemption is
  not the problem; revoking it because the charity criticized the
  governor targets religious exercise and speech for its viewpoint.
  Establishment is not violated by a neutral exemption available to
  all nonprofits.
- **G3.** Prior restraint. *Near v. Minnesota* (1931) governs, with
  *New York Times v. United States* (1971) as the closest modern case:
  the government bears a heavy burden to show grave, irreparable harm
  from publication.
- **I1.** Allowed: private student speech on equal terms with other
  clubs is not government establishment. *Engel* involved
  government-composed prayer; an equal-terms club is private speech
  the school may not exclude because it is religious.
- **I2.** The sidewalk before city hall is a traditional public forum.
  A total ban on demonstrations fails time/place/manner review: it is
  not narrowly tailored (a complete ban rather than a tailored rule),
  and allowing vendors on the same sidewalk undermines the traffic
  rationale. The ban does not survive.
- **I3.** Error: neutrality toward religion is not hostility to it.
  A neutral law of general applicability may incidentally burden
  religion without violating free exercise (*Smith*, 1990); RFRA
  (1993) requires the *federal* government to justify substantial
  burdens, but the state need not exclude religious organizations from
  neutral funding or services — exclusion because of religion would
  itself raise free-exercise problems.
- **I4.** Petition is burdened: the right belongs to "the people,"
  not property owners. The government's best defense —
  administrative verification of local standing — is weak against a
  blanket property qualification; a residency or signature rule would
  be a closer case.
- **Applied (A).** Rules (1) and (2): valid if content-neutral,
  serving safety/hours/aesthetic interests, narrowly tailored, with
  alternatives. Rule (3): valid if applied equally and the deposit is
  not so large as to price out groups (a prohibitive deposit would
  fail tailoring). Fourth rule that clearly violates: any
  viewpoint-based fee, topic ban, or requirement that groups endorse
  the city's positions — viewpoint discrimination is forbidden in
  every forum type.
- **Exit (E).** Establishment: government may not establish/endorse
  religion. Free exercise: government may not burden religious
  practice. Tension: accommodating religion can look like
  establishing it; refusing accommodation can look like burdening it.
  Prior restraint: heavy presumption against validity (*Near*).
  Time/place/manner: content-neutral, significant interest, narrowly
  tailored, ample alternatives. Assembly incorporated: *De Jonge*
  (1937).

## Lesson 04 — Equal protection: Plessy to Brown

- **G1.** *Plessy* majority: separation is not inequality if
  facilities are equal; a reasonable police-power regulation. Harlan
  dissent: "Our Constitution is color-blind." *Brown* adopted
  Harlan's side.
- **G2.** (a) Driving age 16: rational basis (age). (b) Veterans
  preference: rational basis (veterans are not a suspect class).
  (c) Interracial-marriage ban: strict scrutiny (race) — struck down
  in *Loving* (1967). (d) Female-only retirement at 60: intermediate
  scrutiny (sex).
- **G3.** The ladder built precedent where inequality was undeniable
  (graduate schools with plainly unequal facilities) before attacking
  public schools directly, so that by 1954 "separate but equal" had
  already been hollowed out and the Court faced a record, not just an
  abstraction.
- **I1.** De facto segregation: no law mentions race. A *Brown* claim
  would need state action causing the separation (a law, policy, or
  official practice) — demographic homogeneity alone, produced by
  private housing patterns, does not establish a *Brown* violation.
- **I2.** Rational basis: wealth alone has not triggered heightened
  scrutiny. Likely outcome: upheld — education funding is a legitimate
  interest, and a tax credit is rationally related to it; unequal
  benefit alone is not an equal-protection violation.
- **I3.** Error: *Brown* changed the constitutional rule in 1954; it
  did not end school segregation. Accurate sentence: "*Brown* held
  segregated public schools unconstitutional in 1954, but *Brown
  II*'s 'all deliberate speed' standard and Southern resistance meant
  desegregation took decades (e.g., Little Rock, 1957, required
  federal intervention)."
- **I4.** Paradox: the Court recited strict scrutiny's demanding
  language but deferred to the government's wartime claims, upholding
  internment. The test constrains only if the court applies it
  rigorously; *Korematsu* shows a test can be announced and
  simultaneously emptied — which is why it was later repudiated.
- **Applied (A).** Model brief: *Brown* held separate educational
  facilities "inherently unequal" — the harm is separation itself,
  not unequal funding. Identical budgets do not save the law because
  the stigma and denial of equal opportunity flow from the
  state-imposed separation. State's best response (*Plessy* permitted
  separate-but-equal where equal) fails because *Brown* overruled
  *Plessy* in public education. Remedy sought: desegregation order
  (admission without regard to race).

## Lesson 05 — Movements and legislation

- **G1.** Timeline with strategies: *Brown* (1954, litigation);
  Montgomery boycott (1955–56, direct action + litigation);
  sit-ins (1960, direct action); Freedom Rides (1961, direct action
  testing *Boynton*); Birmingham (1963, direct action); Civil Rights
  Act (1964, legislation); Selma + VRA (1965, movement +
  legislation). Each entry's "made possible" sentence must name a
  concrete next step (e.g., Birmingham → Kennedy's 1963 bill proposal;
  Selma → VRA passage).
- **G2.** Model correction: "The 1964 Act banned discrimination in
  public accommodations and employment, but enforcement depended on
  agencies, private lawsuits, and later court readings — and it left
  de facto separation in housing and schools largely untouched."
- **G3.** The clause and the reading changed, not the Fourteenth
  Amendment's text: the *Civil Rights Cases* (1883) read the
  Fourteenth Amendment as reaching only state action, so private
  discrimination escaped it; the 1964 Act reached private
  discrimination through the Commerce Clause, upheld in *Heart of
  Atlanta* (1964).
- **I1–I4.** Open responses; grade on the strategy audit structure
  (strategy named, institution named, date given, limit stated),
  burden-reversal reasoning (preclearance prevents harm before an
  election, where lawsuits arrive too late and devices are replaced),
  the movement/law distinction (attention and pressure vs. legal
  rules), and fair summaries of both 1964 Act positions
  (overreach: private choice; underreach: housing left to 1968 and
  de facto separation untouched).
- **Applied (A).** Accept any campaign brief with the four elements
  (injustice in the campaign's terms, leading strategy, institutional
  action with date, what remained unchanged a decade later).
- **Exit (E).** Three strategies with one example each: litigation
  (*Brown*), direct action (Montgomery boycott / sit-ins),
  legislation (Civil Rights Act 1964 / VRA 1965). *Brown* alone did
  not desegregate schools because its remedy (*Brown II*, "all
  deliberate speed") depended on lower courts, executives, and
  legislatures that resisted or delayed.

## Lesson 06 — Voting rights as the capstone right

- **G1.** Poll tax → Twenty-Fourth Amendment (1964, federal) and
  *Harper* (1966, state). Literacy test → VRA ban (1965). White
  primary → *Smith v. Allwright* (1944). Grandfather clause →
  *Guinn* (1915). Evasion pattern: each device was race-neutral on
  its face and discriminatory in operation; each struck-down device
  was replaced by a new one.
- **G2.** Model: preclearance (VRA §5) required covered jurisdictions
  (identified by the §4(b) formula) to obtain federal approval before
  changing any voting rule. *Shelby County* (2013) struck §4(b) as
  unconstitutional but did *not* strike §5 itself; without a formula,
  preclearance no longer operates except under separate court orders
  (§3(c)).
- **G3.** Data task: answers depend on the table the adult obtained.
  Full marks require: table title, election year, and access date
  recorded; denominator identified (usually citizen voting-age
  population); percentage-point subtraction shown; two limits stated
  (cannot show why nonvoters stayed home; cannot prove a specific
  rule caused the gap).
- **I1.** Evidence the challenger's side needs: the rule's disparate
  effect on a protected group plus interaction with historical and
  social conditions (transport, work hours, past discrimination).
  Evidence the state's side needs: a legitimate interest (fraud
  prevention, administration) and evidence the burden is not
  race-linked. Neither alone proves the case: burden alone does not
  prove a Section 2 violation; a legitimate purpose alone does not
  defeat one.
- **I2.** For a new formula (majority's logic): current data restores
  the constitutional justification and respects equal state
  sovereignty. Against (dissent's logic): deterrence worked through
  preclearance itself, so violations data after *Shelby County*
  understates the need; a formula based on recent violations may
  miss jurisdictions where deterrence is still doing the work. The
  evidence that would settle it: comparable violation and deterrence
  data under both regimes — which is precisely what the sides dispute.
- **I3.** Error: *Shelby County* did not repeal the Act. §4(b)
  (coverage formula) was struck down; §5 (preclearance) survives but
  is inoperative without a formula; §2 litigation (nationwide ban on
  discriminatory voting practices) remains the enforcement path, plus
  §3(c) court-ordered coverage.
- **I4.** The Nineteenth Amendment banned denial on account of sex,
  but Southern devices (literacy tests, poll taxes, white primaries)
  operated on race, not sex, and continued to block Black women (and
  Black men) until the VRA's bans and preclearance in 1965. The
  amendment closed one reason; the devices used other means.
- **Applied (A).** Full marks: three criteria stated, one real dated
  rule applied to each, strongest objection stated and answered.
- **Exit (E).** Fifteenth Amendment banned denial on account of race;
  one evasion device (poll tax, literacy test, white primary,
  grandfather clause). *Shelby County*: §4(b) unconstitutional;
  §5 intact but inoperative; §2 litigation remains. Fair-election
  answer: any reasoned one-sentence criterion set (access, accurate
  counting, public confidence) with one reason.

  3 Liberty (free exercise + assembly burdened by a targeted permit

  (speech). 6 Right (equal protection — *Loving*).

  Yes. Free exercise: *Cantwell*, 1940, Yes. Establishment:
  *Everson*, 1947, Yes. Third Amendment quartering: No — never
  formally incorporated by the Supreme Court. Grand-jury indictment:
  No — never formally incorporated. The last two rows are the best
  evidence of selectivity: if incorporation were total, no Bill of

- **Task 3.** 1 Protected — silent button, debate without disruption

- **Task 4.** 1 Rational basis (age) — likely upheld. 2 Rational

  likely upheld unless purpose is shown. (If the learner argues the

  preferences are contested after *Students for Fair Admissions*
  (2023), so a certain prediction is not required — the tier
  assignment is. 4 Rational basis (education funding distinctions) —
  likely upheld. 5 Fundamental right / strict scrutiny in tier terms;
  *Obergefell* (2015) held same-sex marriage bans violate the Due
  Process and Equal Protection Clauses — the decision rested on
  liberty and equality together, not on a tier label alone. 6 Rational
  basis (residency) — likely upheld.
- **Task 5.** Grade on structure: injustice in the campaign's terms,
  leading strategy, institutional action with date, what remained

- **Task 6 (fictional data).** 1 Presidential gap: 68 − 52 = 16
  percentage points. 2 Midterm gap: 48 − 36 = 12 percentage points.
  3 Drops: Group A 68 − 48 = 20 points; Group B 52 − 36 = 16 points.
  4 Limits (any two): the table cannot show why nonvoters stayed home;
  cannot prove any particular law caused a gap; cannot show who is
  counted without its denominator note; fictional data cannot support
  any real-world claim. 5 Real-table computation: accept the learner's

## Week-12 quiz (25 points)

1. Liberties: freedoms government must not take (e.g., speech,
   press, religion). Rights: equal treatment government must guarantee
   (e.g., equal protection in voting, schooling). (2 pts)
2. *Barron*: the Bill of Rights bound only the federal government.
   The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) supplied the incorporation
   mechanism via its Due Process Clause. (2 pts)
3. Any three incorporation cases from three different decades with
   right and year correct — e.g., *Gitlow* 1925 speech, *De Jonge*
   1937 assembly, *Everson* 1947 establishment, *McDonald* 2010 arms.
   (3 pts)
4. *Brandenburg*: directed to inciting imminent lawless action AND
   likely to incite it. "Rise up someday" is protected: no imminence
   (fails prong one) and no likelihood shown (fails prong two).
   (3 pts)
5. *Tinker*: material, substantial disruption or invasion of others'
   rights, on facts. The ban does not survive: "might upset" is a
   forecast of discomfort, not disruption. (3 pts)
6. Any two unprotected categories with case/test (incitement /
   *Brandenburg*; true threats; obscenity / *Miller*; defamation /
   *Sullivan*; fighting words / *Chaplinsky*). Wrongly believed
   unprotected: hateful or offensive speech. (3 pts)
7. Content-neutral (applies to every group). Time/place/manner test:
   significant government interest, narrowly tailored, ample
   alternative channels. Application: park order and resident quiet
   are significant; a 9 p.m. cutoff is narrowly tailored if less
   restrictive hours would not serve the interest; daytime rallies
   and other venues are alternatives. (3 pts)
8. Establishment forbids government endorsement of religion; free
   exercise forbids burdening religious practice; accommodating one
   can look like violating the other. *Kennedy* (2022) abandoned
   *Lemon*'s three-part test for establishment cases in favor of a
   history-and-tradition approach. (3 pts)
9. Prior restraint governs; the government bears a heavy presumption
   against validity and must show grave, irreparable harm.
   *New York Times v. United States* (1971) is closest on point.
   (3 pts)

## Unit assessment (50 points)

**Part A (4 pts each).** 1 Any liberty/right pair not used in Lesson
01's worked examples, with correct definitions. 2 Selective
incorporation: Due Process Clause mechanism; "selective" because
rights crossed case by case, not all at once; one never formally
incorporated provision (Third Amendment quartering, grand-jury
indictment, or Seventh Amendment civil jury). 3 Content-neutral
(applies regardless of message); the state must show a significant
interest (hospital access, emergency entry), narrow tailoring (100
feet vs. a broader ban), and ample alternatives (other protest
locations). 4 *Plessy*: separate-but-equal upheld if facilities equal.
   *Brown*: separate educational facilities inherently unequal. Identical
   funding does not save segregation because the harm is separation
   itself — stigma and denial of equal opportunity — not the budget.
5 *Shelby County*: §4(b) coverage formula unconstitutional; §5 not
   struck down but inoperative without a formula; enforcement remains
   via §2 lawsuits and §3(c) court-ordered coverage.

**Part B (5 pts each; ≤2 pts without the test).** 6 *Brandenburg*:
   praise of an extremist group, posted from home with no planned
   action, is protected — it fails imminence (no action directed) and
   likelihood (no evidence of likely action). Expulsion on this record
   violates the First Amendment. 7 Religion clauses: revoking a
   neutral exemption because the charity criticized policy is a
   targeted burden on free exercise (and viewpoint-based speech
   regulation); the neutral exemption itself does not establish
   religion. 8 Intermediate scrutiny (*Craig*, 1976): the state must
   show an important interest substantially related to the sex-based
   age difference. Predicted outcome: struck down — administrative
   convenience or a stereotype about drinking behavior is not
   substantially related in the *Craig* sense; the ages differ by sex
   without a fitting justification.

**Part C (15 pts).** Thesis (3): clear position on the combination
claim. Evidence (6): two campaigns/statutes with correct dates and
specific outcomes (e.g., *Brown* 1954 rule change without
implementation; Civil Rights Act 1964 statutory reach; Selma 1965 →
VRA preclearance and registration gains). Objection (4): strongest
counterargument stated fairly (e.g., a single strategy arguably
sufficed in one case, or combined strategies still left de facto
separation) and answered with evidence. Undone (2): one concrete
thing left undone (de facto housing/school separation, enforcement
gaps, or a live dispute such as post-*Shelby County* voting rules or
post-*SFFA* admissions).
