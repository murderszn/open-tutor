# Unit 03 — Civil Liberties, Civil Rights, and Equal Protection

**Grade:** 11 · **Subject:** Social Studies (U.S. government and civics)
**Track weeks:** 11–14 · **Backlog:** [issue #53](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/53), section U03
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Unit 02 asked who gets to decide — the national government, the states,
or both. This unit asks what government may not do to a person, and what
it must do equally for every person. The two questions sound alike and
are not the same. A **civil liberty** is a freedom the government must
not take from you: speak, worship, publish, assemble. A **civil right**
is equal treatment the government must guarantee you: the same law,
applied the same way, regardless of race or other protected traits.
Liberties restrain government; rights oblige it. Much of American
history since 1791 is the argument over where each line falls — and who
gets to draw it.

## Essential question

When one person's liberty collides with another person's equality, who
decides — and by what rule?

**Sensitive-content note:** this unit covers racial discrimination,

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** civil liberties from civil rights, explain the Bill
   of Rights' origins, and trace selective incorporation through the
   Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause, naming at least four
   incorporation cases and their dates.
   (D2.Civ.4.9-12; D2.Civ.12.9-12)
2. **Analyze** First Amendment speech protections and their limits:
   apply the *Brandenburg* imminent-lawless-action test and the
   *Tinker* substantial-disruption test to new scenarios, and name
   the categories of speech the Court has left unprotected.
   (D2.Civ.10.9-12; D2.Civ.12.9-12)
3. **Analyze** religion, press, assembly, and petition conflicts:
   explain the Establishment and Free Exercise Clauses, the heavy
   presumption against prior restraint, and content-neutral
   time/place/manner rules, arguing a public-forum scenario from both
   sides. (D2.Civ.10.9-12; D2.Civ.12.9-12)
4. **Trace** the equal-protection struggle from *Plessy v. Ferguson*
   (1896) to *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) and explain the
   three tiers of judicial scrutiny with a correct example of each.
   (D2.Civ.12.9-12)
5. **Evaluate** how litigation, legislation, and movements expanded
   civil rights — the NAACP litigation ladder, the Civil Rights Act of
   1964, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 — judging what each strategy
   changed and what it did not. (D2.Civ.14.9-12; D2.Civ.5.9-12)
6. **Analyze** voting rights from the Fifteenth Amendment through the
   Voting Rights Act to *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), presenting
   the majority and dissent positions fairly and defending an
   evidence-based answer to "what makes an election fair?"
   (D2.Civ.2.9-12; D2.Civ.12.9-12)

In every unit the learner also develops compelling questions
(D1.1–D1.5.9-12), evaluates sources and their limits (D3.1–D3.4.9-12),
and communicates evidence-based conclusions (D4.1–D4.4.9-12).

Primary sources are quoted in brief public-domain excerpts with links:
the Constitution and amendments (National Archives transcription),
Supreme Court opinions (U.S. government works, public domain), and the
Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act texts (govinfo / Congress.gov).
The unit is nonpartisan: institutions and processes are analyzed
without endorsing parties, candidates, or causes, and contested
questions (how far speech protection extends, whether *Shelby County*
was rightly decided, what makes an election fair) are argued from both
sides with evidence. Current-law examples are verified by the adult at
the time of use and dated in the learner's evidence log — no
current-policy figure in this unit is presented as permanent.
