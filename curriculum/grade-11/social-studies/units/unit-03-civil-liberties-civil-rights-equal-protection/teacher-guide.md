# Teacher Guide — Unit 03: Civil Liberties, Civil Rights, and Equal Protection

**Audience:** the guiding adult. This guide is kept separate from all
student questions. **Status:** validated draft; not educator-reviewed.

## Pacing and placement

- 20 sessions over track weeks 11–14, ~45–60 minutes each. If sessions
  run short, protect the deliberation sessions (W12S4, W14S4) and the
  project defense; compress the W11S5/W13S5 reviews into retrieval
  quizzes rather than cutting argument time.
- Prerequisite check at W11S1: if the learner cannot yet write a claim
  with two pieces of evidence and a counterclaim, spend W11S2 on that
  skill using Lesson 01's G1 sort before moving to incorporation cases.
- The week-12 quiz (W12S5, 25 pts) is a gate: a score below 15 means
  Lessons 01–03 need re-teaching before Lesson 04 — especially the
  liberties/rights distinction and the *Brandenburg*/*Tinker* tests.
  Do not let a weak L1–L3 foundation carry into the equal-protection
  weeks, where every argument assumes it.

## Lesson notes

### L1 — Liberties, rights, incorporation

- Teach the distinction before the cases: learners who memorize the
  incorporation table without the liberty/right distinction will
  misclassify every Lesson 04–06 problem.
- *Barron* (1833) is the anchor fact. Repeat it whenever a learner
  says the Bill of Rights "always" bound the states.
- Watch for: "The Fourteenth Amendment incorporated the Bill of
  Rights in 1868." Correct to: mechanism in 1868, rights crossed case
  by case from 1925 onward, and the crossing is incomplete.
- Sensitive content: none graphic in this lesson; the wharf case and
  amendment texts are documentary.

### L2 — Speech in conflict

- The emotional center is *Brandenburg* protecting a Klan leader's
  speech. Name the discomfort directly: the test protects the speech
  precisely because the speaker is despised. If the learner cannot
  accept that, they have not yet grasped viewpoint neutrality.
- Common error: treating "offensive" or "false" as unprotected
  categories. Drill the short list (incitement, true threats,
  obscenity, defamation, fighting words, speech integral to crime)
  until learners can recite what is *not* on it.
- *Tinker* vs. *Hazelwood* is the highest-value distinction in the
  lesson: student's own speech vs. school-sponsored speech. Use the
  newspaper example in both directions before assigning I1.

### L3 — Religion, press, assembly, petition

- Teach *Lemon* and *Kennedy* as a sequence, not a contradiction to
  hide: *Lemon* (1971) governed for decades; *Kennedy* (2022)
  abandoned it for establishment cases. A learner citing *Lemon* as
  current law needs updating, not scolding.
- The generated forum illustration is an AI illustration, not a
  photograph and not evidence about any real meeting. Say so before
  the activity; learners must not cite the image as a source.
- Public-forum doctrine rewards diagramming: draw three columns
  (traditional / limited / nonpublic) with the viewpoint rule
  underlined across all three — viewpoint discrimination is forbidden
  in every forum type.

### L4 — Equal protection: Plessy to Brown

- Adult preview required: this lesson covers state-enforced racial
  segregation and the language of the period. Sources are documentary
  and textual; no graphic imagery is assigned.
- Harlan's dissent deserves slow reading: it is the unit's best example
  of a dissent becoming later law. Ask learners to track one Harlan
  sentence into *Brown*'s reasoning.
- Scrutiny tiers: drill with the sorting task (practice Task 4) until
  assignments are automatic, then complicate with the exceptions
  (citizenship voting qualification; *Korematsu*).
- De jure vs. de facto is the distinction that prevents the most
  overclaims in the assessment. Insist on it in every answer.

### L5 — Movements and legislation

- The combination thesis is the lesson: litigation + direct action +
  legislation. Grade project drafts against it explicitly.
- Name the cost of direct action (arrest, beating, death) plainly.
  Never present nonviolent protest as safe.
- Figures: the adult pre-selects any figure from
  `resources/black_excellence_figures.md` for the extension and
  verifies every biographical fact before the learner cites it; the
  guide is a research prompt, not learner reading.
- Copyright: movement documents still under copyright are linked or
  briefly quoted, never reproduced in full.

### L6 — Voting rights as the capstone right

- This is the unit's central nonpartisanship exercise. The adult must
  be able to state both *Shelby County* positions fairly before the
  learner is asked to choose. If the adult cannot, re-read Lesson 06's
  Worked example 2 together first.
- Turnout data: the adult obtains the current Census CPS voting
  supplement table and records title, year, and access date. Any
  undated turnout figure in learner work is returned for sourcing.
  The fictional dataset in practice Task 6 is arithmetic practice
  only and must never be cited as real turnout.
- Correct the "repeal" misconception precisely: §4(b) struck down,
  §5 inoperative without a formula, §2 litigation remains.

## Misconceptions register

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| The Bill of Rights always bound the states | *Barron* (1833); incorporation ran 1925–2010+ and is incomplete |
| Free speech means no consequences anywhere | The First Amendment restrains government, not private actors; unprotected categories still apply |
| Offensive or false speech is unprotected | Neither is a category; defamation requires false statements of fact plus fault (actual malice for public figures) |
| *Lemon* is current establishment law | *Kennedy* (2022) abandoned it; teach the sequence |
| *Brown* ended segregation in 1954 | *Brown* changed the rule; *Brown II*'s "all deliberate speed" plus resistance meant implementation took decades |
| *Shelby County* repealed the Voting Rights Act | It struck §4(b); §5 is inoperative without a formula; §2 remains |
| Movements change law directly | Movements change attention and pressure; courts and legislatures change legal rules |
| Turnout gaps prove a specific law's effect | Gaps show outcomes, not causes; a causal claim needs a design, not a gap |

## Readiness and reteaching

- **Ready for U04** when the learner can: distinguish liberties from
  rights with new examples; state *Brandenburg* and *Tinker* with
  both parts; assign a law to a scrutiny tier; present both sides of
  *Shelby County* fairly.
- **Reteach liberties/rights** with Lesson 01's G1 sort as cards,
  adding two new items the adult invents on the spot.
- **Reteach speech tests** with new scenarios in the *Brandenburg*
  shape (change imminence, then likelihood, one variable at a time).
- **Reteach scrutiny** with practice Task 4 re-sorted under time
  pressure, then the exceptions (*Korematsu*, citizenship voting).
- **Reteach *Shelby County*** by having the learner write the
  majority's case first if they favor the dissent, and vice versa —
  the side-switch exercise from Lesson 06.

## Nonpartisanship guardrails

- Contested questions in this unit (speech limits, *Shelby County*,
  race-conscious remedies after *Students for Fair Admissions*,
  what makes an election fair) are taught with multiple sourced
  perspectives. The adult checks that examples stay institutional
  (roles, rules, processes) rather than partisan commentary.
- The unit never endorses parties, candidates, or causes. Movement
  history is taught as documented fact (dates, statutes, holdings),
  with strategies evaluated on evidence.
- If a learner's verdict on a contested question is one-sided, require
  the objection paragraph (project rubric; assessment Part C) before
  accepting the work — the objection is part of the assignment, not
  extra credit.
