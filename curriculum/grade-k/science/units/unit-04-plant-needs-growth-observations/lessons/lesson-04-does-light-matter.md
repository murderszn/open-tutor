# Lesson 04 — Does light matter?

**Goal (read to the learner):** "Today you ask a testable question:
does light matter for a plant? You will make a guess — a **predict** —
and then set up a **fair test**: two plants that are the same in every
way except one gets light and one does not. Then we watch and see."

**Time:** 15–20 minutes. **Materials:** two bean plants as alike as
possible (same kind of seed, planted the same day in the same kind of
cup with the same soil — planted by the adult in advance if the
learner's own plants differ), two labels the adult writes ("Light" and
"Dark"), the science journal, crayons. The adult chooses the dark
place (a cupboard the adult opens and closes; the learner never climbs
inside).

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Tell me the four things a plant needs." (Water, light, air, soil with
room to grow.) "Today we test just one: light. What do you think will
happen to a plant with no light?" Accept any guess — a guess is a
prediction, not a right answer yet.

## Teach and model

Explain in your own words: *A **fair test** changes only ONE thing, so
we know what caused what we see. If one plant gets more water AND more
light, and it grows better, we cannot tell which one helped. So our two
test plants get the same seed, the same soil, the same cup, and the
same water. The only difference is light: one plant lives by the bright
window, and one plant lives in a dark cupboard. A **predict** is what
you think will happen BEFORE the test. Scientists predict, then they
look — and sometimes the plants surprise them, and that is fine.*

**Modeled example 1 — the adult predicts.** "My predict is: the light
plant will grow greener and stronger, and the dark plant will grow
pale and weak, or hardly grow at all — because plants need light. I
might be wrong! We will look in a few days and see." (Modeled
reasoning: name the guess, name the reason from the needs, admit it
might be wrong.)

**Modeled example 2 — the unfair test.** The adult holds up two very
different plants: "What if I test a big plant in the light against a
tiny seed in the dark, and I water only the light one? Then three
things are different, not one. That test is not fair, and it cannot
tell us about light." The learner names the differences (size, water,
light) with help.

## Guided practice (adult-guided)

1. **Guided:** "What must be the SAME for our two test plants?" The
   learner names with prompts: same seed, same soil, same cup, same
   water. "What will be DIFFERENT?" (Light only: window vs. cupboard.)
2. **Guided:** The learner makes a prediction and the adult scribes it
   in the journal: "I predict ___." (Any prediction counts, as long as
   it is about the two plants and light.)

## Independent practice

3. **Independent setup:** The learner places the Light plant by the
   window and hands the Dark plant to the adult, who puts it in the
   cupboard. The learner sticks on (or points to) the correct label
   for each.
4. **Independent journal drawing:** The learner draws both test plants
   today, side by side, before the test starts — so later drawings can
   be compared fairly.
5. **Independent fair-or-not:** The adult describes two mini-tests and
   the learner judges each: (a) "Two same plants, same water, one in
   light, one in dark." (Fair.) (b) "One plant watered, one plant not
   watered, one in light, one in dark." (Not fair — two things differ.)
6. **Error-analysis (guided):** The adult says, "Let us water the light
   plant every day and the dark plant never, so the test is faster!"
   The learner corrects: "No — same water for both, or we will not
   know if it was the light or the water."

## Application

7. **Care routine for the test:** "Who waters the test plants, and how
   much?" (The adult waters both the same small amount on the same
   days; the learner checks and draws. The dark plant is taken out
   only for quick looking and drawing, then put straight back, so it
   stays dark.)

## Exit check

The learner states the prediction in their own words, names what is the
same and what is different in the fair test, and can point to where
each test plant lives (window vs. cupboard). The adult records the
prediction verbatim and checks both plants are placed correctly.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** "Fair" is taught with the two-plants
  example first, the word second. "Predict" is glossed as "your guess
  before we look."
- **Alternate response mode:** Pointing to the window and the cupboard,
  acting out light vs. dark (arms open vs. hands over eyes), or drawing
  the two places counts fully; the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** "What other ONE thing could we test fairly one day?"
  (Water: same light, different water — but only as a question for a
  later unit, not a second test now. One fair test at a time.)
- **Table and floor variants:** Seated at a table with both plants in
  front before placement, or standing by the window and cupboard; both
  work.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** "The dark plant will die at once." Redirect:
"It may still grow for a while — tall, pale, and reaching — because
the seed has food stored inside to start with. We watch what actually
happens over days; we do not decide the ending in advance."

**Common misconception:** "A fair test means the plants must look the
same at the end." Redirect: "Fair means they START the same except
for light. If they end up different, that difference tells us about
light."

**Safety note:** The dark cupboard is for the plant only. The learner
never enters a cupboard or dark enclosed space. The adult retrieves the
dark plant for brief observations.
