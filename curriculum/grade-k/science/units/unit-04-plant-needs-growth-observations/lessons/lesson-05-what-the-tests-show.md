# Lesson 05 — What did the tests show?

**Goal (read to the learner):** "Today you look at the two test plants
again and tell what the test showed. Did the light plant grow
differently from the dark plant? You will use what you see — not what
you guessed — to tell the pattern."

**Time:** 15–20 minutes. **Materials:** both test plants (the adult
takes the dark plant out of the cupboard for this lesson), counting
cubes, the science journal with the Lesson 04 prediction and drawings,
crayons, a hand lens.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"What did you predict in Lesson 04?" The adult reads the learner's
prediction back from the journal. "Today we check: were you right, or
did the plants surprise you? Both answers are good science."

## Teach and model

Explain in your own words: *To tell what a test showed, we compare the
two plants side by side and we look for differences: Which one is
taller? Which leaves look greener? Which stem looks stronger, standing
up straight? We also look at what is the SAME: both had the same seed,
soil, cup, and water — so any big difference is most likely about the
light. We write down what we see, even if it is not what we predicted.
Changing your mind when the plants show you something new is what
scientists do.*

**Modeled example 1 — comparing color and strength.** Adult holds the
plants side by side (or describes the usual outcome, then checks the
real plants): "I **notice** the light plant's leaves look green and its
stem stands up straight. I **notice** the dark plant's stem is pale
yellow-green, bending, and its leaves are small and pale. So I say:
the light plant grew stronger and greener. That matches my prediction
— this time." (Modeled reasoning: observe both, compare, then judge
against the prediction.)

**Modeled example 2 — measuring the difference (practice example).**
"This is a practice example, not our plants' real measurement. A
practice light plant measured 6 cubes tall. A practice dark plant
measured 4 cubes tall, and its stem bent over. 6 is more than 4, so the
practice light plant grew 2 cubes taller — and it stood up straight,
while the dark one bent. Taller is not the whole story: strong and
green matters too." The adult stacks 6 cubes and 4 cubes so the
learner sees the difference — and points out that a tall, pale,
bending plant is NOT the stronger plant, even if it is tall.

## Guided practice (adult-guided)

1. **Guided:** Side by side, the learner touches (gently) and looks at
   both real test plants. "Which leaves look greener?" The learner
   points. "Which stem stands up straighter?" The learner points.
   Prompt with "Look at the color first, then the stem" if stuck.
2. **Guided:** Together, measure both real plants in cubes, from soil
   to tallest leaf, the same way as Lesson 03. The adult scribes both
   counts.

## Independent practice

3. **Independent comparison sentence:** "Tell me in one sentence: how
   is the light plant different from the dark plant?" (For example:
   "The light plant is greener and stands up straight, and the dark
   plant is pale and bendy." Any sentence naming one observed
   difference counts; the adult scribes.)
4. **Independent journal drawing:** The learner draws both test plants
   today, side by side, with the cube counts scribed beside them.
5. **Independent prediction check:** "Was your prediction right, partly
   right, or different from what happened?" (Any of the three counts,
   with one observed reason — for example, "Partly right: the dark
   plant still grew, but it is pale.")
6. **Error-analysis (guided):** The adult says, "The dark plant is
   taller, so the dark plant is better!" The learner corrects: "No —
   tall and pale and bending is not stronger. The light plant is
   greener and stands up straight." (If the learner's real dark plant
   is in fact taller, the adult reframes: "Taller but pale and bending
   — is that stronger? No. Strong means green and standing up.")

## Application

7. **The pattern, in your own words:** "What pattern did our test
   show about plants and light?" (Plants with light grow greener and
   stronger; plants without light grow pale and weak. The adult
   scribes the learner's exact words in the journal as the unit's
   pattern statement.)

## Exit check

The learner compares the two real test plants, names at least one
observed difference (color, strength, or height measured in cubes),
and states the light pattern in their own words. The adult records the
difference named, both cube counts, and the pattern statement.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** "Stronger" is taught by touching the
  stems (straight vs. bending) first, the word second. Color words are
  taught by pointing at the leaves.
- **Alternate response mode:** Pointing to the greener plant, acting
  out straight vs. bending stems, or drawing the two plants counts
  fully; the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** "Where would you put a new plant so it grows strong?"
  (By a bright window, with water when the soil is dry.) "What would
  you tell a friend who keeps a plant in a dark closet?" (Move it to
  the light.)
- **Table and floor variants:** Seated at a table with both plants side
  by side, or on the floor with the plants on the tray; both work.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** "Taller always means better." Redirect with
the plants themselves: "This dark plant stretched tall reaching for
light, but it is pale and it bends. Strong means green leaves and a
stem that holds itself up — not just tall."

**Common misconception:** "My prediction was wrong, so I failed."
Redirect: "A prediction is a guess before looking. Finding out it was
different IS the discovery. Scientists change their minds when the
evidence changes."

**If the test plants look almost the same:** Say so honestly — the
test may have run only a few days, or the cupboard may not have been
fully dark. Extend the test a few more days and repeat this lesson's
comparison; do not invent a difference. Record "almost the same so
far" as the result and re-check.
