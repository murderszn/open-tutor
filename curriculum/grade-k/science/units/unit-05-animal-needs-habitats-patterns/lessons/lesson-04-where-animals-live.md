# Lesson 04 — Where animals live

**Goal (read to the learner):** "Today you match animals to their
homes. A duck lives in a pond. A deer lives in a forest. A rabbit lives
in a burrow. You will tell why each animal lives where it does."

**Time:** 15–20 minutes. **Materials:** three habitat mats (drawn or
printed by the adult: pond — blue water with cattails; forest — trees;
burrow — a grassy hill with a hole), animal cards (duck, fish, frog,
turtle, deer, rabbit, fox, bear), the science journal.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Where does a duck live?" Accept pond or water. "Where do you live?"
Accept home. The adult names the question: "An animal lives where it
can find what it needs. Today we match animals to their homes — and
tell why."

## Teach and model

Explain in your own words: *A **habitat** is the place an animal lives.
An animal lives where it can find its food, its water, and its shelter.
A deer eats buds and leaves, so a deer usually lives in a **forest**,
where trees grow. A duck swims and eats in the water, so a duck lives
in a **pond**. A rabbit eats grass and hides underground, so a rabbit
lives in a **burrow** — a hole dug in the ground. The home matches the
needs. That is the relationship this unit is about: needs and place go
together.*

**Modeled example 1 — deer and forest.** Adult places the deer card on
the forest mat: "I **notice** trees with buds and leaves on this mat.
A deer eats buds and leaves. The forest gives the deer its food. The
trees also give shade and hiding places — shelter. Food plus shelter:
that is why the deer lives in the forest."

**Modeled example 2 — rabbit and burrow.** Adult places the rabbit card
on the burrow mat: "A rabbit eats grass, and grass grows around the
burrow. The burrow — the hole in the ground — is a safe, hidden place
to rest and hide. Food nearby, shelter underground: that is why the
rabbit lives in a burrow."

## Guided practice (adult-guided)

1. **Guided:** "Put the **duck** on its home." (Pond mat.) "Why does
   the duck live in the pond?" (Water to swim in, food in the water.)
   "Put the **frog** on its home." (Pond mat.) "Why?" (Water, flies
   for food, lily pad for resting.) Prompt with "What does this
   animal eat, and where does that food grow?" if stuck.

## Independent practice

2. **Independent sorting:** Mix the animal cards. "Put each animal on
   its home mat." (Pond: duck, fish, frog, turtle. Forest: deer, fox,
   bear. Burrow: rabbit. Accept bear on forest; accept fox on forest.)
3. **Independent why-talk:** For two animals the learner placed, "Why
   does this animal live here?" Accept one need-based reason per animal
   (food, water, or shelter found there).
4. **Independent home match:** "Where do people live?" (In a home — a
   house or apartment.) "Why?" (Food in the kitchen, water from taps,
   shelter from weather, air all around.) The adult scribes the
   learner's reasons.
5. **Error-analysis (guided):** The adult puts the fish card on the
   forest mat and says, "The fish lives in the forest!" The learner
   corrects: "No — a fish needs water. A fish lives in the pond."
   Celebrate the correction.

## Application

6. **Wrong-home game:** The adult deliberately places three animals on
   wrong mats (fish in the forest, deer in the pond, rabbit in the
   pond). The learner moves each to its right home and says why, using
   a need: "The deer needs trees for food, so the deer goes to the
   forest."

## Exit check

The learner places two animals on their correct habitat mats and gives
one need-based reason for each, without the adult naming the reason
first. The adult records both animals, mats, and reasons.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** Habitat words are taught with the mat
  picture first, the word second. Home-language habitat words are
  welcomed alongside the English words.
- **Alternate response mode:** Placing the card on the mat counts
  fully; the adult scribes the learner's reason. A learner may answer
  entirely by showing.
- **Extension:** "Can one animal live in two habitats?" (A duck swims
  in the pond and rests in the grass at the edge — the pond habitat
  includes the edge. A bear walks through the forest and fishes in a
  stream. Habitats touch each other; an animal uses the parts it
  needs.) "Draw an animal that uses two parts of its habitat."
- **Table and floor variants:** Seated at a table with mats side by
  side, or on the floor with mats spread on a blanket; both work.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** "Animals live anywhere they want." Redirect:
"An animal needs food, water, and shelter every day. It lives where
those things are. A fish cannot live in a forest because there is no
water to swim in."

**Common misconception:** "A burrow is a house like ours." Redirect:
"A burrow is a hole in the ground. It is a rabbit's shelter, but it is
not built like a person's house. Different animals, different
shelters — same need."
