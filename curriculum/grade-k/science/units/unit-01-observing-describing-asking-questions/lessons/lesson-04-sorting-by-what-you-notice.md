# Lesson 04 — Sorting by what you notice

**Goal (read to the learner):** "Today you will sort objects. To **sort**
means to put together the things that are alike. Scientists sort what they
observe so they can see patterns — which things go together and why."

**Time:** 15–20 minutes. **Materials:** 8–10 familiar small objects that vary
along at least two properties (e.g., buttons, shells, or blocks in two or
three colors and two sizes); two or three trays or shoebox lids.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Adult: "What is a description?" (Telling details someone can check.) "Name one
detail you can see on these objects." Accept color, size, shape, texture.

## Teach and model

Explain: *When we observe many things, we can **sort** them — put the alike
ones together. The rule we sort by must be something we can observe, like
color or size, not something we guess. After sorting, we **name the rule**:
"I sorted by color."*

**Modeled example 1 — sort by color.** Adult spreads the objects: "I observe
red buttons, blue buttons, and yellow buttons. I will sort by color: all the
red ones in this tray, all the blue ones here, all the yellow ones here."
Points to each group: "My rule is color. You can check it with your eyes."

**Modeled example 2 — the same objects, a different rule.** Adult mixes them
again: "Now I observe big buttons and little buttons. I will sort by size:
big ones here, little ones here. My rule is size." (Modeled reasoning: the
same set can be sorted more than one way — the rule is the observer's
choice, and it must be observable.)

## Guided practice (adult-guided)

1. **Guided:** "Sort these objects your own way. Then tell me your rule."
   If the learner sorts without a rule, ask: "What is the same about the
   things in this tray?" Accept any observable rule (color, size, shape,
   texture, "has holes").

## Independent practice

2. **Independent manipulative:** Re-sort the same objects by a **different**
   rule than the first time, and name the new rule. (Two rules on one set =
   the unit objective.)
3. **Independent oral:** "Look at my sorting" — the adult sorts by one rule
   and the learner names the rule. (Checks that the learner can read someone
   else's rule.)
4. **Independent oral:** "Which tray has more? How do you know?" Accept
   "this one looks bigger/more" — whole-number comparison by looking is the
   K boundary; counting is welcome but not required here.

## Application

5. **Room sort:** "Find three things in this room that could go together and
   tell me the rule." (e.g., three soft things; three wooden things.)

## Exit check

The learner sorts the object set by an observable rule, names the rule, and
re-sorts by a different rule.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** The word "rule" is new — tie it to games the
  child knows ("games have rules; sorting has a rule too"). Home-language
  color/size words are welcomed.
- **Alternate response mode:** Sorting is itself a non-verbal response; the
  adult may name candidate rules ("color? size?") and the learner points to
  the correct one, then confirms by sorting.
- **Extension:** Sort by two rules at once ("red AND big") or explain why a
  rule would NOT work ("sort by which ones are yummiest" — not observable,
  and never taste to check).
- **Table and floor variants:** Table: trays in a row. Floor: hula hoops or
  drawn circles as sorting rings; the learner walks objects to their ring.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** Learners sometimes sort by story ("these are my
favorites") or by hidden properties ("this one is lucky"). Redirect to the
observable: "Can my eyes check 'lucky'? What can they check instead?" This
protects the observation-vs.-guess boundary from Lesson 05.

**Answer-key reference:** [`../answer-key.md`](../answer-key.md#lesson-04) —
a rule counts if the adult can verify it by looking or touching; "my
favorites", "lucky", and other unobservable rules do not count.
