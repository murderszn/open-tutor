# Lesson 02 — Write the Numerals 0–5

**Student goal:** I can trace and write the numerals 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 so a
grown-up can read them.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**

## Materials

- Numeral cards 0–5 (from Lesson 01)
- Paper and thick crayons or markers
- A shallow tray of sand or salt (optional, for finger tracing)
- The learner's Unit 01 counting skills

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Quick-fire: hold up the 0–5 cards shuffled; the learner names each. If 5 or
6 are named correctly, proceed. If fewer, replay
[Practice Session A](../practice.md#session-a-numeral-set-memory-match)
first.

## Teaching

Yesterday we learned to *read* numerals. Today we learn to *write* them.
Every numeral is made of lines and curves. We make each one the same way
every time, so other people can read our writing.

The adult demonstrates each numeral large in the air with a straight arm
while saying the path aloud:

- **1:** "Start at the top. Pull straight down. Stop."
- **0:** "Start at the top. Curve all the way around and close it."
- **2:** "Curve around the top, then slide down to the bottom line."
- **3:** "Curve around the top half, then curve around the bottom half."
- **4:** "Pull down, then across, then down the tall line."
- **5:** "Pull down, curve around the belly, then put the hat on top."

*(These are this unit's own formation words — any consistent wording the
adult and learner share works.)*

**Reasoning to name aloud:** writing the same way every time is what makes
marks readable. A 3 with its curves closed the wrong way is still a 3 if a
reader can tell — but the practiced way is easier to read.

## Worked example 1 — trace, then write, the numeral 3

The adult draws a large **3** on paper.

> "First I trace." *(adult traces over the model slowly with a finger,
> saying "curve around the top half, curve around the bottom half")*
> "Now I try one myself, next to it." *(adult writes a 3, narrating the
> same words)*
> "Now you." The learner traces the model with a finger, then writes a 3
> beside it. The adult checks: two curves? "Readable — I can tell it is a
> 3."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** trace first (the hand learns the path), then
write beside the model (the eyes check the copy).

## Worked example 2 — write the numeral that matches a set

The adult places 4 buttons in a row and asks "how many?"

> "One, two, three, four — four buttons. Now I write the numeral for four."
> *(adult writes **4**, narrating "pull down, then across, then down the
> tall line")*
> "The 4 card and my 4 look the same — they both say 'four.'"

Then the adult places 2 buttons: "Your turn — count, then write the numeral."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the written numeral is the answer to "how many?"
in marks instead of words.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Alternate tracing (sand tray or over a model) and
writing.

1. Trace **1** in the sand tray, saying "start at the top, pull straight
   down." Then write 1 on paper.
   *Hint: "Tall line, top to bottom."*
2. Trace **0** — "curve all the way around and close it." Write 0.
   *Watch for:* an unclosed 0 (looks like a C) — "close the door."
3. Write **2** and **3** side by side. "Which one has two curves? That is 3."
4. The adult shows 5 counters — "Count, then write the numeral." (5)
5. The adult writes a numeral *wrong on purpose* — a 5 missing its top bar —
   and asks: "Can you read this? What is missing?"
   *(Error-analysis: expected answer "it is a 5 but the hat is missing.")*
6. Speed round: adult calls "write 4! write 0! write 2!" — the learner
   writes each quickly; readability over beauty.

## Independent practice

The learner gets a strip of paper and writes 0–5 in order, one numeral per
"parking spot" (boxes drawn by the adult). Then the learner counts out
counters to match each written numeral (0 = empty spot). The adult observes:
readable? matched counts correct?

*Alternate response mode:* form numerals from wiki sticks, pipe cleaners, or
playdough snakes instead of writing; or stamp them with a finger in paint.
For limited grip, tracing in the sand tray counts as the written response.

## Application

Mailbox numbers: the adult writes the learner's "house number" (any 0–5, or
the real house number's first digit) on an envelope. The learner writes the
same numeral on their own envelope and "mails" it (slides it into a shoebox).
Writing numerals delivers real messages.

## Exit check

"Write 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 for me, in any order." The adult records: secure (all
six readable) / developing (4–5 readable) / not yet (fewer than 4). Note any
consistent reversal (e.g., 3 always backwards) for re-teaching — reversals
are normal at 5 and not "wrong," but the practiced path gets retaught.

## Supports

- **Language access:** the formation words are said in the home language
  first if needed; the motion matters more than the English.
- **Alternate response mode:** sand-tray tracing, playdough numerals, or
  building numerals from straight/curved craft sticks.
- **Floor-play variant:** "sky-write" each numeral with a whole arm, then
  walk its shape on the floor (tape the numeral large on the floor).

## Extension

Write 0–5 with the non-dominant hand (fun, and it proves the path is in the
head, not just the hand). Or write the numerals to label the learner's own
drawn groups (••• → 3).

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) drawing the numeral as a picture of the
things (e.g., five dots instead of 5) — "the numeral is the *shortcut*, not
the things"; (2) starting at the bottom — always restart from the top cue;
(3) mirror-writing 3 or 5 — normal at this age; reteach the path ("top
curve first"), never punish.
