# Lesson 01 — More, Fewer, Same by Matching

**Student goal:** I can tell which group has more, which has fewer, or
whether two groups are the same — by matching them up.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**
**Objectives:** 1 (matching comparison)

## Materials

- 10 counters in one color (say, red) and 10 in another (blue)
- Two trays or placemats
- Numeral cards 1–10 (for the closing check only)

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Put out 4 counters. "Touch-count them. How many?" Then 7 counters:
"How many?" If the learner counts one-to-one to 10 and answers the
total (Units 01–02), proceed. If counting wobbles, spend this session
re-touching Unit 01 counting and try matching tomorrow.

## Teaching

Today we compare. **Compare** means: look at two groups and decide
which has more, which has fewer, or whether they are the same.

The trick is **matching**: pair one thing from the first group with one
thing from the second group, like dance partners. Everybody with a
partner is matched. Anybody left without a partner is a **leftover**.

- Leftovers on one side only → that side has **more**; the other side
  has **fewer**.
- No leftovers anywhere → the groups are **the same** (equal). Same
  number, even if the things look different.

Say it with the learner, pointing: "Match, match, match… leftover!
That side has more."

## Worked example 1 — 3 red buttons vs. 5 blue buttons

The adult lays out 3 red buttons in a row and 5 blue buttons in a row
underneath, then matches them one by one, pushing each pair together.

> "One red, one blue — partners. One red, one blue — partners. One
> red, one blue — partners. Now the reds are gone, but look: two blues
> are left over, with no partner. The blues have **more**. The reds have
> **fewer**. Five is more than three."

Then the adult asks the learner to say it back: "Which has more? Which
has fewer?"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we did not count yet — matching alone
proved it. Leftovers tell the story.

## Worked example 2 — same, even though they look different

The adult lays out 4 big blocks in a spread-out row and 4 small beans
squished close together.

> "Which looks like more? The blocks look bigger — but looks can trick
> us. Match them: one block, one bean… one block, one bean… one block,
> one bean… one block, one bean. No leftovers! They are **the same**.
> Four and four — equal — even though the blocks take up more room."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** same number does not mean same size or
same spacing. Matching checks the number, not the looks.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds with fresh groups each time (keep groups to 10 or
fewer). Shuffle which side has more.

1. 2 counters vs. 6 counters — "Match them. Which has more? Which has
   fewer?" *Hint: "Push partners together and look for leftovers."*
2. 5 vs. 5 — "Match them. Any leftovers?" *(Error-watch: the learner
   may say "the same color has more" — re-match slowly together.)*
3. 7 vs. 4 — "Which has fewer?" (answer: the 4-group)
4. 1 vs. 9 — "Wow, big difference! Match and tell me." *(1 has fewer;
   9 has more.)*
5. 0 vs. 3 — empty tray vs. 3 counters. "Match what you can. The empty
   side has no partners at all — it has fewer. Zero is fewer than 3."
6. Learner builds: "Build a group that has more than my 3." (any group
   of 4–10; matching must prove it)

## Independent practice

The learner sets up two groups on their own (adult picks the numbers
secretly, e.g., 6 and 8), matches them, and announces: "This side has
more. This side has fewer." Two rounds. The adult only watches and
asks "how do you know?" — the learner points to the leftovers.

## Application

Snack comparison (real or pretend): two plates, one with 3 crackers and
one with 5. "If you want more crackers, which plate do you pick? Prove
it by matching." Then: "Share fairly — make the plates the same."
(The learner moves crackers until no leftovers either way. This previews
Objective 5; do not require the word *equal* yet.)

## Exit check

Three quick comparisons, matching only (no counting allowed this
time): 4 vs. 6; 8 vs. 8; 2 vs. 10. The learner must name more/fewer/
same correctly on at least 2 of 3, pointing to leftovers (or their
absence) as proof.

## Supports

- **Small numbers first:** if 7 vs. 4 is hard, drop to 2 vs. 3 until
  matching clicks, then climb back up.
- **Color-code partners:** use one red + one blue per pair so partners
  are visible; or draw a line (adult-scribed) joining each pair.
- **Movement variant:** the learner *is* a counter — stand children
  (or toys) in two lines and pair them off by holding hands.
- **Language support:** accept pointing + "this one" at first, then
  model the full sentence: "This side has more."

## Extension

"Build a group that has exactly 2 more than my 5." (7 — check by
matching: 2 leftovers.) Or: three groups — "put them in order from
fewest to most by matching, pair by pair."

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "spread out = more" and "bigger things = more."
Re-run Worked example 2 whenever it appears — spread/scrunch the same
two groups and re-match. Answer key:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
