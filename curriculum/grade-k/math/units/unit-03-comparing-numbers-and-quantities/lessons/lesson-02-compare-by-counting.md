# Lesson 02 — Compare by Counting

**Student goal:** I can count two groups, say both numbers, and tell
which number is greater and which is less.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**
**Objectives:** 2 (counting comparison), 1 (review)

## Materials

- 20 counters (mixed colors fine now — color is not the point)
- Two trays, numeral cards 1–10

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Show me a group with more than 3 and a group with fewer than 3, using
matching." If the learner matches and names more/fewer (Lesson 01),
proceed. If not, re-run one Lesson 01 round first.

## Teaching

Matching is great — but sometimes the groups are in a picture, or
already packed away, and we cannot pair them up. Then we **count**.

Count the first group. Count the second group. Now we have two numbers.
The bigger number is the group with **more**. The smaller number is
the group with **fewer**. Say both numbers every time: "6 and 4. Six
is more than four, so the 6-group has more."

New words today: **greater than** means "more than" for numbers, and
**less than** means "fewer than" for numbers. "6 is greater than 4. 4
is less than 6." Same comparison, number words.

## Worked example 1 — 6 buttons vs. 4 buttons

> "Count the first tray with me: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — six buttons.
> Count the second tray: 1, 2, 3, 4 — four buttons. Six and four.
> Which number is greater? Six. So the first tray has **more**. Four
> is **less than** six, so the second tray has **fewer**."

Then check by matching (push pairs together): 2 leftovers on the
6-side. "Counting and matching agree! Two ways, same answer."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the count is the proof when matching is
not possible. Always say both numbers before deciding.

## Worked example 2 — the spread-out trick, beaten by counting

The adult lays out 5 counters spread far apart and 8 counters bunched
tightly together.

> "Which looks like more? The spread-out row looks big! But looks can
> trick us. Count the spread-out row: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 — five. Count the
> bunched group: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 — eight. Eight is greater than
> five. The bunched group has **more**, even though it looks smaller."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** counting beats looking. When in doubt,
count — never guess from size or spacing.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. The learner counts both groups aloud, states both
numbers, then decides.

1. 3 vs. 9 — "Count both. Which is greater? Which is less?"
   *Hint: "Say the two numbers first: three and nine."*
2. 7 vs. 7 — "Count both. What do you notice?" *(Equal — same number.)*
3. 10 vs. 6 — "Count both. Which group has fewer?" (the 6-group)
4. Scattered 8 vs. lined-up 5 — use a keep-track strategy (move each
   counted counter aside) from Unit 02. "Count carefully, then compare."
5. Hidden round: the adult counts one group behind a screen and
   announces "I have 4." The learner counts their own group (6) and
   decides: "Mine has more — 6 is greater than 4." *(Error-watch: the
   learner may compare without counting their own group — insist on
   both numbers.)*
6. Learner builds: "Build a group that is less than my 8." (any group
   of 1–7; verify by counting both)

## Independent practice

Four comparison cards (adult-drawn or scribed): two groups of dots per
card — (2, 5), (9, 9), (10, 3), (6, 7). The learner counts each group,
writes or points to the counts (adult scribes if needed), and circles
the group with more (or draws a line under both if equal). The adult
checks only at the end.

## Application

Toy-basket check: two baskets of toys (or blocks) around the house.
"Count each basket. Which basket has more toys? Which has fewer? Tell
me the two numbers." If the baskets are equal, the learner announces
"the same!" — equal baskets get a cheer.

## Exit check

Three comparisons, counting only (groups covered after counting, so
matching cannot rescue a guess): 5 vs. 8; 10 vs. 10; 3 vs. 1. The
learner must state both counts and the correct more/fewer/equal call
on at least 2 of 3.

## Supports

- **Count together:** the adult counts aloud in chorus for the first
  two rounds, then fades to learner-only counting.
- **Numeral cards as anchors:** after counting, place the numeral card
  for each count beside its tray — the two cards make the comparison
  visible.
- **Keep-track help:** scattered groups get the move-aside strategy;
  lined-up groups get touch-counting.
- **Small steps:** if 10 vs. 6 overwhelms, work at 5 and under until
  "say both numbers, then decide" is automatic.

## Extension

Three-group ordering: "Count three groups (4, 9, 6). Put them in order
from fewest to most." Then: "How many more does the biggest group have
than the smallest?" (9 − 4 = 5 more — count the difference by matching
leftovers, or count on from 4 to 9.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: deciding from looks (spread/bigness) instead of
counting, and deciding from only one group's count ("this one is big"
without counting the other). Always require both numbers. Answer key:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
