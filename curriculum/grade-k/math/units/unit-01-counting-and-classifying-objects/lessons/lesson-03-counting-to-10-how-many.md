# Lesson 03 — Counting to 10: "How Many?"

**Student goal:** I can count up to 10 objects in a line or a circle and answer
"how many?"
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- 10 counters (linking cubes or blocks work well)
- A tray

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Count these 5 blocks" (Lesson 01 re-check). If one-to-one to 5 is not yet
secure, stay at 5–6 today and extend next session rather than pushing to 10.

## Teaching

When we finish counting, someone will ask **"how many?"** The answer is the
**last** counting word we said. That last word tells the total — this is
called **cardinality**: the last number word is the number of things.

Counting in a **circle** is trickier than in a line: there is no end to start
from. So we pick one object, remember it is the start — "I start here" — and
stop when we get back to it.

## Worked example 1 — seven bears in a line

The adult lines up 7 counters.

> "One, two, three, four, five, six, seven." *(touches each once)*
> "How many? The last word I said was **seven** — so there are **seven**
> bears."
> "Check:" *(recounts slowly)* "One... seven. Same answer: seven."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the last word is not just the last word — it is
the answer. Recounting and landing on the same last word proves the count.

## Worked example 2 — nine buttons in a circle

The adult arranges 9 buttons in a ring.

> "A circle has no end, so I choose a start. I start **here**." *(touches one
> button and keeps a finger near it)* "One, two, three, four, five, six,
> seven, eight, nine — stop! I am back at my start button."
> "How many? Nine. If I kept going I would count my start button twice."

Then the adult models the common error and fixes it: "Watch what happens if I
don't remember my start: 1, 2, 3... 9, 10, 11 — eleven? That can't be right,
I only put out nine buttons. I counted my start twice. Start-here, stop-here."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** in a circle, marking the start is the whole
trick. The error (counting past the start) is how we learn why the trick
matters.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. 6 counters in a line — count, then "how many?" *(Expected: "six.")*
   *Hint: "What was your last word?"*
2. 8 counters in a line — count and answer "how many?"
3. 7 buttons in a circle — "Show me your start button first." Count, stop at
   the start, answer "how many?"
4. 10 linking cubes snapped into a train — count the train. "How many cubes
   long is your train?" *(Expected: "ten.")*
5. Error analysis: the adult counts 6 blocks as "1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 — how many?
   Five!" Ask: "What did I say wrong?" *(Expected: "the last word was six, so
   it is six.")*
6. 9 counters in a circle — learner chooses the start, counts, answers.

## Independent practice

The learner builds a "10-train" from linking cubes, counts it, and answers
"how many cubes?" Then the adult rearranges the same 10 cubes into a circle;
the learner picks a start, counts, and answers "how many?" again. Same cubes,
same total — arrangement doesn't change "how many."

*Alternate response mode:* the learner points while the adult says the words
for the first round, then swaps roles; or the learner moves each counted
object into a "done" bowl.

## Application

Step counting: walk 10 steps together down a hallway, counting each step.
"How many steps to the door?" Then: "Let's walk back — how many steps back?"
(Usually also 10; if the learner miscounts, recount together.) Numbers measure
real walks.

## Exit check

The adult places 8 counters in a loose line. "Count them. How many?" Expected:
touches each once, counts to 8, answers "eight." Record: secure / developing /
not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** pair "how many?" with a gesture (open palms, shrug)
  until the phrase is familiar; accept the answer in the home language.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner holds up fingers to show the total
  instead of saying it; or draws that many dots on paper.
- **Floor-play variant:** count 10 beanbags tossed into a hoop one at a time —
  each toss is one touch, one word.

## Extension

Count 10 objects in a **scattered** arrangement (harder: no line, no circle
order — move each counted one aside). Or count two groups and say the two
totals: "4 reds and 6 blues."

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) answering "how many?" by recounting from 1
instead of stating the last word — practice "last word is the answer" as a
chant; (2) losing the start in a circle — use a sticker or a different-colored
"start" object; (3) rushing past 7–10 — the sequence 6–10 is where most K
counters wobble; slow down, don't add more objects.
