# Lesson 04 — Solving Story Problems to 10

**Goal (read aloud):** "I can solve a mixed addition story to 10 all
by myself, with objects or a drawing."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** 20 counters, paper and
crayons, ten frame

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Adult: "Tell me: is this a putting-together story or an adding-to
story? 'Mia has 3 pencils. Her friend gives her 2 more pencils. How
many pencils does Mia have now?'" (Adding-to: Mia already had
pencils, and more arrived.) Then: "Solve it with counters." (5.)
If the learner cannot yet tell the two story kinds apart, that is
fine — today both kinds are solved the same way: show the parts,
count all, say the sentence.

## Teaching (5 minutes)

Today the training wheels loosen: the adult tells the story once,
and the learner chooses how to solve it — counters, ten frame, or
drawing. Every solution has the same four steps, whatever the tool:

1. **Listen for the two parts.** Who or what is in the first group?
   Who or what joins?
2. **Show both parts.** Counters, ten-frame dots, or a drawing —
   each part shown separately first.
3. **Join and count all.** Push together, touch each one, count from
   1 — or count on from the first part if you are sure of it.
4. **Say the sentence.** "___ and ___ — ___ altogether." The adult
   may write it for you.

The story kind does not change the steps. Putting-together or
adding-to, the answer is always found by showing the parts and
counting the joined group.

One more tool today: the **ten frame** from Unit 04. For a story like
8 + 2, fill the ten frame with 8 dots, then add 2 more dots — the
frame is exactly full, which is a picture of 10 itself. A full ten
frame is a fast check: if the frame is full, the total is 10.

## Worked example 1 — A putting-together story to 8

Adult tells it once: "1 red balloon floats up. 7 blue balloons float
up beside it. How many balloons are there in all?"

- Step 1 — parts: first group 1 red balloon; joining group 7 blue
  balloons.
- Step 2 — show: 1 red counter apart from 7 blue counters.
- Step 3 — join and count all: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. (8 balloons.)
- Step 4 — say it: "1 red balloon and 7 blue balloons — 8 balloons
  altogether." Sentence: `1 + 7 = 8`.

Point out: the small group (1) came first this time, and that is
fine. Small-first stories are solved exactly like big-first stories.

## Worked example 2 — An adding-to story to 10, checked on the ten frame

Adult tells it once: "8 children are on the playground. 2 more
children come out to play. How many children are playing now?"

- Step 1 — parts: start group 8; newcomers 2.
- Step 2 — show: fill the ten frame with 8 dots, leaving 2 spaces.
- Step 3 — join: add the 2 newcomers into the last 2 spaces. The
  frame is now exactly full — 10.
- Step 4 — say it: "8 children, then 2 more children join — 10
  children now." Sentence: `8 + 2 = 10`.
- Check by counting all: 1 through 10. The full frame and the count
  agree.

Point out: 8 + 2 = 10 is the biggest story this unit asks for. If a
story ever totals more than 10, it belongs to a later unit — say so
honestly ("that one is bigger than 10, so we will save it") rather
than letting the learner count past the unit's range without a frame.

## Guided practice (adult tells each story once; learner chooses the tool)

(Solutions are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md).)

1. "3 birds sit on a fence. 6 more birds land on the fence. How many
   birds are on the fence now?" (Adding-to.)
2. "2 green frogs and 6 brown frogs sit by the pond. How many frogs
   are there altogether?" (Putting-together.)
3. "9 stickers are on a page. 1 more sticker is added. How many
   stickers are on the page now?" (Adding-to; ten-frame check.)
4. "4 yellow ducks and 4 white ducks swim together. How many ducks
   are swimming altogether?" (Putting-together.)

## Independent practice

1. The learner solves two new stories alone (adult tells, then stays
   quiet): "5 fish, then 4 more fish — how many fish now?" and
   "3 apples and 3 apples — how many apples altogether?" The learner
   picks counters, ten frame, or drawing for each.
2. Error-analysis story (adult acts it out deliberately wrong):
   "Watch me solve: 3 counters and 5 counters. I count: 1, 2, 3, 4,
   5, 6, 7, 8, 9 — I touched one counter twice! Is my answer of 9
   right? Show me the right count." The learner re-counts carefully
   (8) and explains what went wrong: counting one object twice
   makes the total too big even when the groups are right.

## Application

Real-room story hunt: the learner finds two real groups in the room
(e.g. 3 books on a shelf and 4 books on a table), tells the story,
solves it, and says the sentence. Two hunts, two sentences. The
groups must be countable and movable — no guessing from across the
room.

## Exit check

Adult tells one new mixed story, no hints: "6 spoons are in a drawer.
3 more spoons are put in. How many spoons are in the drawer now?"
The learner must show the parts, count all (9), and say the sentence
"6 plus 3 equals 9." This checks Objective 4.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** totals at 8 or below; counters only (no ten frame
  yet); adult re-tells the story while pointing at each group as it
  is built.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may draw instead of using
  counters, or answer by holding up the total on fingers after
  counting all.
- **Extension:** a two-wave story — "5 ducks, then 2 more join, then
  1 more joins. How many ducks now?" (8.) Or: invent a story for
  `2 + 6 = 8` and solve a partner's invented story.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner answers with one part (usually the
bigger one) because the story was heard but not shown. Fix: require
the showing step before any answer is accepted — "show me the two
parts first, then we count." Another trap: counting on from the
wrong start number in adding-to stories (see Lesson 02). Full
guidance is in the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md).
