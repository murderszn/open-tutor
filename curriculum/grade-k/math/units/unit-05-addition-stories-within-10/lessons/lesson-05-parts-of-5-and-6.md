# Lesson 05 — Parts of 5 and 6: One Number, Many Pairs

**Goal (read aloud):** "I can break 5 and 6 into two parts in more
than one way, and tell a story about a pair."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** 6 counters in two colors
(or 6 counters and a divider), paper and crayons

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Adult: "Show me 5 counters. Now push some to the left and some to
the right. How many on the left? How many on the right? How many in
all?" (Any split of 5; total still 5.) If the learner cannot yet
split a group and recount the total, replay Lesson 01 with a single
group split in two before starting.

## Teaching (5 minutes)

Until now, stories always started with two groups and asked for the
total. Today the story runs the other way: start with **one** number
and break it into **two parts**. The whole stays the same; only the
split changes.

Take 5 counters. Push 2 to the left and 3 to the right. Say it:
"5 is 2 and 3." Now push them back together and split them
differently: 4 to the left, 1 to the right. Say it: "5 is also 4 and
1." Same whole (5), two different pairs. That is what **decompose**
means in grown-up math: break a number into parts. The learner only
needs the words **part** and **whole**: "5 is the whole. 2 and 3 are
its parts. 4 and 1 are also its parts."

Written, a split looks like `5 = 2 + 3`. Read it: "5 equals 2 plus
3" — the whole first, then its parts. It is the same sentence as
before, read from the other end. The `=` still means "the same as":
5 is the same amount as 2 and 3 together.

The same game works for 6, and 6 has even more splits: 1 and 5, 2
and 4, 3 and 3. (0 and 6 is a split too, but this unit keeps both
parts at 1 or more so every part can be acted out.)

## Worked example 1 — Two ways to make 5

Adult shows 5 counters in a row.

- Split 1: push 2 left, 3 right. Count each part: 2, then 3. Count
  all: 5. Say it: "5 is 2 and 3." Sentence: `5 = 2 + 3`.
- Split 2: push all back together, then push 4 left, 1 right. Count
  each part: 4, then 1. Count all: 5. Say it: "5 is also 4 and 1."
  Sentence: `5 = 4 + 1`.
- Story for a pair: "5 birds sit in a tree. 2 birds sit on the low
  branch and 3 birds sit on the high branch. 2 and 3 — 5 birds in
  all."

Point out: both splits use the same 5 counters. Nothing was added or
taken away — the counters were only rearranged. If the total ever
stops being 5, a counter was lost or borrowed: recount.

## Worked example 2 — Three ways to make 6

Adult shows 6 counters in a row.

- Split 1: 3 left, 3 right. "6 is 3 and 3." Sentence: `6 = 3 + 3`.
  (The doubles split — both parts the same.)
- Split 2: 5 left, 1 right. "6 is also 5 and 1." Sentence:
  `6 = 5 + 1`.
- Split 3: 2 left, 4 right. "6 is also 2 and 4." Sentence:
  `6 = 2 + 4`.
- Story for a pair: "6 cookies are on a plate. 3 cookies have
  chocolate chips and 3 cookies are plain. 3 and 3 — 6 cookies in
  all."

Point out: 6 has three splits here, more than 5's two — bigger
numbers usually have more splits. And 3 + 3 is special: when both
parts are the same, the split is called **doubles**.

## Guided practice (learner splits; adult scribes sentences)

(Solutions are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md).)

1. Make 5: show a split that is NOT 2 and 3. Say the sentence.
   (Accept 1 and 4, or 4 and 1.)
2. Make 5: show a second, different split. Say the sentence.
3. Make 6: show the doubles split. Say the sentence.
4. Make 6: show a split that is NOT doubles. Say the sentence.
   (Accept 1 and 5, 5 and 1, 2 and 4, or 4 and 2.)

## Independent practice

1. Part-part story: the learner picks a split of 5 or 6, draws its
   two parts (two colors help), and dictates a story for it. The
   adult scribes the story and the sentence under the drawing.
2. "Same whole, new split" game: the adult names a whole (5 or 6)
   and a first part (e.g. "6, first part 2"). The learner finds the
   other part with counters (4) and says the full sentence
   (`6 = 2 + 4`). Three rounds.

## Application

Snack split (real objects): "Here are 6 crackers. Split them into
two plates in a way you like. Tell me the split as a sentence." Then:
"Now split them a different way. Tell me that sentence too." The
learner eats one plate's crackers only after both sentences are
said — the math comes first, the snack second.

## Exit check

Adult hands over 6 counters, no hints: "Show me two different ways
to split 6 into parts. Say each split as a sentence." The learner
must show two distinct splits (e.g. 3 + 3 and 5 + 1), count each
part, and say both sentences. This checks Objective 5 (for 6; 5 is
checked in Practice E).

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** use two colors (e.g. red counters for the left part,
  blue for the right) so the parts stay visible after the split;
  work with 5 only until two splits of 5 are fluent.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may draw the split instead of
  moving counters, or point at the two parts while the adult says
  the sentence.
- **Extension:** find a third split of 6 (all three: 1 + 5, 2 + 4,
  3 + 3); try a first split of 7 (not assessed — 7's splits are
  previewed here and taught fully in later work); or tell a story
  for `5 = 1 + 4` with the parts swapped (`5 = 4 + 1`) and explain
  why both tell the same story.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner changes the whole while splitting
— e.g. starts with 6, splits into 2 and 3, and calls it "6" without
recounting (2 + 3 = 5, a counter was dropped). Fix: the count-all
check after every split, no exceptions. Another trap: thinking there
is only one right split ("5 is 2 and 3, and that's it"). Fix: ask
"can you split it a different way?" before accepting any split as
finished. Full guidance is in the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md).
