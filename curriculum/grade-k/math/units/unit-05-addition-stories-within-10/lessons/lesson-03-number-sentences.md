# Lesson 03 — Saying It as a Number Sentence

**Goal (read aloud):** "I can say an addition story as a number
sentence, like '4 plus 3 equals 7.'"
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** 20 counters, paper, crayon,
number line 0–10

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Adult: "Act out this story: 2 frogs on a log, then 3 more frogs hop
over. How many frogs altogether?" (5.) The learner should be able to
act out and count a join story before learning its written shorthand.
If not, replay Lesson 01 or 02 first — sentences never replace
acting things out.

## Teaching (5 minutes)

So far every story has been told in words: "4 red apples and 3 green
apples — 7 apples altogether." Grown-up math has a short way to write
that same story. It is called a **number sentence**, and it has three
parts:

- The first group: **4**
- The joining word, written as a cross: **+** (read "plus")
- The second group: **3**
- The total word, written as two lines: **=** (read "equals")
- The total: **7**

All together: `4 + 3 = 7`, read as "4 plus 3 equals 7."

Read it like a sentence, left to right, pointing at each part. The
`+` means "and more join." The `=` means "this is the same as" — the
groups on the left, joined, are the same amount as the number on the
right. At this grade the learner says the sentence and points to its
parts; the adult writes it. Reading a sentence the learner did not
write is real math work, and writing sentences comes later.

Two sentences can tell the same story in a different order. "3 green
apples and 4 red apples" is `3 + 4 = 7` — same apples, same total.
The order of the two groups does not change the total. (Adults call
this the commutative property; the learner only needs: "same groups,
either order, same total.")

## Worked example 1 — From story to sentence

Adult acts out: "2 frogs sit on a log. 4 more frogs hop over. How
many frogs altogether?"

- Act it: 2 counters, then 4 more join; count all — 6 frogs.
- Say it: "2 frogs and 4 more frogs — 6 frogs altogether."
- Write it (adult writes, learner watches and points): `2 + 4 = 6`.
- Read it together, pointing at each part: "2… plus… 4… equals… 6."
- Check: does the sentence match the counters? 2 frogs, plus 4 frogs,
  equals 6 frogs. Yes.

Point out: the sentence is a shortcut for the story, not a replacement
for it. If the sentence ever looks wrong, go back to the counters.

## Worked example 2 — From sentence to story

Adult writes `5 + 5 = 10` and reads it: "5 plus 5 equals 10."

- Make up a story together: "5 children play ball. 5 more children
  join the game. How many children are playing now?"
- Act it: 5 counters, then 5 more; count all — 10.
- Say it: "5 children and 5 more children — 10 children altogether."
- Check: the sentence says 5 plus 5 equals 10, and the counters say
  10. They agree.

Point out: a sentence can be read two ways — as a story (children
joining a game) and as a count (5, then 5 more, is 10). Both readings
are correct, and switching between them is the skill.

## Guided practice (adult leads)

For items 1–2, the adult tells a story; the learner acts it out, says
the sentence, and the adult writes it for the learner to read back,
pointing at each part. For items 3–4, the adult writes a sentence;
the learner makes up a story, acts it out, and checks it.
(Solutions are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md).)

1. Story: "3 stars shine. 2 more stars appear. How many stars
   altogether?" Say the sentence; read it back from the adult's
   writing.
2. Story: "6 ants march. 1 more ant joins. How many ants now?" Say
   the sentence; read it back.
3. Sentence: `1 + 7 = 8`. Make up a story, act it out, check by
   counting all.
4. Sentence: `4 + 4 = 8`. Make up a story, act it out, check by
   counting all.

## Independent practice

1. The learner draws two groups (each 1–5), the adult writes the
   sentence the learner dictates, and the learner reads it back,
   pointing at each part.
2. Sentence sort: the adult writes `3 + 2 = 5` and `2 + 3 = 5` on two
   cards. The learner acts out one story (3 counters, then 2 more)
   and says which sentences match it — and why both do.

## Application

Story-card game: the adult prepares 4 story cards and 4 sentence
cards (matching pairs, totals to 8). The learner draws a story card,
acts it out, and finds the sentence card that tells the same story.
Play until all pairs are matched; a mismatch is fixed by acting the
story out again, not by guessing.

## Exit check

Adult acts out a new story with counters (no sentence shown): "4
beads, then 3 more beads. How many beads altogether?" The learner
counts all (7), says the sentence "4 plus 3 equals 7," and points to
each part as the adult writes `4 + 3 = 7`. This checks Objective 3.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** use only the words "plus" and "equals" aloud for the
  first rounds; introduce the written `+` and `=` one at a time, and
  always keep the counters beside the written sentence.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may point to the two groups
  and then to the total pile while the adult says the sentence, then
  repeat just the total.
- **Extension:** the learner writes the numerals (not the symbols)
  of a dictated sentence; or tells the same story with the groups
  swapped (`3 + 4 = 7` and `4 + 3 = 7`) and explains why both work.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: reading `=` as "the answer comes next" rather
than "both sides are the same amount" — harmless here, but it makes
later sentences like `5 = 2 + 3` (Lesson 05) confusing, so use the
"same as" language from the start. Another trap: the learner reads
the sentence's numbers without acting anything out and trusts a
remembered fact over a count — always keep counters on the table for
this lesson. Full guidance is in the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md).
