# Lesson 04 — Writing Teen Numerals

**Student goal:** I can write the numerals 11–19, ten first, extras second.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 11 of 16**
**Objectives:** 4 (read/write numerals 11–19; match numeral to build)

## Materials

- Paper, thick crayon or marker, numeral cards 11–19
- Two ten-frame mats, 20 counters

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Show a build: full frame + 6 extras. "What number?" (Sixteen.) Show
the numeral card 16. "Does this card match the build? How do you
know?" If the learner connects 16 to ten-and-six (Lesson 03), proceed.
If the numeral looks arbitrary to the learner, replay Lesson 03's
worked example 1 before writing anything.

## Teaching

Now we write the teen numbers — and there is one rule that saves us
from the trickiest mistake in kindergarten math.

**Write the ten first.** Every teen number starts with a 1, because
every teen number has one ten. Then write the extras digit. Fourteen?
Think: "Ten and four more." Write 1 (the ten), then 4 (the extras):
**14**. Not 41. Your ears hear "four" first in "four-teen," but your
pencil writes the ten first.

Try it in the air first: big 1, then the extras digit. Say the
sentence while you write: "Ten and four more — one, four — fourteen."

The special names need care too:

- Eleven (11) = ten and 1 — it does not sound like "ten-one," but it is.
- Twelve (12) = ten and 2 — same story.
- Thirteen (13) = ten and 3. Say "thir-teen" slowly: it hides "three"!
- Fifteen (15) = ten and 5. Say "fif-teen": it hides "five"!

For 16–19 the names say it plainly: six-teen, seven-teen, eight-teen,
nine-teen.

## Worked example 1 — writing 14 (and catching 41)

The adult builds 14 (frame + 4 extras), then deliberately writes 41 on
paper.

> "Hmm. I built fourteen — ten and four more. But look at what I
> wrote: 4, then 1. Read it: forty-one! That is a different number —
> four tens and one, way bigger than our group. My ears tricked me: I
> heard 'four' first and wrote it first. Fix it: ten first — 1 — then
> the extras — 4. **14**. Fourteen. Now the writing matches the build."

The learner then writes 14 beside the build, saying "ten and four
more" as they write 1, then 4.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** writing is checked against the build, not
against the sound of the word. Build first, then write, then check.

## Worked example 2 — writing 17 from a build

The adult builds 17 (frame + 7 extras) and asks the learner to write
it, narrating each step:

> "How many in the frame? Ten — so the first digit is 1. How many
> extras? Seven — so the second digit is 7. Write: 1, 7. Seventeen!
> Read it back: 'Ten and seven more make seventeen.' The writing
> matches the build — done."

Then the reverse: the adult writes 12 and asks, "Build this number."
The learner makes a frame + 2 extras. "Ten and two more make twelve."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the two directions — build → write, and
write → build — must agree. If they disagree, the build is the referee.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. The learner always builds first (or looks at a
build), then writes, then reads back the sentence.

1. Build 13 → write it. *(13. Hint: "Ten first — what digit?")*
2. Build 19 → write it. *(19.)*
3. Build 11 → write it; say why the name is tricky. *(11; "eleven
   does not say 'ten-one,' but it means ten and one.")*
4. Adult writes 15 → learner builds it, then reads the sentence.
   *(Frame + 5; "Ten and five more make fifteen.")*
5. Adult writes 41 → learner says: "That is forty-one, not fourteen!
   Fourteen is 14." *(Reversal check — praise the catch.)*
6. Dictation: adult says "eighteen" → learner writes 18 without a
   build, then builds it to check. *(18 = frame + 8.)*

## Independent practice (adult watches, no hints)

The learner writes the numerals 11–19 in order on a strip of paper
(adult scribes the number names beside them if the learner wants a
word bank). Then the learner circles the numeral that matches a build
the adult shows (frame + 3 extras → circle 13).

## Application

"Mailbox numbers": the adult says the learner's "house" is number 17.
"Write your house number on an envelope. Now your friend lives at 14.
Write that too. Which house number has more extras — 17 or 14?"
(17 has 7 extras; 14 has 4. Both have one ten, so the extras decide.)

## Exit check

Dictation, no build shown first: "Write 16. Write 12. Write 19." Then
the learner builds 16 to prove the first one. All three numerals
correct, ten first, = ready for Lesson 05.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** dotted-line numeral models to trace; a "1 first" arrow
  card on the table; write in sand, on a whiteboard, or with a finger
  in the air before paper.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner points to the correct numeral
  card from a set (11–19) instead of writing; adult scribes the chosen
  numeral.
- **Extension:** write the equation beside the numeral: "14 = 10 + 4."
  Then: "Write 20. How is it different?" (Two tens — 2 first, not 1.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: reversal (41 for 14, 31 for 13, 21 for 12) is
normal at this age and is not a sign of a problem — it is the sound of
the word fighting the writing rule. Correct it the same way every
time: point at the build, say "ten first," rewrite together. Never
punish or drill reversals; the frame habit dissolves them.
