# Lesson 01 — Do These Rhyme?

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can hear when two words rhyme — when they
sound the same at the end."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** none required; a hand drum or
wooden spoon on a pot for the beat (optional). The adult's voice is the
main material.
**Standards:** RF.K.2, RF.K.2.a; SL.K.1.a (talk rules).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Sing or chant a short original rhyme slowly, with a clear beat:

> "The cat in the hat / sat on the mat."

Ask: "Did you hear any words that sounded the same at the end?" Accept any
noticing — *cat/hat*, *hat/mat*, or just "they sounded funny." Do not
correct yet; you are mapping what the ear already hears.

## Explicit teaching

Words can **rhyme** — that means they sound the same at the **end**. Say
*cat*. Now say *hat*. Hear it? *Cat ... hat.* The ends match: "-at, -at."
Your mouth makes almost the same shape at the end of both words.

Rhymes hide in songs and poems. When you hear the same ending twice, your
ears can catch it — like catching a ball. We are going to train our ears
to catch rhymes.

A word rhymes with another word when the **end sounds match**. It does not
matter what the beginning sounds like: *cat* and *hat* start differently
but rhyme. *Cat* and *dog* do **not** rhyme — the ends sound different.

### Modeled example 1 — catching a rhyme

*Adult says the pair slowly, stretching the endings, and thinks aloud:*
"Listen: *sun ... fun*. Hmm. *Sunnn ... funnn.* The ends sound the same —
'-un, -un.' My mouth does the same thing at the end. **Those rhyme!**" *(Adult
claps once, the 'rhyme catch' signal.)*

"Now listen: *sun ... moon*. *Sunnn ... mooon.* The ends are different —
'-un' and '-oon.' **Those do not rhyme.**" *(Adult shakes head, no clap.)*

### Modeled example 2 — rhyme in a song line

*Adult sings or chants slowly:*
> "A frog on a log in the fog."

"Did you hear rhymes? *Frog ... log ... fog.* Three words, all ending
'-og'! Every time I hear a rhyme, I catch it." *(Clap on each rhyme word.)*
"Rhyme words are like a family — they match at the end."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Do *pig* and *wig* rhyme? Listen: *piggg ... wiggg.*" (Yes — clap the
   catch together.)
2. "Do *bed* and *red* rhyme?" (Yes.)
3. "Do *fish* and *dish* rhyme?" (Yes.)
4. "Do *ball* and *dog* rhyme? Listen carefully: *ballll ... doggg.*"
   (No — ends differ.)
5. "Do *cake* and *snake* rhyme?" (Yes — '-ake.')

If the learner hesitates, stretch the endings yourself and invite them to
watch your mouth: "Watch my mouth at the end — same shape or different?"

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

The learner answers with the rhyme-catch clap (yes) or a head shake (no).
Speech is welcome but not required.

1. *bee / tree* (Yes)
2. *mop / top* (Yes)
3. *pan / moon* (No)
4. *box / fox* (Yes)
5. *sun / cup* (No — tricky: same beginning sound /s/, different endings;
   praise careful listening)

## Application

"Be the teacher." The learner picks any two words (real words, or asks the
adult for a pair) and teaches a stuffed animal whether they rhyme,
using the clap-or-shake signal and saying why: "They rhyme — same at the
end!" or "No rhyme — different ends." The adult scribes the learner's
explanation.

## Exit check

1. "Do *cat* and *bat* rhyme?" (Yes — clap.)
2. "Do *pig* and *sun* rhyme?" (No — shake.)
3. "What makes words rhyme?" (They sound the same at the end — any
   wording accepted.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** teach *same/different* with objects first (two red
  crayons = same; red and blue = different), then apply to word endings.
  Home-language rhyme pairs are welcome alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** clap for "yes, rhymes," shake head for "no";
  point to a smiley/frowny card; no speech required.
- **Seated-table variant:** all work at the table; the drum marks each
  word pair.
- **Floor-play variant:** "rhyme hop" — hop forward on *yes*, freeze on
  *no*.
- **Extension:** the learner names the shared ending ("-at!", "-og!");
  try a near-rhyme discussion ("Do *cat* and *cut* rhyme? Almost — but the
  middle is different, so no").

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *Same beginning = rhyme:* many children hear the matching /s/ in
  *sun/cup* and call it a rhyme. Name it plainly: "The beginnings match,
  but rhymes match at the **end**. Listen to the ends." This is the most
  important correction in the lesson — do not skip item 5 of independent
  practice.
- *Rhyme must mean something:* children may reject real rhymes ("that's
  not a word!") or accept non-rhymes that look related. Rhyming is about
  **sound**, not meaning — say so explicitly.
- *Clapping without listening:* if the learner claps on rhythm rather than
  on rhymes, slow down and separate the words with a full breath between
  them.
