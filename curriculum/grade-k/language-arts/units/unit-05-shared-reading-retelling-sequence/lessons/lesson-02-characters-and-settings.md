# Lesson 02 — Characters and Settings

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell who is in a story and where the
story happens."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** the story *Ana Plants a
Seed* below (adult reads aloud), picture cards or simple drawings of
Ana, Ben, a seed packet, a garden, a house (for the sort in guided
practice).
**Standards:** RL.K.3; SL.K.1.a (talk rules).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Quick recall of Milo: "Who was in yesterday's story? Where did it
happen?" (Milo and Biscuit; the snowy path to Grandma's house.)
Then: "Today we meet a new story. Listen for the *who* and the
*where* — those are our two jobs today."

## The story — *Ana Plants a Seed* (original, written for this unit)

> Ana had a packet of sunflower seeds. She went out to her garden
> with her friend Ben. "Let's plant a seed," said Ana.
>
> Ana dug a little hole in the soil. She dropped one seed in. Ben
> poured water from a blue watering can. "Grow, little seed,
> grow," said Ana.
>
> Every day, Ana came back to look. One morning she saw a tiny green
> sprout! The sprout grew taller and taller. At the end of summer,
> a big yellow sunflower stood in the garden. Ana and Ben smiled
> up at it. "We grew that," said Ben.

## Explicit teaching

Every story has **characters** — the people or animals the story is
about — and a **setting** — where (and, for older readers, when) the
story happens. A quick way to remember:

- **Character** = *who*. Ask: "Who is doing things in the story?"
- **Setting** = *where*. Ask: "Where are they?"

The seed, the watering can, and the sunflower are important, but
they are not characters: they do not talk, choose, or feel. They
are *things in the setting*. Ben is a character even though he
speaks only twice — a character does not have to talk a lot. And a
story can have more than one setting, but this story stays in one
place: Ana's garden.

### Modeled example 1 — characters and setting in Milo's story

*Adult thinks aloud:*

"Yesterday's story: **who**? Milo — he walks and loses a mitten.
Biscuit — she finds it and brings it back. Both do things, so both
are characters. **Where**? On a snowy path, on the way to Grandma's
house. That is the setting. The mitten is not a character. It does
not choose anything — it just falls!"

### Modeled example 2 — characters and setting in Ana's story

*Adult reads Ana's story once, then thinks aloud:*

"**Who** is doing things? Ana — she digs, plants, and looks every
day. Ben — he pours the water and smiles at the end. Two
characters. **Where** are they? In Ana's garden, all the way
through. The seed packet is a thing they use. The sunflower is what
grows. The garden is the setting — the place where everything
happens."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Who are the characters in Ana's story?" (Ana and Ben.)
2. "Where does Ana's story happen?" (In Ana's garden.)
3. Sort the picture cards: put *who* cards (Ana, Ben) in one pile
   and *where/things* cards (garden, seed packet, watering can) in
   another. The adult asks about each card: "Is this a *who* or a
   *where*?"
4. "What does Ana do in the story?" (Plants a seed, waters it with
   Ben, looks every day.) "What does Ben do?" (Pours the water;
   smiles at the sunflower.)
5. Compare, gently: "In Milo's story, who was the animal character?
   In Ana's story, is there an animal character?" (Biscuit; no —
   Ana's characters are both people.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Name the characters of Ana's story without help. (Ana, Ben.)
2. Name the setting of Ana's story without help. (Ana's garden.)
3. The adult shows the garden card and the Ben card and asks: "Which
   one is a character? Which one is the setting?" (Ben; the garden.)
4. "Tell me one thing Ana does and one thing Ben does." (Any true
   action each: Ana digs/plants/looks; Ben pours water/smiles.)
5. "Is the sunflower a character? Why or why not?" (No — it does
   not talk, choose, or do things on purpose; it grows.)

## Application

"New friend in the garden." The learner imagines one new character
visiting Ana's garden — a neighbor, a cat, a bird — and tells the
adult: who the new character is, what they would do there, and
whether the setting changes. (It does not: the garden stays the
setting.) The adult scribes one sentence: "___ came to Ana's
garden." This is the seed of Lesson 06's comparison work: changing
the *who* changes the adventure, but the *where* can stay the same.

## Exit check

1. "Who are the characters in *Ana Plants a Seed*?" (Ana and Ben.)
2. "Where does the story happen?" (In Ana's garden.)
3. "Is the watering can a character?" (No — it is a thing.)
4. "Who was the animal character in Milo's story?" (Biscuit.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** *who* = point to a person picture; *where* =
  point to a place picture. The two piles in guided practice stay
  visible all lesson as a word-free anchor.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the correct picture card
  instead of naming; act out Ana digging or Ben pouring instead of
  describing it.
- **Seated-table variant:** cards sorted on the table into two
  labeled piles (a person drawing and a house/garden drawing as
  labels, not words alone).
- **Floor-play variant:** two hoops or tape circles on the floor —
  step into the *who* hoop or the *where* hoop as the adult names
  cards.
- **Extension:** the learner names the setting of a familiar book
  from home ("Where does *that* story happen?") and one character
  who is an animal and one who is a person.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

The most common error is calling every named thing a character —
especially the sunflower, because it "grows" and feels alive. Hold
the line gently with the test: "Does it talk, choose, or do things
on purpose?" Growth alone is not character action at this level.
The second error is naming the *first* place mentioned as the
setting when a story moves; Ana's story stays put on purpose, so
this lesson builds the habit cleanly before later units move stories
around.
