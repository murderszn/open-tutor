# Lesson 01 — Ask and Answer Questions About a Story

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can listen to a story and ask and answer
questions about the important parts."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** the story *Milo and the
Lost Mitten* below (adult reads aloud), a familiar picture book from
home for the author/illustrator talk, the talking stick (optional).
**Standards:** RL.K.1; SL.K.2; RL.K.6 (author/illustrator roles).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Ask: "What do we do when someone is reading to us?" (Listen, look at
the pictures, wait for a turn to talk.) Then: "Today I will read a
story twice. The first time, just listen. The second time, I will
stop and ask you questions — and you can ask me questions, too."

## The story — *Milo and the Lost Mitten* (original, written for this unit)

> Milo put on his red coat, his red hat, and his two blue mittens.
> He was walking to Grandma's house in the snow. His dog Biscuit
> walked beside him, wagging her tail.
>
> On the snowy path, one blue mitten slipped off Milo's hand. Milo
> did not see it fall. He kept walking. Biscuit stopped. She sniffed
> the snow. She picked up the blue mitten in her mouth.
>
> Biscuit ran to Milo and dropped the mitten at his feet. Milo
> laughed. "Thank you, Biscuit!" he said. He put the mitten back on.
> Then Milo and Biscuit walked on together to Grandma's house.

## Explicit teaching

A **key detail** is a part of a story you cannot leave out, or the
story stops making sense. If you leave out the mitten, there is no
story at all! The color of Milo's hat is a nice detail, but the
story still works if you forget it. Today we practice finding the
key details by asking questions.

Question words help us find key details:

- **Who** — who is in the story? (Milo and Biscuit.)
- **What** — what happened? (A mitten fell off.)
- **Where** — where did it happen? (On the snowy path to Grandma's
  house.)
- **When** — when did it happen? (On the walk, before they got to
  Grandma's house.)
- **Why** — why did it matter? (Milo needed both mittens to stay
  warm, and Biscuit helped him.)
- **How** — how did it end? (Biscuit brought the mitten back, and
  Milo said thank you.)

Every book also has two makers, and they have different jobs. The
**author** writes the words. The **illustrator** draws the pictures.
Show the familiar picture book's cover. Point to the author's name
and the illustrator's name if both are printed, and say: "The author
wrote these words. The illustrator drew these pictures. In our
stories, I am reading the words, and we will look at pictures
together — but the jobs are still different."

### Modeled example 1 — the adult asks, the adult answers, thinking aloud

*Adult reads the story once, then thinks aloud:*

"Now I ask myself a question. **Who** is this story about? Milo —
and his dog Biscuit. That is a key detail, because without Milo
there is no story. **What** happened? A blue mitten fell off. That
is key too. **Where** were they? On the snowy path to Grandma's
house. If I ask a question and the story answers it, I found a key
detail."

### Modeled example 2 — asking a question that is NOT a key detail

*Adult thinks aloud:*

"Here is another question: What color was Milo's hat? Red. Is that
a key detail? If his hat were green, would the story still work?
Yes — the mitten would still fall, and Biscuit would still bring it
back. So hat color is a detail, but not a *key* detail. Good
listeners keep both kinds, but they tell the key ones first."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

The adult reads the story a second time, stopping at the marked
places. The learner answers orally or by pointing; the adult scribes
nothing here — this is talk.

1. After paragraph 1, the adult asks: "Who is walking to Grandma's
   house?" and "What is Milo wearing on his hands?" (Milo and
   Biscuit; two blue mittens.)
2. After paragraph 2, the adult asks: "What fell off?" and "Who
   found it?" (One blue mitten; Biscuit.)
3. After paragraph 3, the adult asks: "How did the story end?"
   (Biscuit brought the mitten back; Milo said thank you and put
   it on.)
4. The learner asks the adult one question about the story, using a
   question word. (Any story question counts; the adult answers it
   from the story and names the question word used.)
5. Author/illustrator: the adult holds up the familiar picture book
   and asks, "Who wrote the words?" and "Who drew the pictures?"
   (The adult names the people if the cover does not, and the
   learner repeats each job: "The author writes the words. The
   illustrator draws the pictures.")

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. "Tell me one key detail from the beginning of the story."
   (Milo and Biscuit walking to Grandma's house in the snow.)
2. "Tell me one key detail from the middle." (The mitten fell off,
   and Biscuit picked it up.)
3. "Tell me one key detail from the end." (Biscuit brought it back;
   Milo said thank you.)
4. "Ask me a question about the story." (Any *who/what/where/when/
   why/how* question about Milo; the adult answers and praises the
   question word.)
5. Point to the picture book and say which job is which: "Show me
   the words the author wrote. Show me a picture the illustrator
   drew." (Pointing to text vs. picture.)

## Application

"Reporter." The learner pretends to be a reporter and asks the adult
three questions about Milo — one *who*, one *what*, and one
*where* question. The adult answers only from the story. Then they
switch: the adult asks, the learner answers. If a question cannot be
answered from the story ("What is Grandma's dog's name?"), the adult
says so plainly: "The story does not tell us that. Good reporters
notice what a story does *not* say, too."

## Exit check

1. "Who found the lost mitten?" (Biscuit.)
2. "Where did the mitten fall?" (On the snowy path.)
3. "Who writes the words in a book?" (The author.) "Who draws the
   pictures?" (The illustrator.)
4. "Is Milo's red hat a key detail? Why or why not?" (No — the
   story still works without it; the mitten is the key detail.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** question words are shown with gestures —
  point to a person for *who*, point around the room for *where*,
  shrug-and-ask for *why*. Home-language questions are welcome; the
  adult translates the question word, not the whole answer.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to a picture card (Milo,
  Biscuit, mitten, path) instead of speaking; the adult says the
  answer aloud and the learner confirms.
- **Seated-table variant:** story cards on the table; the learner
  touches the card that answers each question.
- **Floor-play variant:** the learner walks a masking-tape "snowy
  path" on the floor while the adult reads, stopping at question
  spots.
- **Extension:** the learner invents one more question the story
  does *not* answer ("What did Grandma say when they arrived?") and
  the adult writes it on a "Wonder Questions" list for later stories.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

Learners often answer with the *last* thing they heard, or with a
favorite detail ("He had a red hat!") instead of a key detail. Do
not correct by saying "wrong." Ask: "If we take that part out, does
the story still work?" That test — used in Modeled example 2 — is
the whole skill, and it transfers to every later unit. Also watch
for author/illustrator confusion: at this age "the person who made
the book" is one job in the learner's mind. Keep the two jobs
physically separate: two hands, two names, two actions (writing
fingers vs. drawing fingers).
