# Lesson 03 — Events in Order: First, Next, Last

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can put what happened in a story in
order — first, next, last."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** three Milo sequence cards
(adult-made before the session: 1 — Milo walking in snow with
Biscuit; 2 — the blue mitten on the snowy ground; 3 — Biscuit
bringing the mitten back to Milo), three word cards reading *first*,
*next*, *last* (adult reads them aloud; the learner is not asked to
decode them alone).
**Standards:** RL.K.3 (major events); RL.K.2 (retell with key
details, as the ordering supports it).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Lay the three Milo cards face up in a mixed-up order. Ask: "Do
these pictures belong to Milo's story? Can you tell which one
happened first just by looking?" Let the learner try, without
correcting yet. Then: "Today we learn the words that put a story in
order."

## Explicit teaching

Stories happen in order, and retellers use order words so listeners
can follow:

- **First** — what happened at the beginning.
- **Next** (or **then**) — what happened in the middle, after the
  first thing.
- **Last** — what happened at the end.

An **event** is something that happens in the story — not a person,
not a place, not a thing. "Milo walks to Grandma's house" is an
event. "The mitten" is not an event; "the mitten falls off" is.
Milo's story has three big events:

1. **First:** Milo and Biscuit walk through the snow to Grandma's
   house.
2. **Next:** The blue mitten slips off, and Biscuit picks it up.
3. **Last:** Biscuit brings the mitten back, and Milo says thank you.

Notice the middle event has two parts that belong together: the
mitten falls *and* Biscuit finds it. If you split them, the story
breaks — Biscuit cannot bring back a mitten that never fell. Events
that cause each other stay together in the same step.

### Modeled example 1 — ordering Milo's cards, thinking aloud

*Adult picks up the three cards in mixed order and thinks aloud:*

"This card shows Biscuit giving Milo the mitten. Did that happen
first? No — Milo has to lose the mitten before Biscuit can bring it
back. So this card goes **last**. This card shows the mitten on the
ground. That happened **next**, after they started walking. And
this card — Milo and Biscuit walking in the snow — that is
**first**. First: walking. Next: mitten falls, Biscuit finds it.
Last: Biscuit brings it back."

### Modeled example 2 — catching a wrong order

*Adult deliberately lays the cards: mitten on ground, walking,
Biscuit bringing it back, and thinks aloud:*

"First: the mitten is on the ground? But wait — whose mitten is it?
Milo has not even started walking yet in this order! The walking
has to come first, because that is when the mitten falls. Let me fix
it: first walking, next the mitten falls, last Biscuit brings it
back. If the order is wrong, the story stops making sense — and
that is how you know."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. The adult mixes the three Milo cards; the learner puts them in
   order and says *first*, *next*, *last* for each. (Adult re-reads
   the matching story sentence for any card the learner hesitates
   on.)
2. The learner tells Milo's story in order using only the cards as
   prompts — one sentence per card. (Adult supplies a word only if
   the learner stalls.)
3. The adult tells Milo's story in the wrong order (last event
   first) and asks: "Did that sound right? What is wrong?"
   (The order is wrong; Biscuit cannot return a mitten that has
   not fallen yet.)
4. The adult names three Ana events out of order — "the sunflower
   stands tall," "Ana plants the seed," "a sprout comes up" — and
   the learner puts them in order with *first*, *next*, *last*.
   (First plant, next sprout, last sunflower.)
5. The learner picks one event from Milo's story and says whether
   it is first, next, or last, and why. ("Biscuit brings the mitten
   back is last, because the story ends there.")

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Order the three Milo cards without help, saying *first*,
   *next*, *last*.
2. "What happened first in Milo's story?" (Milo and Biscuit walked
   to Grandma's house in the snow.)
3. "What happened next?" (The mitten fell off and Biscuit picked
   it up.)
4. "What happened last?" (Biscuit brought it back; Milo said thank
   you and they walked on.)
5. The adult shows the middle card alone and asks: "Is this first,
   next, or last? How do you know?" (Next — it happens after the
   walk starts and before the mitten comes back.)

## Application

"My morning in order." The learner tells three real events from
their own morning in order — for example, "First I woke up. Next I
ate breakfast. Last I came to read with you." The adult scribes the
three sentences under *first / next / last*. This proves the order
words are not just for stories: they are for any telling. (W.K.8
connection: recalling information from experience to answer the
question "What did you do this morning, in order?")

## Exit check

1. Put the three Milo cards in order. (Walking; mitten falls and
   Biscuit finds it; Biscuit brings it back.)
2. "What word do we say for the beginning?" (*First*.) "For the
   end?" (*Last*.)
3. "Is 'the mitten' an event, or is 'the mitten falls off' an
   event?" (The mitten falls off — an event is something that
   happens.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** the order words are paired with gestures —
  hold up one finger (*first*), two (*next*), three (*last*); the
  learner can answer with fingers alone.
- **Alternate response mode:** arrange the cards silently, then nod
  while the adult narrates the order; acting out the three events
  in order counts as a full response.
- **Seated-table variant:** cards in a row left to right; the
  learner slides them into place.
- **Floor-play variant:** three tape spots on the floor labeled
  with the word cards; the learner steps to each spot and acts out
  that event.
- **Extension:** the learner orders *four* events: split Milo's
  middle event into "mitten falls" and "Biscuit picks it up," and
  explains why the split order still works (fall must come before
  pickup) — an early taste of cause and effect.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

Two errors dominate. First, learners order by *preference* ("I like
the dog part, so it goes first") rather than by story logic; the
"can this happen before that?" test from Modeled example 2 is the
fix. Second, learners split cause-and-effect pairs apart ("Biscuit
finds it" before "it falls") — keep paired events on one card in
this unit on purpose, and name why. Do not introduce four- or
five-card sequences until the three-card order is solid; the
assessment uses three cards for exactly this reason.
