# Lesson 05 — What Pictures Tell Us

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can look at a picture and tell what
moment of the story it shows."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** the generated illustration
[`../assets/milo-lost-mitten.png`](../assets/milo-lost-mitten.png)
shown on a screen or printed large, drawing paper and crayons, the
story *Sam's Rainy-Day Picnic* below (adult reads aloud at the end
of the lesson for the draw-and-tell practice).
**Standards:** RL.K.7; RL.K.1 (questions about the picture's story).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Show the generated illustration, covered except for the dog and the
mitten. Ask: "What do you see? What do you think is happening?"
Uncover the rest. Then: "Today we learn to read pictures the way we
read stories — by asking what moment they show, and what they do
*not* show."

## Explicit teaching

An **illustration** is a picture in a story. The illustrator draws
it, and it works together with the words: sometimes it shows exactly
what the words say, and sometimes it shows something the words never
say — like how a character feels from their face.

To "read" a picture, ask three questions:

1. **Who and what do I see?** Name the people, animals, and things.
2. **What moment is this?** Is it the beginning, the middle, or the
   end of the story?
3. **What can I guess?** What probably happened just before, or
   will happen just after? A guess from a picture is fine — but say
   it as a guess ("I think..."), not as a fact from the story.

What a picture does *not* show matters too. Our picture does not
show Grandma, and it does not show the mitten falling. That does not
mean those parts are unimportant — it means one picture can only
hold one moment.

### Modeled example 1 — reading the Milo picture, step by step

*Adult thinks aloud, pointing to each part:*

"**Who and what do I see?** A child in a red coat and red hat,
kneeling in the snow. A small brown dog. The dog holds a blue
mitten in its mouth. The child wears one blue mitten. A pine tree,
a small house with a warm window, snowflakes. **What moment is
this?** The dog is giving the mitten back — that is the **last**
part of Milo's story. **What can I guess?** I guess the child is
happy, because he is smiling — and I guess the mitten fell off
earlier, or the dog would not be carrying it. The picture does not
*show* the mitten falling, but it helps me remember it."

### Modeled example 2 — the same picture, a wrong reading, corrected

*Adult thinks aloud:*

"Could this picture be the beginning of the story? At the
beginning, Milo is walking with *two* mittens on. Here he wears
only one, and the dog holds the other. So no — it cannot be the
beginning. Could it be the middle? In the middle, the mitten is on
the ground and Biscuit is just finding it. Here Biscuit already has
it and is bringing it to Milo. So it must be the end. Checking the
details is how you prove which moment a picture shows."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Who and what do you see in this picture?" (Child in red coat
   and hat, brown dog, blue mitten in the dog's mouth, one blue
   mitten on the child's hand, pine tree, house, snow.)
2. "What moment of Milo's story does it show — beginning, middle,
   or end? How do you know?" (End; the dog is returning the mitten
   Milo lost.)
3. "What does this picture NOT show?" (Grandma; the mitten falling;
   Milo walking at the start.) "Does that mean those parts do not
   matter?" (No — one picture holds one moment.)
4. "How does Milo feel in the picture? How can you tell?" (Happy /
   pleased; he is smiling and reaching for the mitten.)
5. The adult covers the dog with a hand and asks: "Now what moment
   could this be?" (The learner must notice the story changes
   without the dog — a good moment to say that pictures and words
   need each other.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. "Tell me everything you see in the picture, in your own words."
   (Any complete naming of child, dog, mittens, tree, house, snow.)
2. "Point to the part that proves this is the end of the story."
   (The dog holding the mitten out to Milo, or Milo's single worn
   mitten.)
3. "What do you think happened just before this picture?" (The
   mitten fell off in the snow and Biscuit found it.)
4. "What do you think will happen just after?" (Milo takes the
   mitten, says thank you, and they walk on to Grandma's house.)
5. "Is the house Grandma's house? How do you know, and what are
   you guessing?" (Guessing: it is probably the house they are
   walking to, because of the warm window and the path — but the
   story does not name it in the picture. Saying "I think" counts
   as correct.)

## The story — *Sam's Rainy-Day Picnic* (original, written for this unit)

> Sam packed a picnic basket with sandwiches, apples, and juice.
> He was going to eat outside under the big tree with his sister
> June. "Perfect picnic day!" said Sam.
>
> Then dark clouds came. Rain pattered down on the grass. "Oh no,"
> said Sam. "Our picnic is getting wet!"
>
> June laughed. "Let's have the picnic inside!" They spread a
> blanket on the living-room floor. They ate the sandwiches, apples,
> and juice right there. "Best picnic ever," said Sam. "Rain or
> no rain!"

## Application — draw-and-tell

The adult reads *Sam's Rainy-Day Picnic* once. The learner draws
one moment from it — any moment: packing the basket, rain on the
grass, or the blanket on the living-room floor. Then the learner
tells the adult: "My picture shows..." and names the moment
(beginning, middle, or end) and one detail visible in the drawing.
The adult scribes the learner's sentence under the drawing. This
drawing becomes Practice Session E's starting point and a model for
the project book.

## Exit check

1. "What moment does the Milo picture show?" (The end — Biscuit
   bringing the mitten back.)
2. "Name two things you can see in the picture." (Any two: child,
   dog, mittens, tree, house, snowflakes.)
3. "What is one thing you can guess from the picture, and what is
   one thing the picture does not show?" (Guess: Milo is happy;
   the mitten fell earlier. Not shown: Grandma, the fall itself.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** the three picture questions are always asked
  in the same order (see / guess / not shown), so the learner can
  predict them; home-language answers are welcome and the adult
  repeats the key words in English.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to picture parts instead of
  naming them; draw the answer (for example, draw what happened
  just before) instead of saying it.
- **Seated-table variant:** printed picture on the table; the
  learner points with a crayon or a pointer stick.
- **Floor-play variant:** the learner poses like Milo (kneeling,
  reaching) and like Biscuit (holding an imaginary mitten) while
  describing the picture.
- **Extension:** the learner draws a picture of a moment from Ana's
  story and challenges the adult: "Which moment is this — and how
  do you know?" Reversing the roles tests the skill hardest.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

Learners often treat a picture as "the whole story" and narrate
events it does not show as if they were visible. The correction is
not to stop them — it is the language: "The picture *shows*...;
you *remember*...; you *guess*..." Keep those three verbs distinct
all lesson. A second trap: reading feelings as facts ("He IS happy"
vs. "He looks happy, so I guess he is happy"). At K, naming the
difference between what is shown and what is guessed is the whole
RL.K.7 skill; do not demand certainty about feelings.
