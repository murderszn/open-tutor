# Lesson 06 — Compare Two Stories, Answer from Information

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell how two stories are the same
and how they are different, and I can answer a question from
something I learned."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** the two stories *Milo and
the Lost Mitten* (Lesson 01) and *Ana Plants a Seed* (Lesson 02)
for re-reading or recap, the information text *How Seeds Grow*
below, a real seed or seed packet if available (or a picture of a
seedling).
**Standards:** RL.K.9; W.K.8; SL.K.2.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Ask: "Who helped Milo?" (Biscuit.) "Who helped Ana?" (Ben.)
"Did both stories end happily?" (Yes.) Then: "Today we compare —
we find what is the same and what is different. Comparing is how
readers notice patterns across books."

## Explicit teaching

To **compare** two stories, ask two questions:

- **What is the same?** Something true in both stories.
- **What is different?** Something true in one story but not the other.

Milo and Ana, side by side:

| | Milo | Ana |
|---|---|---|
| Characters | Milo and Biscuit (a dog) | Ana and Ben (two people) |
| Setting | Snowy path to Grandma's house | Ana's garden |
| Problem or job | A mitten is lost | A seed must be planted and grown |
| Help | Biscuit finds and brings the mitten | Ben pours the water |
| Ending | Milo says thank you; they walk on | A sunflower stands tall; Ana and Ben smile |

**Same:** both stories have two characters; both have a helper; both
end happily; both happen outside. **Different:** snow vs. garden;
a lost thing found vs. a seed grown; an animal helper vs. a person
helper; one day's walk vs. a whole summer of growing.

The second skill today is different: answering a question from
**information**, not from a story. Stories tell what happened to
characters. Information tells how the world works. Listen to this
short information text — it is not a story, and it has no characters
with a problem:

> **How Seeds Grow** (original informational text, written for this unit)
>
> A seed needs soil, water, and sun. First, the seed sprouts a root
> under the soil. Then it grows a stem and leaves above the soil.
> With soil, water, and sun, a small seed can grow into a big plant.

To answer "What does a seed need?" you do not guess and you do not
make up a story — you go back to the information: soil, water, and
sun. That is what "gather information from a provided source" means
at this age: the answer lives in the text, and you fetch it.

### Modeled example 1 — comparing, thinking aloud

*Adult thinks aloud:*

"Same or different? Both Milo and Ana get help — Biscuit helps
Milo, Ben helps Ana. That is the **same**. But the help is
different: Biscuit is a dog who finds something; Ben is a person who
pours water. And the settings are different: snow path vs. garden.
So I can say: 'Both stories have a helper and a happy ending, but
Milo's story happens in the snow and Ana's happens in a garden.'"

### Modeled example 2 — answering from information

*Adult reads *How Seeds Grow* once, then thinks aloud:*

"Question: What does a seed need? Let me think back to the
information. It said: soil, water, and sun. I will answer with
those three words, in order: soil, water, sun. I did not guess —
the text told me. If I forget, I ask to hear it again. Re-listening
is part of the skill, not a failure."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Name one way Milo's story and Ana's story are the same."
   (Both have a helper; both end happily; both happen outside.)
2. "Name one way they are different." (Snow vs. garden; dog helper
   vs. person helper; lost mitten vs. growing seed.)
3. "Who helped in each story?" (Biscuit helped Milo; Ben helped
   Ana.)
4. After hearing *How Seeds Grow*: "What does a seed need?" (Soil,
   water, and sun.)
5. "What grows first — the root or the stem?" (The root, under the
   soil; then the stem and leaves.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. "Tell me one same and one different about Milo and Ana, in your
   own words." (Any true pair from the table above.)
2. "Which story happens in the snow?" (Milo's.) "Which happens in
   a garden?" (Ana's.)
3. "Was there an animal helper in Ana's story?" (No.) "In Milo's?"
   (Yes — Biscuit.)
4. From *How Seeds Grow*, without re-hearing: "Name the three
   things a seed needs." (Soil, water, sun — any order counts if
   all three are named; the adult re-reads once if one is missing
   and the learner tries again.)
5. "What does Ana's seed grow into?" (A big yellow sunflower —
   answered from the story, not from the information text; the
   adult names the source of each answer: "That one came from
   Ana's story.")

## Application

"Same-and-different detective." The learner picks any two things
from the room (two crayons, two shoes, a book and a ball) and says
one same and one different about them. Then back to stories: "Is
Sam's picnic story more like Milo's or more like Ana's?" (Either
answer is acceptable if defended: like Milo's, because something
goes wrong and is fixed the same day; like Ana's, because friends
do something together outside. The defense, not the pick, is the
skill.)

## Exit check

1. "One same about Milo and Ana?" (Helper; happy ending; outside.)
2. "One different?" (Snow vs. garden; dog vs. person helper.)
3. "What three things does a seed need?" (Soil, water, sun.)
4. "Where did you find that answer — in a story or in
   information?" (In information — *How Seeds Grow*.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** *same* = two hands clapping together;
  *different* = hands apart. The comparison table is spoken, not
  read, and the learner may answer with gestures plus one word.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the Milo card or the Ana
  card to answer "which story...?" questions; sort two piles
  (same / different) with picture cards.
- **Seated-table variant:** two story cards on the table; the
  learner places a shared token (a button) between them for each
  "same" and keeps it on one side for each "different."
- **Floor-play variant:** a tape line on the floor — stand on the
  Milo side or the Ana side to answer "which story?" questions,
  and in the middle for "both."
- **Extension:** the learner compares Milo and Sam (both stories
  have a same-day problem fixed with help, but one is lost-and-found
  and the other is a changed plan) and adds a row to the table in
  words the adult scribes.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

The classic error is comparing *details* instead of *story parts*
("Milo has a red coat and Ana has a garden" — that is not a
comparison, it is two facts). Steer with the frame: "In Milo's
story ___, and in Ana's story ___" with the same slot filled on both
sides (helper, setting, ending). The second error is answering
information questions from imagination ("seeds need love") — sweet,
but not the skill. Gently re-read the text and praise the fetch:
"You found it in the words. That is what good readers do."
