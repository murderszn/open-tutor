# Lesson 03 — Pulling Words Apart

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can pull a word apart into its three
sounds, one sound at a time."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** three drawn sound boxes on
paper, three chips or buttons, picture cards (sun, pig, mop, bed,
fan), letter tiles (optional).
**Standards:** RF.K.2.d (isolate sounds in CVC words); RF.K.2.c
(segment onsets and rimes, extended here to three sounds).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Ask: "What is the first sound in *sun*?" (/s/.) "The middle
sound?" (/u/.) "The last sound?" (/n/.) Say: "You found all three
sounds yesterday. Today you will pull the whole word apart yourself,
one chip at a time."

## Explicit teaching

**Segmenting** is blending in reverse. Blending squishes sounds into
a word; segmenting pulls a word apart into its sounds. The rule is
simple: **one sound, one chip, in order, left to right.**

1. **Say the word** normally: *sun*.
2. **Say it slowly**, stretching it: /s/…/u/…/n/.
3. **Push one chip** into a box for each sound you hear, left to
   right: chip 1 for /s/, chip 2 for /u/, chip 3 for /n/.
4. **Count the chips:** three chips means three sounds. A CVC word
   always gives three chips — if you have two or four, say the word
   slowly again and listen for the sound you missed or doubled.

Chips come before letters on purpose: segmenting is an ear job, not
an eye job. Once the chips are right, letters can replace them —
one tile per chip, in the same order — and the word is spelled.

### Modeled example 1 — segmenting *pig*

*Adult thinks aloud, pushing chips:*

"Word: *pig*. Slowly: /p/…/i/…/g/. First sound /p/ — push chip
one. Middle sound /i/ — push chip two. Last sound /g/ — push chip
three. Count: one, two, three. Three sounds, three chips. Now say
the sounds back, touching each chip: /p/ /i/ /g/. And squish them:
*pig*. Pulling apart and squishing back are the same word, both
ways."

### Modeled example 2 — segmenting *mop* (and catching a mistake)

*Adult thinks aloud, deliberately starting wrong, then fixing it:*

"Word: *mop*. Slowly: /m/…/o/…/p/. Hmm, I almost pushed only two
chips — /m/ and /op/ together. That is onset-and-rime, the Unit 02
way. But today we want every single sound. Again, slowly:
/m/ (chip one), /o/ (chip two), /p/ (chip three). Three chips.
If your chips say two, you squished two sounds together — stretch
the word again and split them."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Segment *sun* with the adult: /s/ /u/ /n/, three chips.
2. Segment *bed* with the adult: /b/ /e/ /d/, three chips.
3. The learner pushes the chips while the adult says *fan* slowly:
   /f/ /a/ /n/.
4. The learner segments *cup* alone, then says the sounds back
   touching each chip: /c/ /u/ /p/.
5. Chip-count check: the adult segments *dog* as /d/ /og/ (two
   chips) and asks: "Is that right for today?" (No — three sounds:
   /d/ /o/ /g/, three chips.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Segment *hat*: /h/ /a/ /t/, three chips.
2. Segment *pin*: /p/ /i/ /n/, three chips.
3. Segment *rug*: /r/ /u/ /g/, three chips.
4. Segment *jet*: /j/ /e/ /t/, three chips.
5. Segment *tub*: /t/ /u/ /b/, three chips. Then replace the chips
   with letter tiles in order: T U B.

## Application

"Chip counter." The adult says five words from the word bank, one at
a time (hen, mop, sit, dog, bun). For each, the learner segments it
with chips, counts the chips aloud ("three!"), and sorts the word
card into a "3 sounds" pile. Every word in this unit lands in the
"3 sounds" pile — that is the discovery: CVC words always have three
sounds. The adult scribes the five words and their sound counts.

## Exit check

1. Segment *log*: /l/ /o/ /g/, three chips.
2. Segment *wig*: /w/ /i/ /g/, three chips.
3. "How many sounds are in *sun*?" (Three: /s/ /u/ /n/.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** let the learner segment a home-language word
  with three clear sounds first, then transfer the routine to
  English CVC words.
- **Alternate response mode:** tap the table once per sound instead
  of pushing chips; the adult counts the taps. Or clap-and-point:
  one clap per sound while pointing at each box.
- **Seated-table variant:** sound boxes on paper; chips slide in
  from below.
- **Floor-play variant:** three hoops or floor spots; the learner
  steps into one hoop per sound while saying it.
- **Extension:** the learner segments a word, then swaps the chips
  for letter tiles and reads the word back (segment → spell → read).

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *Two chips instead of three:* the learner segments onset-and-rime
  (/m/ /op/) because Unit 02 rewarded it. Praise the Unit 02 skill,
  then re-stretch: "That was two parts. Today, three sounds: /m/
  /o/ /p/."
- *Four chips for one sound:* doubling a sound ("sssun") adds a chip.
  Say the word at normal speed first, then stretch once — never
  stretch the same sound twice.
- *Chips out of order:* sounds pushed right-to-left spell the word
  backwards. Always start at the left box and move right, the way
  reading goes.
