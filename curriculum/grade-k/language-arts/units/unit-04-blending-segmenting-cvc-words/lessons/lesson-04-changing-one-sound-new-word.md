# Lesson 04 — Change One Sound, Make a New Word

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can change one sound in a word and make
a brand-new word."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** letter tiles (c, a, t, p,
m, o, h, b, i, g), three sound boxes, the talking stick (optional).
**Standards:** RF.K.2.e (add or substitute individual sounds in
simple, one-syllable words to make new words).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Segment *cat* together: /c/ /a/ /t/. Say: "Three sounds. What if we
keep two sounds and change just one? Then we get a new word — like
magic, but it is really just listening."

## Explicit teaching

**Changing one sound** means keeping two of the three sounds exactly
the same and swapping the third for a different sound. You can change:

- the **first** sound: *cat* → *hat* (only /c/ → /h/ changes);
- the **middle** sound: *pin* → *pan* (only /i/ → /a/ changes);
- the **last** sound: *cat* → *cap* (only /t/ → /p/ changes).

Two rules keep the game honest:

1. **Change exactly one sound.** Changing two sounds is a different
   word, not a one-sound change (*cat* → *dog* changes all three).
2. **The new word must be a real word.** *Cat* → *cag* changes one
   sound, but *cag* is not a word, so it does not count. Real words
   only.

Word families make this easy: words that share their ending (like
*-at*: cat, hat, mat, bat, sat, rat) differ only in the first sound.
Change the first tile, read the new word, and you have walked down
the whole family.

### Modeled example 1 — a chain: cat → cap → map → mop → top → hop

*Adult builds with tiles, thinking aloud:*

"Start: C A T — *cat*. Change the last sound only: /t/ → /p/.
C A P — *cap*! Change the first sound only: /c/ → /m/. M A P —
*map*! Change the middle sound only: /a/ → /o/. M O P — *mop*!
Change the first sound only: /m/ → /t/. T O P — *top*! Change the
first sound only: /t/ → /h/. H O P — *hop*! Five changes, five
real words, one sound changed each time. That is a word chain."

### Modeled example 2 — changing the middle sound: pig → big → bag → bat → hat

*Adult thinks aloud:*

"Start: P I G — *pig*. Change the first sound: /p/ → /b/. B I G —
*big*! Change the middle sound: /i/ → /a/. B A G — *bag*! Change
the last sound: /g/ → /t/. B A T — *bat*! Change the first sound:
/b/ → /h/. H A T — *hat*! Notice the middle change: *big* to *bag*
sounds small — one vowel — but it makes a completely different
word. Middle sounds matter most, because they are the easiest to
miss."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. *Cat* → change the first sound to /h/: *hat*.
2. *Hat* → change the last sound to /m/: *ham*.
3. *Pin* → change the middle sound to /a/: *pan*.
4. *Mop* → change the first sound to /t/: *top*.
5. Chain with the adult: *sun* → change the first sound to /b/
   (*bun*) → change the middle sound to /u/? (Trick: *bun* already
   has /u/ in the middle; changing /u/ to /u/ changes nothing. The
   learner must say "no change" and instead change the last sound
   to /g/: *bug*.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. *Dog* → change the first sound to /l/: *log*.
2. *Log* → change the middle sound to /e/: *leg*.
3. *Sit* → change the last sound to /p/: *sip*.
4. *Red* → change the first sound to /b/: *bed*.
5. Build a chain of three, one sound at a time, starting from *fan*:
   one correct example is *fan* → *pan* (first /f/ → /p/) →
   *pin* (middle /a/ → /i/) → *pig* (last /n/ → /g/).

## Application

"Word-chain race." The adult gives a start word (*cat*) and an end
word (*hop*); the learner builds the shortest chain they can between
them, one sound at a time, with tiles. (*Cat* → *cap* → *map* →
*mop* → *top* → *hop* is five steps; any correct chain counts.)
The adult scribes the learner's chain.

## Exit check

1. *Hen* → change the first sound to /p/: *pen*.
2. *Pen* → change the middle sound to /i/: *pin*.
3. "How many sounds did you change to turn *cat* into *cap*?"
   (One — only the last sound, /t/ → /p/.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** act out each new word where possible (*hop*
  means hopping) so the meaning of "new word" is visible, not just
  heard.
- **Alternate response mode:** the adult swaps the tile; the learner
  reads the new word, or points to its picture, instead of moving
  tiles.
- **Seated-table variant:** tiles in three boxes; only the changed
  box's tile moves.
- **Floor-play variant:** three floor spots; the learner stands in
  the spot of the sound being changed.
- **Extension:** the learner invents a chain and challenges the adult
  to find a shorter route between the same two words.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *Changing two sounds at once:* *cat* → *hat* is fine (first sound
  only), but *cat* → *hot* changes first and middle. Count the
  changes: "How many tiles moved? Two — that is too many. Move one
  back."
- *Accepting non-words:* *cag* or *pog* (for *pig* → *pog* the middle
  change is real, but *pog* is not a word in this unit's bank). The
  rule "real words only" is what makes the game a reading game, not
  a sound game.
- *Middle-sound changes feel invisible:* learners swap first and last
  sounds easily but miss the vowel. Slow the middle sound and use
  the mirror: /i/ (smile) versus /a/ (open mouth) look different.
