# Lesson 05 — Sight Words and Look-Alike Words

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can read four special words by sight,
and I can tell look-alike words apart."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** sight-word cards (the, of,
to, you), letter tiles, picture cards (cat, cap, pin, pan, mop, map).
**Standards:** RF.K.3.c (read common high-frequency words by sight);
RF.K.3.d (distinguish similarly spelled words by the sounds of the
letters that differ).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Show the card *the* and ask: "Can you sound this one out?" Let the
learner try, then say: "This word is tricky to sound out — *th* is
not a sound we blend letter by letter yet. So we learn it a different
way: by sight, like remembering a face. Today we meet four face-words."

## Explicit teaching

A **sight word** is a word you know the moment you see it — you do
not sound it out, you just know it, the way you know a friend's face.
This unit's four sight words are:

- **the** — the word we use before a thing: *the* cat, *the* sun.
- **of** — pronounced /uv/, not /o/ /f/ — that is why we memorize
  it instead of sounding it out.
- **to** — the *o* says /oo/ here, not the short /o/ from *mop*.
  Another reason to memorize it.
- **you** — starts with /y/ and ends with the /oo/ sound; we
  memorize the whole shape.

Learn them by look, by say, and by use: look at the card, say the
word fast ("the!"), then use it: "the cat." Three touches per word,
every time.

**Look-alike words** are the opposite skill: two words that look
almost the same, where one letter — and its sound — is different.
*Cat* and *cap* look alike except the last letter: T says /t/,
P says /p/. *Pin* and *pan* look alike except the middle letter:
I says /i/, A says /a/. To tell look-alikes apart, find the letter
that is different and say its sound. That sound is the whole answer.

### Modeled example 1 — meeting the four sight words

*Adult shows each card, thinking aloud:*

"*The*. I do not sound it out — I just know it: *the*. Say it fast
with me: the! Use it: *the* cat." *(Repeat for of, to, you.)*
"*Of* — say it: of! It sounds like /uv/, which is silly, and that
is exactly why we remember it whole. *To* — to! *You* — you!"

"Now mixed up, fast: you, the, to, of, the, you. Sight words are
fast words. If you have to sound it out, it is not a sight word
yet — and that is fine, we will practice."

### Modeled example 2 — telling look-alikes apart

*Adult lays out CAT and CAP and thinks aloud:*

"These two words look almost the same. Same first letter C, same
middle letter A. Different last letter: T here, P here. T says /t/,
P says /p/. So the first word is *cat* and the second is *cap*.
The last sound is the detective: /t/ versus /p/."

"Now PIN and PAN. Same first letter P, same last letter N.
Different middle letter: I says /i/, A says /a/. *Pin*, *pan*.
The middle sound is the detective this time."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Read the four sight-word cards mixed up: you, of, the, to.
2. Use each sight word in a spoken phrase: "the pig," "a cup of
   water" (adult supplies *a* and *water*; the learner supplies
   *of*), "to the sun," "you and the dog" (adult supplies *and*).
   The point is the learner's sight word, not the whole phrase.
3. Look-alikes, last letter: *mop* vs. *mob* — which letter differs?
   (Last: P /p/ vs. B /b/.) Read both words.
4. Look-alikes, middle letter: *sit* vs. *sat* — which letter
   differs? (Middle: I /i/ vs. A /a/.) Read both words.
5. Look-alikes, first letter: *sun* vs. *bun* — which letter
   differs? (First: S /s/ vs. B /b/.) Read both words.

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Read the sight-word cards in this order: to, you, the, of.
2. Point to *you* when the adult says "you"; point to *of* when the
   adult says "of" (cards mixed up).
3. *Hen* vs. *pen*: which letter differs, and what sounds?
   (First: H /h/ vs. P /p/.)
4. *Dog* vs. *log*: which letter differs, and what sounds?
   (First: D /d/ vs. L /l/.)
5. *Bug* vs. *bag*: which letter differs, and what sounds?
   (Middle: U /u/ vs. A /a/.)

## Application

"Sight-word hunt." The adult writes three short sentences on paper
(the learner reads only the sight words and CVC words; the adult
reads the rest if any): "The cat sat." "The pig ran." "The dog
sat." The learner points to every sight word (*The*) and reads
every CVC word by blending (*cat, sat, pig, ran, dog*). The adult
scribes which words the learner read alone.

## Exit check

1. Read these sight words mixed up: of, you, to, the.
2. *Cap* vs. *map*: which letter differs? (First: C /k/ vs. M /m/.)
3. "Why do we memorize *of* instead of sounding it out?" (Because
   it is pronounced /uv/, not the way its letters sound.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** sight words are shape-and-say words in every
  language; if the learner knows a home-language sight word, compare
  routines ("We remember that word whole, too").
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the named sight word instead
  of reading it aloud; the adult reads it and the learner confirms.
- **Seated-table variant:** cards in a row; the learner slaps the
  named card.
- **Floor-play variant:** sight-word cards on the floor; the learner
  jumps to the named word.
- **Extension:** the learner writes one sight word from memory
  (tracing the adult's model first is fine) and uses it in a spoken
  sentence with a CVC word: "You see the cat" is too hard (see is
  untaught) — instead: "The cat" + a drawn picture, captioned by
  the adult.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *Sounding out sight words:* a learner who tries /t/ /h/ /e/ for
  *the* is applying a good skill to the wrong word. Say plainly:
  "That is a face-word. We know it whole. This is the one kind of
  word we do not sound out."
- *Guessing look-alikes from the first letter:* *cap* read as *cat*
  means the learner stopped at the first letter. Rule: "Read to the
  end. The last letter can change the word."
- *Confusing of with off:* *of* (/uv/) and *off* are different
  words. This unit teaches only *of*; if *off* comes up, note it as
  a later word and move on.
