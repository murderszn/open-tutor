# Lesson 01 — The Whole Uppercase Alphabet

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can name all the uppercase letters, A to Z,
in order and mixed up."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** uppercase letter cards A–Z
(large print), the alphabet arc or a tabletop line of cards in order,
the talking stick (optional).
**Standards:** RF.K.1.d; SL.K.1.a (talk rules).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Lay out the cards A–M in order. Ask the learner to name them with you,
pointing to each card. Then add N–Z face up in a small pile beside the
arc and say: "You already know the first half. Today we meet the second
half, and then we will know the whole alphabet."

## Explicit teaching

The alphabet is the **names of all the letters, in order**. There are
26 letters. Uppercase letters are the **big** letters — the ones that
start names and sentences. You already know A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I,
J, K, L, M. The second half is N, O, P, Q, R, S, T, U, V, W, X, Y, Z.

Say the new letters slowly, in four small groups, pointing to each
card as you say it. The groups are how we will practice them:

- **N, O, P, Q** — "N-O-P-Q."
- **R, S, T** — "R-S-T."
- **U, V, W** — "U-V-W."
- **X, Y, Z** — "X-Y-Z, the end!"

Naming a letter means saying its **name**, not its sound. The name of
the letter S is "ess." Its sound — the noise it makes in a word — comes
in Lessons 03 and 04. Today, names only.

A letter keeps its name no matter where it sits. N is N at the start
of the line, in the middle, or all by itself in a pile. That is why we
practice mixed up, too: real reading never lines letters up in order
for you.

### Modeled example 1 — learning the second half in groups

*Adult points to each card in turn and thinks aloud:*

"Here is N. N. Here is O. O. Here is P. P. Here is Q. Q. Now together:
N, O, P, Q." *(Learner echoes.)*

"Now the next group: R, S, T." *(Echo.)* "And U, V, W." *(Echo.)* "And
the last group: X, Y, Z." *(Echo.)*

"Now the whole second half, fast as we can: N, O, P, Q, R, S, T, U, V,
W, X, Y, Z." *(Adult and learner say it together, pointing.)*

### Modeled example 2 — a letter out of order keeps its name

*Adult shuffles five cards — Q, A, Z, M, T — face up in a row and
thinks aloud:*

"First card: Q. I know Q because it has a big circle and a little
tail. Next: A — pointy at the top, a line across the middle. Next: Z —
a zig-zag, the last letter. Next: M — two humps, like mountains.
Next: T — a tall stick with a hat on top. The order changed, but the
names did not change. That is what 'mixed up' means."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Say the second half with the adult, pointing: N through Z.
2. Say the whole alphabet with the adult, A through Z, pointing to
   the arc. (Slow is fine; stopping to remember is fine.)
3. The adult points to cards out of order — R, B, W, E, X — and the
   learner names each. (Adult names any the learner misses, and the
   learner repeats it.)
4. The adult holds up a card and asks, "Is this in the first half
   (A–M) or the second half (N–Z)?" Cards: Q, C, Z, H, U.
5. The learner finds the card the adult names, from a mixed pile of
   ten cards: "Find M." "Find Z." "Find G." "Find T." "Find A."

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

The learner works alone with the arc visible; the adult watches and
notes which letters are secure.

1. Name these cards in order, left to right: N, O, P, Q, R.
2. Name these cards mixed up: Z, S, K, V, D.
3. Point to the card for each name the adult says: "W," "B," "Y,"
   "J," "R."
4. Say the alphabet from A to Z without pointing help. (The adult
   steps in only if the learner stalls for more than a few seconds,
   giving the next letter as a hint.)
5. Pick any three cards from the pile, name them, and put them back in
   the right places on the arc.

## Application

"Alphabet teacher." The learner teaches a stuffed animal the second
half of the alphabet: lay out N–Z in order, point to each letter, and
say its name, then mix up five cards and name them. The adult scribes
any letter the learner wants to remember for tomorrow's practice.

## Exit check

1. Name these cards mixed up: P, A, X, L, U. (All five correct =
   secure; 3–4 = nearly there, re-check in Session 4.)
2. "How many letters are in the alphabet?" (26.)
3. "What is the last letter?" (Z.) "What letter comes right before
   Z?" (Y.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** pair each new letter with a word the learner
  already knows (N for a name in the family, S for *sun*, Z for
  *zebra* in a picture book). Home-language alphabet songs are welcome
  alongside the English order.
- **Alternate response mode:** point instead of speaking; the adult
  says the name and the learner confirms with a nod or a thumbs-up.
  No speech required for the exit check's pointing items.
- **Seated-table variant:** all work at the table; the arc is a row of
  cards along the table edge.
- **Floor-play variant:** "alphabet walk" — cards taped to the floor
  in a curve; the learner steps on each letter as they name it.
- **Extension:** the learner names letters from a book's cover or a
  cereal box (environmental print) and sorts them into first-half and
  second-half piles.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *Reciting the song is not naming letters:* a child who sings the
  alphabet perfectly may still not recognize Q alone. That is why this
  lesson always follows singing with mixed-up cards. If the song is
  strong but single letters are weak, spend the extra time on guided
  practice task 3, not on more singing.
- *L, M, N run together:* the song blurs "elemeno." Slow the arc walk
  at L–M–N and point to each card separately until the three names are
  distinct.
- *Q without U confuses:* some children expect Q to travel with U.
  Say plainly: "Q's name is Q, all by itself. U is its friend in
  words, not in the alphabet line."
