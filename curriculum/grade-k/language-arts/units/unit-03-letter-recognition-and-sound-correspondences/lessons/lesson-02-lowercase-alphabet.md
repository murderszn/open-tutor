# Lesson 02 — The Lowercase Alphabet

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can name all the lowercase letters, a to
z, and match each one to its big uppercase partner."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** uppercase cards A–Z and
lowercase cards a–z (large print, high contrast), a pocket chart or
tabletop sorting space.
**Standards:** RF.K.1.d; SL.K.1.a (talk rules).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Show the uppercase arc. Ask: "Which of these letters look almost the
same big and small?" Let the learner guess, then show three true
look-alikes: C/c, O/o, S/s. Say: "Some small letters are twins of
their big letters. Some are completely different. Today we meet all
the small ones."

## Explicit teaching

**Lowercase** letters are the **small** letters — the ones most words
are written in. Every uppercase letter has a lowercase partner with
the same name. A and a are both called "ay." B and b are both called
"bee." Same name, different shape.

Some partners look alike:

- **Look-alike partners:** C/c, O/o, S/s, U/u, V/v, W/w, X/x, Z/z —
  the small letter is mostly a smaller copy of the big one.
- **Different-shape partners:** A/a, B/b, D/d, E/e, F/f, G/g, H/h,
  I/i, J/j, K/k, L/l, M/m, N/n, P/p, Q/q, R/r, T/t, Y/y — learn these
  one pair at a time.

Two pairs are **tricky pairs** because the small letters look like each
other flipped:

- **b and d:** b has its stick first, then its belly: "bat before the
  ball" — say /b/ and think of a **b**at and **b**all. d has its belly
  first, then its stick: the belly faces the other way.
- **p and q:** p's stick goes **down** below the line; q's stick also
  goes down, but its belly faces the other way and q has a little
  tail. In this lesson, the rule is simpler: point to the belly and
  say which way it faces; the adult names the letter until the
  learner's eyes learn the difference.

When a letter is hard, look at its **shape parts**: tall stick, short
stick, belly (round part), tail, dot. Naming the parts helps the eyes
remember.

### Modeled example 1 — matching partners

*Adult lays out uppercase A, B, C, D and the matching lowercase cards
mixed up, and thinks aloud:*

"Big A. Which small card is a? This one — a, with a pointy top and a
belly. A and a are partners; both are called 'ay.'"

"Big B. Which small card is b? This one — stick first, then belly.
B and b are partners; both are called 'bee.'"

"Big C. Which small card is c? This one — it looks just like big C,
only smaller. C and c are partners."

"Big D. Which small card is d? This one — belly first, then stick.
Careful — it is not b! b's stick comes first. This is d, partner of
big D."

### Modeled example 2 — telling b from d

*Adult holds up b and d side by side and thinks aloud:*

"Look at this one: stick, then belly. Stick first — that is **b**,
like **b**at. Now this one: belly, then stick. Belly first — that is
**d**, like **d**og. I check the stick: if the stick comes first, it
is b. If the belly comes first, it is d."

*Adult covers the letters and asks the learner to repeat the rule
back: "Stick first?" (b.) "Belly first?" (d.)*

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Match these uppercase cards to their lowercase partners from a
   mixed pile: A, M, S, T, G. (Adult names any missed partner and the
   learner repeats it.)
2. Name these lowercase cards in order: a, b, c, d, e.
3. Name these lowercase cards mixed up: m, s, g, k, z.
4. Tricky-pair drill: the adult shows b and d in random order, four
   times each; the learner names each, saying the rule ("stick
   first — b" or "belly first — d") before the name.
5. The learner sorts six lowercase cards into two piles: "looks like
   its big partner" (c, o, s) and "looks different" (a, g, y).

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Match all the lowercase cards for N–Z to their uppercase partners
   on the arc, working left to right.
2. Name these lowercase cards mixed up: r, p, q, f, j, w.
3. Point to the lowercase card for each name the adult says: "e,"
   "h," "t," "n," "x."
4. Tricky-pair check: name b, d, p, q shown in this order: d, b, q,
   p, b, d.
5. Say the lowercase alphabet from a to z with the arc visible.
   (The adult steps in only if the learner stalls.)

## Application

"Partner hunt." The adult hides ten lowercase cards around the table
or room (in plain sight). The learner finds each one, names it, and
places it on its uppercase partner on the arc. The adult scribes any
partner the learner wants to practice again tomorrow.

## Exit check

1. Name these lowercase cards mixed up: a, m, g, s, q, z.
   (5–6 correct = secure; 3–4 = nearly there, re-check in Session 8.)
2. "Which small letter is b's tricky partner?" (d — belly first.)
3. Point to the lowercase partner of big R. (r.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** teach *belly*, *stick*, *tail*, *dot* with the
  learner's own body and with real objects before applying them to
  letters; home-language letter names are welcome as a bridge, with
  the English name taught alongside.
- **Alternate response mode:** match by placing cards together
  instead of naming aloud; the adult names the pair and the learner
  confirms. A textured or sandpaper letter set may be used for
  low-vision learners.
- **Seated-table variant:** all matching at the table in a pocket
  chart.
- **Floor-play variant:** uppercase cards in a curve on the floor;
  the learner walks each lowercase card to its partner.
- **Extension:** the learner finds three lowercase letters in a
  picture book and names each one and its uppercase partner.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *b/d reversal is normal and persistent:* do not treat it as a
  failure. Keep the stick-first/belly-first rule identical every
  session, and check it in the Session 7 game before any test. If
  reversals stay frequent after Session 8, note it for the teacher
  guide's re-teach triggers — it is a watch item, not a verdict.
- *Same name, different letter:* a child may call lowercase a "a
  different letter" because it looks different from A. Repeat the
  partner rule every time: "Same name, different shape — partners."
- *Uppercase-only reading:* children who learned A–M big may try to
  read every word in capitals. Point out lowercase letters in a book
  and count how many more small letters than big letters appear on
  one page.
