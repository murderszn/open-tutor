# Lesson 06 — Long Vowel Sounds and Letter–Sound Match

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can say the long sound for each vowel —
the vowel says its own name — and match any letter to its sound and
any sound to its letter."
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Materials:** all letter cards A–Z (upper-
and lowercase), vowel cards A, E, I, O, U, picture cards or objects
for: cake, bee, bike, rope, unicorn, plus the long-vowel chant cards
from the teacher guide.
**Standards:** RF.K.3.b (long sounds); RF.K.3.a, RF.K.3.b (matching);
L.K.2.c (writing letters for sounds — introduced here, assessed in
Sessions 15–16).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Quick-fire review of Lesson 05: the adult shows A, E, I, O, U and the
learner says each short sound with its action. Then say: "Short
sounds are one job vowels do. Today we learn their other job: the
**long sound**. A long vowel says its own name."

## Explicit teaching

A **long sound** is when a vowel says its own **name**. That is the
whole rule, and it is why long sounds are easier to remember than
short ones once you know the vowel names:

| Vowel | Long sound | Picture word | Why it is long |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | /ā/ — "ay" | *cake* | A says its name in *cake* (the silent e at the end is a helper; the adult names it, the learner does not need to explain it) |
| E | /ē/ — "ee" | *bee* | E says its name in *bee* (two e's together say "ee") |
| I | /ī/ — "eye" | *bike* | I says its name in *bike* (silent-e helper again) |
| O | /ō/ — "oh" | *rope* | O says its name in *rope* (silent-e helper again) |
| U | /ū/ — "yoo" | *unicorn* | U says its name in *unicorn* |

Say each one as **name, long sound, word**: "A, /ā/, cake."

Now the difference, said plainly, because children mix the two jobs:

- **Short A** says /a/ — *apple*. **Long A** says /ā/ — *cake*.
- **Short E** says /e/ — *egg*. **Long E** says /ē/ — *bee*.
- **Short I** says /i/ — *igloo*. **Long I** says /ī/ — *bike*.
- **Short O** says /o/ — *octopus*. **Long O** says /ō/ — *rope*.
- **Short U** says /u/ — *umbrella*. **Long U** says /ū/ — *unicorn*.

**Matching** is the unit's finish line: a letter and its sound must
connect in **both** directions. Letter → sound: see M, say /m/.
Sound → letter: hear /m/, point to M. If only one direction works,
the connection is half-built, and this lesson's games practice the
missing direction.

**Writing letters for sounds** starts here and is assessed in Sessions
15–16: the adult says a sound, and the learner writes the letter that
says it. Start with the sounds the learner knows best (usually M, S,
B, and the short vowels). A recognizable letter counts — perfect
formation does not.

### Modeled example 1 — long sounds with the name rule

*Adult holds up A and thinks aloud:*

"A. The name is 'ay.' The long sound is also 'ay' — the vowel says
its own name! A, /ā/, cake. Listen: c-ay-k — hear 'ay' in the middle?
That is long A."

*Adult holds up I:*

"I. The name is 'eye.' The long sound is also 'eye' — the vowel says
its own name! I, /ī/, bike. Listen: b-eye-k — hear 'eye' in the
middle? That is long I."

### Modeled example 2 — matching in both directions

*Adult lays out M, S, A, T and thinks aloud:*

"Direction one — letter to sound. I see S. S says /s/. I see A.
A's short sound is /a/ and its long sound is /ā/."

"Direction two — sound to letter. I hear /m/. Which letter says
/m/? M — here it is. I hear /t/. Which letter says /t/? T — here
it is. Both directions — that is a real match."

*Adult then asks the learner to do one round each way before guided
practice begins.*

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Long-vowel chant, with the adult: "A, /ā/, cake. E, /ē/, bee.
   I, /ī/, bike. O, /ō/, rope. U, /ū/, unicorn."
2. Short-or-long sort: the adult says *apple, cake, egg, bee,
   igloo, bike, octopus, rope, umbrella, unicorn* one at a time; the
   learner says "short" or "long" and names the vowel.
3. Letter → sound round: the adult shows B, R, O (long), U (short),
   Z; the learner says the sound for each. (For vowels, the learner
   says which sound the adult asked for — the adult names "short"
   or "long" before showing the card.)
4. Sound → letter round: the adult says /v/, /ā/ (long A), /g/,
   /u/ (short U), /ks/; the learner points to the right letter
   each time.
5. Writing round: the adult dictates three sounds — /m/, /s/, /a/
   (short A) — and the learner writes the letter for each on paper.
   (The adult reads the letters back with the learner.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. The learner holds up each vowel card A, E, I, O, U in turn and
   says name, long sound, and picture word for each.
2. The learner sorts ten picture cards into short and long piles by
   vowel sound: apple, cake, egg, bee, igloo, bike, octopus, rope,
   umbrella, unicorn.
3. Mixed match, letter → sound: the adult shows K, E (long), P, I
   (short), W; the learner says the sound for each.
4. Mixed match, sound → letter: the adult says /d/, /ō/ (long O),
   /f/, /e/ (short E), /y/; the learner points to the right letter
   each time.
5. Writing round: the adult dictates five sounds — /b/, /t/, /o/
   (short O), /n/, /i/ (short I) — and the learner writes the
   letter for each.

## Application

"Letter–sound race." Two piles: letter cards face up on one side,
picture cards on the other. The adult calls a sound. The learner
races (walking, not running, indoors) to touch the letter card and
then the picture card that starts with that sound, saying both:
"/s/ — S — sun!" Five rounds. Then the learner calls the sounds
and the adult races.

## Exit check

1. Say the long sound for each vowel: A, E, I, O, U.
   (4–5 correct = secure; 3 = nearly there, re-check in Session 15.)
2. "Is the vowel in *cake* short or long? Which vowel is it?"
   (Long — A.)
3. Sound → letter: the adult says /z/; the learner points to Z and
   says its name and sound ("Z, /z/").

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** the name rule ("long vowels say their own
  names") is taught in words before it is tested; home-language
  examples of long vowel sounds are welcome as extra anchors.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the letter for a spoken
  sound instead of saying it; write the letter in sand, on a
  whiteboard, or with a finger in the air instead of on paper.
- **Seated-table variant:** all card work at the table; the race
  becomes a pointing race.
- **Floor-play variant:** letter cards in a line on the floor; the
  learner steps on the letter that says the sound the adult calls.
- **Extension:** the learner finds one long-vowel word in a picture
  book (any sensible word where the vowel says its name counts) and
  one short-vowel word, and says both sounds.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *Every vowel is long because it "says its name":* children may call
  every vowel long after this lesson. Keep the short/long sort
  (guided practice task 2) in daily review until the two jobs are
  distinct; the sort, not a lecture, fixes this.
- *Silent e confusion:* do not explain silent e as a rule to
  memorize. The adult's line is: "The e at the end is a helper that
  lets the other vowel say its name." Unit 06's decodable work
  revisits the pattern; it is not tested here.
- *One-direction matching:* a child who names sounds from letters
  but cannot find letters from sounds has memorized a chant, not a
  correspondence. The Session 15 quiz tests both directions on
  purpose; re-teach with sound → letter games if only one direction
  is secure.
