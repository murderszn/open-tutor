# Lesson 05 — When a Rule Gets Broken

**Student goal:** I can repair a broken rule: stop, fix what I can, say the
rule again, and try again.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 13 of 16**

## Materials

- Two puppets or stuffed animals
- Wooden blocks (for a tower to knock over in the scenario)
- The sand timer
- The class rule poster or rule cards from the [project](../project.md)
  (if the project has started; otherwise use the L1 rule cards)

**Privacy note for the adult:** restate the safety rule at the start:
"Names, addresses, and photos stay private." Repairs are about actions, not
about labeling children — never keep a record of "who broke rules."

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Remember our repair steps from Unit 01 — when feelings got hurt, we
stopped, said what happened, said sorry, and made a plan? Today: what do we
do when a RULE gets broken? It uses the same idea — with one change." If the
learner recalls any of the Unit 01 steps, celebrate the retrieval and begin.

## Teaching

Rules get broken. Sometimes by accident — you forget, or you're in a hurry.
Sometimes on purpose — you're angry, or you really want the toy. **What
matters is not that the rule broke. What matters is the repair.**

Here are the four rule-repair steps. Say them with me, with a gesture for
each:

1. **Stop.** (Hand up, palm out.) Freeze. No more breaking while we fix.
2. **Fix what you can.** (Hands rebuilding.) Pick up the blocks. Give the
   turn back. Put it back where it goes. Some things can't be un-broken —
   then we fix what we CAN.
3. **Say the rule again — or say sorry and mean it.** (Hand on heart.) Say
   the rule in your own words: "Our rule is we build together, we don't
   knock." If someone got hurt or upset, say sorry slowly, like you mean it.
4. **Try again.** (Two thumbs up.) Do it the rule way this time. Trying
   again is the most important step — it proves the repair is real.

**Repair is not punishment.** Nobody is "in trouble" for needing a repair —
grown-ups repair rules too. A repair is how we keep the safe-and-fair
promise after a wobble.

## Worked example 1 — the puppet's broken rule, repaired slowly (modeled)

The adult and a puppet build a block tower together under the rule "we build
together, we don't knock."

> The puppet gets excited and SWIPES the tower down. Freeze! The adult holds
> up a hand: "STOP."
>
> "Step 1: stop." The puppet freezes. "Step 2: fix what you can." The puppet
> (with the adult's help) picks up every block. "Some towers can't be
> un-knocked — but every block can be picked up."
>
> "Step 3: say the rule again." The puppet says, slowly: "Our rule is… we
> build together… we don't knock." The adult: "And?" The puppet, slowly and
> meaning it: "Sorry I knocked it."
>
> "Step 4: try again." The puppet places ONE block gently on the rebuilt
> base. "See? The rule way."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** notice the order — we fixed BEFORE we talked.
Fixing first shows the repair is real, not just words.

## Worked example 2 — the grown-up's repair (modeled, real)

The adult models a genuine small repair from real life — this is the most
powerful teaching in the lesson.

> "Yesterday I broke one of our rules. Our rule is 'one person talks at a
> time' — and I talked over you while you were telling me about your
> drawing. Let me repair it now, for real."
>
> "Step 1: stop." *(The adult stops and puts a hand up.)* "Step 2: fix what
> I can. I can't un-talk… but I can give you your turn back. Your turn to
> talk — I'm listening now." *(The adult listens to the learner for a full
> minute.)* "Step 3: our rule is one person talks at a time. Sorry I broke
> it." "Step 4: try again — from now on, I wait for your whole sentence."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** grown-ups break rules too, and grown-ups repair
them the same way. If the adult can repair, the learner can repair — repairs
are for everyone, not just children.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds with the puppets; the learner directs or performs the
repair.

1. Scenario: a puppet grabs the sand timer mid-turn. "Freeze! Walk me
   through the four steps." (The learner directs the puppet: stop → give the
   timer back → say the rule → try a fair turn.)
2. Scenario: a puppet runs in the hallway (walks fast with the puppet,
   narrating). "Repair it." (Stop → walk back → "our rule is walking feet" →
   walk it the rule way.)
3. Error-analysis round: a puppet knocks the blocks, shouts "SORRY!" super
   fast, and immediately grabs the toy. Ask: "Did the repair work? Which
   steps did the puppet skip?" *(Expected: no — a rushed sorry is not step
   3, and nothing was fixed (step 2 skipped), and there was no try-again
   (step 4 skipped). A fast sorry without fixing is just noise.)*
4. "What if you CAN'T fix it?" (The tower is broken and blocks are lost
   under the couch.) Teach: "Fix what you CAN — pick up what's there, say
   the rule, say sorry, try again with what's left. The repair still counts."
5. Puppet round: a puppet whispers, "I broke a rule yesterday but nobody
   saw. Do I still repair?" Ask: "What do you think?" *(Expected: yes —
   repairs are for the promise, not for getting caught. You can still fix
   it, say it, and do better today.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Tell me the four repair steps in order."
   *(Stop, fix what you can, say the rule / say sorry, try again.)*

## Independent practice

The learner chooses one puppet scenario card (drawn by the adult: knocked
tower / grabbed timer / ran in hallway / shouted over someone) and performs
the full four-step repair as the puppet, slowly and completely, while the
adult watches silently. Then the learner flips roles: the ADULT performs a
sloppy repair (skips step 2), and the learner must catch it: "You skipped
fixing!" The adult observes: does the learner sequence all four steps?
Catch the skipped step? The adult scribes one dictated sentence ("The rule
was ___. I repaired it by ___.") for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner directs the adult's hands through the
repair instead of performing it; or sequences four picture cards (stop sign,
rebuilding hands, heart, thumbs-up) and narrates each.

## Application

"Today, if a rule gets broken — by you, by me, by anyone — let's repair it
together, all four steps. Tonight tell your grown-up the four steps and
teach them the gestures." The learner becomes the repair teacher at home.

## Exit check

The adult asks: "Show me the four rule-repair steps with the puppets." The
learner should demonstrate or name all four in order (stop, fix what you
can, say the rule / say sorry and mean it, try again). Then: "Is a repair a
punishment?" (No — it's how we keep the promise.) The adult records: secure
/ developing / not yet, for both parts.

## Supports

- **Language access:** the four gestures (palm out, rebuilding hands, hand
  on heart, thumbs up) carry the sequence before the words do. Practice the
  gesture chain as a chant. Accept home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** sequencing picture cards, directing the
  adult's hands, puppet performance.
- **Floor-play variant:** do the knock-and-repair scene big on the floor;
  the learner physically picks up every block for step 2 — the doing IS the
  learning.
- **Big feelings:** if the learner gets upset during repair practice, pause
  and co-regulate first (breaths, hug, water). Repairs are never demanded
  mid-meltdown — model waiting for calm, then repairing.

## Extension

"Write" (dictate) a repair note for the rule book: "If this rule breaks,
here is how we fix it." One sentence per rule, added to the project rule
book. (Connects the lesson to the project artifact.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "saying sorry fixes everything" — no: sorry
is step 3 of 4; without fixing and trying again it is noise; (2) "repair
means I'm in trouble" — reframe relentlessly: repairs are for everyone,
including the adult (Worked example 2 is the proof); (3) "if nobody saw it,
it doesn't count" — repairs serve the promise, not an audience; (4)
confusing U01's hurt-feelings steps with rule repair — bridge explicitly:
"Same idea, one change: step 3 says the RULE again, because rules are the
promise we all share." Never demand a repair as punishment, and never accept
a rushed sorry as complete.
