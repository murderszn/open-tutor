# Lesson 02 — Fair Turns and Sharing

**Student goal:** I can take a fair turn: wait, use the timer, and pass the
turn when the sand runs out.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**

## Materials

- The generated illustration
  [`assets/fair-turns.png`](../assets/fair-turns.png) (and its
  [text-only alternative](../assets/README.md#text-only-alternative))
- A sand timer or simple one-minute timer
- One "coveted" toy (a single item the learner likes — one toy, not a set)
- Wooden blocks

**Privacy note for the adult:** restate the safety rule at the start:
"Names, addresses, and photos stay private."

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Hold up the coveted toy. "There is ONE of these and it is very fun. If we
both want it right now, what could we do?" Accept any answer — including
"I want it!" — then: "Today we learn the fair way: turns and a timer."

## Teaching

**Fair** does not mean everyone gets it first. **Fair** does not mean
everyone gets the same thing at the exact same second. **Fair means everyone
gets a turn.**

A **turn** is your time with the toy. When your turn ends, you **pass** it —
you hand the toy to the next person. The **timer** is the fair boss: nobody
argues with the timer. When the sand runs out, the turn is over. No
grabbing, no pushing, no "just one more minute."

**Waiting** is part of a fair turn. While you wait, you watch. Waiting is
hard, and waiting fairly is a skill — your hands stay calm, your voice stays
kind, and you know your turn is coming.

The most important fair-turn sentence is **"not yet."** "Not yet" means your
turn is coming — it is not "no." Practice it: "Not yet. Your turn is coming."

## Worked example 1 — the timer turn, modeled (adult + puppet)

The adult sets the coveted toy between themself and a puppet. The sand timer
sits beside it.

> "My turn first." The adult plays with the toy while the puppet watches.
> The puppet whines: "I want it NOW!" The adult answers, calmly: "Not yet.
> Your turn is coming. Watch the sand." When the sand runs out, the adult
> hands the toy to the puppet: "I am passing my turn. Your turn now." The
> puppet plays. When the sand runs out again, the adult says, "My turn again
> — see? The turn comes back around."
>
> Then the puppet tries to keep the toy when the sand runs out. The adult
> freezes: "The sand ran out. What does the fair boss say?" *(The turn
> passes.)* The puppet hands it over — slowly, with a grumble, which the
> adult accepts warmly: "Grumbling is allowed. Keeping the toy is not."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the timer decides, not the loudest voice. And
the turn always comes back around — that is what makes waiting bearable.

## Worked example 2 — reading the picture (modeled)

Show `assets/fair-turns.png`.

> "Look at these three children. One child is **passing** something — what
> is it?" *(The sand timer.)* "Who is passing it?" *(The child in the middle
> with the glasses, handing it to the child on the left.)* "What does the
> timer passing mean?" *(The turn is changing hands — it is the next child's
> turn now.)*
>
> "Now find the child who is **waiting**. How can you tell?" *(The child on
> the right, hands folded, smiling.)* "Is the waiting child upset? No —
> waiting fairly looks calm. And look: every child has their own small stack
> of blocks. Everyone gets to build. That is what fair looks like."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** you can *see* fairness in bodies — a hand
passing the timer, folded waiting hands, a calm face. Fairness is not just
an idea; it is something people do.

*(If the image is unavailable, read the [text-only
alternative](../assets/README.md#text-only-alternative) aloud and ask the
same questions.)*

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds with the real timer and the blocks.

1. Three real timer turns with the blocks: the learner builds while the sand
   runs, then passes the timer to the adult, then gets it back. Narrate:
   "Your turn… the sand is running… pass! Now you wait. Now it's back."
2. "Show me waiting fairly." The learner practices the waiting pose from the
   picture: hands folded or in lap, calm face. Celebrate the pose.
3. Error-analysis round: the adult grabs the toy mid-turn and says, very
   seriously, "My turn is more important than yours!" Ask: "Fair or not
   fair? What should I do?" *(Expected: not fair — nobody's turn is more
   important; pass the toy back; wait for the timer.)*
4. "Not yet" practice: the learner holds the toy; the adult (or puppet)
   asks for it. The learner practices saying "Not yet — your turn is coming"
   in a kind voice. Swap roles.
5. Sharing round: one pile of blocks, two builders. "How can we share so it
   is fair?" Accept: take turns adding, split the pile, build together.
   Try the learner's idea for one timer round.
6. If the learner is verbal: "Tell me: what does the timer do?" *(It tells
   us when the turn passes — it is the fair boss.)*

## Independent practice

The learner and the adult (or a puppet "friend") build one tower together
with a single shared set of blocks and the timer: alternate adding one block
per turn for three full rounds. The adult stays silent except to flip the
timer, and observes: does the learner pass without grabbing? Accept "not
yet" calmly? When finished: "Was that fair? How do you know?" The adult
scribes one dictated sentence for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner directs the adult's hands ("your
turn — add the red block… now pass") instead of building themself; or points
to the picture to answer "who is passing? who is waiting?"

## Application

"Today, notice one turn you take — with a toy, on the slide, in a game.
Tonight tell your grown-up: 'I took a fair turn today. I waited and I
passed.'" The learner practices naming their own fair behavior.

## Exit check

The adult asks: "Show me a fair turn with the timer." The learner should:
take the toy for one sand run, pass it when the sand runs out, and show a
calm waiting pose. Then: "What does 'not yet' mean?" (Your turn is coming —
not "no.") The adult records: secure / developing / not yet, for both parts.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "turn," "pass," "wait," and "not yet" by doing
  first, naming second. The gestures (handing over, folded hands) carry the
  meaning before the words do. Accept home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing at the picture, directing the adult's
  hands, puppet play.
- **Floor-play variant:** sit on the floor with the blocks spread out; the
  timer sits in the middle like a referee; the learner crawls to "pass" the
  timer with ceremony.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table; use a small
  tray so the toy and timer have a defined "passing zone."

## Extension

"Three puppets, one toy. You are the fair boss. Run three fair turns and
narrate what you do." (The learner practices the adult's role — previews
the Turn-Captain Fair project.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "fair means I get it first / I get more" —
return to the definition every time: fair means everyone gets a turn, and
the timer decides the order; (2) "waiting means doing nothing, so it's
boring" — teach waiting as an active skill (watch, plan your next move);
(3) grabbing and calling it "sharing" — name it: "Taking is not sharing.
Sharing is when the giver chooses to pass"; (4) the learner believes the
adult's turn doesn't count — it does; the adult must pass too, visibly.
