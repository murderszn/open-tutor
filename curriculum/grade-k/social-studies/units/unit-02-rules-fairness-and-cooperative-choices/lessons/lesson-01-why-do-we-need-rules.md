# Lesson 01 — Why Do We Need Rules?

**Student goal:** I can say why we need rules: rules keep people safe and
keep things fair.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- Two puppets or stuffed animals
- A small set of wooden blocks
- 4–6 rule picture cards (drawn by the adult): a child walking in a hallway,
  children sharing blocks, a child running with scissors (crossed out),
  children shouting over each other, a hand holding a door, a child grabbing
  a toy

**Privacy note for the adult:** this lesson never asks the learner to say or
write their name, address, school, or family details. Restate the Unit 01
safety rule at the start: "Names, addresses, and photos stay private."

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Raise your hand if you have ever played a game." (Most children will.)
"Today we play a game with NO rules, and then the SAME game with one rule.
Watch what changes." If the learner is ready to watch and react, begin.

## Teaching

A **rule** is a "we" agreement — it tells everyone in the group what to do.
Our classroom has rules. Your home has rules. The playground has rules.

Rules exist for two reasons. Say them with me:

1. **Safe.** A rule keeps bodies safe. "Walking feet in the hallway" keeps
   everyone safe because running feet can knock someone down.
2. **Fair.** A rule keeps things fair. "One person talks at a time" is fair
   because then every person's words get heard.

(Gesture: arms crossed over chest like a shield for "safe"; hands held out
even, like a balance scale, for "fair.")

Here is the big test for any rule: **does it keep someone safe, or does it
keep something fair?** If yes, it is probably a good rule. If it does
neither, it is probably not a rule at all — just someone's wish.

## Worked example 1 — the no-rules game vs. the one-rule game (modeled)

The adult and two puppets build a small block tower.

> "Round one: NO rules. Go!" The adult lets the puppets knock the tower
> down, run off with blocks, and shout over each other for about 30 seconds.
> Then freeze. "How did that feel? Was anyone safe? Was it fair? Who got to
> build?" *(Unsafe, unfair — nobody got a real turn.)*
>
> "Round two: the SAME game, but with ONE rule: we build together, and we
> do not knock." The puppets take turns adding one block each, slowly and
> carefully. The tower stands. "How does this feel now? Who got to build?"
> *(Everyone. The rule made it fair — and nobody's tower got smashed, so it
> stayed safe too.)*

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the rule did not take the fun away — it made
the fun possible. Without the rule, only the loudest puppet got to play.

## Worked example 2 — a safety rule and a fairness rule (modeled)

Hold up two picture cards.

> "This card shows walking feet in the hallway. Why is that a rule? Because
> running feet can crash into someone and hurt them. It keeps bodies
> **safe**." *(Shield gesture.)*
>
> "This card shows children sharing blocks. The rule is: everyone gets a
> turn with the blocks. Why? Because if one child keeps all the blocks, the
> others get nothing. The rule keeps things **fair**." *(Scale gesture.)*

**Reasoning to name aloud:** every good rule answers the question "safe or
fair?" — sometimes both at once.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Hold up the "child running with scissors" card (crossed out). "Is 'no
   running with scissors' a rule? Safe or fair?" *(A rule — safe.)*
2. Hold up the "children shouting over each other" card. "What rule would
   fix this? Safe or fair?" *(One person talks at a time — fair.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult announces, very seriously: "New rule!
   Everyone must eat pickles for lunch!" Ask: "Is that a real rule? Does it
   keep someone safe? Does it keep something fair?" *(Expected: no — it is
   not about safety or fairness. It is just someone's wish. Rules are about
   safe and fair.)*
4. "Give me a rule from YOUR day — at home or here." Accept anything; help
   the learner test it: "Safe or fair?" (If the learner offers "bedtime,"
   help them find it: "Bedtime keeps your body rested and safe — good.")
5. Puppet round: a puppet says, "Rules are only for little kids. Grown-ups
   do not need rules." Ask: "Is the puppet right? Do grown-ups follow
   rules?" *(Expected: no — grown-ups follow rules too: stop signs, quiet
   voices in the library. Rules are for everyone.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: rules keep us ___ and
   ___." *(Safe and fair.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws "a rule that keeps us safe" on one half of a paper and "a
rule that keeps things fair" on the other half. The adult labels each half
with the dictated rule ("no running by the pool" / "everyone gets a turn").
The adult sits nearby and observes: did the learner choose two different
ideas? When finished, the adult asks, "Which one is the safe rule? Which one
is the fair rule?" and scribes the answers for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner sorts pre-drawn rule picture cards
into two piles — "safe rules" and "fair rules" — and explains one card from
each pile.

## Application

"Tonight, notice one rule at home. Ask your grown-up: 'Is this rule about
safe or fair?' Tell me tomorrow." The learner practices the safe/fair test in
a real setting. (If there is no "tomorrow" — one-on-one setting — the learner
reports back at the next session.)

## Exit check

The adult asks: "Why do we need rules?" The learner should indicate (words,
the two gestures, or pointing to the two sides of their drawing) that rules
keep people **safe** and keep things **fair**. The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "Is 'everyone must wear blue socks' a
real rule? Why not?" (It is neither safe nor fair — it is a wish.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "safe" and "fair" by doing first, naming second.
  Act out unsafe/unfair before saying the words. Accept home-language words
  alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, gesture (shield/scale), sorting
  cards, drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** build the no-rules/one-rule tower game on the
  floor with big soft blocks; the learner plays the "rule keeper" who calls
  "freeze!" when the rule breaks.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the puppets
  and picture cards.

## Extension

"Invent a rule for our stuffed animals' classroom. Test it: safe or fair?"
(Previews the rule co-creation in the project.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "rules are punishments" — reframe from day
one: rules are *protections* and *promises*, not punishments; the repair
lesson (L5) carries this further; (2) "fair means everyone gets the same
thing at the same time" — park this for L2, but plant the seed: "fair means
everyone gets a turn"; (3) "rules are only for children" — the puppet round
handles it; revisit whenever the learner says "you're the grown-up, you don't
have to."

expected responses for the error-analysis rounds and exit check.
