# Lesson 04 — Rules Beyond Our Classroom

**Student goal:** I can name a school rule and a neighborhood rule, and say
what each one keeps safe or fair.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- Two puppets or stuffed animals, a toy car, a small figure
- 6 picture cards (drawn by the adult): walking feet in a hallway; quiet
  voices near a classroom door; a child stopping at a curb and looking both
  ways; a grown-up holding a child's hand near a street; a dog on a leash at
  a park; children cleaning up toys on a playground
- 3 "helper" cards: a crossing guard with a stop sign; a teacher; a
  lifeguard with a whistle

**Privacy note for the adult:** restate the safety rule at the start:
"Names, addresses, and photos stay private." When discussing the
neighborhood, keep it general — "near our street," never addresses or
specific locations tied to the learner.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Our class has rules. Raise your hand if you think the WHOLE school has
rules too." (Yes.) "What about the street outside? The park?" Today rules
leave our classroom.

## Teaching

Rules live **everywhere** people share a space — not just our classroom.
Everywhere, the test is the same: **safe or fair?**

**School rules** — rules for the whole school building:
- *Walking feet in the hallway.* Why? So nobody crashes and gets hurt.
  (Safe.)
- *Quiet voices near classroom doors.* Why? So other classes can learn.
  (Fair — everyone gets their learning time.)
- *Clean up the playground when you finish.* Why? So the next children find
  it ready. (Fair.)

**Neighborhood and community rules** — rules for streets, parks, and shared
places:
- *Stop at the curb. Look both ways. Hold a grown-up's hand.* Why? Cars are
  big and fast; this rule keeps bodies safe. (Safe.)
- *Dogs stay on leashes at the park.* Why? So every person and every dog
  stays safe. (Safe and fair.)
- *Put trash in the bin.* Why? So the park stays nice for everyone. (Fair.)

Some people have the job of helping everyone follow the rules — **rule
helpers**: the crossing guard at the street, the teacher in the hallway, the
lifeguard at the pool. They are not rule punishers; they are rule helpers.
They keep the safe-and-fair promise for the whole group.

## Worked example 1 — the curb rule, acted out (modeled)

Set up: a "street" (a strip of paper), a "curb" (a book), the toy car, the
small figure.

> "Scene one: NO rule. The figure runs straight into the street without
> stopping." Roll the car past, close to the figure. Freeze. "What could
> happen? Was that safe?" *(No — the car is big and fast.)*
>
> "Scene two: WITH the rule. Stop at the curb." The figure stops at the
> book. "Look both ways." Turn the figure's head left, then right. "Hold a
> grown-up's hand." The adult's hand "holds" the figure. "Now cross." Walk
> the figure across after the car passes. "What kept the figure safe?"
> *(Stopping, looking, holding hands — the rule.)*

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the rule is not "don't cross the street" — it
is "cross the street the safe way." Good rules tell you what TO do, not just
what not to do.

## Worked example 2 — match the rule to the helper (modeled)

Lay out the three helper cards. Hold up rule picture cards one at a time.

> "Who helps with the street-crossing rule?" *(The crossing guard — hold up
> the stop-sign card.)* "The crossing guard's job is to help everyone cross
> safely. The guard does not make the rule to be bossy — the guard keeps the
> safe promise."
>
> "Who helps with hallway walking-feet?" *(The teacher.)* "Who helps with
> pool rules?" *(The lifeguard with the whistle.)*
>
> "Notice: every helper's job is the same — help everyone stay safe and
> fair. Just like our classroom helper jobs in Unit 01, every job helps."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** rule helpers exist because rules belong to
everyone — and everyone sometimes needs help remembering.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds.

1. Sort the six picture cards into two piles: "school rules" and
   "neighborhood rules." For each card: "Safe or fair?"
2. "Tell me a rule from YOUR school or your walk." Accept anything; test it
   together: "Safe or fair?" (Redirect gently if the learner offers an
   address or specific location: "Near our street is enough — no addresses.")
3. Helper match: hold up a new rule card (e.g., "no running by the pool").
   "Which helper keeps this rule?" (The lifeguard.) Do two more.
4. Error-analysis round: the adult announces, very seriously: "New pool
   rule: everyone must splash as much as possible!" Ask: "Is that a real
   rule? What IS the pool rule for?" *(Expected: no — splashing more is not
   safe or fair; the real rule is about staying safe in the water, like
   "walk, don't run" and "a grown-up stays with you.")*
5. Puppet round: a puppet says, "I don't need the curb rule — I'm fast!"
   Ask: "What do you tell the puppet?" *(Expected: the rule is for everyone;
   fast does not make cars slower. Stop, look, hold hands.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Name one school rule and one neighborhood
   rule. Safe or fair — for each?" (The exit check, previewed.)

## Independent practice

The learner draws a two-panel "rules map": panel one shows a school rule
being followed (e.g., walking feet in the hallway); panel two shows a
neighborhood rule being followed (e.g., stopping at the curb). The adult
scribes each rule under its panel in the learner's words. The adult observes:
did the learner choose one school and one neighborhood rule? Can they say
the safe/fair reason for each? The drawing goes to private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner acts out both rules with the puppets
while the adult narrates and scribes; or sorts additional rule cards the
adult draws on the spot.

## Application

"On your next walk or drive, spot one rule helper — a crossing guard, a
stop sign, a lifeguard chair, a 'quiet please' sign. Tell your grown-up what
rule they help keep, and whether it is about safe or fair." The learner
practices rule-spotting in the real world.

## Exit check

The adult asks: "Name one school rule and one neighborhood rule." For each:
"What does it keep safe or fair?" The learner may point to their drawing or
the picture cards. The adult records: secure / developing / not yet, for the
rule-naming and the safe/fair reasoning separately.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "curb," "leash," and "lifeguard" with the
  picture cards first, word second. Act out "stop, look, hold hands" as a
  three-beat chant with motions. Accept home-language words alongside
  English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, sorting cards, puppet acting,
  drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** build the street-and-curb scene big on the floor;
  the learner IS the figure (stops at the "curb" pillow, looks both ways,
  holds the adult's hand to cross).
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the toy
  car, figure, and cards.

## Extension

"If YOU were a rule helper for one day, which job would you pick — crossing
guard, teacher, or lifeguard? Show me doing the job." (Dramatic play;
previews Unit 03's community helpers.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "rule helpers are punishers" — reframe every
time: helpers keep the promise, they don't hand out trouble; (2) "the curb
rule is only for little kids" — no: grown-ups stop and look too; (3) the
learner names a family rule as a "neighborhood rule" — accept it warmly and
help sort: "that one is a home rule — home rules count too, but let's find
one for the street"; (4) anxiety about streets/cars — keep the tone
matter-of-fact and empowering: the rule is the tool that makes crossing
safe; never use fear as the teacher.
