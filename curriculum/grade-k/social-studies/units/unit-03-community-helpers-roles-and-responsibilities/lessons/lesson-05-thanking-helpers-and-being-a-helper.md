# Lesson 05 — Thanking Helpers and Being a Helper

**Student goal:** I can say thank you to a helper respectfully, and I can
play a helper's role using the right tools and words.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 13 of 16**

## Materials

- The Helper Fair stations built in Session 11 (project Part 1)
- Thank-you cards made in Session 8 (drawings + the adult-scribed words
  "thank you" — no names, addresses, or photos)
- Helper picture cards and dress-up pieces from earlier lessons
- A "thank-you" sentence frame on a card (adult-written): "Thank you for
  ___." / "You help us by ___."

**Privacy note for the adult:** thank-you cards are delivered in person, by
the learner with a trusted adult, to school helpers the learner already
knows — never mailed to strangers, never posted, never photographed for
sharing. Cards carry drawings and "thank you," never the learner's name,
address, school name, or photo. Restate the Unit 01 safety rule at the start.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Who is one helper you are glad exists?" Take one or two answers. "Today
we practice the two parts of being grateful: saying thank you well, and
trying the helper's job ourselves." If the learner is ready to practice
both, begin.

## Teaching

Helpers do important work, and most of it is invisible — the custodian
cleans before we arrive, the sorter sorts letters we never see, the road
worker fixes the road at night. Saying thank you is how we show we noticed.

A good thank-you has three parts:

1. **Look and listen.** Face the helper, wait for a pause in their work,
   and do not interrupt something dangerous or busy (never interrupt a
   firefighter at work, a driver driving, or anyone using a machine).
2. **Say what they did.** "Thank you for keeping our school clean" is
   better than just "thanks," because it names the work.
3. **Say how it helps.** "You help us by keeping our rooms safe" tells the
   helper you understand the job, not just the person.

City helpers deserve thanks, too. **City helpers** are the people whose job
is to care for the whole city: road workers who fix roads, trash collectors
who keep streets clean, librarians who keep the library open for everyone.
The city is like a big school, and these helpers are how the city takes
care of itself. (This is the kindergarten version of the C3 idea that
governments provide goods and services — D2.Civ.5.K-2 and D2.Eco.12.K-2,
adapted. The word "government" itself is not tested.)

And then the second part: **being a helper.** Anyone can help, including
children — in small, safe ways, with a grown-up's permission: holding a door,
helping a friend who dropped their crayons, putting toys away, saying kind
words. Being a helper is not about having the tools; it is about noticing
what is needed and doing your part.

## Worked example 1 — a good thank-you, modeled (modeled)

The adult plays the custodian (with a broom), and a puppet plays the
learner:

> Puppet: (waits until the adult pauses) "Excuse me. Thank you for
> sweeping our floor." Adult: "You are welcome!" Puppet: "You help us by
> keeping our room clean and safe."
>
> "Did you hear the three parts? Wait for a pause — say what they did —
> say how it helps. Now the puppet tries a weak thank-you: 'Thanks.'
> That is polite, but it does not name the work. Strong thank-yous name
> the work."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** naming the work shows you actually noticed
it. That is what makes a thank-you feel real instead of automatic.

## Worked example 2 — playing a helper, with a mistake (modeled)

The adult plays a doctor with the listening tube, but does it wrong:

> "I am the doctor! I listen to the fire hose!" (Holds the tube to the
> coiled rope.) "Hmm, the hose has no heartbeat. Something is wrong!"
>
> "What went wrong? The doctor used the wrong tool on the wrong thing.
> A doctor listens to people, not hoses. Let me try again: I am the
> doctor. I listen to your heartbeat — lub-dub, lub-dub. That is a
> healthy heartbeat. The tool, the person, and the job all match."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** playing a helper means matching three things:
the uniform, the tool, and the job words. If one is wrong, the play shows
it — and fixing it is the learning.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Puppet thank-you: a puppet plays a grocer. The learner practices the
   three parts: wait, name the work ("thank you for selling food"), name
   the help ("you help us by giving us food to eat"). *(Any version with
   all three parts counts.)*
2. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "I will thank
   the bus driver while she is driving fast on a busy road — I will shout
   through the window!" Ask: "Is that a good time? Why not?" *(Expected:
   no — never interrupt someone doing a dangerous or busy job. Wait for
   a safe pause, or thank them later with a card.)*
3. City helper round: "Name one city helper and one thing they do for the
   whole city." *(Road worker fixes roads; trash collector keeps streets
   clean; librarian keeps the library open.)*
4. Role-play: the learner picks a helper station (firefighter, nurse,
   mail carrier, grocer), puts on the dress-up piece, picks the right
   tool, and says one job sentence ("I deliver mail!" / "I check your
   heartbeat!"). *(Tool, uniform clue, and job words should match.)*
5. "Name one small, safe way a child can be a helper at school."
   *(Hold a door, help pick up crayons, put toys away, use kind words —
   with a grown-up's permission.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: thank you for ___.
   You help us by ___." *(Any matched pair counts: "thank you for driving
   the bus; you help us by getting us to school safely.")*

## Independent practice

The learner finishes one thank-you card for a real school helper: a drawing
of the helper at work (with the right tool) and the adult-scribed words
"thank you for ___" with the learner's dictated job words. The adult
observes: does the drawing show the helper's actual job and tool? Then the
learner practices delivering it to a puppet first: wait for a pause, say
the sentence, hand over the card. The card is kept for Session 14's real
delivery.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner points to the helper card, points
to the tool, and mimes handing over the card while the adult says the
sentence aloud.

## Application

"At home, notice one helper — a grown-up cooking, a neighbor carrying
groceries, anyone doing work that helps. Say a three-part thank-you, with
your grown-up's permission, or tell me about it at our next session."
The learner practices noticing invisible work and naming it.

## Exit check

The adult plays a helper (for example, the school nurse with a bandage) and
asks the learner to (a) give a three-part thank-you and (b) then swap
roles: the learner plays the nurse, picks the bandage, and says one job
sentence. The learner should complete at least two of the three thank-you
parts and match tool to job in the role-play. The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "When should you NOT interrupt a
helper?" *(When they are driving, using a machine, or doing dangerous
work — wait for a safe pause.)*

## Supports

- **Language access:** the sentence frame ("Thank you for ___ / You help
  us by ___.") carries the language; the learner supplies only the job
  words. Accept home-language thank-yous alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, mime, handing over the card,
  drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** set up the Helper Fair stations on the floor;
  the learner walks the "thank-you route" from station to station.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  puppet, the cards, and the thank-you frame.

## Extension

"Which helper would you like to be when you grow up? What tools would you
need, and what would you need to learn?" (Any answer counts; the adult
names one real skill for the chosen job — "a doctor learns about bodies
for many years" — without turning it into a career lesson.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "thanking means giving a gift" — words and
drawings are enough; never send money or personal items; (2) "I can thank
any helper anywhere" — the safety rule comes first: in person only with a
trusted adult, only to helpers the learner already knows at school, never
by approaching strangers; (3) "being a helper means doing grown-up jobs" —
children's helping is small, safe, and permitted: doors, crayons, kind
words — never machines, roads, or fires.
