# Lesson 03 — Helpers Work Together: A Letter's Journey

**Student goal:** I can tell, in order, how a letter travels from a mailbox
to a house, naming at least two helpers who pass it along.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 7 of 16**

## Materials

- Four envelopes (one marked with a drawn star, to follow through the
  journey)
- A shoebox "mailbox," a shoebox "mail truck," and a paper "house" (a folded
  card with a door drawn on it)
- Helper picture cards: mail carrier, mail sorter (a second mail carrier
  card is fine — sorting is part of the mail carrier's chain), truck driver,
  plus the Lesson 02 cards for retrieval
- A strip of paper with four boxes: mailbox → sorting → truck → house

**Privacy note for the adult:** the letter in this lesson is pretend. It
carries a drawn star, never a real name or address. If the learner wants to
"send" a real letter, the adult handles addressing privately, outside the
lesson, and nothing with personal details is shared in class. Restate the
Unit 01 safety rule at the start.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Who delivers our mail?" (The mail carrier.) "Does the mail carrier do it
all alone — does one person carry your letter the whole way?" Take guesses;
do not correct yet. "Today we follow one letter and count how many helpers
touch it." If the learner is ready to follow a journey, begin.

## Teaching

Helpers rarely work alone. Most jobs are a **chain**: one helper does a
part, then passes the work to the next helper, who does the next part.
When helpers work together like this, big jobs get done that no one person
could do alone.

Here is a letter's journey, in order:

1. **A person posts the letter.** They drop it in a mailbox. (In our story,
   the letter is a thank-you card with a star on it.)
2. **A mail carrier collects the letter.** The mail carrier opens the
   mailbox and puts the letter in a mailbag.
3. **A mail sorter sorts the letter.** At a big mail building, a sorter
   reads where the letter goes and puts it with letters going to the same
   place. Sorting means "putting things in the right piles."
4. **A truck driver drives the letter.** A truck carries bags of sorted
   letters toward the town they belong to.
5. **A mail carrier delivers the letter.** A mail carrier near the house
   carries the letter the last part of the way and puts it in the house's
   mailbox.

Five steps, several helpers, one letter. If any helper in the chain stops,
the letter stops too. That is why helpers need each other — and why we say
thank you to all of them, not just the last one we see.

The same idea works for people, not just letters. If a child feels sick at
school: the school nurse helps first, then a grown-up takes the child to
the doctor, and the doctor helps next. Nurse → grown-up → doctor is a
helping chain, too.

## Worked example 1 — acting out the letter's journey (modeled)

The adult acts out the journey with the props, narrating each step, while
the learner moves the star envelope along the paper strip:

> "Step one: I drop the star letter in the mailbox. Step two: I am the mail
> carrier — I collect it into my bag. Step three: I am the sorter — I put
> it in the pile for the star town. Step four: I am the truck driver — vroom,
> the truck drives the bag to the town. Step five: I am the mail carrier
> again — I put the letter in the house's mailbox. The star letter arrived!
> Count the helpers with me: carrier, sorter, driver, carrier. Four helper
> jobs, one letter."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the letter did not teleport. It moved because
people moved it, one step at a time, in order. Order matters: the truck
cannot drive the letter before the sorter sorts it.

## Worked example 2 — a mixed-up journey (modeled)

The adult deliberately acts the journey out of order:

> "Step one: the truck driver drives an empty truck. Step two: the mail
> carrier delivers a letter she never collected. Step three: the sorter
> sorts letters that never arrived."

Then freeze. "Did that work? No — the steps are mixed up. A chain only
works in order: collect, sort, drive, deliver. Put them right, and the
letter arrives."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** helpers working together is not just "lots of
people." It is people doing their parts in the right order, like links in
a chain. One missing link breaks the chain.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. "What happens first: the truck drives, or the mail carrier collects?"
   *(The mail carrier collects first.)*
2. Lay out the four step cards out of order (truck, deliver, collect,
   sort). "Put them in order, left to right." *(Collect → sort → drive →
   deliver.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "The letter flew
   straight from the mailbox to the house all by itself!" Ask: "Is that
   right? Who moved it?" *(Expected: no — helpers moved it, step by step:
   carrier, sorter, driver, carrier.)*
4. Second chain: "A child feels sick at school. Who helps first? Who helps
   next?" *(The school nurse first; then a grown-up takes the child to the
   doctor.)*
5. "Name one helper from the letter chain and tell me their step."
   *(Any of: carrier collects, sorter sorts, driver drives, carrier
   delivers.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: helpers work together
   because ___." *(One helper cannot do the whole job alone; accept any
   version of "the job is too big for one person.")*

## Independent practice

The learner draws the letter's journey in four boxes on a strip of paper:
mailbox, sorting piles, truck, house — with a star on the letter in every
box. The adult labels each box with the dictated step word ("collect,"
"sort," "drive," "deliver"). The adult observes: are the boxes in the
right order? When finished, the adult asks, "Who helped at each step?" and
scribes the answer for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner moves the star envelope along the
props in order and names each helper's step as they go.

## Application

"Watch for a chain at home or school: one person starts something, another
person finishes it. For example, someone cooks, someone else sets the
table. Tell me the chain at our next session." The learner practices
spotting helping chains in real life.

## Exit check

The adult hands the learner the star envelope and the four props out of
order and asks: "Show me the letter's journey, in order, and name the
helper at each step." The learner should place at least three of the four
steps in order and name at least two helpers. The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "What happens if the sorter does not
sort?" *(The letter does not get to the right town; the chain breaks.)*

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "chain" by linking paper rings or holding
  hands in a line first, word second. "Sort" is taught by sorting blocks
  into piles before it is used for letters. Accept home-language words
  alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, moving props in order, drawing
  the four boxes.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay the props across the floor as a path; the
  learner walks the letter along the path, stepping on each station.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  paper strip and the star envelope.

## Extension

"City helpers work in chains, too. Who fixes a road? First a worker marks
the broken spot, then a truck brings the fix, then workers smooth the road.
And who keeps the library open? Librarians, cleaners, and the people who
bring new books — a chain again." (Previews the city-helper idea in Lesson
05 and the Helper Fair stations.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "the mail carrier I see does everything" —
the visible helper is usually only the last link; name the invisible links
(sorter, driver) explicitly; (2) "order does not matter" — the mixed-up
journey shows why it does; (3) "machines do it, not people" — trucks and
sorting machines are tools; people still drive, sort, and deliver. Keep the
focus on people and their jobs.
