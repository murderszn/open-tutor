# Lesson 04 — Tools Helpers Use

**Student goal:** I can match helpers to their tools, say what each tool is
for, and tell what would go wrong if a helper had the wrong tool.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- Helper picture cards for all twelve helpers met so far: teacher,
  custodian, school nurse, bus driver, firefighter, mail carrier, grocer,
  doctor, librarian, crossing guard, trash collector, road worker
- Tool cards or toy stand-ins: book, broom, bandage, toy bus, hose (coiled
  rope), mailbag, basket of apples, listening tube, book (again — librarian
  and teacher share the book tool; see Worked example 2), stop sign,
  trash bin, shovel
- A "toolbox" (any box) to hold the tool cards

**Privacy note for the adult:** no personal information is needed in this
lesson. Tools are matched to jobs, never to real people the learner knows.
Restate the Unit 01 safety rule at the start.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Hold up the broom. "Whose tool is this? What job does it do?" (Custodian;
sweeping.) "Today every helper brings a tool — and some tools try to sneak
into the wrong job." If the learner is ready to match and catch mistakes,
begin.

## Teaching

A **tool** is something a helper uses to do a job. Tools are not magic —
they are matched to the job. A hose sprays water, so it matches a job that
needs water. A stethoscope listens to bodies, so it matches a job that
checks bodies. A mailbag carries letters, so it matches a job that delivers
letters.

Three rules for tools:

1. **Every job has its tools.** Ask: "What does this job need?"
2. **Tools can be shared.** A teacher and a librarian both use books — the
   same tool, different jobs. Sharing a tool does not mean sharing a job.
3. **The wrong tool cannot do the job.** A firefighter cannot put out a
   fire with a basket of apples. A grocer cannot sell food with a fire
   hose. Matching matters.

Helpers also need **skills** — things they know how to do. A bus driver
knows how to drive safely. A nurse knows how to put on a bandage gently. A
firefighter knows how to stay safe near a fire. Tools plus skills make a
helper ready: the tool is the thing, the skill is the knowing.

## Worked example 1 — the tool test, three helpers (modeled)

Lay out the hose, mailbag, and listening tube. For each, ask the three
tool questions:

> "Hose: who uses it? The firefighter. What is it for? Spraying water.
> What job needs water? Putting out fires. Match!"
>
> "Mailbag: who uses it? The mail carrier. What is it for? Carrying
> letters. What job needs letters carried? Delivering mail. Match!"
>
> "Listening tube: who uses it? The doctor (and the nurse, for listening
> to bodies at school). What is it for? Hearing a heartbeat. What job
> needs to hear a heartbeat? Checking that a body is healthy. Match!"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we matched by asking "what is it for?" first,
not "who looks like they hold it?" The purpose of the tool points to the
job.

## Worked example 2 — one tool, two jobs (modeled)

Hold up the book, then the teacher card and the librarian card.

> "Whose tool is a book? The teacher — she reads stories to teach us. And
> the librarian — he helps us find books in the library. Same tool, two
> different jobs. The teacher's job is teaching; the librarian's job is
> helping us find and borrow books. A shared tool does not make them the
> same helper."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** this stops the "one tool, one helper" trap.
Learners who insist every tool belongs to exactly one helper will be
confused by books, by thermometers (nurse and doctor), and by trucks
(bus driver, trash collector, mail truck driver). Tools match jobs by
purpose, and purposes can repeat.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Hold up the stop sign. "Whose tool is this? What does it do?" *(The
   crossing guard's; it tells cars to stop so children can cross safely.)*
2. Hold up the shovel. "A road worker uses this. What job needs digging
   and smoothing?" *(Fixing roads.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult lays out the firefighter card with the
   basket of apples and says, very seriously: "All ready! The firefighter
   will put out the fire with apples!" Ask: "Will that work? What tool
   does the firefighter really need?" *(Expected: no — apples cannot spray
   water. The firefighter needs a hose.)*
4. "Which two helpers use a book? Are their jobs the same?" *(Teacher and
   librarian; no — teaching vs. helping find and borrow books.)*
5. Toolbox scramble: put six tool cards in the box. The learner draws one,
   names the tool, and places it with its helper card. Repeat for all six.
   *(Book → teacher or librarian; broom → custodian; bandage → nurse;
   hose → firefighter; mailbag → mail carrier; apples → grocer.)*
6. Skill round: "A tool is the thing. A skill is the knowing. What does a
   bus driver need to KNOW, besides having a bus?" *(How to drive safely;
   when to stop; how to keep children safe on the bus.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws a "toolbox page": three helpers down the left side and
each helper's tool on the right, joined by a drawn line (helper → tool).
The adult labels each drawing with the dictated words. The adult observes:
are the lines matched by purpose (hose to firefighter, not to grocer)?
Then the adult asks, "What would go wrong with the wrong tool?" for one
pair, and scribes the answer for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner places toy tools on the matching
helper cards and mimes using each tool (sweeping, listening, spraying).

## Application

"Look at the tools in your home with a grown-up. Pick one tool and tell
me at our next session: what is it for, and whose job needs it?" (A spoon
for cooking, a key for locking — any sensible pair counts.) The learner
practices the "what is it for?" test outside the helper set.

## Exit check

The adult lays out four helper cards (firefighter, grocer, mail carrier,
doctor) and four tool cards (hose, apples, mailbag, listening tube) mixed
up, and asks the learner to match them all and say one tool's purpose for
each match. The learner should match at least three of the four correctly
and state at least two purposes. The adult records: secure / developing /
not yet. A second probe: "Can the grocer use the hose to sell apples?"
*(No — wrong tool for the job.)*

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "tool" and "skill" by showing first: hold the
  tool, mime the skill. "Tool = the thing. Skill = the knowing." Accept
  home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, placing toys on cards, drawing
  lines, mime.
- **Floor-play variant:** scatter helper cards on the floor; the learner
  "delivers" each tool to its helper by walking it over.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  cards and the toolbox.

## Extension

"Which helper has the most tools? Which helper needs no tools at all — just
their voice and their kindness?" (A crossing guard's stop sign plus voice;
a teacher's voice is a tool, too. This previews Lesson 05: thanking helpers
is about the person, not only the tools.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "one tool belongs to one helper only" —
Worked example 2 handles books and thermometers; (2) "bigger tools mean
more important jobs" — a bandage is small and a fire truck is big, but both
jobs matter; importance is not size; (3) "a tool does the job by itself" —
the skill round corrects this: a hose without a trained firefighter sprays
water nowhere useful. Tools plus skills make a helper ready.
