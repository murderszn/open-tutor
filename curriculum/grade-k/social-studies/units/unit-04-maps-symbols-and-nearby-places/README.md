# Unit 04 — Maps, Symbols, and Nearby Places

**Grade:** Kindergarten · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track weeks:** 15–18
**Backlog:** [issue #9](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/9), section U04
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

A map is a picture of a place seen from above. In this unit the learner
first learns to see a room the way a bird sees it — from overhead — and
then reads a picture map of a classroom, makes symbols and a legend for
it, and uses words like *near*, *far*, *left*, *right*, *above*,
*below*, *next to*, and *between* to find things and give directions.
Then the map grows: from the classroom to the school to the
neighborhood, and finally to a globe, where the learner finds land and
water and where "our town" sits — on land, among other places. The unit
ends with a Classroom Map Museum, where the learner's own map guides a
friend (or the adult) on a treasure hunt. Week 18 is also the midyear
review: the final sessions revisit Units 01–04 together (community,
rules, helpers, maps) before the track moves on to Unit 05.

**Privacy by design:** maps in this unit show classrooms, schools, and
an imaginary neighborhood — never a real home address, a real school
name, a real street, or a route a child actually walks alone. The
neighborhood map is a made-up practice neighborhood with a school, a
park, a store, a library, and a fire station. The learner never draws
their own home, never labels where they live, and never shares a map
outside the learning setting. The adult keeps all learner maps in
private storage. School walks happen only with a trusted adult, only on
school grounds or paths the adult has approved, and with an indoor
tabletop alternative for any learner who cannot or should not walk the
route.

## Essential question

How can a map show us where things are?

## Prerequisites

- Unit 01: identity, belonging, and classroom community (belonging
  routines; the safety rule "names, addresses, and photos stay private"
  is restated every session — it is the rule that makes map work safe)
- Unit 02: rules, fairness, and cooperative choices (walking rules and
  fair turns, retrieved in Sessions 4 and 10: we walk quietly, we wait
  our turn to point, we follow the adult's route)
- Unit 03: community helpers: roles and responsibilities (helpers'
  workplaces — school, fire station, library, store — are the places on
  the neighborhood map in Lesson 04; retrieved in Sessions 4 and 15)
- Can sit for a 5–10 minute circle time with movement breaks
- Follows one-step directions; two-step directions emerging
- No reading or writing required. The adult reads everything aloud and scribes.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. Explain, in child words, what a map is: a picture of a place seen
   from above that shows where things are.
2. Read a simple pictorial classroom map: using the legend, point to at
   least four places or symbols on the generated classroom map (for
   example, the rug, the reading corner, a table, the door).
3. Use positional words — *near*, *far*, *left*, *right*, *above*,
   *below*, *next to*, *between* — to describe where things are on a
   map and in the room, with at least four correct uses.
4. Follow a simple two-step route on a classroom or school map (for
   example, "go to the door, then turn to the rug") and give a
   two-step route for someone else to follow.
5. Build a pictorial map of a familiar place with at least three
   symbols and a legend, and, on a globe, point to water (blue) and
   land (the other colors) and say that our town is on land.

**Teacher background (verified 2026-10-29):** National Geographic
Education, "Places in the Park" activity
(education.nationalgeographic.org) — a map key tells what each symbol
stands for, and learners practice location words (*near*, *far*,
*between*, *next to*) on a park map before drawing a map of a familiar
place with symbols. National Geographic Education blog, "Introducing
Maps to Little Kids" (a kindergarten teacher's classroom-mapping
activity) — the bird's-eye shift ("imagine you are a fly on the
ceiling") and translating between a projected map and the real room by
walking to pointed-to spots. U.S. Geological Survey, "Cartographic
representation" (usgs.gov) — symbols are how information is displayed
on a map, and water has long been shown in blue. See
[resource-pack.md](resource-pack.md).

## Accessibility

Every check accepts oral, pointing, drawing, or gesture responses; the adult
scribes. Each lesson includes a seated-table and a floor-play variant, a
language-access support, an alternate response mode, and an extension.
Symbols are never color-only: every symbol is also a distinct shape, so a
learner who cannot distinguish colors can still match by shape or place.
The generated illustration ships with a text-only alternative carrying
every place and symbol the activities ask about. The globe work has a
touch alternative (a textured land-and-water ball) for low-vision
learners, and the school walk always has a tabletop alternative.
