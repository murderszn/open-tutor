# Lesson 05 — Land and Water on a Globe

**Student goal:** I can point to water (blue) and land (the other
colors) on a globe, and say that our town is on land.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 12 of 16**

## Materials

- A simple globe (or a ball colored blue with green/brown patches —
  a "land and water ball"; a beach ball with taped paper patches
  works)
- Blue and green/brown crayons or paper squares for the sort
- A small sticker or dot (for "our town" — placed on land, on any
  land patch; the adult does not name a real town)

**Privacy note for the adult:** "our town" is generic. The adult
never names the learner's real town, school, or address, and never
asks the learner to point to a real home on the globe. The sticker
goes on "some land, somewhere" — the idea is "towns are on land,"
not "here is where you live."

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Hold up the globe. "What shape is this? A ball. What do you think it
is a picture of?" (The Earth — accept "the world.") "Today we learn
the globe's two big colors: blue means water, and the other colors
mean land. And we find out: is our town on land or in water?" If the
learner is ready to touch and turn the globe, begin.

## Teaching

A **globe** is a map shaped like a ball. Why a ball? Because the
**Earth** — our planet, the world we live on — is shaped like a
ball. A flat map has to stretch the Earth out; a globe shows it the
way it really is shaped.

A globe has two main kinds of places:

- **Water** — oceans, seas, lakes, rivers. On a globe, water is
  **blue**. This is the oldest map color rule there is: mapmakers
  have drawn water blue for a very long time, so blue means water on
  almost every map and globe you will ever see.
- **Land** — the ground we walk on, build on, and grow things on.
  On a globe, land is the other colors: green, brown, yellow, tan.
  Different globes use different land colors, and that is fine —
  land is "not blue."

Turn the globe slowly together.

> "See all this blue? That is water — so much water! Now see these
> patches of green and brown? That is land. Land is where towns are.
> Land is where schools are. Land is where we are."

Put the sticker on a land patch.

> "This dot is 'our town.' Is it on blue or on land colors? Land.
> Towns are on land, because people live on land. We do not live in
> the ocean — fish do. So: our town is on land."

One more idea, said simply: "The Earth has more water than land. If
you look at the whole globe, you see more blue than green and brown.
That is why Earth is sometimes called the blue planet — but at this
age, 'more blue than land' is enough. We do not count oceans or name
continents today."

**Gesture:** for "water," make waves with a flat hand; for "land,"
pat a flat, firm surface. The learner waves for water and pats for
land each time the adult points to a patch.

## Worked example 1 — sorting patches, step by step (modeled)

Point to three patches on the globe, one at a time.

> "This patch is blue. Wave your hand — water! This patch is green.
> Pat the table — land! This patch is blue again. Wave — water!
> Blue means water, every time. Green, brown, yellow, tan mean land,
> every time. That rule never changes on this globe."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we did not guess from the shape of the
patch. We used the color rule: blue = water, not-blue = land. Color
rules are like legends — they tell you what a color means.

## Worked example 2 — is our town on land? (modeled)

Point to the sticker on the land patch, then point to a big blue area.

> "Our town's dot is here, on green. Is green water or land? Land.
> So our town is on land. Could a town be here, in the middle of the
> blue? No — towns are not in the ocean. If someone put a town dot
> in the blue, that would be a mistake, and we would fix it by
> moving the dot to land."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** this is error-checking with a rule. A
town in the ocean breaks the rule "towns are on land," so we know it
is wrong without anyone telling us — the rule tells us.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. The adult points to a blue patch. "Water or land? Show me the
   gesture." *(Wave — water.)*
2. The adult points to a green patch. "Water or land? Show me the
   gesture." *(Pat — land.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult puts a second sticker in the
   middle of a blue patch and says, very seriously: "Here is another
   town!" Ask: "Is that right? What is wrong?" *(No — towns are on
   land, not in water. Move the dot to a land patch.)*
4. "Is there more blue or more land on this globe? Look at the whole
   ball." *(More blue — more water than land.)*
5. "Point to land. Point to water. Point to our town's dot. Is our
   town on land or water?" *(Land.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: on a globe, blue
   means ___." *(Water.)* "Our town is on ___." *(Land.)*

## Independent practice

The learner gets the blue and green/brown paper squares and a blank
globe outline (a big circle drawn by the adult). The learner glues
or places blue squares for water and green/brown squares for land
inside the circle, then places the "our town" dot on a land square.
The adult sits nearby and observes: is the dot on land, and can the
learner say why ("towns are on land")?

*Alternate response mode:* the learner sorts the squares into two
piles — water pile, land pile — and places the dot on the land pile,
without gluing.

## Application

"Next time you see a ball, a picture of Earth, or a weather map on a
screen, look for the blue. Ask a grown-up: 'Is that blue water?'"
(In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back at the next
session.) The learner practices the blue = water rule beyond the
globe.

## Exit check

The adult points to four patches on the globe in mixed order (blue,
green, blue, brown) and asks for each: "Water or land?" The learner
should answer at least three of the four (words or gestures). Then:
"Is our town on land or in water?" *(On land.)* The adult records:
secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "land" and "water" with touch first: a
  bowl of water to dip fingers in, a tray of dry soil or sand to pat.
  Accept home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** gestures (wave/pat), pointing,
  sorting squares, placing the dot.
- **Floor-play variant:** roll the land-and-water ball on the floor;
  wherever the learner's hand lands when it stops, they name water
  or land.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with
  the globe held still by the adult and the paper-square sort.
- **Low-vision variant:** use a textured ball (smooth blue patches
  for water, rough green/brown patches for land) so the sort works by
  touch; the adult always names the patch as well.

## Extension

"If we zoomed out from our town, past the neighborhood, past the
whole country, what would we see? The whole Earth — this globe!"
(Connects Lesson 04's zoom-out to the globe as the final zoom-out.
If the learner asks for ocean or continent names, the adult may name
one or two as a story, but names are never tested in this unit —
land and water are the tested ideas.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Blue means sky" — on a globe, the
blue is water, not sky; the sky is not drawn on a globe at all;
(2) "Land is only green" — globes vary: brown, yellow, and tan are
also land; the rule is "blue = water, other colors = land," not
"green = land"; (3) "Our town is the dot's exact spot" — the dot is
a stand-in for "a town on land," not a claim about any real place;
never let the lesson become a geography quiz about where the learner
lives.
