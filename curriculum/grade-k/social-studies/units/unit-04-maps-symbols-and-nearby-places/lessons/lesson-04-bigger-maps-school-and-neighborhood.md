# Lesson 04 — Bigger Maps: School and Neighborhood

**Student goal:** I can read a bigger map: our classroom sits inside
our school, and our school sits inside a neighborhood with other
places around it.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- Three nested paper rectangles (drawn by the adult): a small one
  labeled (by the adult) "classroom," a medium one "school," a large
  one "neighborhood" — the small one fits inside the medium one, and
  the medium one fits inside the large one
- A simple neighborhood picture map drawn by the adult on large paper:
  a school building, a park with trees, a store, a library, a fire
  station, and roads between them (all invented places — see the
  privacy note)
- The generated classroom illustration
  [`assets/classroom-birdseye-map.png`](../assets/classroom-birdseye-map.png)
  for the "zoom out" moment

**Privacy note for the adult:** the neighborhood map is invented. It
is not a map of any real town, street, or school, and it never shows
where the learner lives, what their school is called, or any route
the learner walks alone. If the learner volunteers a real place name,
thank them, do not write it on the map, and steer back to the
invented places ("On our practice map, the store is called 'the
store'"). Keep all learner maps in private storage.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Hold up the classroom picture map. "What place is this?" (A
classroom.) "Is a classroom the whole world? No! It sits inside a
bigger place. What bigger place holds a classroom?" (A school.)
"And what holds a school?" (A neighborhood — a town area with
houses, shops, and parks.) If the learner can name one bigger place,
begin.

## Teaching

Maps come in sizes. A small map shows a small place in detail: every
table, every cushion. A bigger map shows a bigger place — but it has
to leave details out, or it would not fit on the page. That trade is
the whole lesson: **the bigger the place, the fewer details the map
shows.**

Lay the three nested rectangles down, one inside the other.

> "This small rectangle is a classroom. It sits inside this medium
> rectangle — a school. The school has many classrooms, a hallway, a
> lunchroom, a playground. And the school sits inside this big
> rectangle — a neighborhood. A **neighborhood** is the area around
> a school or a home, with streets, houses, shops, parks, and places
> where helpers work."

Now show the invented neighborhood map.

> "Here is our practice neighborhood. Find the school. (Point.)
> What is next to the school? The park. What is across the road from
> the park? The store. Where do helpers work here? The fire station
> — remember the firefighters from Unit 03. The library — remember
> the librarian. A neighborhood map shows where helpers' workplaces
> are, the same way our classroom map showed where our tables are."

Point out the **roads**: the gray bands that connect the places.
"Roads are how people and helpers move from place to place. On a
neighborhood map, roads are lines and bands — you do not draw every
car, just the road."

And the zoom-out rule, said out loud: "Our classroom map showed
tables and a rug. Our school map would show classrooms and hallways
— not tables. Our neighborhood map shows whole buildings — not
rooms. Each time we zoom out, we see more places and fewer details."

**Gesture:** start with hands close together (classroom), spread
them wider (school), then wider still (neighborhood) — the "zoom
out" gesture, reused in the project.

## Worked example 1 — zooming out, step by step (modeled)

Start with the classroom picture map.

> "Step one: this is our classroom. Step two: zoom out. Now the
> classroom is one small rectangle inside a school — we can see the
> hallway and the lunchroom, but the tables are gone; they are too
> small to draw now. Step three: zoom out again. Now the school is
> one building inside a neighborhood — we can see the park, the
> store, and the fire station, but the hallway is gone too. Same
> world, three maps. Each map shows what fits at its size."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** losing details when you zoom out is
not a mistake. A neighborhood map that tried to draw every table in
every classroom would be unreadable — so it draws buildings instead.

## Worked example 2 — a route on the neighborhood map (modeled)

Put a toy (or a finger) on the school on the neighborhood map.

> "A helper at the fire station needs a book from the library. What
> is the route? First, leave the fire station and follow the road to
> the park. Then turn and follow the road to the library. Two steps:
> fire station → park → library. A **route** is the path you take
> from one place to another. On a map, a route is a line you can
> trace with your finger."

Trace it slowly, naming each place as you pass it.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** routes are why maps matter. A map is
not just for finding things — it is for getting from one thing to
another without getting lost.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. "Point to the school on the neighborhood map. Point to the park.
   Is the park near the school or far from it?" *(Near — accept the
   learner's reading of the adult's drawing; the adult's map should
   place them close together.)*
2. "What is across the road from the store?" *(Depends on the
   adult's drawing — the adult must know the answer before asking;
   a good default layout: school top-left, park top-right, store
   bottom-right, library bottom-left, fire station center-right,
   with roads crossing between them. With that layout: across the
   road from the store is the library side — the adult answers from
   their own drawing, and the key records the layout rule, not a
   fixed answer.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "On this
   neighborhood map, I drew every table and chair inside the school
   building!" Ask: "Would that work? Why not?" *(No — too small to
   see, and the map would be a mess. A neighborhood map shows
   buildings, not tables.)*
4. "Name one helper workplace on this map." *(Fire station, library,
   store — any of these.)* "What helper works there?" *(Firefighter;
   librarian; grocer — Unit 03 retrieval.)*
5. Trace-a-route round: "Trace the route from the school to the
   park with your finger. What do you pass?" *(The road, and
   whatever the adult's drawing puts along it — name it from the
   drawing.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: a neighborhood
   is ___." *(The area around a school or home, with streets,
   houses, shops, and parks — accept any part of that.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws their own tiny neighborhood map on large paper:
four invented places (school, park, store, library — or any four the
learner chooses from the lesson's list), roads between at least two
of them, and a legend naming each symbol. The adult scribes the
legend words. The adult sits nearby and observes: does the map show
buildings (not rooms), and does the legend match the symbols?

*Alternate response mode:* the learner places four picture cards on
a blank sheet and draws roads between them with a crayon, while the
adult scribes the legend.

## Application

"On your way home or around your building tomorrow, notice one
place near another place — a tree near a door, a shop near a road —
and tell a grown-up: 'The ___ is near the ___.'" (In a one-on-one
setting, the learner reports back at the next session.) The learner
practices neighborhood thinking — places in relation — in the real
world, without mapping anything real on paper.

## Exit check

The adult shows the three nested rectangles and asks: "Which one is
the classroom? Which one is the school? Which one is the
neighborhood?" The learner should order all three correctly (small →
medium → large). The adult records: secure / developing / not yet.
A second probe: "On a neighborhood map, do we draw tables or
buildings?" *(Buildings.)*

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "neighborhood" by example first —
  "the area around our school" — before the word is tested. Accept
  home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, tracing routes with a
  finger or toy, placing cards, drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay the neighborhood map on the floor;
  the learner drives a toy car along the roads from place to place.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with
  the nested rectangles and the drawn map.

## Extension

"If we zoomed out one more time, past the neighborhood, what would
we see? A whole town? A whole country? The whole Earth?" (Previews
Lesson 05's globe. Accept any sensible answer — the point is that
zooming out never has to stop, and the Earth is the biggest map of
all.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Bigger maps show more detail" — the
zoom-out demonstration corrects this directly: bigger place, fewer
details; (2) "The neighborhood map is a real place" — restate that
it is invented practice, and never let a real address or school name
onto it; (3) "Roads are for cars only" — roads carry helpers, buses,
walkers, and deliveries too; the route example uses a helper on
foot-plus-vehicle without specifying, and the adult should name
walking as a way to travel a route.

**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-04) has the
expected responses for the error-analysis round and exit check, and
the default layout the adult should draw if they do not invent their
own.
