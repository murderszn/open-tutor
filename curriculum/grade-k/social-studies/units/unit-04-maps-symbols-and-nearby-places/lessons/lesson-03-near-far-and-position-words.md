# Lesson 03 — Near, Far, and Position Words

**Student goal:** I can use near, far, left, right, above, below,
next to, and between to say where things are on a map and in the room.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 7 of 16**

## Materials

- The generated illustration
  [`assets/classroom-birdseye-map.png`](../assets/classroom-birdseye-map.png)
- Two toys (a bear and a duck, or any two distinct toys) and a small
  box to hide one in during the treasure hunt preview
- A strip of paper with a drawn arrow (for "left" and "right" practice
  — the adult holds it pointing left, then right)

**Privacy note for the adult:** this lesson never asks the learner to
say or write their name, address, school name, or family details.
Position words are practiced on the picture map and on the room — not
on any real place the learner goes alone.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Point to something near you. Now point to something far away."
(Near: the table, the adult. Far: the door, the window.) "Today those
words go onto our map — and we add left, right, above, below, next
to, and between." If the learner is ready to move and point, begin.

## Teaching

A map shows where things are. But "where" is not one word — it is a
whole family of words. Learn them on your own body first, then on the
map.

- **Near** means close to you. **Far** means a long way from you.
  (Hold a toy close, then hold it at arm's length.)
- **Left** is the side of your left hand. **Right** is the side of
  your right hand. (Hold both hands up, palms out. A quick adult
  trick for later: the left hand makes an "L" with thumb and finger
  — but at this age, "your writing hand side" or a sticker on the
  left hand works just as well, and the lesson never tests the trick.)
- **Above** means higher than something. **Below** means lower than
  something. On a map, "above" usually means "toward the top of the
  page" and "below" means "toward the bottom of the page" — because
  the map is flat on the table.
- **Next to** means right beside something, touching or almost
  touching.
- **Between** means in the middle of two things, with one on each
  side.

Now use them on the picture map. The adult points and the learner
answers — or the learner points and the adult asks:

- "The reading corner is **above** the rug or **below** the rug?"
  (Above — toward the top of the page.)
- "The plant is **near** the bottom-right table or **far** from it?"
  (Near — it sits right beside that table's corner.)
- "What is **between** the two windows on the left wall?" (Wall
  space — nothing is drawn between them; accept "nothing" or "the
  wall." This teaches that "between" sometimes answers "nothing is
  there," which is still an answer.)
- "The door is **below** the rug." (Yes — toward the bottom of the
  page.)
- "Which table is **next to** the plant?" (The bottom-right table.)

One warning, said out loud: left and right change when you turn
around. If you face the map from the other side of the table, your
left becomes the map's right. So when we give directions on a map,
we always say "on the map, left" — we mean the left side of the
picture, not the left side of your body after you turn. At this age,
the adult keeps the map in one orientation for the whole lesson and
says so: "We are not turning the map today."

## Worked example 1 — describing the rug's neighbors (modeled)

Point to the rainbow rug in the center of the picture map.

> "What is above the rug? The reading corner is above it — toward
> the top of the page. What is below the rug? The toy shelf is below
> it — toward the bottom of the page. What is next to the rug, on
> the right side? Tables. So I can say: the rug sits between the
> reading corner and the toy shelf, with tables next to it on the
> right. One place, four words, and now you know exactly where it
> is."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** one word alone ("near the rug") is
vague. Two or three position words together pin a place down — that
is why maps and directions use several of them at once.

## Worked example 2 — left or right? (modeled, with the body trick)

Stand beside the learner, both facing the picture map the same way.
Hold up the arrow strip pointing to the map's left side.

> "This is the map's left side. The windows are on the left side of
> the map. The teacher's desk is on the right side of the map. Now —
> I turn around." (The adult turns to face the learner.) "Now my left
> hand points to the desk side! Did the map move? No. I moved. So on
> a map we say 'the left side of the map,' and we keep the map still
> while we talk about it."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** left and right belong to the person
looking — so map talk fixes the map in place and names the map's
sides, not the speaker's sides after turning.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. "Point to something above the rug." *(Reading corner, bookshelf,
   cushions — any of these.)*
2. "Point to something below the rug." *(Toy shelf.)*
3. "Is the teacher's desk near the toy shelf or far from it?"
   *(Far — the desk is in the top-right corner and the toy shelf is
   in the bottom-left corner, opposite corners of the map.)*
4. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "The plant
   is above the rug, and the reading corner is below the rug!"
   Ask: "Is that right? Fix it." *(No — swapped. The reading corner
   is above the rug; the toy shelf is below it; the plant is to the
   right and below the rug, near the bottom-right table.)*
5. Two-toy game in the real room: put the bear on the table and the
   duck under the table. "Where is the duck?" *(Below the table /
   under the table.)* "Where is the bear?" *(Above the duck / on the
   table.)* Move them: bear next to the box, duck between the bear
   and the box. Ask each time.
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish this: the windows are on the
   ___ side of the map." *(Left.)* "The teacher's desk is on the
   ___ side." *(Right.)*

## Independent practice

The learner gets the two toys and the picture map. The adult gives
three placements, one at a time: "Put the bear next to the plant.
Put the duck between the rug and the toy shelf. Put the bear far
from the door." The learner places each toy on the map (or points to
the spot) and says the position word back. The adult sits nearby and
observes: does the learner use the word, or only point? Both count —
but the word is the goal, so the adult models it after each
placement ("Yes — the bear is next to the plant").

*Alternate response mode:* the learner places the toy and the adult
says the sentence; the learner nods or shakes their head for
right/wrong.

## Application

"At your next meal or play time, tell a grown-up where one thing is
using a position word: 'My cup is next to the plate.' 'The toy is
below the table.'" (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back
at the next session.) Position words leave the map and enter everyday
talk — that is how they stick.

## Exit check

The adult points to the picture map and asks four quick questions:
"Where is the reading corner — above or below the rug?" *(Above.)*
"Where is the toy shelf?" *(Below the rug.)* "Which side are the
windows on?" *(Left.)* "Is the teacher's desk near the toy shelf or
far from it?"
*(Far.)* The learner should answer at least three of the four
(words, pointing, or gesture). The adult records: secure / developing
/ not yet. A second probe: "What does 'between' mean?" *(In the
middle of two things.)*

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach each word with the body first (near/far
  with arms, above/below with hands, left/right with a sticker on the
  left hand). Accept home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, placing toys, mime, drawing
  an arrow.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay the picture map on the floor; the
  learner stands "above" it like the bird and walks a toy to each
  named place.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with
  the printed map, the arrow strip, and the two toys.

## Extension

"Give me directions from the door to the reading corner using two
position words: first go ___, then go ___." (For example: "Go above
the rug, then to the left side." Accept any route that lands in the
reading corner — this previews Lesson 04's routes and the treasure
hunt.) If the learner invents a route through the tables, celebrate
it and ask: "Would you bump into a table on that way? Is there a
clearer way?" — early route-checking, without penalty.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Left and right are fixed like north
and south" — the turn-around demonstration exists for exactly this;
keep the map in one orientation all lesson; (2) "Above means up in
the sky" — on a flat map, above means toward the top of the page;
say both wordings every time ("above — toward the top of the page");
(3) "Near and far are exact" — they are relative: the plant is near
the table but far from the door; both can be true at once.
