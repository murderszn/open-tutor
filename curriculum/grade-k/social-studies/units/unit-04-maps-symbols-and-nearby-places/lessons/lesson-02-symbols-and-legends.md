# Lesson 02 — Symbols and Legends

**Student goal:** I can use a legend to read a picture map: a symbol
stands for a real thing, and the legend says which thing.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**

## Materials

- The generated illustration
  [`assets/classroom-birdseye-map.png`](../assets/classroom-birdseye-map.png)
  (printed large, or shown on a screen the learner can point at)
- The printable symbol sheet
  [`assets/map-symbols-legend.svg`](../assets/map-symbols-legend.svg)
- 6 picture cards: rug, reading corner, table, teacher's desk, door,
  plant (drawn by the adult, or cut from the symbol sheet)

**Privacy note for the adult:** this lesson never asks the learner to
say or write their name, address, school name, or family details. The
classroom in the picture is a made-up classroom, not our real room —
that is why we can name everything in it freely.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Point to the picture map. "This is a map of a classroom, seen from
above — like our bird saw it. What is the big rainbow shape in the
middle?" (The rug.) "Today we learn the secret code that tells us
what every shape on a map means." If the learner is ready to point
and listen, begin.

## Teaching

A **symbol** is a small picture that stands for a real thing. On a
map, we cannot draw a whole table with legs and chairs and crumbs —
so we draw a circle, and we agree: "this circle means a table." A
symbol is a shortcut.

But a shortcut only works if everyone knows the code. That code is
the **legend** — also called a **map key**. The legend is a little
list on the side of a map that says: "this symbol means this thing."
Whenever you do not know what a shape means, you check the legend.
That is the map reader's first rule: **if you don't know, check the
legend.**

Look at the picture map together, slowly, and build its legend out
loud. The adult points; the learner names or points back:

- The big rainbow circle in the middle → **the rug**.
- The long shelf with colorful books and two cushions in the
  top-left → **the reading corner**.
- The three tan circles with little chairs around them → **tables**.
- The plain brown rectangle in the top-right → **the teacher's
  desk**. (Say this one plainly: the picture alone does not say
  "desk" — the legend does. That is exactly why legends exist.)
- The white gap in the bottom wall → **the door**. (Same point: a
  gap is a funny-looking door until the legend tells you it is one.)
- The two blue rectangles on the left wall → **windows**.
- The low shelf of colorful squares at the bottom-left → **the toy
  shelf**.
- The green leaves in a pot at the bottom-right → **the plant**.

Now read the map *with* the legend: "Find the reading corner. How
did you know? The legend said the bookshelf and cushions mean the
reading corner." Reading a map is not guessing — it is checking.

**Gesture:** for "legend," hold one hand flat like a list and tap it
each time you check a symbol — the checking tap becomes the class
signal for "look at the legend."

## Worked example 1 — reading with the legend, step by step (modeled)

Cover the legend list (or turn the symbol sheet face down), then ask:

> "Point to a table on this map." (The learner points to a tan
> circle — or hesitates.) "How do we know it is a table and not a
> rug? We check the legend. The legend says: tan circle with chairs
> = table. So that circle is a table. We did not guess. We checked."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a map reader never has to guess. When
two shapes look alike (a circle rug and a circle table), the legend
is what tells them apart — the rug is rainbow rings; the table is tan
with chairs.

## Worked example 2 — a symbol that lies without its legend (modeled)

Point to the brown rectangle in the top-right.

> "What is this brown rectangle? It could be a box. It could be a
> mat. It could be a desk. The picture alone cannot tell us. So what
> do we do? We check the legend — and the legend says: teacher's
> desk. Now we know. A symbol without a legend is just a shape. With
> a legend, it is information."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** this is why every map the learner makes
from now on must have a legend. A map without a legend is a puzzle
with no answers.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. "Point to the rug. How did you know?" *(Rainbow circle in the
   middle; the legend says rug.)*
2. "Point to the door. Where is it?" *(The white gap in the bottom
   wall.)* "Would you have known without the legend?" *(Probably
   not — and that is fine; that is what legends are for.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult points to a table and says, very
   seriously: "This rainbow circle is a table, and this tan circle
   is the rug!" Ask: "Is that right? Check the legend." *(No —
   mixed up. Rainbow circle = rug; tan circle with chairs = table.)*
4. Symbol sort: lay out the six picture cards. "Give me the card for
   the reading corner. Give me the card for the plant." Continue
   until all six are placed on the matching spot on the map.
   *(Reading corner → top-left bookshelf and cushions; plant →
   bottom-right; door → bottom-wall gap; desk → top-right brown
   rectangle; rug → center; table → any tan circle.)*
5. "How many tables are on this map? Count them on the map, not from
   memory." *(Three.)* "How many chairs at each table?" *(Four.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: when I don't know
   a symbol, I ___." *(Check the legend.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws three symbols of their own on paper — any three
things from the picture map (for example, a rug circle, a window
rectangle, a plant) — and then dictates a legend for them to the
adult, who writes each symbol's meaning beside it: "circle = rug."
Then the learner swaps papers with the adult (or covers the legend)
and reads the map back using only the legend. The adult sits nearby
and observes: does the learner check the legend instead of guessing?

*Alternate response mode:* the learner points to a symbol and then
to the matching picture card instead of drawing.

## Application

"Tonight, look at any picture in a book that shows a room or a
place. Ask: if this were a map, what would I need a legend for?"
(In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back at the next
session.) The learner practices spotting "symbols that need a
legend" in everyday pictures.

## Exit check

The adult points to three symbols on the picture map — the plant, a
table, and the door — and asks for each: "What is this? How do you
know?" The learner should answer at least two of the three, naming
the legend as the way they know ("the legend says so" counts even if
the symbol name is approximate). The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "What is a legend for?" *(It
tells what each symbol means.)*

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "symbol" by action first: hold up a toy
  and a drawing of it — "this drawing stands for the toy." Accept
  home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, matching cards, drawing,
  covering and uncovering the legend.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay the picture map on the floor; the
  learner walks a toy around it and "checks the legend" (taps the
  list) at each stop.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with
  the printed map and the symbol cards.

## Extension

"Invent a symbol for something we did not put on this map — a fish
tank, a computer, a coat rack. Draw it, then write its legend line."
(Previews the learner's own map in Lesson 03's follow-up and the
project. Accept any sensible symbol — the legend line is what makes
it a map symbol, not the drawing skill.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "I can just tell what it is" — the
brown-rectangle example teaches that guessing fails and legends fix
it; (2) "The legend is decoration" — every practice round forces a
legend check before an answer counts; (3) "Every map uses the same
symbols" — warn explicitly: on another map, a circle might mean
something else entirely; that is why you always read *that* map's
legend.
