# Lesson 01 — What Is a Map? The Bird's-Eye View

**Student goal:** I can say what a map is: a picture of a place seen
from above that shows where things are.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- A toy bird (or a paper bird on a stick) and a small toy
- 3–4 blocks and a small rug or towel on the floor (a tiny "room")
- A large sheet of paper and crayons for the adult's first sketch

**Privacy note for the adult:** this lesson never asks the learner to
say or write their name, address, school name, or family details.
Restate the Unit 01 safety rule at the start: "Names, addresses, and
photos stay private." Maps in this unit show our classroom, our
school, and a made-up neighborhood — never a real home.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"Stand up. Look down at your shoes. What do you see from up here?"
(Feet, floor.) "Now crouch down low and look at the table leg. It
looks different from down here, doesn't it? Today we learn to see a
whole room the way a bird sees it — from above." If the learner is
ready to listen and point, begin.

## Teaching

A **map** is a picture of a place. Not a photograph of a face, not a
story — a picture that shows **where things are**.

The trick to a map is the view. We usually see a room from the side:
we see the fronts of chairs and the sides of tables. A map sees a
room **from above** — the way a bird flying over the room would see
it. That is called a **bird's-eye view**. From above, a round table
looks like a circle. A rug looks like a flat shape on the floor. A
bookshelf looks like a long rectangle along the wall.

Put the blocks and the toy on the rug "room" on the floor. Stand over
it and look down together.

> "From up here, what shape is the rug? A rectangle. Where is the toy?
> Next to the blocks. If I drew what I see from up here — just the
> shapes and where they sit — I would have a map of this little room."

A map does three jobs:

1. It shows a **place** (this room, our classroom, our school).
2. It shows **things in that place** (tables, rug, door, shelves).
3. It shows **where those things are** — which one is near which, and
   which one is far away.

A map is not the place itself. It is smaller, flatter, and simpler —
and that is why it is useful: you can hold a whole room in your hands
and find things on it.

**Gesture:** hold the toy bird high above the little room and "fly"
it slowly over the rug, the blocks, and the toy, while the learner
names each thing the bird "sees."

## Worked example 1 — the bird over the table (modeled)

Hold the bird above one round table (or a plate standing in for a
table).

> "The bird flies over the table. What does the bird see? Not the
> legs. Not the side. The bird sees a circle — the top of the table.
> So on our map, a round table is a circle. The bird flies over the
> rug. What does the bird see? A big flat shape. So on our map, the
> rug is that flat shape. A map draws what the bird sees."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we did not draw the table the way it
looks when we sit at it. We drew the way it looks from above. That
change of view is the whole idea of a map.

## Worked example 2 — map or not a map? (modeled)

Show two quick adult sketches: (a) a circle with a smaller circle
inside it, labeled by the adult "a round table on a rug"; (b) a
smiling face.

> "Is picture (a) a map? It shows a place — a rug — and a thing in
> it — a table — and where the table sits — in the middle of the rug.
> Yes, that is a tiny map. Is picture (b) a map? It is a nice face,
> but it does not show a place or where things are in it. So it is a
> picture, but it is not a map."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a map must answer "where is it?" If a
picture cannot answer that, it is not a map — even if it is beautiful.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Fly the bird over the blocks. "What shape does the bird see?"
   *(A rectangle or square, from above.)*
2. "Show me with your hands: how does the bird see the rug — flat
   like this (palm down, flat) or tall like this (palm upright)?"
   *(Flat, palm down.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult draws a chair from the side (legs
   and back showing) and says, "Here is my map of the chair!" Ask:
   "Is that a map view? What would the bird see instead?" *(No — that
   is a side view. The bird would see the seat from above, a small
   square or circle.)*
4. Point around the real room. "If a bird flew over our room, what
   would it see first — the rug, the door, or the ceiling?" *(The rug
   and the things on the floor. A bird above the room cannot see the
   ceiling from inside — and the ceiling is not on our map. Accept
   any answer that names floor things, and gently correct "ceiling.")*
5. "Finish the sentence: a map is a picture of a ___ seen from ___."
   *(A place; above.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Tell me one thing a map shows, and one
   thing a map does not show." *(Shows: where things are. Does not
   show: faces, stories, sounds — accept sensible answers.)*

## Independent practice

The learner builds a tiny room from blocks on the floor (two blocks
for tables, one flat cloth for a rug, one toy for a "friend"), then
stands on a chair with the adult's help — or holds the bird high —
and tells the adult what the bird sees, in order: "The bird sees a
rug. The bird sees two tables." The adult scribes the list on paper:
that list is the learner's first map in words.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner points to each thing from
above and the adult names it; pointing in order counts as the answer.

## Application

"Tomorrow, when you walk into this room, pretend you are the bird for
ten seconds. Look down (in your mind) and name two things the bird
would see." (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back at the
next session.) The learner practices the bird's-eye view in a real
room.

## Exit check

The adult shows the two sketches from Worked example 2 again and asks:
"Which one is a map? How do you know?" The learner should pick the
table-on-a-rug sketch and give one reason (it shows a place and where
things are). The adult records: secure / developing / not yet. A
second probe: "What does a bird see when it flies over a round
table?" *(A circle.)*

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "from above" by action first: the adult
  crouches, then stands on a safe step, then holds the bird high —
  the words follow the movement. Accept home-language words alongside
  English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, mime (flying the bird),
  arranging blocks, drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** do the whole lesson on the floor with the
  block room and the bird.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with a
  small tray "room" (a cloth, two blocks, a toy).

## Extension

"What would a bird see if it flew over a playground? Over a whole
school?" (Previews Lesson 04's bigger maps. Accept any sensible
answer — swings as shapes, a building as a big rectangle — and note
that bigger places make bigger maps.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "A map is a picture of anything" —
the map-or-not-a-map check teaches the "where is it?" test; (2) "From
above means from the sky far away" — keep the bird just above the
room, not in space; (3) "The map has to look exactly like the room"
— a map is simpler than the room on purpose; missing details are not
mistakes.
