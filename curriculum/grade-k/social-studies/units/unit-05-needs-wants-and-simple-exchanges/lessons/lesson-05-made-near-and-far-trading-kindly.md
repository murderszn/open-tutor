# Lesson 05 — Made Near and Far: Trading Kindly

**Student goal:** I can name one thing made far away and sold here and
one thing made nearby, and I can explain trading as giving something
and getting something back.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- Picture cards: banana, apple from a nearby tree (drawn apple with a
  sun), loaf of bread (baker nearby), crayons, toy ball
- A simple map from Unit 04 (classroom/school map) plus a "far away"
  picture: a ship or truck traveling a long road
- Two baskets: NEAR (picture of a house) and FAR AWAY (picture of a
  ship)
- 5 play coins for the trade game

**Privacy note for the adult:** this lesson never asks the learner to say or
write their name, address, school name, or family details, and never
asks where the learner's family shops or what it buys. All examples are
pretend or general knowledge. Restate the Unit 01 safety rule at the
start: "Names, addresses, and photos stay private."

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Hold up the apple card. "Need or want? Good or service?" (Need; good.)
"Today we ask a new question: where did this come from — nearby, or far
away?" If the learner is ready to listen and point, begin.

## Teaching

Things in a shop come from places. Some things are made **nearby** —
close to us. Bread is baked by a baker nearby. Apples can grow on trees
nearby. Crayons can be made in a factory nearby. Some things are made
**far away** — a long way from us. Bananas grow in warm places far away
from our town, and then they travel a long way — by truck, by ship, by
truck again — until they reach our shop. That is why the shop can sell
bananas even though bananas do not grow here.

When people in one place give something to people in another place and
get something back, that is **trade** — another word for exchange.
Trade is kind when both sides agree and both sides get something they
need or want. We trade with our neighbors when we swap a crayon for a
sticker. Towns trade when a faraway place sends bananas and our place
sends something back, like bread or apples. Helpers move things: the
Unit 03 grocer sells the bananas, the truck driver drives them, the
ship's crew sails them. Goods travel because people work together.

Retrieval from Unit 04: near and far are map words. Point to the
classroom map: our shop is near. Point to the ship picture: far away
means many, many steps past the edge of our map — too far to walk.

The kindness rule for trading: trade only what is yours to trade, trade
only with a trusted adult nearby, and never trade something you need
(like your coat on a cold day) for something you merely want. A trade
you feel sad about afterwards was not a kind trade — a trusted adult
helps you undo it and try again.

## Worked example 1 — the banana's journey, step by step (modeled)

Hold up the banana card and move it along the road picture, step by step.

> "Where do bananas grow? In a warm place far away. First, a farmer
> picks the bananas. Then a truck carries them to a ship. The ship
> sails a long way across the water. Then another truck carries them to
> our shop. Then the grocer sells them. Banana, far away, long journey,
> our shop — four pieces, every time. When you eat a banana, you are
> connected to a faraway place."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we did not just say "far away." We named
the journey: pick, carry, sail, carry, sell.

## Worked example 2 — bread nearby, and a wrong-journey check (modeled)

Hold up the bread card.

> "Where does bread come from? A baker nearby bakes it — flour, water,
> an oven, and work. The baker is near, so the bread's journey is
> short: bake, carry to the shop, sell. Bread, nearby, short journey."
>
> "Now — did the bread sail across the sea on a ship?" (Let the learner
> answer.) "No! Bread is made nearby. Not everything travels far. Some
> things are made nearby, and some things are made far away. The
> journey tells us which."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the journey, not the size of the thing,
tells us near from far. Bread is big and nearby; a banana is small and
far away.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Hold up the banana card. "Near or far away? How did it get here?"
   *(Far away — grown in a warm, faraway place; traveled by truck and
   ship to our shop.)*
2. Hold up the bread card. "Near or far away?" *(Nearby — baked by a
   baker nearby; short journey.)*
3. "Trade game: I give you my crayon, and you give me your sticker.
   What did we do?" *(We traded — we gave something and got something
   back. Both sides agreed.)*
4. Error-analysis round: the adult announces, very seriously: "Bananas
   grow on the tree outside our school, so they are nearby!" Ask: "Is
   that right? What did the banana's journey tell us?" *(Expected: no
   — bananas grow in warm places far away and travel a long way to our
   shop. A banana in our shop still came from far away.)*
5. "Name one helper who moves goods from far away to our shop."
   *(The truck driver, the ship's crew, or the grocer who sells them —
   any one counts.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: trading means ___."
   *(Giving something and getting something back.)*

## Independent practice

The learner sorts 5 cards into the NEAR and FAR AWAY baskets: bread
(near), apple from a nearby tree (near), crayons (near), banana (far),
toy ball (far — made in a factory far away; accept "far" with the reason
"it traveled a long way" — if the learner says the ball could be made
nearby, accept it with a reason, since balls are made in many places;
the reasoning is the learning). Then the learner plays one kind trade
with the adult: swap a crayon for a sticker, both agreeing, and says
"I traded." The adult scribes the sort and the trade for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner points to the basket instead of
naming it, and acts out the journey (driving a truck, sailing a ship)
for far-away items.

## Application

"Tomorrow, notice one thing in our classroom and guess: was it made
nearby or far away? Just guess, in your head. Tomorrow, tell me your
guess and your reason." (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports
back at the next session.)

## Exit check

The adult holds up the banana card and the bread card. "Which one came
from far away? Which one was made nearby? What is trading?" The
learner should sort both correctly and give a version of "giving
something and getting something back" (words, pointing, or acting out
a swap). The adult records: secure / developing / not yet. A second
probe: "Name one more thing made far away." (Any sensible answer with a
journey reason counts — for example, oranges from a warm, faraway
place.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach each word by action first, word second.
  Mime a long journey (walking far, sailing) and a short journey (one
  step) before saying "far away" and "nearby." Accept home-language
  words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, acting out journeys, sorting
  cards, drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay a long paper road on the floor; the
  learner drives the banana card along it to the shop and walks the
  bread card one step.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  picture cards and baskets.

## Extension

"If our town had no trucks or ships, could our shop still sell
bananas?" (No — without helpers moving goods, faraway things could not
reach us. We would still have nearby things: bread, apples, crayons.
This is why helpers who move goods matter — Unit 03's chain, with a
longer road. Preview: the project market will have a near stall and a
far stall.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "it is in our shop, so it is nearby" —
the banana example separates where a thing is sold from where it was
made; (2) "big things travel far, small things stay near" — the
wrong-journey check teaches that the journey, not the size, decides;
(3) "trading means taking" — the trade game teaches that both sides
give and both sides agree, with a trusted adult nearby.
