# Lesson 04 — Choosing When We Cannot Have Everything

**Student goal:** I can choose one thing when I cannot have two, say
what I get and what I give up, and explain saving as keeping money for
later.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 7 of 16**

## Materials

- 5 play coins, ball card (3 coin dots), teddy bear card (3 coin dots),
  apple card (1 coin dot), bread card (2 coin dots)
- Two jars: SPEND (picture of an open hand) and SAVE (picture of a
  closed jar with a lid)
- A small "later" picture: a sun behind a hill, meaning "another day"

**Privacy note for the adult:** this lesson uses pretend choices only.
Never ask what the learner's family buys, saves, or cannot afford, and
never frame a pretend choice as a real family's situation. Restate the
Unit 01 safety rule at the start: "Names, addresses, and photos stay
private."

## Readiness check (1 minute)

"You have 5 coins. A ball costs 3. A teddy bear costs 3. Can you buy
both?" (Let the learner try: 3 + 3 = 6, and 6 is more than 5 — so no.)
"Today we learn what to do when we cannot have everything." If the
learner is ready to listen and count, begin.

## Teaching

**Scarcity** is a grown-up word for a simple truth: there is not enough
of everything for everyone to have it all at once. Our purse has 5
coins. The ball costs 3 and the teddy bear costs 3. Together they cost
6 coins. 6 is more than 5. So we cannot buy both. We must **choose**.

When we choose, we get one thing and **give up** the other. Say both
parts out loud: "I get the ___, and I give up the ___." That sentence
is the whole lesson. There is no wrong choice here — the ball and the
teddy bear are both wants, and both are fine. What matters is saying
what you gave up, honestly, without pretending you got both.

**Saving** means keeping money for later instead of spending it now.
Put a coin in the SAVE jar, and it waits for another day. Why save?
Because later you might want something bigger — or something you need
more. If you spend all 5 coins today, your purse is empty tomorrow. If
you save 2 coins, tomorrow you still have 2 coins to choose with.
Spending is using coins now; saving is keeping coins for later. A
**bank** is a safe place where grown-ups keep money they are saving —
our save jar is our classroom bank.

Two choice rules to practice: (1) count first, then choose — never
choose before counting; (2) say what you give up — never pretend you
got both.

## Worked example 1 — ball or teddy bear, step by step (modeled)

Set out 5 coins, the ball card, and the teddy bear card.

> "I have 5 coins. The ball costs 3. The teddy bear costs 3. 3 + 3 = 6.
> 6 is more than 5, so I cannot buy both. I choose the ball. I give the
> shopkeeper 3 coins. How many are left? 5 − 3 = 2 coins are left. Now
> I say the whole sentence: 'I get the ball, and I give up the teddy
> bear.' I did not get the teddy bear — and that is all right. I chose."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we counted before choosing, and we said
both parts: what we get and what we give up.

## Worked example 2 — spending all or saving some (modeled)

> "Now I have 2 coins left. An apple costs 1 coin. If I spend 1 coin on
> the apple, I have 1 coin left. If I save that last coin, I put it in
> the SAVE jar. Tomorrow I still have 1 coin. If I spend it too, my
> purse is empty — 0 coins — and tomorrow I cannot buy anything.
> Saving is keeping a coin for later. Watch: I put 1 coin in the SAVE
> jar. My purse has 0 coins now, but my save jar has 1 coin waiting
> for another day."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** saving does not make the coin disappear.
It moves the coin to later.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. "You have 5 coins. A ball costs 3 and a teddy bear costs 3. Can you
   buy both? Why not?" *(No — 3 + 3 = 6, and 6 is more than 5.)*
2. "Choose one: ball or teddy bear. Say the whole sentence." *(Any
   choice counts, as long as both parts are said: "I get the ___, and
   I give up the ___." Coins left after either choice: 5 − 3 = 2.)*
3. "You have 5 coins. You buy bread for 2 coins. How many are left?
   You could save them or spend them on an apple for 1 coin. What do
   you choose, and why?" *(3 left. Either choice counts with a reason:
   "I spend 1 on an apple because I am hungry" or "I save 3 for later
   because I want something bigger tomorrow.")*
4. Error-analysis round: the adult announces, very seriously: "I bought
   the ball AND the teddy bear with 5 coins, and I still have 5 coins
   left!" Ask: "Is that right? Count with me." *(Expected: no — the
   ball and the teddy bear cost 3 + 3 = 6 coins, more than 5, and
   paying 3 leaves 2, not 5. You cannot get both and keep everything.)*
5. "Show me saving." *(The learner puts a coin in the SAVE jar and says
   "for later" in words, gesture, or by pointing to the "later"
   picture.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: saving means ___."
   *(Keeping money for later.)*

## Independent practice

The learner gets 5 coins and three cards: ball (3), apple (1), bread
(2). The learner buys two items that fit in 5 coins (for example, ball
+ apple = 4, leaving 1; or bread + apple = 3, leaving 2), says the coins
left, and puts any leftover coin in the SAVE jar or spends it — then
says the choice sentence for the item they did NOT buy: "I give up
the ___." The adult scribes the purchases, the coins left, and the
give-up sentence for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner points to the chosen card, counts
coins onto the counter, and points to the SAVE jar or the SPEND mat
instead of explaining in words.

## Application

"Tomorrow, when you choose between two games or two toys in play, say
in your head: 'I get this, and I give up that.' Tomorrow, tell me one
choice you made." (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back at
the next session. The choice is about play, never about real family
purchases.)

## Exit check

The adult sets out 5 coins, the ball card, and the teddy bear card.
"Can you buy both? Choose one and say the whole sentence." The learner
should say no (6 is more than 5), choose one, and say both parts of the
sentence. The adult records: secure / developing / not yet. A second
probe: "What does saving mean?" (Keeping money for later — any version
counts.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach each word by action first, word second.
  Mime counting, choosing, putting a coin in a jar before saying the
  words. Accept home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, counting piles, mime, drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay the cards on the floor; the learner
  stands on the chosen card and points to the given-up card.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  coins, cards, and jars.

## Extension

"You have 5 coins. You buy an apple for 1 coin and bread for 2 coins.
How many coins are left, and what could you save them for?" *(1 + 2 =
3 spent; 5 − 3 = 2 left. Any sensible "later" idea counts — a ball
tomorrow, a bigger want later, a need another day. Preview: Lesson 05
asks where the things in our shop came from — near or far.)*

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "I can have both if I want them enough"
— the counting check teaches that wanting does not change the total;
(2) "saving means losing the coin" — the jar makes saving visible: the
coin is still there, waiting; (3) "giving something up means I chose
wrong" — correct this at once: a give-up is part of every choice, not a
mistake, and both choices in this lesson are fine.
