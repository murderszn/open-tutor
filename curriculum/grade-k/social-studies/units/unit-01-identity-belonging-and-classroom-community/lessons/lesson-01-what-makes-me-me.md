# Lesson 01 — What Makes Me, Me

**Student goal:** I can show one thing that makes me unique — a favorite, a
talent, or an interest — with words, a drawing, or a gesture.
**Time:** 15–20 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- Large paper and crayons
- 6–8 picture cards: favorite foods, animals, toys, activities (cut from
  magazines or drawn by the adult)

**Privacy note for the adult:** this lesson never asks the learner to say or
write their name, address, school, or family details. Favorites and talents
only. Any drawing the learner makes goes to private storage, not a shared or
public product.

## Readiness check (1 minute)

Hold up the picture cards one at a time. "Point to one you like." If the
learner points or smiles at a card, they are ready. If they seem unsure, lay
three cards down and let them touch the one they want — choice by touch counts.

## Teaching

**Unique** means there is no one else exactly like you. You have your own
favorites. You have your own talents — things you can do. A **favorite** is
something you like best of all. A **talent** is something you are good at:
running fast, singing, building tall towers, making people laugh.

Say it with me: "I am unique. No one else is exactly like me." (Gesture: point
to self, then spread arms wide.)

Here is the safety rule for our whole unit: **we never put our name, address,
or photos on things we share.** Favorites and drawings are safe to share.
Names and addresses stay private. We will say this rule at the start of every
lesson.

## Worked example 1 — the adult's favorite (modeled)

The adult picks the apple picture card and holds it up.

> "My favorite fruit is the apple. Watch: I am showing you something unique
> about me — my favorite. Your turn to guess: do you think my favorite animal
> is also an apple?" *(No — apples are not animals; favorites are different
> for different things.)*
> "Now I draw it." The adult draws a big red apple on the paper and says,
> "This drawing tells you my favorite fruit. It does not have my name on it.
> That is the safety rule."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a favorite tells people something true about you,
and a drawing can share it without any private information.

## Worked example 2 — the adult's talent (modeled)

> "My talent is whistling." The adult whistles a short tune. "See? I showed
> you my talent with a sound, not with words. You can show a talent with your
> body too." The adult does a silly jump. "Jumping high is a talent. Making
> funny faces is a talent."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** talents are things you *do*. Some people show
them, some people tell about them — both ways count.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Lay out three food cards. "Point to your favorite." *(Hint: "There is no
   wrong favorite. Point to the one you like best.")*
2. "Show me with your face: do you LOVE it, like it, or is it just okay?"
   (big smile / small smile / flat face — a gesture mode)
3. "Show me a talent with your body — no words." (The learner might clap fast,
   spin, hop, or make a face. Celebrate whatever they offer.)
4. "Draw your favorite on this paper." Adult scribes nothing but the drawing;
   no name on the paper.
5. Error-analysis round: the adult holds up a card and says, "My favorite
   color is… my address!" Ask: "Did I follow the safety rule? What should I
   have said instead?" *(Expected: no — addresses stay private; say a
   favorite or talent instead.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Tell me about your favorite. What do you like
   about it?"

## Independent practice

The learner gets paper and crayons and draws "something that makes me, me" —
a favorite or a talent. The adult sits nearby, silent, and observes: did the
learner choose on their own? Did they stay with one idea? When finished, the
adult asks, "Tell me about your drawing" (or "point to your favorite part"),
and scribes one dictated sentence on the back for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner brings a favorite toy or object from
home and shows it instead of drawing; or points to picture cards in sequence
while the adult narrates. For limited mobility, eye-gaze choice between two
cards held up by the adult works.

## Application

"Tonight at dinner, show someone at home your drawing and tell them one thing
that makes you unique. Remember the safety rule: favorites and talents are
fine to share; names and addresses stay private." The learner practices the
rule in a real setting.

## Exit check

The adult asks: "Show me one thing that makes you unique." The learner may
point to their drawing, name a favorite, or do a gesture. Then: "What is our
safety rule?" The learner should indicate (words, nod, or point to a drawn
"no-name" symbol the adult made) that names/addresses stay private. The adult
records: secure / developing / not yet, for both parts.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "favorite" and "unique" by doing first, naming
  second. Accept home-language words alongside English ("mi favorito" counts).
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, gesture, favorite object from home,
  eye-gaze choice.
- **Floor-play variant:** sit on the floor with the picture cards spread out;
  the learner "visits" their favorite by crawling or walking to it.
- **Shy learners:** let them whisper to a puppet or stuffed animal, which
  "tells" the adult.

## Extension

Draw TWO things that make you unique and tell which one is a favorite and
which one is a talent. Or: "Find something in this room that is unique — only
one of it." (Previews observation.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "unique" means "better than everyone" — reframe:
unique means *different*, and different is what makes the community
interesting; (2) the learner offers their name or address as "something about
me" — gently redirect every time: "That stays private. Give me a favorite
instead"; (3) the learner copies the adult's favorite — model first, then
insist on their own choice with the card spread.
