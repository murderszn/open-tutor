# Teacher Guide — Unit 05 (Grade 9 Language Arts)

**Unit:** Narrative and creative writing workshop ·
**Track weeks:** 20–23 · **Sessions:** 20 × ~50 minutes ·
**Issue:** [#44](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/44), section U05 ·
**Status:** validated draft; not educator-reviewed, not merged

## How this unit works

U01 taught learners to read with a pen and build evidence-based cases
about theme. U02 turned the lens to how authors *make* meaning. U03
taught argument evaluation, and U04 gave learners the grammar, syntax,
and vocabulary to control their sentences. U05 is where all of that
becomes **making**: the learner writes a complete short story
(1,200–2,000 words), from seed to first draft to three-pass revision,
and then reads an excerpt aloud. The throughline question: **"What does
your story want to be about — and what craft choices will make a reader
feel it?"**

The mentor text is the original story ["The Long Way
Home"](texts/the-long-way-home.md), with a generated illustration of
its decision moment (the rail-yard gate). The learner studies how that
story is built, then builds their own — from a provided story seed or
one of their own — with the same tools: want vs. need, event sequences
that cause one another, point of view, dialogue, pacing, sensory
language, reflection, and a reflective conclusion.

## Unit misconceptions to watch

1. **"Want vs. need" as a vocabulary quiz.** Learners will parrot the
   definitions without finding the *pull*. Insist on the exact sentence
   where the two halves pull in opposite directions (mentor story ¶6,
   "pulling like a rope in her hands"). If a learner's own seed has
   want and need pointing the same way, send them back to the seed —
   do not let them draft a story with no tension.
2. **First person is "easier" or "more personal."** First person is
   harder in one specific way: the narrator cannot know anything they
   did not witness. Learners who choose it because it "feels natural"
   often write a narrator who conveniently knows things they could not
   know. The POV memo exists to catch that.
3. **Dialogue as announcement.** Every character speaks in full
   sentences, answers every question directly, and says exactly what
   they mean. Have the learner read dialogue aloud and mark every line
   no real fourteen-year-old would ever say.
4. **"Show, don't tell" as a ban on summary.** Summary is a legitimate
   gear. A story that is all scene is exhausting; the skill is choosing
   the gear *for the moment*. The gear check (Practice E, task 4)
   exists to teach the choice.
5. **More adjectives = better description.** Learners answer a
   sensory-language task by adding adjectives ("very dark, super
   creepy, extremely old") instead of better details. The
   one-detail-per-sense table is the antidote.
6. **Proofreading disguised as revision.** The learner fixes five
   commas and calls the story revised. The three-pass structure exists
   to prevent that — check the cut/keep/move list before the language
   pass begins. If it is empty, no structure revision happened.
7. **The announced moral.** A conclusion that says "and the lesson
   is…" should be deleted, not debated. Ask the learner to remove that
   sentence and read the paragraph without it — nine times out of ten
   it is better, and they can see it themselves.

## Workshop round 1 (session 8): modeling feedback language

This is the unit's only group-workshop moment, and the adult runs it.
The adult reads two seed sketches aloud (the learner's, or the adult's
own if the learner prefers) and models the feedback language the
learner will use for the rest of the unit:

- **Name the specific line** ("In the second paragraph, the line
  about the lantern…"), never "I liked it."
- **Ask, don't tell** ("What were you hoping the reader would feel
  here?" before "This part didn't work for me.")
- **One note at a time.** The reader gives exactly one "I got lost
  here…" or "I wanted more here…" note per sketch. (This is the same
  rule as Practice I, task 4.)
- **The writer decides.** Feedback is an offer, not an order. The
  writer records which notes they accepted and why in the craft log.

## Discussion prompts (sessions 8, 20, and beyond)

- "Samira chooses the long way. Is the story's climax the choice at
  the gate, or the revelation in the kitchen — and does your answer
  change what the story is *about*?"
- "Mrs. Okafor says the grandfather walked the dog every Tuesday. What
  does that one fact do to the story's timeline — and to Samira's
  understanding of her own family?"
- "The mentor story never shows Samira's mother except at the very end.
  What does the story gain by keeping her offstage?"
- "Your story's narrator cannot be present for your climax. Is that a
  problem with the narrator, or with the climax?"

## Differentiation

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the mentor story aloud while
  the learner follows; the analysis is the work, not the decoding.
  "The Gift of the Magi" (Resource Pack) is an optional strong-reader
  extension, not required reading.
- **Alternate response modes:** story maps, charts, and timelines may be
  drawn, dictated, or built with index cards; drafts may be dictated or
  recorded as audio first, then transcribed. Exit checks may be answered
  orally. The author reading may be recorded rather than performed live.
- **Extensions:** write a second story from a different seed in a
  different point of view; revise the mentor story's ending from Mrs.
  Okafor's point of view; study "The Gift of the Magi" for its twist
  structure and write a 300-word analysis of how O. Henry withholds
  information.
- **Support:** for learners new to long-form drafting, the 1,200-word
  minimum may be reached across two sittings with the adult scribing
  the plan; the unit assessment may be split across two sittings
  without penalty; score its two tasks separately and conference before
  recording.

## Reteaching guide

- **Quiz < 24:** use session 16's first 15 minutes for targeted
  reteach of the missed skill (want/need → redo Practice B task 1
  orally; event chain → redo the removal test on one link; POV → the
  Vignette D two-version comparison; dialogue → the announcement fix
  in Practice D task 1; pacing → Vignette A expand/compress; sensory
  → the one-detail-per-sense table). Do not reteach the whole unit.
- **Assessment Task 1 weak, Task 2 strong:** the learner can revise
  but not yet analyze — assign two more Practice B-style chain tasks
  on the independent reading book, with the mechanism named each time.
- **Assessment Task 2 weak:** the revision habit is missing — return
  to L6's worked examples and require the change log (before → after
  + technique + gain) before any rewriting, in U06's writing work.
- **Draft stalled at the opening:** set a 20-minute timer, forbid
  editing, require forward motion with `[FIX]` marks. If the draft
  jumps from problem straight to climax, send the learner back to the
  chain (Lesson 1): which events were skipped?
- **Project story under 1,200 words:** conference about what is
  missing (usually the middle — the chain between problem and climax)
  rather than padding the ending.

## Answer-key use

[answer-key.md](answer-key.md) solves every lesson item, practice task,
quiz question, assessment task, and project criterion independently.
Keep it adult-only. When a learner's answer differs from the key,
check first whether it is *defensibly different* (the key notes
acceptable variation) — in narrative writing, a well-defended creative
choice is a success, not an error. The key's sample responses are
models of *reasoning*, not sentences to memorize.
