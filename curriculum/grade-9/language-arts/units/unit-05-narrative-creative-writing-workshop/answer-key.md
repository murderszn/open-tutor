# Answer Key — Unit 05 (Grade 9 Language Arts)

**Adult-only.** This key solves every lesson item, practice task, quiz
question, assessment task, and project criterion independently. When a
learner's answer differs from the key, check first whether it is
*defensibly different* — in narrative writing, a well-defended creative
choice is a success, not an error. Sample responses below are models of
*reasoning*, not sentences to memorize.

---

## Lesson 01

**Illustration check (guided practice 1).** (1) The girl is deciding
whether to take the shortcut through the rail yard or the long way
around. (2) The shortcut is not the safe choice: the gate is rusted and
leaning, the tracks are overgrown and disappear into mist, the light is
failing, and she is alone at dusk. (3) The dog sits beside her looking
up — patient, trusting, waiting for her decision; the dog wants to be
with her, and (as the story reveals) to be taken home. What the picture
cannot show: the grandfather's rule ("never cut through the yard after
dark," ¶5) and the fact that the dog's tag names an address in the
other direction. **Teaching point:** an illustration can *show* a
decision (posture, light, the two roads) but cannot *tell* the history
that makes the decision matter; words can tell the history but cannot
show the two roads at once. Accept any answer that makes this
show/tell distinction with a specific detail.

**Want/need for "The Recipe Box" (guided practice 2).** Acceptable:
"Ana wants the recipe back exactly as it was, so this year's birthday
can feel normal again." / "Ana needs to accept that the cake was never
really about the recipe — it was about the person who made it, and the
person who will have to make it now." They pull in opposite directions
because getting the recipe exactly right would let Ana pretend nothing
changed, while accepting the loss requires her to admit that everything
changed — the recipe and the acceptance cannot both win.

**Chain and climax (guided practice 3).** The chain: the bus leaves
→ (she must walk, so) the lantern comes out → (the light opens a
circle around her feet, so) she hears the dog → (the tag gives her the
address, so) she knows the long way → (the choice at the gate) → (the
walk ends at Number 14, so) the kitchen and Mrs. Okafor. **Climax:**
the choice at the gate (¶6–¶8) — that is where want (fast, dry,
shortcut) and need (the long way) finally collide and Samira chooses.
The kitchen (¶11–¶13) is the *resolution*: it shows what the choice
unlocked. Accept the kitchen as the climax only if the learner argues
it as the moment of *understanding* — but note that the decision itself
happens at the gate, and the kitchen cannot happen without it.

**Exit check.** (a) A want is what the character *thinks* will fix the
problem; a need is what will *actually* fix it — usually something
deeper they do not see yet. (b) The removal test: remove an event; if
the story still works, the event was decoration, not plot. A strong
chain is one where every link matters.

## Lesson 02

**POV audit (guided practice 1).** Three acceptable sentences (any
three that report Samira's interior state in third person):
- ¶6: "She could feel the two halves of the decision pulling like a
  rope in her hands." — only Samira can feel that pull.
- ¶8: "The dog thumped its tail once against the wet concrete and
  looked up at her with the patient, unearned trust of an animal that
  has decided you are its person now." — the judgment "unearned trust"
  is Samira's reading of the dog, not a fact the narrator could know
  independently.
- ¶15: "Samira understood something she had not understood at the bus
  stop." — the narration reports an understanding that belongs to
  Samira alone.
Accept any sentence that reports a feeling, judgment, or understanding
belonging only to Samira, with the explanation of *why* only she could
know it.

**First-person rewrite (guided practice 2).** Sample (same events,
first person):
> I looked at the gate. The fence leaned where the ivy had pulled it
> down, and beyond it the tracks disappeared into the mist. The
> shortcut would get me home before Mum really started to worry. The
> long way would get the dog home, and it would make me forty-five
> minutes late. I could feel the two halves of the decision pulling
> like a rope in my hands. "Well," I said to the dog. "You picked the
> wrong bus stop, Barnaby."
**Gain:** immediacy and confession — the reader is inside the decision
as it happens. **Loss:** the reader can no longer see Samira from the
outside; the small observations the third-person narrator offers (the
dog's "patient, unearned trust") must now come through Samira's own
words, which changes their tone. Accept any rewrite that keeps the
events identical and names one real gain and one real loss.

**Spot the break (guided practice 3).** The error is a **POV break**
(also called head-hopping): a third-person-limited narration staying
close to one character cannot report another character's private
thoughts. Two fixes: (1) change the POV to third-person omniscient and
signal the shift honestly, or (2) keep the POV and rewrite the climax
so the other character's thoughts are shown through observable action
and dialogue instead.

**Exit check.** (a) First person: gains immediacy/confession; costs
everything the narrator cannot see. Third-person limited: gains a
little room for the reader to judge; costs raw immediacy. Third-person
omniscient: gains the widest view; costs closeness to everyone. (b)
The narrator must be present for the story's most important moments —
especially the climax.

## Lesson 03

**Beat hunt (guided practice 1).** Acceptable beats in ¶7–¶13, with
what each shows:
- ¶8: the dog "thumped its tail once against the wet concrete" — the
  tail shows trust and patience that the dialogue ("Come on") does not
  state.
- ¶8: Samira "clipped the lantern to her backpack strap so both hands
  were free" — the practical action shows the decision is made; her
  body commits before her words do.
- ¶13: Mrs. Okafor "took the dog's face in both hands" — she speaks to
  the dog as much as to Samira; the beat shows affection before the
  revelation.
- ¶13: "She laughed, and then she stopped laughing, because Samira's
  face had done something she could not control." — the laugh stopping
  is the beat that tells the reader this is no longer a funny story.
Accept any three beats with a correct "what it shows" for each.

**Announcement fix (guided practice 2).** Sample:
> Ana stood in the doorway of the practice room, her violin case
> banging once against the doorframe. She did not come in.
> "Ana?" her friend said.
> "I keep losing the third line," Ana said. She looked at the floor.
> "Every time. The third line."
> Her friend put her own case down. "Then play the first two for me.
> Just the first two."
Accept any rewrite that (a) removes the announced feelings ("nervous,"
"talented," "I believe in you"), (b) keeps the information (she is
nervous, she practiced, her friend supports her), and (c) includes at
least one beat. Deduct if a feeling is still announced rather than
shown.

**Subtext check (guided practice 3).** "Next time, come on Tuesday,
when there's still time to fix it" means: *I am not angry, and I will
help you — but the help has to come earlier next time.* It is kinder
than "I forgive you" because it treats June as capable rather than
guilty: it gives her a plan instead of a pardon, and it does not make
her say "thank you for forgiving me." Accept any answer that names the
plan-vs.-pardon distinction.

**Exit check.** (a) Dialogue's three jobs: reveal character, advance
the story, carry subtext. (b) A beat is a small action or description
between lines of dialogue; it shows what the body is doing while the
mouth talks, and it controls the pace (a beat is a breath). (c)
Subtext is what a character means but does not say.

## Lesson 04

**Gear check (guided practice 1).** Paragraph labels for "The Long Way
Home": ¶1 scene (the problem, moment by moment) · ¶2 scene/summary
borderline — accept *summary* (it compresses the streetlights' week-long
outage and the sky) or *scene* (it is one moment at the bus stop);
require the defense either way · ¶3 scene · ¶4 scene · ¶5 summary
(years of walks in one paragraph) · ¶6 scene (the decision) · ¶7–¶8
scene · ¶9 scene (the walk) · ¶10–¶13 scene (the door, the revelation)
· ¶14 summary with scene moments (the kettle, the crosswalk story —
accept either label with a defense) · ¶15 reflection/summary (the
understanding, looking back) · ¶16 scene (the final exchange). Accept
any labeling that defends the two required choices with the rule:
*scene the turning points; summarize the travel between them.*

**Expand and compress (guided practice 2).** Sample expansion (one
sentence of the summary → scene): from "On Friday he finally told her"
→ "Dev stood in the doorway of the kitchen with the camera in both
hands, the cracked lens catching the light every time he breathed.
'Maya,' he said, and then he stopped, because her name sounded
different when it was the beginning of an apology." The scene gains the
*feeling* of the telling — the shaking hands, the stopped sentence —
which the summary can only report. Sample compression of the scene →
summary: "Dev told Maya he had broken her camera, and after a moment
she forgave him because he had been honest." The summary loses the
question "You carried it all the way down here to tell me?" — which is
where the forgiveness actually happens. Accept any pair that names a
real gain and a real loss.

**Sensory rewrite (guided practice 3).** Sample (no judgment
adjectives — no "creepy," "scary," "quiet" as a judgment):
> The hallway light buzzed and went out when Maria stepped toward it.
> Her shoes stuck faintly to the floorboards, and the air smelled of
> damp plaster and the lemon soap someone had used years ago. Somewhere
> behind the wall, a pipe ticked as it cooled.
Accept any rewrite with three sensory details (one per sense) and zero
judgment adjectives. Flag any remaining judgment adjective ("creepy,"
"scary," "eerie") as not yet revised.

**Exit check.** (a) Scene for turning points (decisions, confrontations,
revelations); summary for the travel between them. (b) A telling detail
is one small, specific thing that implies a large truth (mug rings like
tree rings imply years). (c) Short sentences speed a passage up; long
sentences slow it down.

## Lesson 05

**Skeleton check (guided practice 1).** The learner's skeleton must
have four labeled rows (Opening / Middle chain / Climax / Reflective
conclusion) with scene/summary marks. **Require:** the climax is marked
*scene*. If any climax is marked summary, send it back — a climax told
in summary is a news report, not a story.

**Second line test (guided practice 2).** Acceptable: the second line
named in one sentence and a meeting scene named exactly (for example,
for the mentor story: the dog line meets the grandfather line in the
kitchen, ¶13). If the learner cannot name the meeting scene, the
correct response is either to write the meeting scene or to cut the
second line with a reason — both are acceptable; leaving a floating
second line is not.

**The ugly sentence (guided practice 3).** Any deliberately bad first
sentence followed by a second sentence that continues as if the first
were fine earns full marks. The point is the *skill* (keep going past
a bad sentence), not the quality of either sentence.

**Exit check.** (a) Three rules that matter most (any three of the
five, stated in the learner's words): draft in order but do not get
stuck; write past the ugly parts with `[FIX]` marks; hit the
load-bearing moments; aim for 1,200–2,000 words; use the techniques
without performing them. (b) A subplot is a second thread that runs
beside the main story; it must meet the main line, and it must be
shorter than the main line.

## Lesson 06

**Conclusion audit (guided practice 1).** The learner scores their own
last paragraph against the four items (answers the story's question;
names want and need; looks forward as well as back; does not announce
a lesson). Any "no" is a revision task, not a wrong answer. The adult
verifies the self-score against the paragraph.

**Bad-conclusion fix (guided practice 2).** Sample:
> On the drive home, Barnaby asleep on the back seat with his head on
> her shoes, Samira watched the lantern swing from its hook by the
> door. The long way had taken forty-five minutes. She would have
> missed all of it — the rain, the dog, the kitchen — if she had saved
> twenty. "I'm late," she said at the front door, and she was smiling.
> "I'll tell you why."
Accept any 3–4 sentence rewrite that (a) uses a concrete image instead
of an announced lesson, (b) does not claim knowledge about the dead
grandfather's feelings, and (c) does not end with "The end."

**Reading rehearsal (guided practice 3).** Full marks for a chosen
excerpt (300–400 words), two marked pauses, one marked speed change,
and two timed readings with the timing recorded. The delivery choice
the learner is unsure about is a *question to bring to session 20*,
not an error.

**Exit check.** (a) The three passes, in order: structure (does the
story hold together?), language (does every sentence do its job?),
conventions (is the manuscript clean?). (b) The four parts of a working
reflective conclusion: answers the story's question; names the want
and need; looks forward as well as back; does not announce a lesson.
(c) Any two delivery choices: pausing, speed changes, eye contact at
paragraph ends, choosing the excerpt, formal English.

## Practice A — story-seed sketches

Sample for **Seed 1 ("The Unsent Letter")**: Problem — "Marcus finds
a forty-year-old apology letter in a returned library book, addressed
to someone named E., who still lives three streets away." Want —
"Marcus wants to deliver the letter — it feels like the good thing to
do, and it would make a great story." Need — "Marcus needs to learn
that some apologies belong to the people who wrote them, not to whoever
finds them." Chain — (1) Marcus finds the letter, which causes (2) him
to look up the borrower, which causes (3) him to walk to E.'s house,
which causes (4) E. to open the door and not recognize the name on the
envelope. Pull — delivering the letter would feel good to Marcus but
might hurt E.; not delivering it would protect E. but leave Marcus
feeling like a coward.

Sample for **Seed 2 ("The Last Game")**: Problem — "Priya's team has
lost every game, tonight is the last one, and the opposing striker is
her former best friend Lena." Want — "Priya wants to win — one win, to
end the season with something other than a zero." Need — "Priya needs
to stop keeping score against Lena and play the game in front of her."
Chain — (1) Priya learns Lena is the striker, which causes (2) her to
hesitate on a save, which causes (3) her team to turn on her, which
causes (4) her to see the missed foul and have to choose. Pull — winning
would end the losing streak but cost the friendship; losing would keep
the friendship possible but confirm the season's failure.

Full marks: two sketches, each with a specific problem, a want, a need
that pulls the other way, a four-event chain, and a one-sentence pull
explanation. A sketch whose want and need point the same way is not yet
a story — send it back.

## Practice B — want/need charts and the cause-effect chain

**Samira chart (at least two pairs).** Pair 1: Want — the shortcut home
in twenty minutes ("The shortcut would get her home before her mother
really started to worry," ¶6). Need — the long way, the walk, the
kitchen ("what she had needed was the long way," ¶15). Evidence: ¶6
and ¶15. Blocker: her grandfather's rule and the dog's address. Pair 2
(acceptable): Want — to get home dry and be done with the evening.
Need — to have an evening with her grandfather again, through the
kitchen where his name is still spoken. Evidence: ¶14–¶15. Blocker:
the rain, the distance, the fact that he is dead. Accept any second
pair that is genuinely distinct from the first (not a rewording).

**Removal test (three links).** Lantern removed: Samira never takes out
the light, so she never hears the dog whining at the edge of the
shelter — no dog, no tag, no choice. Tag removed: she finds a dog but
does not know where it lives, so there is no long way and no kitchen —
she would have to leave the dog or take it home with her. Choice at
the gate removed: there is no decision, so there is no story — the walk
is just a walk, and the kitchen never happens. Accept any three removals
that name what breaks concretely.

**Climax verdict.** Defended answer: the choice at the gate (¶6–¶8) is
the climax — want and need collide there and Samira chooses. The
kitchen (¶11–¶13) is the resolution: it shows what the choice unlocked.
Accept the kitchen as the climax *only* if the learner argues it as the
moment of understanding and names what the gate does instead (the
decision). An undefended answer ("the kitchen because it is the most
emotional part") is not acceptable — emotion is not the test; the
want/need collision is.

## Practice C — POV rewrites and the decision memo

**First-person kitchen scene.** Sample (events identical, first person):
> Before I could knock, the door opened, and a woman with silver hair
> and a blue cardigan stood there, one hand pressed to her chest.
> "Barnaby," she said. "Oh, you found him. You found my boy." "He was
> at the bus stop," I said. "The tag said fourteen Alder Lane. I'm
> Samira. I missed the last bus." She took the dog's face in both hands
> and then looked up at me. "Your grandfather used to walk this dog for
> me," she said. "Every Tuesday."
Accept any 6–8 sentence rewrite with identical events and a consistent
first person.

**Third-person omniscient kitchen scene.** Must contain at least two
places where the narrator reports a thought neither character could
know — for example: "Mrs. Okafor thought, *she has his eyes*" and
"Samira did not yet know that Mrs. Okafor had been waiting three years
for someone to knock." The learner must *mark* the two places. If the
rewrite reports no private thoughts, it is not omniscient — it is
third-person limited with different pronouns.

**Decision memo.** Full marks: POV and narrator named; one paragraph on
gains (specific to the learner's story, not POV in general); one
paragraph on losses (what the narrator cannot see, and the workaround).
**A memo with no losses is not approved** — send it back.

**Mrs. Okafor's POV.** Gain: the reader learns what Mrs. Okafor knows
— that she has waited years for a knock, that the dog's walks with the
grandfather were the best part of her week. Loss: the reader loses
Samira's surprise, and the scene where the grandfather is revealed
(¶13) stops being a revelation and becomes a memory — the story's
central turn disappears. Accept any answer that names one scene whose
meaning changes and says how.

## Practice D — dialogue drills

**Announcement fix (task 1).** Sample:
> Ana stood in the doorway of the practice room, her violin case
> banging once against the doorframe. She did not come in.
> "Ana?" her friend said.
> "I keep losing the third line," Ana said. She looked at the floor.
> "Every time. The third line."
> Her friend put her own case down. "Then play the first two for me.
> Just the first two."
Full marks: no announced feelings, same information, at least two
beats (the case banging, looking at the floor, putting the case down).

**The refusal (task 2).** Any three-exchange dialogue in which one
character asks and the other refuses without either saying what they
actually want, with at least two beats and a subtext line under each
spoken line. **Check:** if any subtext line is identical to its spoken
line, the dialogue is announcing — send it back.

**Beat map (task 3).** See Lesson 03 key (beats in ¶7–¶13) — any three
with a correct "what it shows."

**Error analysis (task 4).** The reader loses the characters' bodies,
the room, and the rhythm — two heads talking in an empty room, with no
way to picture where anyone is or what they are doing. The fix is
mechanical: put a beat at least every two or three lines — a gesture, a
glance, a movement, a silence.

**Compress (task 2).** Sample: "Dev told Maya he had broken her

detail.

**Gear check (task 4).** See Lesson 04 key. Full marks: all sixteen

## Practice F — multiple plot lines and reflection

**Weave plan (task 1).** Full marks: two parallel lines with 3–4 events
each, a meeting scene named exactly, and one sentence saying what the
second line answers that the main line cannot. For the mentor story:
main line — bus, lantern, dog, tag, gate, kitchen; second line —
grandfather's walks, the rule, the name still spoken; meeting scene —
the kitchen, ¶13.

**Meeting test (task 2).** Either a written meeting scene (4–6
sentences) or a cut second line with a reason. Both earn full marks; a
floating second line earns none.

**Reflection draft (task 3).** Sample (modeled on ¶15, no announced
lesson):
> On the walk back from Rosa's house, Ana held the cake box with both
> hands, the way her grandmother used to. The recipe was still missing.
> But the kitchen had smelled like cinnamon again, and Rosa had laughed
> at the same joke her grandmother always laughed at, and for the first
> time since the move, the new house had felt like a place where someone
> had lived before her.
Full marks: 4–6 sentences, looks back, names what changed, uses a
concrete image, never announces a lesson.

**Error analysis (task 4).** The problem: "And so Marcus learned that
curiosity is not kindness" *announces* the lesson and tells the reader
what to think; "He would never forget that day" is a cliché that
reports nothing. Sample fix:
> Marcus put the letter back in the book and shelved it where he had
> found it. E.'s door stayed closed. He walked home the long way, past
> the house with the blue shutters, and did not stop.

## Practice G — revision pass 1: structure

Full marks: a cut/keep/move list covering the whole draft, **at least
two cuts** with a one-sentence reason each, every MOVE with old and new
positions, and a draft 2 produced from the list. If the learner cannot
find two cuts, the adult finds one for them — a draft with nothing to
cut has not been read honestly. The one-sentence summary ("The
structural change that matters most is ___, because ___.") must name a
real change, not a generality.

**Removal test on three longest scenes:** any scene whose removal
breaks nothing goes on the CUT list. If the learner claims all three
scenes are load-bearing, the adult checks one of them — if the story
survives without it, it is a cut.

---

## Practice H — revision pass 2: language; pass 3: conventions

**Pass 2.** Full marks: a change log with **at least 8 changes** in
before → after pairs with a reason each, including at least **two
conciseness cuts** (redundant pair, unnecessary qualifier, phrase →
word). Sample entries:
- Before: "She was very, very tired after the really long walk." →
  After: "Her legs ached by the third mile." (Technique: conciseness +
  sensory detail. Gain: the tiredness is felt, not announced.)
- Before: "I am so angry with you," he said. → After: "He set the cup
  down without drinking from it. 'Don't,' he said." (Technique:
  dialogue with a beat. Gain: the anger is shown in the action.)
- Before: "The house was old and scary." → After: "The gate hung open
  on one hinge, and the porch light buzzed and went out." (Technique:
  telling detail. Gain: the reader concludes the house is old instead
  of being told.)

**Pass 3.** Full marks: the checklist complete (dialogue paragraphs,
quotation punctuation, two correct semicolons or a note, three varied
phrase/clause types underlined, spelling/paragraphing checked, MLA
format, word count 1,200–2,000 written at the top). Accept a note
explaining why no semicolon fits the draft — but check it; a story with
no closely related independent clauses is rare.

## Practice I — reflective conclusion and reading rehearsal

**Conclusion audit (task 1).** The learner's self-score is verified
against the paragraph. Any "no" must be revised until all four are yes
— the revision is the task, not the score.

**Bad-conclusion fix (task 2).** See Lesson 06 key. Full marks: 3–4
sentences, concrete image, no announced lesson, no claim about the dead
grandfather's feelings, no "The end."

**Reading rehearsal (task 3).** Full marks: excerpt chosen (300–400
words), two pauses and one speed change marked, two timed readings
with timings recorded, and one delivery question named. The question is
a question to bring to session 20, not an error.

**One honest note (task 4).** Full marks: one margin note recorded and
exactly that spot revised. If the note is vague ("good job"), the adult
rewrites it as a real note — "I got lost here" or "I wanted more
here" — and the learner revises that spot.

## Week-22 quiz

**A1 (2 pts).** Want (1 pt): to get across town to the library before
it closes and return the three-weeks-overdue book. Need (1 pt): to mail
the letter to her grandmother — to finally say what she has been
carrying unsent. Accept any wording that keeps the fast fix (library)
separate from the deeper one (the letter).

**A2 (2 pts).** Problem (any specific version): "Lena has one hour to
cross town and return an overdue book before the library closes, but
her only bus broke down — and stopping at the post office to mail the
letter would cost her the library trip." Deduct 1 pt if the answer
omits the choice (the library *or* the letter) — the choice is the
problem.

**A3 (2 pts).** Any event that tightens the pull with a correct reason.
Examples: it starts raining (running gets harder, the letter gets wet);
her phone dies (she cannot check the time or call for a ride); the
post-office line is long (mailing the letter now costs even more time);
she learns the library is closing early today. 1 pt for the event, 1 pt
for the reason it tightens rather than resolves.

**B4 (3 pts).** Correct order: (b) finds the wallet → (d) classmate
accuses → (c) teacher asks her to stay → (a) Maya tells the teacher.
1 pt for the order, 2 pts for the causes on the arrows (finding the
wallet causes the accusation; the accusation causes the teacher's
intervention; staying after class causes the explanation). Accept any
causal wording that links each pair.

**B5 (3 pts).** Without the wallet: there is no object for the
accusation, no reason for the classmate to accuse Maya, and no reason
for the teacher to intervene — the chain collapses. The story would
have to invent a replacement cause (for example, someone else's missing
item blamed on Maya, or a rumor with no object). 2 pts for naming what
disappears, 1 pt for the replacement.

**C6 (3 pts).** The error is a **POV break**: third-person limited,
staying close to Theo, cannot report his mother's private thoughts
("thought he had finally learned his lesson"). Fix (any one): report
only what Theo can observe ("His mother watched from the doorway, her
arms folded, and said nothing"), or change the POV to omniscient. 2 pts
for naming the error, 1 pt for a correct fix.

**C7 (3 pts).** Any observable substitute for the mother's thoughts:
a beat (she sets his favorite dinner on the table without a word), a
line of dialogue ("Your father would have done the same thing at your
age"), or something she avoids saying. 2 pts for the technique, 1 pt
for a one-sentence example in action.

**D8 (3 pts).** Sample:
> Tom stared at the floor, his jaw working. "You took my seat," he
> said.
> Sara's hands tightened around her bag strap. "Tom —"
> "Don't." He turned away. "Just don't."
Full marks: no announced feelings, same information (he is angry, she
is sorry), at least one beat. Deduct 1 pt if a feeling is still
announced; deduct 1 pt if there is no beat.

**D9 (3 pts).** The error is a **pacing reversal**: the routine elevator
ride (travel between moments) is told as a scene, while the
friendship-ending argument (the turning point) is told as a summary.
Fix: summarize the elevator ride in a sentence or two, and stage the
argument as a scene. 2 pts for naming the error, 1 pt for the correct
gear for each moment.

**E10 (3 pts).** Sample: "The gate hung open on one hinge, and the
porch light buzzed and went out when she stepped toward it." Full
marks: one telling detail, zero judgment adjectives. Deduct 1 pt for
each judgment adjective that remains ("scary," "creepy," "old" used as
a judgment rather than a fact).

**E11 (3 pts).** Adding more adjectives did not fix the sentence
because every adjective is the *same empty judgment* — "very dark,
super creepy, extremely old, terrifying" all tell the reader what to
think without giving them anything to perceive. Intensifiers intensify
a claim; they do not add evidence. What to do instead: replace the
judgments with one precise sensory detail per sense (sound, sight,
smell). 2 pts for the diagnosis, 1 pt for the correct alternative.

**Scoring:** 30 points total. Reteach if below 24 (see the teacher

## Unit assessment

### Task 1 — Craft analysis (30 pts)

**1. Want vs. need (10 pts).** Want (3 pts): to get through her four
lines flawlessly — to perform perfectly, without freezing ("Her part
was four lines. She had rehearsed them two hundred times, in the
shower, on the bus, to her little brother," ¶2). Need (3 pts): to
perform for a real person who needs her, instead of for an imagined
judge — to talk *to* someone rather than *at* a wall ("talk to *him*…
The back wall doesn't need you. He does," ¶6). Pull (4 pts): the more
she chases perfection (rehearsing to the wall, to the empty chairs),
the worse she performs — her throat closes, the lines come out flat,
she forgets the third line entirely (¶3–¶4). The want and the need
pull in opposite directions because perfection requires an audience
that cannot judge her, while connection requires an audience that can.

**2. Two techniques (10 pts, 5 pts each).** Any two of the following,
each with a named technique, an exact quote, and a step-by-step
mechanism:

- **Sensory language (¶1):** "The auditorium smelled like dust and old
  paint, and the stage lights buzzed faintly, the way old lights do
  when they are deciding whether to stay on." Mechanism: one detail per
  sense (smell: dust and old paint; sound: the buzzing lights) tells
  the reader the room is old and tired without ever using the word
  "old" as a judgment; the comparison ("deciding whether to stay on")
  gives the lights a small hesitation that mirrors Nadia's own.
- **Pacing — scene vs. summary (¶2 vs. ¶4):** the month of rehearsal
  is summarized in one sentence ("She had rehearsed them two hundred
  times, in the shower, on the bus, to her little brother"), while the
  freezing is staged moment by moment ("It came out flat. The second
  line came out worse. On the third line, she forgot the words
  entirely and stood there, the silence stretching"). Mechanism: the
  summary covers the routine fast so the scene can slow down for the
  moment that changes everything — the short sentences in ¶4 *are* the
  freezing, beat by beat.
- **Dialogue with a beat (¶5–¶6):** the brother "holding up her script
  like a flag" and mouthing the third line; the director's pen "still"
  in ¶4. Mechanism: the beat shows what the dialogue cannot say — the
  brother's flag is encouragement without words, and the still pen is
  the director waiting instead of judging.
- **Reflection (¶7):** "She did it again. This time she did not look at
  the wall at all." Mechanism: one sentence of looking back — the
  reflection names what changed (the audience) without explaining the
  lesson; the reader does that work.
- **Point of view (throughout):** third-person limited, staying close
  to Nadia — the narration reports her throat closing and her lines
  going flat, but never enters the director's or the brother's head.
  Mechanism: the reader feels the freeze from inside it, and the
  brother's clap lands as a surprise because the narration could not
  warn us.
Deduct 2 pts per technique if the mechanism is missing (a label with a
quote but no step-by-step explanation is not an analysis).

**3. The turning point (10 pts).** The turning point is **¶5** — the
single slow clap from the back of the auditorium. What changes: the
audience changes from empty chairs and a back wall (an imagined judge)
to one real person (her little brother, holding up her script like a
flag); Nadia stops performing *at* something and starts performing
*to* someone; the director's instruction in ¶6 ("talk to *him*") only
makes sense because ¶5 has already shown her what that means. If ¶5
were cut: there would be no reason for Nadia to say the third line, no
one for the director to point at, and the ending ("She did it again")
would be unearned — the story would be a girl failing twice instead of
a girl learning who she is performing for. Accept ¶6 as the turning
point only if the learner argues that the director's instruction is the
moment of change *and* names what ¶5 does instead (the clap is the
evidence that makes the instruction possible). An answer that names a
paragraph without explaining what changes there earns at most 4 pts.

### Task 2 — Revision (20 pts)

**1. Rewrite (12 pts).** Sample (same events, two techniques: dialogue
with a beat; sensory language; sentence-length control):

> Elena stood in the wings with her violin case still closed, the
> rosin dust on her fingers from the last practice. The hall smelled
> like old wood and floor polish. "Two minutes," the stage manager
> said, not looking up. Elena nodded. Her throat was dry. She walked
> out, set the violin under her chin, and drew the bow — and the first
> note came out thin and sharp, like a wire snapping. Her hand shook.
> She stopped. Breathed. The second note was steadier. By the third
> phrase, her shoulders had dropped, and the hall was quiet in the way
> a room is quiet when it is listening. When she finished, the
> applause came all at once, and her teacher, in the third row, was
> already standing.

Scoring: 12 pts — same events (4 pts), at least two techniques used
well (4 pts), scene used for the turning moment (2 pts), no new events
added (2 pts). Deduct 2 pts for each missing element; deduct 4 pts if
new events are added (the task says keep the same events).

**2. Change log (8 pts).** Sample (at least four changes, each with
technique + gain):

| Before | After | Technique and gain |
|---|---|---|
| "Elena was very nervous before her violin recital." | "Elena stood in the wings with her violin case still closed, the rosin dust on her fingers from the last practice." | Sensory language. Gain: the reader feels the nervousness in the unopened case and the dusty fingers instead of being told "very nervous." |
| "She was scared she would make a mistake." | "Her throat was dry." | Telling detail. Gain: one physical fact replaces a judgment about fear. |
| "Her teacher was nice and told her to relax." | "Her teacher, in the third row, was already standing." (at the end) | Beat + scene. Gain: the teacher's support is shown in an action at the moment it matters, not announced in advance. |
| "She played the first notes and they sounded bad." | "The first note came out thin and sharp, like a wire snapping." | Sensory language (sound + comparison). Gain: the reader hears the bad note. |
| "Then she remembered to breathe and she played better." | "She stopped. Breathed. The second note was steadier." | Sentence-length control. Gain: the short sentences *are* the slowing down; the reader feels the breath. |
| "Everyone was happy at the end." | "When she finished, the applause came all at once." | Scene instead of summary. Gain: the ending is shown in an action rather than reported as a feeling. |

Scoring: 8 pts — 2 pts per change (up to four changes scored), each
change earning its points only if it names the technique *and* the gain.
A change log that lists before/after pairs without technique and gain
earns at most 3 pts.

**Scoring:** 50 points total (Task 1: 30; Task 2: 20). Conference before
recording if one task is much weaker than the other (see the teacher
guide).

## Project notes

Annotated guidance for scoring the Story Workshop Portfolio (40 pts).
See [project.md](project.md) for the rubric.

**Architecture (10).** Excellent: the problem is specific (a named
situation, not "a bad day"); want and need pull in opposite directions
and the learner can point at the sentence where they collide; every
event causes the next (run the removal test on two links during
scoring); the climax is the want/need collision. Proficient: all
elements present, but the chain has one gap or the climax is
identifiable rather than inevitable. Developing: the problem is vague,
or want and need point the same way, or the chain has gaps the learner
cannot defend. Beginning: no clear problem or chain; events are a list.
**Common error:** the learner's climax is a coincidence (a stranger
arrives, a letter is found) rather than a choice. A climax that happens
*to* the character is not a climax — send it back.

**Craft techniques (10).** Excellent: dialogue reveals character
(beats + subtext — check the subtext lines in the craft log); at least
one deliberate scene/summary choice the learner can defend; sensory
language with one detail per sense in at least one passage; multiple
plot lines that meet. Proficient: techniques present and mostly
purposeful; one technique decorative (usually the subplot, which never
quite meets the main line). Developing: dialogue announces feelings;
description is judgment adjectives; pacing uncontrolled. Beginning: no
technique is visible, or techniques are named in the log but absent
from the story. **Check:** the craft log must name exact lines — a log
that says "I used sensory language" without a line reference is not
evidence.

**Revision (10).** Excellent: three passes visible — real cuts (at
least two, with reasons), a move or a defended reason why none was
needed, 8+ language changes with reasons, conventions clean (MLA
format, dialogue punctuation, word count in range). Proficient:
passes visible; cuts and changes present; minor convention errors.
Developing: one pass missing or superficial — most commonly the
structure pass is skipped and the change log is proofreading only.
Beginning: no revision evidence; the submitted story is the first
draft. **Check:** compare draft 1 and the final story. If they are
identical except for commas, the revision score is Beginning
regardless of what the log claims.

**Conclusion + reading (10).** Excellent: the reflective conclusion
passes all four audit items (answers the story's question; names want
and need; looks forward as well as back; does not announce a lesson);
the reading excerpt is well chosen (climax or conclusion) with marked,
audible delivery choices; the one-choice answer is precise ("I want
you to notice the moment she says 'Long way it is' — it is the first
time she says the choice out loud"). Proficient: conclusion passes 3 of
4; reading rehearsed with some delivery choices. Developing:
conclusion announces a lesson or only ends the story; reading
unrehearsed. Beginning: no reflective conclusion; no reading.
**Check:** the conclusion audit in Practice I is the evidence — if the
learner's own audit says "no" on any item and the paragraph was not
revised, the conclusion cannot score above Developing.
