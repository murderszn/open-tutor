# "The Long Way Home" — Mentor Story (Original)

**Author note:** this story was written for this unit. It is an original
work, not a reproduction of any published story. Paragraphs are numbered
so lessons and practice tasks can cite exact locations (¶1, ¶2, …).
Reading time: about 6–8 minutes. The story demonstrates the unit's
craft targets: a problem that forces a choice, a character whose **want**
and **need** pull in opposite directions, an event sequence in which each
event causes the next, dialogue that reveals character, scene and summary
used for different jobs, precise sensory language, and a reflective
conclusion.

---

¶1. The last bus had already left when Samira reached the stop, and her
phone was dead.

¶2. She stood under the shelter and watched the rain bead on the
timetable glass. The streetlights on Mill Road had been out for a week —
somebody had filed a report, somebody else had promised a crew — and the
sky over the rooftops was the flat gray of a bruise. Home was forty-five
minutes away on foot. Her mother would be standing at the kitchen window
by now, watching the road.

¶3. Samira unzipped the side pocket of her backpack and took out her
grandfather's brass lantern. It was heavier than it looked, dented on one
corner where he had dropped it on the pier the summer she was nine. She
had borrowed it that morning from the hook by the front door, because the
forecast had said storms and because — she could admit this only to
herself — she liked the weight of it in her hand. She turned the small
brass key. The wick caught, and a circle of warm light opened around her
feet.

¶4. That was when she heard it: a thin whine, almost lost under the rain.
A scruffy brown dog stood at the edge of the shelter, shaking water off
its ears in slow, defeated shakes. Its collar was red and frayed, and
the tag, when Samira crouched to read it, said BARNABY — 14 ALDER LANE.

¶5. Fourteen Alder Lane was not on her way home. It was in the other
direction, past the old rail yard — the long way around, an extra half
hour at least. The shortcut was the rail yard itself: through the rusted
gate, along the overgrown tracks, out the far fence, and home in twenty
minutes. Her grandfather had walked her through that yard a hundred
times when she was small, naming the weeds for her — chicory, Queen
Anne's lace, milkweed — and he had given her exactly one rule about it:
*never cut through the yard after dark.*

¶6. Samira looked at the gate. The fence leaned where the ivy had pulled
it down, and beyond it the tracks disappeared into blue evening mist.
The shortcut would get her home before her mother really started to
worry. The long way would get the dog home, and it would make her
forty-five minutes late. She could feel the two halves of the decision
pulling like a rope in her hands.

¶7. "Well," she said to the dog. "You picked the wrong bus stop,
Barnaby."

¶8. The dog thumped its tail once against the wet concrete and looked up
at her with the patient, unearned trust of an animal that has decided
you are its person now. Samira sighed, clipped the lantern to her
backpack strap so both hands were free, and turned away from the gate.
"Come on," she said. "Long way it is."

¶9. The long way was longer than she remembered. The rain found the gap
between her collar and her neck. Her shoes filled slowly with cold
water. But the lantern light moved with her, and it turned the ordinary
street into something worth looking at: the wet leaves stuck to the
sidewalk like dark coins, the smell of rain on hot pavement from the
afternoon sun, the way Barnaby stopped every few steps to sniff a fence
post as if he were reading the neighborhood's mail. Twice she thought
about turning back. Twice the dog looked up at her, and twice she kept
walking.

¶10. Number 14 was the last house on Alder Lane, with a porch light on
and a garden gone wild behind a low fence. Before Samira could knock, the
door opened, and a woman with silver hair and a blue cardigan stood
there, one hand pressed to her chest.

¶11. "Barnaby," she said. "Oh, you found him. You found my boy."

¶12. "He was at the bus stop," Samira said. "The tag said fourteen Alder
Lane. I'm Samira. I missed the last bus."

¶13. Mrs. Okafor — the name came off the mailbox as Samira said it —
took the dog's face in both hands and then, without letting go of him,
looked up at Samira. "Your grandfather used to walk this dog for me,"
she said. "Every Tuesday. He said Barnaby was the only creature in this
town with worse knees than his own." She laughed, and then she stopped
laughing, because Samira's face had done something she could not
control. "Oh," Mrs. Okafor said softly. "Oh, honey. Come inside. You're
soaked through. I'll drive you home."

¶14. In the kitchen, while the kettle boiled, Mrs. Okafor told her that
her grandfather had taught Barnaby to sit at every crosswalk. "He said a
dog that waits at a crosswalk is a dog that thinks," she said. "He said
most people could learn from that." Samira sat with her hands around a
mug and felt the house warm her from the outside in. She thought about
the gate, and the mist, and the twenty minutes she had not saved.

¶15. On the drive home, Barnaby asleep on the back seat with his head on
her shoes, Samira understood something she had not understood at the bus
stop. She had wanted the short way home — fast, dry, done. But what she
had needed was the long way: the walk itself, the rain, the dog's patient
trust, the kitchen where her grandfather's name was still spoken like
someone who might walk in at any moment. The long way had given her back
an evening with him. She would have missed it entirely if she had saved
those twenty minutes.

¶16. When her mother opened the front door, Samira was already smiling.
"I'm late," she said. "I'll tell you why. It's a long story — and it's
worth the long way home."

## Why this story is the unit's mentor text

- **Problem:** missed bus, dead phone, lost dog — a situation that forces
  a choice (¶1–¶4).
- **Want vs. need:** Samira *wants* the fast, dry shortcut home; she
  *needs* the long way — to return the dog and to grieve her grandfather
  properly (¶5–¶6, ¶15).
- **Event sequence:** every event causes the next — the dead phone means
  no call home; the lantern means she can walk; the tag means she knows
  the address; the address means the long way; the long way means the
  kitchen and the story about her grandfather (¶1–¶14).
- **Dialogue that reveals character:** Samira talks to the dog (¶7–¶8);
  Mrs. Okafor's lines reveal the grandfather connection (¶13).
- **Scene vs. summary:** the walk is a scene (¶9); the grandfather's
  history is summary and reflection (¶5, ¶14–¶15).
- **Reflective conclusion:** ¶15–¶16 look back and name what the events
  meant — the story's answer to its own question.
