# Craft Vignettes — Unit 05 (Grade 9 Language Arts)

Four original paired passages. Each pair shows the **same moment told
two ways** so the learner can see exactly what one craft choice does.
All passages are original to this unit. Use them in Lessons 2–4 and
Practices C–F; the answer key gives the teaching point of each pair.

---

## Vignette A — Scene vs. summary (Lesson 4)

The same moment — a boy, Dev, telling his sister he broke her camera —
told two ways.

**Version 1 — Summary:**
> Dev broke his sister's camera on Tuesday. He felt bad about it for
> three days. On Friday he finally told her, and she was angry at
> first, but then she forgave him because he had been honest.

**Version 2 — Scene:**
> Dev stood in the doorway of the kitchen with the camera in both
> hands, the cracked lens catching the light every time he breathed.
> "Maya," he said, and then he stopped, because her name sounded
> different when it was the beginning of an apology. "I dropped it. On
> the stairs. I tried to fix it — I watched three videos — but the lens
> won't turn anymore." Maya did not take the camera. She looked at his
> hands, which were shaking, and then at his face, and she said, "You
> carried it all the way down here to tell me?" Dev nodded. "Okay," she
> said. "Okay. Thank you for telling me."

**Teaching point:** the summary *reports* the forgiveness; the scene
*earns* it. The scene shows the shaking hands, the three videos, and the
question "You carried it all the way down here to tell me?" — and that
question is where the forgiveness actually happens. A summary can cover
three days in two sentences; only a scene can show a person changing
their mind.

## Vignette B — Dialogue that tells vs. dialogue that reveals (Lesson 3)

The same exchange — a student, June, asking a teacher for an extension —
told two ways.

**Version 1 — Dialogue that tells:**
> "I am very stressed about this assignment," June said. "I have a lot
> of other homework, and I am worried I will not finish in time."
> "I understand that you are stressed," the teacher said. "I will give
> you two more days because I am a kind teacher."

**Version 2 — Dialogue that reveals:**
> June stood in the doorway holding her notebook like a shield. "The
> assignment — " she started, and then stopped. "It's just. My little
> brother was sick all week, so I was up. And the bus — " She looked
> down at her shoes. "I know it's late. I know."
> Ms. Alvarez capped her pen. "How much do you have?"
> "Half. Maybe a little more than half."
> "Bring me the half on Friday," Ms. Alvarez said. "And June — next
> time, come on Tuesday, when there's still time to fix it."

**Teaching point:** Version 1 has the characters announce their
feelings ("I am very stressed") and their traits ("because I am a kind
teacher") — the reader is told, not shown. Version 2 reveals the same
information through what June *does* (holds the notebook like a shield,
looks at her shoes, trails off mid-sentence) and what the teacher
*does* (caps her pen — the small gesture that means *I'm listening
now*). Notice also what is left unsaid: June never says "please," and
the teacher never says "I forgive you" — the extension *is* the answer.

## Vignette C — Flat description vs. sensory description (Lesson 4)

The same room — an old kitchen at night — described two ways.

**Version 1 — Flat:**
> The kitchen was old and messy. There was a table and some chairs. It
> was dark except for one light. The room smelled bad.

**Version 2 — Sensory:**
> The kitchen light buzzed faintly, the way old lights do when they are
> deciding whether to stay on. The table was ringed with mug stains,
> one overlapping the next like the rings of a tree, and the air smelled
> of yesterday's onions and the lemon soap someone had used to wash the
> floor. Through the window, the yard was black except for the shape of
> the fence, and somewhere in the walls the pipes ticked as they cooled.

**Teaching point:** Version 1 uses adjectives that judge (*old*,
*messy*, *bad*) without giving the reader anything to perceive. Version
2 gives one detail per sense — a sound (the buzzing light, the ticking
pipes), a sight (mug rings like tree rings), a smell (onions, lemon
soap) — and lets the reader *conclude* that the kitchen is old and
lived-in. Precise sensory language (W.9-10.3.d) does not mean *more*
adjectives; it means *better-chosen* details, one per sense, doing the
work of ten vague ones.

## Vignette D — Two points of view, one moment (Lesson 2)

The same moment — a girl, Ada, finding her lost key in the school
garden — told from two points of view.

**Version 1 — First person (Ada):**
> I was on my knees in the wet dirt, pushing the leaves aside with both
> hands, when my fingers found it — cold, small, and exactly where I had
> told myself it couldn't be. I sat back on my heels and laughed, one
> short laugh, because I had spent forty minutes being angry at everyone
> in the world and the key had been under a leaf the whole time.

**Version 2 — Third-person limited (staying close to Ada):**
> Ada was on her knees in the wet dirt, pushing the leaves aside with
> both hands, when her fingers found it — cold, small, and exactly where
> she had told herself it couldn't be. She sat back on her heels and
> laughed, one short laugh. Forty minutes of anger at everyone in the
> world, and the key had been under a leaf the whole time.

**Teaching point:** the events are identical, and even most of the
sentences are identical — only the pronouns and one small phrase change
("I had told myself" / "she had told herself"). But the *distance*
changes: first person puts the reader inside Ada's head at the moment
she laughs at herself; third-person limited keeps the reader one step
back, watching her laugh. Neither is better in general — the choice
depends on what the story needs: confession and immediacy (first
person) or a little room for the reader to judge (third-person
limited). The POV decision memo (Practice C) asks the learner to make
this call for their own story and defend it.
