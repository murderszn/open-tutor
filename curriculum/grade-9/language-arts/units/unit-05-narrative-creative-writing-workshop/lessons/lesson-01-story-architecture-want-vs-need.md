# Lesson 01 — Story Architecture: Problem, Want vs. Need, Event Sequences

**Goal (student-readable):** I can name a story's problem, tell its

## A story is a problem that forces a choice

Every story starts the same way: somebody wants something, and
something stands in the way. That "something in the way" is the
**problem** (or situation). Without a problem, there is no story — there
is only a description of a nice day.

"The Long Way Home" opens with its problem in the very first sentence:

> "The last bus had already left when Samira reached the stop, and her
> phone was dead." (¶1)

Two facts, both bad, both stated plainly. The bus is gone (she cannot
get home the easy way) and the phone is dead (she cannot call for help).
Notice what the sentence does *not* do: it does not explain why she is
late, or tell you her life story. A problem stated cleanly is an
invitation — the reader immediately wants to know what she will do.

A good problem has three qualities:

1. **It is specific.** Not "Samira had a bad day" but "the last bus
   left and her phone is dead."
2. **It forces a choice.** Samira cannot stay at the bus stop all
   night; she must walk, and she must choose *how*.
3. **It gets worse before it gets better.** The rain, the dead
   streetlights (¶2), the lost dog (¶4) — each new fact tightens the
   problem instead of solving it.

## Want vs. need: the engine of a story

Here is the most useful distinction in this whole unit. A character's
**want** is what they think will fix their problem. Their **need** is
what will actually fix it — usually something deeper, which they may
not see yet. A story happens in the gap between the two.

Samira's **want** is simple: get home fast, dry, and done. The shortcut
through the rail yard would do it in twenty minutes (¶5–¶6).

Samira's **need** is harder to name, and the story does not name it
until the end. She needs the long way — the walk itself, the rain, the
dog's trust, the kitchen where her grandfather's name is still spoken.
She needs to grieve her grandfather properly, and the shortcut would
have stolen the evening that lets her do it:

> "She had wanted the short way home — fast, dry, done. But what she
> had needed was the long way: the walk itself, the rain, the dog's
> patient trust, the kitchen where her grandfather's name was still
> spoken like someone who might walk in at any moment." (¶15)

The story's central image says the same thing without words: "She could
feel the two halves of the decision pulling like a rope in her hands"
(¶6). That rope *is* the plot. When want and need pull in opposite
directions, the reader feels the tension — and the story's job is to
show which side wins, and what it costs.

**The test:** if a character's want and need point the same way, there
is no tension and no story — just a character doing the obvious thing.
If you cannot find the pull in opposite directions in your own story
seed, you have not found your story yet.

## Event sequences: each event causes the next

A plot is not a list of things that happen. It is a **chain reaction**:
each event *causes* the next one. The Purdue OWL's "Fiction Writing
Basics" puts it this way: "Plot is created by the manner in which the
writer arranges and organizes particular actions in a meaningful way,"
and "a sequence of events causes other events to happen."

Watch the chain in "The Long Way Home":

| Event | What it causes |
|---|---|
| The last bus leaves; the phone is dead (¶1) | Samira must walk home |
| The streetlights are out (¶2) | She takes out her grandfather's lantern (¶3) |
| The lantern's light opens around her feet (¶3) | She hears the dog whining at the edge of the shelter (¶4) |
| The dog's tag reads "Barnaby — 14 Alder Lane" (¶4) | She knows the dog's home — and that it is the *long* way (¶5) |
| The shortcut would break her grandfather's rule (¶5) | She must choose: shortcut or long way (¶6) |
| She chooses the long way (¶8) | The walk, the rain, the sensory street (¶9) |
| The walk ends at Number 14 (¶10) | She meets Mrs. Okafor, who knew her grandfather (¶11–¶13) |
| Mrs. Okafor tells the crosswalk story (¶14) | Samira understands what she needed (¶15) |

Now try the **removal test**: take out any one event and the story
breaks. Remove the dead phone and Samira calls her mother — no walk.
Remove the lantern and she never hears the dog. Remove the tag and she
never learns the address. A strong event sequence is one where every
link matters. If an event can be removed without changing anything, it
is decoration, not plot.

## Worked example 1 — Naming the want and the need

**Seed:** "The Unsent Letter" (Marcus finds a forty-year-old apology
letter in a library book).

- **Problem:** Marcus holds a letter that was never delivered, and he
  knows who it was meant for.
- **Want:** deliver the letter — it feels like the good thing to do,
  and it would make a great story to tell.
- **Need:** learn that some apologies belong to the people who wrote
  them, not to whoever finds them — and that curiosity is not the same
  as kindness.
- **Why the pull matters:** if Marcus simply delivers the letter, the
  story ends in five minutes and nothing changes. The story only starts
  when delivering it and *not* delivering it both feel right for
  different reasons — and Marcus has to choose which kind of person he
  is.

## Worked example 2 — Building a cause-effect chain from a seed

**Seed:** "The Last Game" (Priya, goalkeeper, final game, former best
friend Lena on the other team).

1. Priya learns Lena is the opposing striker → she stops playing the
   game in front of her and starts playing the friendship.
2. Playing the friendship makes her hesitate on a save → the other team
   scores, and her own team turns on her.
3. Her team turning on her forces the question: is she playing for the
   win or for Lena?
4. Lena goes down injured and only Priya sees the missed foul → Priya
   must choose between the win and the truth.
5. She tells the referee → her team loses the game and the season ends
   0–8 — but Lena, helped off the field, says the first words between
   them in a year.

Each event causes the next, and the last event answers the first
question: the friendship was never going to be fixed by a scoreline.
Notice that step 5 is the **climax** — the moment the want (win) and
the need (stop keeping score) finally collide and the need wins.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. **Illustration check.** Return to your three notebook answers about
   the illustration. Now read ¶5–¶6 of the story. Which of your
   inferences were right? Which did the story add that the picture
   could not show (for example, the grandfather's rule)? Write one
   sentence: what can an illustration *show* about a decision that
   words cannot, and what can words *tell* about a decision that a
   picture cannot?
2. **Name the want and the need** for "The Recipe Box" seed (Ana and
   the missing birthday-cake recipe). Write them as two sentences
   beginning "Ana wants…" and "Ana needs…". Then write one sentence
   explaining why they pull in opposite directions.
3. **Chain the mentor story.** Copy the six events below into your
   notebook in order, then draw an arrow from each event to the event
   it causes — and write the *cause* on the arrow (for example, "the
   tag gives her the address, so she knows the long way"):
   the bus leaves → the lantern comes out → the dog appears → the tag
   is read → the choice at the gate → the kitchen at Number 14.
   Which link in the chain is the story's climax, and why? (Hint: the
   climax is not the loudest event; it is the event where want and
   need finally collide.)

## Independent practice

Choose **one** of the three story seeds (or your own idea) and write
in your notebook:

1. The problem, stated in one specific sentence (not "a bad day" —
   name the facts).
2. The want ("My character wants…") and the need ("My character
   needs…") as two sentences.
3. A six-event chain in which each event causes the next. Number the
   events 1–6 and, after each one, write "which causes…" and the next
   event.
4. Circle the event that will be your climax — the moment want and
   need collide — and write one sentence saying what the character
   chooses.

Bring this page to the next session: it is the skeleton of your final
story.

## Applied task

Write a **two-sentence opening** for your story — the problem, stated
as cleanly as ¶1 states Samira's ("The last bus had already left when
Samira reached the stop, and her phone was dead."). Read it aloud. If
a listener cannot immediately say what the character must do next, the
problem is not specific enough yet — revise it until they can.

## Exit check

In your own words, without looking back: (a) What is the difference
between a character's want and their need? (b) What is the removal
test, and what does it prove about an event? The adult checks both
answers before the next session.

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the story aloud while the
  learner follows; the analysis is the work, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** the event chain may be drawn as a row of
  boxes and arrows on paper or index cards; the want/need sentences
  may be dictated.
- **Extension:** rewrite ¶1 of "The Long Way Home" three ways — one
  that states the problem even more plainly, one that hides the problem
  for two sentences, one that opens *after* the choice at the gate.
  Which opening makes the strongest promise, and what does each version
  cost?

**Teacher note:** the common misconception here is that "want vs. need"
is a vocabulary quiz — learners will parrot "want is what they want,
need is what they need." Insist on the *pull*: make them point at the
exact sentence where the two halves pull in opposite directions (¶6,
"pulling like a rope in her hands"). If a learner's own seed has want
and need pointing the same way, send them back to the seed — do not let
them draft a story with no tension. See the answer key for the
illustration-check and chain answers.
