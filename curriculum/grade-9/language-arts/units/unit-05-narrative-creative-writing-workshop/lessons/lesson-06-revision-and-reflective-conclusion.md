# Lesson 06 — Revision and the Reflective Conclusion

**Goal (student-readable):** I can revise my story in three passes —
structure, language, conventions — write a conclusion that reflects on
what the story meant, and rehearse reading an excerpt aloud.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, first draft,
[craft log](../project.md), [UNC Writing Center "Writing
Concisely"](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/conciseness-handout)
(handout, for the language pass) · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L6](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Read your draft's last paragraph aloud.
Now answer: does it *end* the story, or does it *reflect* on it? (A
paragraph that only reports what happened last — "And then she went
home" — ends the story. A paragraph that looks back and names what it
meant — "The long way had given her back an evening with him" — reflects
on it. A narrative conclusion must do the second thing.)

## Revision is re-seeing, not proofreading

Revision is not fixing typos. Revision is **re-seeing** the story —
looking at it again with fresh eyes and asking whether it does what you
meant it to do. This unit revises in **three passes**, each with a
different question, and each pass happens on a different day so the
eyes stay fresh:

| Pass | Question | What you change |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Structure** (Practice G) | Does the story hold together? | Cut, keep, or move whole scenes; fix the chain; make sure the climax is earned |
| **2 — Language** (Practice H) | Does every sentence do its job? | Word choice, sensory details, dialogue beats, sentence length, conciseness |
| **3 — Conventions** (Practice H) | Is the manuscript clean? | Spelling, punctuation, paragraphing, dialogue punctuation, MLA manuscript format (U04 tools) |

**Never do all three passes at once.** A writer who proofreads
sentence by sentence while the structure is still broken is polishing
a house that has no roof. Structure first. Always.

## The structure pass: cut, keep, move

Read your draft once, start to finish, without a pen. Then read it a
second time with three colors (or three marks):

- **CUT** — any scene, paragraph, or line that does not advance the
  story, reveal character, or carry subtext. (The three-line test from
  Lesson 3 applies to the whole draft now, not just dialogue.)
- **KEEP** — the load-bearing moments: the problem, the inciting
  incident, the climax, the reflective conclusion.
- **MOVE** — any scene that works but sits in the wrong place. (A
  flashback that arrives too early, a revelation that comes after the
  moment it would have mattered most.)

The mentor story's structure pass would look like this: ¶5 (the
grandfather's history) is a KEEP — it is the reason the shortcut costs
something. ¶9 (the walk) is a KEEP — it is the scene where the need is
met. If the story had included a paragraph about Samira's school day
that morning, it would be a CUT: it advances nothing, and the story
starts at the bus stop for a reason. If ¶13 (Mrs. Okafor's revelation)
had come before ¶6 (the choice at the gate), it would be a MOVE: the
revelation only matters *after* Samira has chosen the long way.

**The removal test, again:** for every scene, ask what breaks if it is
removed. If nothing breaks, it is decoration. Cut it — and note the
cut in your craft log, because cutting is evidence of revision, and
revision is scored.

## The language pass: one word at a time, with a purpose

Now read the draft sentence by sentence. The UNC Writing Center's
"Writing Concisely" handout gives the mechanical moves: eliminate
redundant pairs ("each and every"), delete unnecessary qualifiers
("really," "very," "basically"), reduce prepositional phrases, replace
a phrase with a word. But conciseness is not the whole language pass.
Ask four questions of every paragraph:

1. **Is there a telling detail?** (Lesson 4) If a paragraph judges
   ("it was scary") instead of showing, replace the judgment with one
   precise detail.
2. **Does the dialogue pass the three-line test?** (Lesson 3) Cut or
   rewrite any line that does none of the three jobs.
3. **Does the sentence length match the moment?** (Lesson 4) Shorten
   the sentences in tense moments; lengthen one in reflective moments.
4. **Is the POV consistent?** (Lesson 2) Flag any sentence that reports
   a thought your narrator could not know. Either cut it or change the
   narrator's position — do not leave it.

Keep a **change log**: every change, in pairs (before → after). The
change log is not busywork; it is the evidence that revision happened,
and it is what the adult scores.

## The conventions pass: the manuscript

Apply the U04 tools to your own story: varied phrases and clauses
(L.9-10.1.b), semicolons used correctly (L.9-10.2.a), dialogue
punctuation (commas and periods inside closing quotation marks;
a new paragraph for each new speaker), and MLA manuscript format
(double-spaced, 12-point readable font, name and page number header,
title centered). A clean manuscript is not vanity — it is respect for
the reader, and it is the difference between a draft and a finished
piece.

## Worked example 1 — A reflective conclusion that works

Read ¶15–¶16 of the mentor story again:

> "On the drive home, Barnaby asleep on the back seat with his head on
> her shoes, Samira understood something she had not understood at the
> bus stop. She had wanted the short way home — fast, dry, done. But
> what she had needed was the long way: the walk itself, the rain, the
> dog's patient trust, the kitchen where her grandfather's name was
> still spoken like someone who might walk in at any moment. The long
> way had given her back an evening with him. She would have missed it
> entirely if she had saved those twenty minutes."

> "When her mother opened the front door, Samira was already smiling.
> 'I'm late,' she said. 'I'll tell you why. It's a long story — and
> it's worth the long way home.'"

Why this conclusion works (W.9-10.3.e — "a conclusion that follows
from and reflects on what is experienced, observed, or resolved"):

- **It answers the story's own question.** The story asked: shortcut or
  long way? The conclusion answers: the long way — and says *why* in
  terms the reader has earned (the walk, the rain, the kitchen).
- **It names the want and the need, finally, in the character's own
  understanding.** "She had wanted… But what she had needed…" — the
  two halves of the rope from ¶6 are named together for the first time.
- **It looks forward as well as back.** "It's a long story" promises
  that Samira will *tell* it — the story ends with storytelling itself,
  which is what the whole unit is about.
- **It does not explain the moral.** It never says "and the lesson is…"
  The reader does that work. A conclusion that explains its own meaning
  is a summary wearing a conclusion's clothes.

## Worked example 2 — A reflective conclusion that does not work

> "And so Samira learned that doing the right thing is always worth it,
> even when it is hard. She would never forget that night, and she knew
> her grandfather would be proud of her. The end."

Three problems: (1) it *announces* the lesson ("doing the right thing
is always worth it") instead of letting the reader feel it; (2) it
claims knowledge nobody can have ("her grandfather would be proud") —
a dead man cannot confirm that; (3) "The end" closes the story like a
door, instead of leaving the reader with an image or a question. Fix it
by cutting the announcement, cutting the claim about the grandfather,
and ending on a concrete image or a line of dialogue that *shows* the
change.

## The author reading: performing your own story

In session 20 you will read an excerpt of your final story aloud
(2–3 minutes, about 300–400 words). Reading aloud is a different skill
from writing — it is **delivery**, and delivery is a set of choices:

- **Choose the excerpt** where the language is doing the most work —
  usually the climax or the reflective conclusion, not the opening.
- **Mark the beats:** where do you pause? Where do you speed up? Where
  does your voice drop? (Short sentences: pause after each one. Long
  sentences: let them run.)
- **Look up at the end of a paragraph**, not in the middle of a
  sentence — the audience needs your eyes at the moments that matter.
- **Rehearse twice:** once alone, once for the adult, who times it and
  tells you the one place where the delivery and the words disagreed.
- **Formal English** (SL.9-10.6): the reading is a presentation, not a
  chat. Pronounce the words as written; do not paraphrase your own
  story on the fly.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. **Conclusion audit.** Read your draft's last paragraph. Does it (a)
   answer the story's question, (b) name the want and the need, (c)
   look forward as well as back, and (d) avoid announcing the lesson?
   Score each one yes/no. Any "no" is a revision task for Practice I.
2. **The bad conclusion fix.** Rewrite the "does not work" example
   above (the "And so Samira learned…" paragraph) into a working
   reflective conclusion of 3–4 sentences, using a concrete image
   instead of an announced lesson. Read both versions aloud: which one
   trusts the reader more?
3. **Reading rehearsal.** Choose your 300–400 word excerpt. Read it
   aloud once, slowly. Mark two places to pause and one place to speed
   up. Read it again with the marks. What changed?

## Independent practice

1. Complete the three revision passes (Practices G–H) and record the
   cut/keep/move list and the change log in your craft log.
2. Write or rewrite your reflective conclusion (Practice I) so that it
   passes the four-part audit above.
3. Rehearse your author reading twice and note the timing and the one
   delivery choice you are most unsure about — bring that question to
   session 20.

## Applied task

Trade drafts with the adult (or re-read your own with a stranger's
eyes). The reader writes **one** margin note, beginning "I got lost
here…" or "I wanted more here…" — nothing else. Revise exactly that
spot. One honest note, one real revision, is worth more than ten vague
compliments.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) What are the three revision passes, in
order, and what question does each ask? (b) What are the four parts of
a working reflective conclusion? (c) Name two delivery choices for the
author reading. The adult checks all three answers before the unit
assessment.

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading access:** the adult may read ¶15–¶16 aloud while the
  learner follows; the analysis is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** the author reading may be recorded
  rather than performed live; the conclusion audit may be a checklist
  rather than paragraphs.
- **Extension:** revise the mentor story's conclusion *without* ¶15 —
  end the story at ¶14 (the kitchen) and write a new ¶15 of your own.
  What does your version lose, and what does it gain? (This is the
  hardest revision task in the unit: it asks you to improve on a
  finished story and defend the change.)

**Teacher note:** the most common revision failure is **proofreading
disguised as revision**: the learner fixes five commas and calls the
story revised. The three-pass structure exists to prevent that — and
the structure pass must be *checked* before the language pass begins.
Ask to see the cut/keep/move list; if it is empty, no structure
revision happened, and the learner should go back. The second most
common failure is the **announced moral** in the conclusion. When a
conclusion says "and the lesson is…", do not argue about it — ask the
learner to delete that sentence and read the paragraph without it.
Nine times out of ten, the paragraph is better without it, and the
learner can see that for themselves.
