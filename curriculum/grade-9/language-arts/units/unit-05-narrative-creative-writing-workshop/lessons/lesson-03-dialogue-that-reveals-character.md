# Lesson 03 — Dialogue That Reveals Character

**Goal (student-readable):** I can write dialogue that reveals who a
character is — through what they say, what they avoid saying, and what
they do while they speak — instead of dialogue that just delivers
information.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, ["The Long
Way Home"](../texts/the-long-way-home.md), [craft
vignettes](../texts/craft-vignettes.md) Vignette B ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Read this exchange aloud with the adult,
one voice each: *"I am very angry with you," she said. "I am also very
angry with you," he said.* Now say it again, but neither character is
allowed to use the word "angry." What did you have to invent instead?
(Answer: actions, tone, and things left unsaid — a slammed door, a
voice gone quiet, a sentence that stops halfway. That invention is the
whole lesson.)

## Dialogue is action, not announcement

Beginning writers use dialogue the way a newsreader uses a script: to
announce information. *"I am very stressed about this assignment,"*
June says in Vignette B, Version 1 — and the reader learns nothing
except that June is stressed, which the sentence has already told us.
Real people almost never announce their feelings. They *show* them —
by what they say, what they avoid, and what their hands are doing.

The Purdue OWL's "Fiction Writing Basics" makes the same point from
the writer's side: character should be presented "indirectly, through
dialogue and action" — the reader should come to understand a character
by what she *does*, and dialogue is one of the things she does.

Dialogue in a story has three jobs, and a good line usually does at
least two of them at once:

1. **Reveal character** — the words a person chooses, and the words
   they avoid, show who they are.
2. **Advance the story** — a line should change something: a decision,
   a relationship, what the reader knows.
3. **Create subtext** — the meaning *under* the words. Subtext is what
   a character means but does not say. ("You carried it all the way
   down here to tell me?" means *I forgive you* — but Maya's sister
   never says the word "forgive.")

A line that does none of these three jobs is decoration. Cut it.

## Worked example 1 — Telling vs. revealing (Vignette B)

Read Vignette B in both versions.

**Version 1 (telling):** June says, "I am very stressed about this
assignment. I have a lot of other homework, and I am worried I will not
finish in time." The teacher replies, "I understand that you are
stressed. I will give you two more days because I am a kind teacher."
Every feeling is announced; every motive is labeled. The reader is a
stenographer, not a participant.

**Version 2 (revealing):** June "stood in the doorway holding her
notebook like a shield." She starts a sentence and stops: "The
assignment — " and then, "It's just. My little brother was sick all
week, so I was up. And the bus — " She looks down at her shoes. "I
know it's late. I know."

What the reader learns without being told:

| What is shown | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| Notebook held "like a shield" | June feels under attack; she expects judgment |
| Sentences that trail off ("And the bus — ") | She is embarrassed; she cannot finish the excuse because she knows it sounds like an excuse |
| Looking at her shoes | Shame — she cannot meet the teacher's eyes |
| "I know it's late. I know." | She has already judged herself; she does not need the teacher to do it |
| The teacher caps her pen | The small gesture that means *I'm listening now* — attention, given physically |
| "Bring me the half on Friday" | The extension *is* the answer; no speech about kindness is needed |

Notice the **beats**: the small actions written between lines of
dialogue (holding the notebook, capping the pen, looking at shoes). A
**beat** is a line of action or description that breaks up dialogue. A
beat does two jobs: it shows the reader what the body is doing while
the mouth is talking, and it controls the *pace* of the exchange — a
beat is a breath.

## Worked example 2 — Dialogue in the mentor story

Two exchanges in "The Long Way Home" do three jobs at once.

**Samira talks to the dog (¶7–¶8):**

> "Well," she said to the dog. "You picked the wrong bus stop, Barnaby."
> …
> "Come on," she said. "Long way it is."

Samira is alone. Nobody hears her except a dog. Why does the story
give her lines at all? Because the dialogue *is* the decision. Saying
"Long way it is" out loud is how she commits — she could not have
committed silently and had the reader believe it. And the joke ("You
picked the wrong bus stop") reveals her character: she is the kind of
person who makes a joke when she is scared, and who talks to animals
as if they were people. One line, three jobs: it advances the story
(the choice is made), it reveals character (humor under pressure), and
it carries subtext (she is *not* saying "I'm afraid of the dark and I
don't want to walk forty-five minutes in the rain" — but that is what
she means).

**Mrs. Okafor's revelation (¶13):**

> "Your grandfather used to walk this dog for me," she said. "Every
> Tuesday. He said Barnaby was the only creature in this town with
> worse knees than his own."

This line changes everything the reader thought the story was about.
It is not a line about a dog anymore; it is a line about a dead man who
is still present in this kitchen. And it works because of what Mrs.
Okafor does while she says it: she takes "the dog's face in both
hands" first — the beat tells us she is speaking to the dog as much as
to Samira. Then she laughs, "and then she stopped laughing, because
Samira's face had done something she could not control." The laugh
stopping is the beat that tells the reader this is not a funny story
anymore.

**The rule of the beat:** if two characters talk for more than three
exchanges with no beat, the reader loses track of where they are and
what their bodies are doing. Put a beat at least every two or three
lines — a gesture, a glance, a movement, a silence.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. **Beat hunt.** Find every beat in ¶7–¶13 of the mentor story (the
   dog's tail thumping, Samira clipping the lantern to her strap, Mrs.
   Okafor taking the dog's face in her hands, the laugh that stops).
   For each beat, write what it shows that the dialogue alone does not.
2. **Fix the announcement.** Rewrite this exchange so that neither
   character announces a feeling or a trait — reveal both through
   words, beats, and subtext:
   > "I am so nervous about the audition," Ana said. "I have practiced
   > for weeks, but I am afraid I will forget the words." "Do not be
   > nervous," her friend said. "You are very talented and I believe in
   > you."
   Keep the same information (she is nervous, she practiced, her friend
   supports her) but let the reader *infer* it. 6–8 lines.
3. **Subtext check.** In Vignette B, Version 2, what does the teacher
   mean by "next time, come on Tuesday, when there's still time to fix
   it" — and why is it kinder than saying "I forgive you"? Two
   sentences.

## Independent practice

1. Write a **three-exchange dialogue** between two characters from your
   story seed: one character asks for something, the other refuses —
   but neither character is allowed to say what they actually want.
   (For example: a character asks to borrow something when what they
   really want is an apology.) Use at least two beats.
2. Now write the **subtext line** underneath each spoken line: what the
   character actually means. If the subtext is identical to the spoken
   words, the dialogue is announcing — revise it.
3. Read both versions aloud. The spoken lines should sound like
   something a real person would say; the subtext lines should sound
   like something a real person would *think*.

## Applied task

Take one dialogue passage from your draft-in-progress (or from
Practice D's drills) and apply the **three-line test**: does each line
(1) reveal character, (2) advance the story, or (3) carry subtext? Any
line that does none of the three gets cut or rewritten. Mark the cut
lines in your craft log — cutting is revision, and revision is scored.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) What are dialogue's three jobs? (b) What is
a beat, and what two things does it do? (c) In one sentence, what is
subtext? The adult checks all three answers before the next session.

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading access:** the adult may read Vignette B aloud in both
  versions, taking one voice each; the learner follows along.
- **Alternate response mode:** dialogues may be performed and recorded
  as audio before they are written down; the subtext lines may be
  spoken instead of written.
- **Extension:** take the ¶13 exchange from the mentor story and rewrite
  it so that Mrs. Okafor *withholds* the grandfather information for
  one more exchange — what does Samira have to say or do to earn it?
  What does the delay cost, and what does it gain?

**Teacher note:** the most common error is dialogue that is *too*
polite and complete — every character speaks in full sentences, answers
every question directly, and says exactly what they mean. Real speech
is interrupted, trailing, evasive, and repetitive. When a learner's
dialogue reads like an essay with quotation marks, have them read it
aloud and mark every line that no real fourteen-year-old would ever
say. The second most common error is the **floating heads** problem:
dialogue with no beats at all, so the reader cannot picture where
anyone is. The beat rule (one beat every two or three lines) is a
mechanical fix for a mechanical problem — teach it as a rule, not a
talent.
