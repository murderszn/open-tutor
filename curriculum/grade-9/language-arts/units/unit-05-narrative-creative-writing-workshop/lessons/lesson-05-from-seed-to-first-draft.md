# Lesson 05 — From Seed to First Draft

**Goal (student-readable):** I can turn my story plan into a complete
first draft — opening through climax to a reflective conclusion — and
keep the draft moving even when it is imperfect.
**Time:** ~50 minutes (lesson) + drafting sessions 13–14 · **Materials:**
this page, notebook, story plan (Lessons 1–4), [craft
log](../project.md) · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md §
L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Look at your story plan: the problem,
the want and need, the six-event chain, the POV memo, the dialogue
drills, the pacing notes. Circle the one part you are most sure about
and the one part you are least sure about. Tell the adult both. (The
part you are least sure about is where the drafting will teach you the
most — a first draft is how a writer finds out what the story actually
is.)

## A first draft is a discovery, not a performance

By now you have planned a great deal: a problem, a want and a need, an
event chain, a point of view, dialogue, pacing. It is tempting to
believe the draft should now write itself, cleanly, in order. It will
not. A first draft is where the plan meets the actual sentences, and
the sentences always talk back: a character refuses to say the line you
wrote for them, a scene runs long, a second plot line appears that you
did not plan. That is not failure. That is drafting.

The rules for the first draft:

1. **Draft in order, but do not get stuck in order.** If the opening
   will not come, write the climax first and come back. (The opening
   is often the last thing a writer truly finishes.)
2. **Write past the ugly parts.** A sentence you hate is still a
   sentence. Put a mark in the margin — `[FIX]` — and keep going.
   Revision exists; the blank page does not accept revisions.
3. **Hit the load-bearing moments.** The draft must contain, in some
   form: the problem, the want/need tension, the inciting incident,
   the rising action, the climax (want vs. need collide), and a
   reflective conclusion. Everything else can be thin for now.
4. **Aim for 1,200–2,000 words.** That is roughly 4–7 handwritten pages
   or 3–5 typed pages. Shorter than 1,200 words and the story cannot
   hold a real want/need arc; longer than 2,000 and it is a different
   assignment.
5. **Use the techniques, but do not perform them.** Dialogue, pacing,
   sensory language, reflection, multiple plot lines — these are tools,
   not decorations. If a technique does not serve the story, leave it
   out. A quiet story told well beats a loud story showing off.

## Worked example 1 — Opening, middle, and end from one seed

Take Seed 3, "The Recipe Box" (Ana, the missing birthday-cake recipe,
Rosa). Here is how the seed becomes a draft skeleton — the same
skeleton you will build for your own story:

**Opening (the problem, W.9-10.3.a):** Ana finds the recipe box in the
new house; every card is there except the birthday cake; in its place,
a note: "Ask Rosa." One paragraph of scene (the box, the smell of
cinnamon and pencil shavings) and one paragraph of summary (who
grandmother was, what the cake meant). The opening's job: orient the
reader, set out the problem, and establish the narrator.

**Middle (the chain, W.9-10.3.b–c):** Ana asks her mother about Rosa
and is told not to bother her — the first complication. She goes anyway
— the second. Rosa answers the door and does not invite her in — the
third. Ana tells Rosa about the box, and Rosa's face changes — the
fourth. Each event causes the next: the refusal creates the visit, the
visit creates the closed door, the closed door creates the moment Ana
has to say the thing out loud. **Multiple plot lines:** the recipe
quest (the outer line) and the family argument nobody will explain
(the inner line) run side by side; the reader learns the argument's
shape from what people *avoid* saying about Rosa.

**Climax (want vs. need collide):** Rosa finally says what the argument
was about — and it was about the cake itself: grandmother had promised
the recipe to Rosa, and the family had taken it back. Ana must choose:
take the recipe and leave (her want — the cake exactly as it was), or
stay and listen to why it mattered to Rosa (her need — to understand
that the cake was never really about the recipe).

**Reflective conclusion (W.9-10.3.e):** Ana bakes the cake with Rosa,
not from the card but from Rosa's memory, and it comes out wrong in
exactly the way her grandmother's used to. The conclusion looks back
and names what changed: "The cake was never the recipe. It was the
person standing next to you while you made it."

Notice what the skeleton does *not* do: it does not write the sentences
for you. It tells you which moments must be scenes (the door, the
revelation, the baking) and which can be summaries (the move, the
history of the cake) — and it leaves the dialogue to be discovered in
the drafting.

## Worked example 2 — Multiple plot lines without losing the reader

A **multiple plot line** (or subplot) is a second thread that runs
beside the main story and meets it at the end. The mentor story has
one, quietly: the *outer* line is the lost dog (find Barnaby, get him
home); the *inner* line is the grandfather (remember him, grieve him).
They meet in the kitchen, when Mrs. Okafor says, "Your grandfather used
to walk this dog for me" (¶13). The outer line *delivers* the inner
line's ending.

Rules for a second line:

- **It must meet the main line.** If the two lines never touch, the
  second one is a different story stapled to the first. Cut it.
- **It must be shorter than the main line.** A subplot that takes over
  is a new main plot. Give it two or three scenes, no more.
- **It should answer the main line's question from a different angle.**
  The dog answers "what does Samira need?" from the outside (she needs
  to care for something) while the grandfather answers it from the
  inside (she needs to remember someone).

**Reflection** is the narrator's (or character's) thinking *inside* the
story — the pause where the story stops to consider what just happened.
¶15 of the mentor story is pure reflection: "Samira understood something
she had not understood at the bus stop." Reflection is not a lecture;
it is the character catching up with their own experience. One or two
reflection passages per story is enough. More than that, and the story
is explaining itself instead of happening.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. **Skeleton check.** Lay out your story's skeleton in four labeled
   rows — Opening, Middle (chain of 4–6 events), Climax, Reflective
   conclusion — and mark each row *scene* or *summary*. Which rows are
   scenes? If your climax is a summary, fix it now, before you draft.
2. **Second line test.** Name your story's second plot line (if it has
   one) in one sentence, and name the exact scene where it meets the
   main line. If you cannot name the meeting scene, the second line is
   not a subplot yet — it is a stray idea. Either give it a meeting
   scene or cut it.
3. **The ugly sentence.** Write the worst possible first sentence of
   your story — deliberately bad, clumsy, obvious. Then write a second
   sentence that continues from it as if the first one were fine.
   (This is a real drafting skill: the ability to keep going past a bad
   sentence is worth more than the ability to write a good first one.)

## Independent practice (drafting sessions 13–14)

1. **Drafting day 1 (session 13):** write the opening through the
   inciting incident and the first half of the rising action
   (600–1,000 words). Stop at a moment of tension, not at a stopping
   point — write the next sentence's first three words in the margin
   so tomorrow you can start mid-thought.
2. **Drafting day 2 (session 14):** finish the draft — rising action
   through climax to reflective conclusion (total 1,200–2,000 words).
   Use `[FIX]` marks freely; do not stop to revise. When the draft is
   done, write the word count at the top and one sentence in your craft
   log: "The part of the draft that surprised me was ___."
3. **Self-check before the quiz:** does your draft contain the
   load-bearing moments (problem, want/need, inciting incident, rising
   action, climax, reflective conclusion)? Check each one off in your
   craft log. Any missing moment is a drafting task for session 14, not
   a revision task for next week.

## Applied task

Read your draft's opening paragraph aloud. Ask the adult: "After this
paragraph, do you know what the character wants, what stands in the
way, and what I want you to wonder about?" If the answer to any part
is no, revise the opening — not by adding explanation, but by making
the problem more specific (see Lesson 1).

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) What are the three rules that matter most
for a first draft? (b) What is a subplot, and what are the two rules
that keep it from taking over? The adult checks both answers before
the quiz.

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the worked examples aloud;
  the learner follows the skeleton rows.
- **Alternate response mode:** the draft may be dictated or recorded
  as audio first, then transcribed; the skeleton may be built with
  index cards, one event per card, laid out on a table.
- **Extension:** after the first draft, write a 150-word "director's
  commentary" — three decisions you made while drafting and why.
  (This becomes the seed of your craft log's revision section.)

**Teacher note:** the most common drafting failure is the **stalled
opening**: the learner rewrites the first paragraph five times and
never reaches the story. The antidote is mechanical: set a timer for
20 minutes, forbid editing, and require forward motion — the `[FIX]`
mark gives them permission to be imperfect in public. The second most
common failure is the **missing middle**: a strong opening and a strong
ending with nothing between them. If a draft jumps from the problem
straight to the climax, send the learner back to the chain (Lesson 1):
which events were skipped, and which one, if added, would make the
climax earned rather than announced? Do not accept a draft under 1,200
words without a conference about what is missing — but do not punish a
draft that is long and messy. Messy is draftable; short and perfect is
a summary wearing a story's clothes.
