# Lesson 02 — Point of View and Narrator: Choosing the Teller

**Goal (student-readable):** I can choose a point of view and narrator
for my story, keep them consistent, and explain what the choice gains
and what it loses.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, ["The Long
Way Home"](../texts/the-long-way-home.md), [craft
vignettes](../texts/craft-vignettes.md) Vignette D ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Tell the adult about something that
happened to you yesterday — first as if you are telling a friend
("I…"), then as if a camera is describing you from across the room
("She…"). What changed — the events, or something else? (Answer: the
events are identical; what changed is *how close the reader sits to
your thoughts*. That closeness is point of view.)

## The teller is a choice, not a default

Every story is told by *someone*. That someone is the **narrator**, and
the **point of view (POV)** is the position the narrator tells from.
U02 taught you to *analyze* POV in other people's stories; now you
have to *choose* one for your own. The three working options:

- **First person ("I"):** the narrator is a character in the story.
  The reader sits inside one head and knows only what that character
  knows, feels, and notices. Gains: immediacy, confession, a voice
  with a personality. Costs: the reader can never see anything the
  narrator cannot see — no other character's thoughts, no scene the
  narrator was not in.
- **Third-person limited ("she/he/they," staying close to one
  character):** the narrator is outside the story but stays near one
  character's thoughts and feelings. Gains: a little room for the
  reader to judge the character, plus the freedom to describe things
  the character would not describe about themselves. Costs: less
  raw immediacy than first person.
- **Third-person omniscient ("she/he/they," moving between minds):**
  the narrator can report what *any* character thinks. Gains: the
  widest view. Costs: the reader is kept at a distance from everyone,
  and the story can feel like a report instead of an experience.
  (Use it only if your story genuinely needs more than one mind.)

"The Long Way Home" is told in **third-person limited, staying close
to Samira**. Watch how the narration stays inside her head without
ever saying "I":

> "She could feel the two halves of the decision pulling like a rope in
> her hands." (¶6)

That sentence reports a *feeling* only Samira could have — and it is
written in third person. The narrator knows what Samira feels, but
never tells us what Mrs. Okafor is thinking when she opens the door
(¶10–¶11). We learn about Mrs. Okafor the way Samira does: from what
she says and does.

## Worked example 1 — The same moment in two points of view

Read Vignette D ("Ada finds her key") in both versions.

**First person:** "I was on my knees in the wet dirt… I sat back on my
heels and laughed, one short laugh, because I had spent forty minutes
being angry at everyone in the world and the key had been under a leaf
the whole time."

**Third-person limited:** "Ada was on her knees in the wet dirt… She
sat back on her heels and laughed, one short laugh. Forty minutes of
anger at everyone in the world, and the key had been under a leaf the
whole time."

The events are identical. What changes:

| | First person | Third-person limited |
|---|---|---|
| **Closeness** | Inside Ada's head; the laugh is *hers* | One step back; the laugh is *observed* |
| **What the reader can judge** | Only what Ada judges about herself | Ada *and* the gap between Ada and the reader's judgment |
| **Voice** | "because I had spent forty minutes being angry" — a confession | "Forty minutes of anger…" — a summary the narrator offers |
| **Best for** | A story about shame, guilt, or a secret | A story where the reader should see the character a little more clearly than the character sees themselves |

Neither is better in general. The question is always: *what does this
story need the reader to feel close to?*

## Worked example 2 — When the POV choice breaks a story

Imagine "The Long Way Home" told in **first person by Mrs. Okafor**.
Mrs. Okafor was not at the bus stop, did not see the lantern, did not
stand at the gate. She could only report what Samira told her in the
kitchen — and the story's central scene, the choice at the gate, would
become a secondhand summary. The story needs a narrator who was *at the
gate*. That is why Samira's POV — first person or third-person limited
— is the only honest choice for this story, and why Mrs. Okafor's POV
would break it.

The rule: **the narrator must be present for the story's most important
moments.** If your climax happens somewhere your narrator cannot see,
you have the wrong narrator.

## The POV decision memo

Before you draft, you will write a short **decision memo** (Practice
C): the POV you chose, the narrator, and — in two paragraphs — what
the choice *gains* and what it *loses*. A memo that only lists gains is
not a decision; every POV costs something, and naming the cost is how
you prove you chose on purpose.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. **POV audit of the mentor story.** Find three sentences in "The
   Long Way Home" that prove the narration stays close to Samira and
   never enters another character's head. (Hint: ¶6, ¶8, ¶15 are good
   hunting grounds.) For each sentence, write what it tells us that
   only Samira could know or feel.
2. **Rewrite the gate scene.** Rewrite ¶6–¶8 of the mentor story in
   **first person as Samira** (4–6 sentences, same events, only the
   teller changes). Then write one sentence: what did first person
   gain, and what did it lose, compared with the original?
3. **Spot the break.** A classmate drafts their story in third-person
   omniscient but writes the climax as a scene inside one character's
   head, using thoughts no other character could know. Explain in 2–3
   sentences what is wrong — and name the two fixes (change the POV,
   or change the scene).

## Independent practice

1. Take the story seed you chose in Lesson 1. Write the opening
   paragraph of your story **twice**: once in first person, once in
   third-person limited (4–6 sentences each, same events).
2. Read both aloud. Circle the version that sounds more like *your*
   story — the one where the voice fits the character.
3. Draft your POV decision memo (Practice C will ask for the finished
   version): POV chosen, narrator named, one paragraph on what it
   gains, one paragraph on what it loses.

## Applied task

Answer in your notebook: **"Whose story is this?"** — not the
character's name, but *whose head the reader will live in*. Then
answer the follow-up: **"What is the one scene my narrator must be
present for?"** If your narrator cannot be present for your climax,
change the narrator now, before you draft.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) Name the three POV options and one gain and
one cost of each. (b) What is the rule about the narrator and the
climax? The adult checks both answers before the next session.

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading access:** the adult may read Vignette D aloud in both
  versions while the learner follows; the *comparison* is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** the two opening paragraphs may be
  dictated or recorded as audio; the memo may be a labeled chart
  (Gains / Losses) instead of paragraphs.
- **Extension:** rewrite ¶13 of the mentor story (Mrs. Okafor's
  revelation about the grandfather) from **Mrs. Okafor's first-person
  POV**. What does she know that Samira does not — and what does the
  scene lose when Samira's surprise disappears?

**Teacher note:** the common misconception is that first person is
"easier" or "more personal" and therefore always better. Push back:
first person is *harder* in one specific way — the narrator cannot know
anything they did not witness, so the writer must smuggle every fact
through the character's own eyes. Learners who choose first person
because it "feels natural" often write a narrator who conveniently
knows things they could not know. The memo exists to catch that. Also
watch for POV drift in drafts: a third-person-limited draft that
suddenly reports another character's private thoughts has broken its
own rule — flag it in the revision pass, not as a moral failure but as
a craft error with a fix.
