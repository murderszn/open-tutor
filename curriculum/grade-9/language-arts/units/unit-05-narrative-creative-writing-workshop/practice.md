## Practice A — Session 2: story-seed sketches

**Goal:** I can grow a story seed into a sketch: problem, want, need,
and a first event chain.
**Success:** two sketched seeds, each with a specific problem, a want,
a need that pulls the other way, and a four-event chain.

1. Choose **two** of the three story seeds in
   [texts/story-seeds.md](texts/story-seeds.md) (or one seed + your own
   idea). For each seed, write:
   - **The problem** in one specific sentence.
   - **The want** ("My character wants…") and **the need** ("My
     character needs…") as two sentences.
   - **A four-event chain** in which each event causes the next. Number
     the events 1–4 and write "which causes…" between them.
2. For each seed, write one sentence answering: **why do the want and
   the need pull in opposite directions?** If they do not pull apart,
   revise the need until they do — a seed with no pull is not a story
   yet.
3. **Choose your story.** Circle the seed you will write for the
   project. Write two sentences: why this seed, and what is the one
   thing you are most unsure about in it? (That uncertainty is your
   drafting question — see Lesson 5.)

## Practice B — Session 3: want/need charts and the cause-effect chain

**Goal:** I can chart a character's want against their need and prove
an event chain with the removal test.
**Success:** two completed charts; one complete chain of the mentor
story with a defended verdict on the climax.

Copy this chart twice — once for **Samira** ("The Long Way Home"),
once for **the character of your chosen seed**:

| Want (what they think will fix it) | Need (what will actually fix it) | Evidence (quote or line) | What blocks the need |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |
| | | | |

1. For Samira, chart **at least two** want/need pairs (the shortcut
   vs. the long way is one; find another — for example, what she wants
   from the evening vs. what the evening actually gives her). Quote the
   story for each pair.
2. For your seed's character, chart **at least one** pair, using your
   Practice A sketch. (If your chart has empty cells, your sketch needs
   more work — go back to it before you draft.)
3. **The chain.** Write out the mentor story's event chain from Lesson
   1 (bus leaves → lantern → dog → tag → choice → kitchen) and apply
   the **removal test** to three links: remove the lantern, remove the
   tag, remove the choice at the gate. For each removal, write what
   breaks — one sentence each.
4. **Explain your reasoning:** is the story's climax the choice at the
   gate (¶6–¶8) or the revelation in the kitchen (¶13)? Defend your
   answer in a paragraph: name where want and need finally collide, and
   say what the other moment does instead. (There is a well-defended
   answer either way; there is no undefended one.)

## Practice C — Session 6: POV rewrites and the decision memo

**Goal:** I can rewrite a scene in a different point of view and defend
a POV choice in a decision memo.
**Success:** two rewrites of the same scene (6–8 sentences each) plus
an approved memo naming gains and losses.

1. Take the kitchen scene from "The Long Way Home" (¶10–¶13: the door
   opens, Mrs. Okafor speaks, the grandfather is revealed). Rewrite it
   in **first person as Samira** (6–8 sentences). Keep every event the
   same — only the teller changes.
2. Now rewrite the same scene in **third-person omniscient** — a
   narrator who can report what *both* Samira and Mrs. Okafor are
   thinking. Mark the two places where your narrator knows something
   neither character could know.
3. **Decision memo.** For your own story, write the memo:
   - **POV chosen and narrator named** (one sentence).
   - **What it gains** (one paragraph: closeness, voice, judgment —
     name the specific gain for *your* story, not POV in general).
   - **What it loses** (one paragraph: what your narrator cannot see,
     and how you will work around it — for example, by putting the
     information in dialogue instead).
   The adult approves the memo before you draft. A memo with no losses
   is not approved — every POV costs something.
4. One paragraph: the mentor story is third-person limited, staying
   close to Samira. What would be *gained* and what would be *lost* if
   it were told by Mrs. Okafor in first person instead? Name one
   specific scene whose meaning would change.

## Practice D — Session 7: dialogue drills

**Goal:** I can write dialogue that reveals character through words,
beats, and subtext.
**Success:** three revised exchanges, each with at least two beats and
a subtext line for every spoken line.

1. **The announcement fix.** Rewrite this exchange so neither character
   announces a feeling or a trait. Keep the same information, but let
   the reader infer it (6–8 lines, at least two beats):
   > "I am so nervous about the audition," Ana said. "I have practiced
   > for weeks, but I am afraid I will forget the words." "Do not be
   > nervous," her friend said. "You are very talented and I believe
   > in you."
2. **The refusal.** Write a three-exchange dialogue between two
   characters from your story: one asks for something, the other
   refuses — but neither character is allowed to say what they actually
   want. (For example: a character asks to borrow something when what
   they really want is an apology.) Use at least two beats. Under each
   spoken line, write the **subtext line**: what the character actually
   means. If the subtext is identical to the spoken words, revise.
3. **The mentor-story beat map.** Find three beats in ¶7–¶13 of "The
   Long Way Home" (the dog's tail, the lantern clipped to the strap,
   the laugh that stops). For each beat, write what it shows that the
   dialogue alone does not.
4. **Error analysis:** a classmate's dialogue has four exchanges with
   no beats at all — two heads talking in an empty room. Explain in 2–3
   sentences what the reader loses, and name the mechanical fix (the
   beat rule from Lesson 3).

## Practice E — Session 11: pacing rewrites and sensory revision

**Goal:** I can choose scene or summary for a purpose and revise flat
description into sensory language.
**Success:** one scene expanded, one passage summarized, one sensory
revision with a change log.

1. **Expand.** Take Vignette A, Version 1 (the summary of Dev and the
   camera) and expand *one sentence* of it into a scene of 4–6
   sentences — pick the sentence where the forgiveness happens. Then
   write one sentence: what did the scene gain that the summary could
   not give?
2. **Compress.** Take Vignette A, Version 2 (the scene) and compress
   it into a two-sentence summary. Then write one sentence: what did
   the summary lose, and was the loss worth the speed?
3. **Sensory revision.** Rewrite this flat paragraph with one detail
   per sense (sound, sight, smell, touch — pick three) and no judgment
   adjectives at all:
   > The hallway was creepy. The old house was quiet and scary. Maria
   > did not want to go inside.
   Keep a **change log**: list every judgment adjective you removed and
   the sensory detail that replaced it.
4. **Gear check.** Label each paragraph ¶1–¶16 of the mentor story as
   *scene* or *summary*. For two of your labels, write one sentence
   defending the choice: why does *this* paragraph need to be a scene
   (or a summary)?

## Practice F — Session 12: multiple plot lines and reflection

**Goal:** I can weave a second plot line into a story and write a
reflection passage that earns its place.
**Success:** a two-line weave plan with a named meeting scene; one
reflection paragraph that looks back without announcing a lesson.

1. **Weave plan.** Draw two parallel lines for your story: the **main
   line** (the outer problem — the dog, the letter, the recipe, the
   game) and the **second line** (the inner question — grief, apology,
   friendship, belonging). Place 3–4 events on each line. Mark the
   **meeting scene**: the exact scene where the two lines touch. Write
   one sentence: what does the second line answer that the main line
   cannot answer alone?
2. **The meeting test.** If your two lines never touch, write the
   meeting scene now (4–6 sentences) — or cut the second line and say
   why in one sentence. (A cut second line is a revision, not a
   failure.)
3. **Reflection draft.** Write one reflection paragraph (4–6 sentences)
   for your story's ending: your character looks back at what happened
   and names what changed — **without** announcing a lesson ("and so I
   learned…"). Use the mentor story's ¶15 as your model: it names the
   want and the need, it uses a concrete image (the walk, the rain,
   the kitchen), and it never says the word "lesson."
4. **Error analysis:** a classmate's reflection paragraph reads: "And
   so Marcus learned that curiosity is not kindness. He would never
   forget that day." Explain in 2–3 sentences what is wrong — and
   rewrite the paragraph in 3–4 sentences using a concrete image
   instead of an announced lesson.

## Practice G — Session 16: revision pass 1 — structure

**Goal:** I can revise a draft's structure: cut what does not earn its
place, keep the load-bearing moments, move what sits in the wrong
place.
**Success:** a cut/keep/move list covering the whole draft; at least
two cuts and one move (or a defended reason why no move is needed).

1. Read your first draft once, start to finish, **without a pen**.
2. Read it a second time with three marks:
   - **CUT** — any scene, paragraph, or line that does not advance the
     story, reveal character, or carry subtext.
   - **KEEP** — the load-bearing moments: problem, inciting incident,
     climax, reflective conclusion.
   - **MOVE** — any scene that works but sits in the wrong place.
3. Write the **cut/keep/move list** in your craft log: every CUT with
   one sentence saying why it goes; every MOVE with its old position
   and its new position. **At least two cuts.** (If you cannot find two
   things to cut, you are not looking hard enough — ask the adult to
   find one for you.)
4. Apply the cuts and moves to produce **draft 2**. Write one sentence
   in your craft log: "The structural change that matters most is ___,
   because ___."
5. **Removal test, again:** pick your draft's three longest scenes.
   For each, write what breaks if it is removed. Any scene whose
   removal breaks nothing goes on the CUT list.

## Practice H — Session 17: revision pass 2 — language; pass 3 — conventions

**Goal:** I can revise a draft's language (word choice, dialogue,
sensory detail, sentence length, conciseness) and then clean its
conventions (spelling, punctuation, dialogue punctuation, MLA
manuscript format).
**Success:** a language change log (at least 8 changes) and a clean
conventions pass with a checklist.

**Pass 2 — Language.** Read draft 2 sentence by sentence. For every
paragraph, ask the four questions from Lesson 6 (telling detail?
three-line test for dialogue? sentence length matches the moment? POV
consistent?). Make the changes and keep a **change log** — at least
**8 changes**, each in before → after pairs:

| Before | After | Why |
|---|---|---|
| | | |

Use the UNC Writing Center's "Writing Concisely" handout (Resource
Pack) for the mechanical moves: cut redundant pairs, delete unnecessary
qualifiers, replace a phrase with a word. At least **two** of your eight
changes must be conciseness cuts.

**Pass 3 — Conventions.** Apply the U04 tools to draft 3:

- [ ] Every line of dialogue starts a new paragraph for a new speaker.
- [ ] Commas and periods sit inside closing quotation marks.
- [ ] At least two semicolons used correctly (or a note saying why none
      fits this draft).
- [ ] At least three varied phrase/clause types from U04 (participial,
      prepositional, absolute phrases; noun, relative, adverbial
      clauses) — underline them.
- [ ] Spelling checked; paragraphing checked; MLA manuscript format
      (double-spaced, 12-point readable font, name/page header, title
      centered).
- [ ] Word count written at the top (1,200–2,000 words).

Write the finished word count and one sentence in your craft log: "The
language change I am proudest of is ___, because ___."

## Practice I — Session 18: reflective conclusion and reading rehearsal

**Goal:** I can write a reflective conclusion that passes the four-part
audit and rehearse an author reading with deliberate delivery choices.
**Success:** a conclusion that passes all four audit items; a rehearsed
excerpt (300–400 words) with marked pauses and one speed change.

1. **Conclusion audit.** Read your draft's last paragraph. Score each
   item yes/no:
   - (a) It answers the story's own question.
   - (b) It names the want and the need (in the character's
     understanding, not the narrator's lecture).
   - (c) It looks forward as well as back.
   - (d) It does **not** announce a lesson.
   Any "no" is a revision task — rewrite the paragraph until all four
   are yes. (Use the mentor story's ¶15–¶16 as your model.)
2. **The bad-conclusion fix.** Rewrite this failed conclusion into a
   working one of 3–4 sentences, using a concrete image instead of an
   announced lesson:
   > "And so Samira learned that doing the right thing is always worth
   > it, even when it is hard. She would never forget that night, and
   > she knew her grandfather would be proud of her. The end."
3. **Reading rehearsal.** Choose your 300–400 word excerpt (usually the
   climax or the reflective conclusion). Read it aloud once, slowly.
   Mark **two places to pause** and **one place to speed up**. Read it
   again with the marks. Time both readings. Write the timing and one
   sentence: "The delivery choice I am most unsure about is ___."
4. **One honest note.** Trade drafts with the adult. The reader writes
   **one** margin note, beginning "I got lost here…" or "I wanted
   more here…" — nothing else. Revise exactly that spot. Record the
   note and the revision in your craft log.

**Reading log (all four weeks):** continue the daily 20–30 minute
independent reading log begun in U01. Each week the adult checks:
pages read, one observation and one inference about the independent
book, and — new in U05 — one **craft move in the wild** (a want/need
tension, a beat, a scene/summary choice, a telling detail), with the
page number where you found it.
