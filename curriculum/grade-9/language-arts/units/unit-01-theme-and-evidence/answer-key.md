# Answer Key — Unit 01: Theme and Evidence

*For the guiding adult only. Never place beside a student question. Every
item below was solved independently of the lessons; where multiple readings
are defensible, the key says so and gives the acceptance rule.*

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice (sample; accept any honest, well-located set):**

1. Three supported inferences, e.g.:
   - *Maya feels guilty about the rolls* ← "Maya stared at that one a long
     time" (par. 11) + "nobody had taught her the rolls" (par. 11).
   - *The grandmother's move was recent and the family is still
     adjusting* ← "the counters were bare," "black bags by the door"
     (par. 1–2) + "moved to the assisted-living apartment in March"
     (par. 11).
   - *The mother is grieving, not just organizing* ← "said 'Oh,' in a
     voice like a door closing" (par. 13) + "cried a little into her bowl
     and pretended it was the pepper" (par. 20).
2. A commonly revised first inference: that the grandmother has *died*.
   The text never says so — she moved to assisted living (par. 11). Accept
   and praise the correction; this is the skill working.
3. Good `?` questions point at lines, e.g.: "What does 'the kitchen was
   winning' mean in par. 2?" / "Why did the grandmother keep recipes in a
   *salt box* specifically?"

**Independent practice:** accept any six observations / four inferences
correctly sorted with named support. The anti-inference (arguing against
one's own inference, e.g. "maybe the boxes are for storage, not moving")
counts if it cites the same observations.

**Exit check:** must contain a quotation or a precise location reference;
"she seems sad" with no words cited is not ready — return it.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice (sample ranking):** for the claim "the salt box mattered
long before Maya opened it": 1) "She kept everything in the salt box"
(par. 13, mother's testimony — direct, from a witness); 2) "I used to steal
the cookie card out of it when I was your age" (par. 13 — proves
long-standing family use); 3) the worn label "in letters you could only
read if you already knew what it said" (par. 4 — indirect but physical);
4) "collecting the dust nobody wiped" (par. 4 — weakest for *this* claim;
it shows neglect, which cuts against "mattered" — a good learner notices
the tension).

**Independent practice 1 (sample):** mother's grief — "in a voice like a
door closing" (par. 13); "She told me that too... She brought it up more
than once" (par. 15); "cried a little into her bowl and pretended it was
the pepper" (par. 20). Three parts of the story: beginning/middle/end.
**Independent practice 2:** accept any quotation that *seems* damning but
is insufficient, e.g. "Her brother had taken the garage and disappeared
into it with his phone" (par. 2) — one scene of avoidance does not prove a
character trait; the soup scene (par. 20: "her brother had thirds") shows
belonging. The careful reader's verdict: *insufficient evidence for a
general claim; sufficient only for "he avoided the sorting that day."*

**Exit check:** the "why stronger" sentence must compare — e.g. "B is
stronger than D because B shows Maya *acting* on the guilt while D only
shows she admired the handwriting."

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice:** accept any theme that (a) is a full sentence, (b) is
arguable, (c) survives the break-it test. Sample final: "A home is not a
place but the practices people choose to carry forward — memory survives
only when someone re-enacts it." Reject morals ("you should appreciate
your grandparents") — redirect: *what does the text show*, not *what
should the reader do*.

**Independent practice 1:** central idea (sample): "Human memory
reconstructs the past instead of recording it; the reconstruction is
unreliable in detail but preserves meaning and helps future decisions."
Evidence: the Loftus experiment (par. 2) — strongest: controlled,
falsifiable, specific numbers/words; the worn-path analogy (par. 3);
the adaptive-purpose argument (par. 4). Accept "strongest" = par. 2 or
par. 4 with a defended reason; reject par. 3 as strongest (analogies
illustrate; they don't prove).
**Independent practice 2:** summary sample (58 words): "The passage argues
that memory works by reconstruction, not playback. It cites Elizabeth
Loftus's experiments, in which leading questions altered witnesses'
memories, to show how easily memories change. It concludes that this
editing, while unreliable in detail, preserves meaning and helps us make
future decisions."
**Application:** accept any paragraph that names both an agreement (both
texts treat memory as active reconstruction — Maya *re-makes* the rolls
ritual) and a tension (the passage warns memory is unreliable; the story
treats the reconstructed ritual as redemptive).

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice (sample chart rows):**

| Excerpt | Key words/details | What it adds or changes | Revised theme |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 — Ch. 16 | "I am malicious because I am miserable" — causal, blunt, first-person | Gives ambition's damage a *victim's* causal sentence; the theme is now about what makers owe what they make | v4: *Unregulated ambition harms the ambitious — and gives its victims both the injury and the clearest account of it.* |
| 5 — Ch. 24 | "avoid ambition... Yet why do I say this? I have myself been blasted in these hopes, yet another may succeed" | The warning doubts itself; ambition is condemned and half-excused in one breath | v5: *The novel condemns unchecked ambition while admitting its pull is never fully renounced — even the dying Victor cannot quite disown it.* |

For the excerpt-5 debate: **either** verdict is defensible. "Weakens" is
defensible if argued from "yet another may succeed" as authorial
hedging; "completes" is defensible if argued that the crack *is* the
point — the theme includes ambition's seductiveness. Grade the *defense*,
not the verdict.

**Independent practice 1:** the paragraph must show revision logic across
at least three excerpts with brief, located quotations. **Practice 2
(counter-reading):** for responsibility — "I ought to render him happy
before I complained of his wickedness" is the novel's own language (the
chain's context note, Ch. 10); Victor's flight from the apartment (Ch. 5)
is abandonment. Against — Walton's ambition harms no creature; the
creature *chooses* revenge ("I am malicious"), which is his responsibility.
Full marks for a fair fight on both sides.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice 1 (sample):** Ms. Okafor — wants: talented students to
take risks ("ambitious for a sophomore... try it anyway," par. 1);
fairness (the callback rule, par. 19). Gap: none significant — she is
*pointedly simple*, a fixed standard against which Marcus's complications
play. Mr. Abernathy — wants: the schedule covered with minimum fuss
("The schedule's the schedule," par. 6); actions: lets Darnell take the
shift without interrogation (par. 23) — reveals pragmatic kindness under
gruffness.
**Guided practice 2:** accept either turning point with a defended
quotation: the quiet door close (par. 13 — the decision) or Darnell
walking in at 4:15 (par. 21 — the decision made visible). Full marks
require the *argument*, not the choice.

**Independent practice 2 (error analysis):** the "irresponsible" reading
fails because: (a) Marcus *was* at work at 3:40 (par. 21) — he did not
skip; (b) he arranged coverage in advance (par. 7–8); (c) the action/word
gap test: his words to Darnell are transactional ("Cover Thursday and
I'll cover your Saturday," par. 8) but his actions show he carried the
conflict for days without burdening his mother (par. 2). Irresponsibility
would be no-showing; Marcus did the opposite.

**Application (Darnell POV):** check consistency — must include the cereal,
the door, the showing up; must not invent facts contradicting the story
(e.g., Darnell cannot "hate" Marcus — par. 28 refutes it). Grade on
motivational consistency, not prose polish.

## Lesson 06

No single correct essay. Grade with the revision checklist from the
lesson: every quotation introduced + cited + commented; theme arguable;
development shown across the text; objection named and answered. The
skeptic's paragraph is graded on *fairness* — does it steelman the
opposition? — not on whether the essay survives it.

## Week-05 quiz

1. **(2)** Sample — observation: "Yellowed cards lie fanned on the floor
   around the girl." Inference: "The family is moving out" (bare counters,
   moving boxes). 1 pt per correctly labeled item.
2. **(3)** Strongest: "Her brother had taken the garage and disappeared
   into it with his phone." (par. 2). It beats the fragment "Her brother
   had taken the garage" because the fragment states only location while
   the full sentence adds deliberate avoidance — *disappeared* and *with
   his phone* show intent, not just position. 1 pt quote, 1 pt location,
   1 pt explanation.
3. **(2)** "and her brother had thirds" (par. 20) shows participation and
   belonging — the opposite of avoidance. It supports instead the claim
   "the brother is part of the family's rituals" (or "...belongs despite
   skipping the work"). 1 pt per sentence.
4. **(3)** Strong = relevant, direct, sufficient for the claim; thorough =
   multiple pieces spread across the text. A single perfect quotation
   lacks *thoroughness*. 2 pts definitions, 1 pt naming the missing one.
5. **(2)** "Memory" = topic; the sentence = theme. A theme is a complete-
   sentence claim the whole text must support; a topic only names the
   subject. 1 pt identification, 1 pt explanation.
6. **(4)** Sample theme: "Showing up is a choice you make before you feel
   like it — care is proven by action, not declared in words."
   Complicating detail: Marcus telling Darnell about the callback *first*
   (par. 27) — his brother's regard may matter more than the audition
   itself, which complicates a "follow your dreams" draft; or Darnell
   taking Saturdays back "regular" (par. 28) — the theme must cover both
   brothers. 2 pts theme, 2 pts complication with detail.
7. **(3)** Sample (62 words): "Sophomore Marcus wins a Thursday audition
   for the school play but is scheduled to work at the hardware store. His
   older brother Darnell, who quit the store months ago, unexpectedly
   covers the shift so Marcus can audition. Marcus earns a callback and
   tells Darnell first. Darnell agrees to take his Saturday shifts back
   regularly." Deduct 1 per smuggled opinion or missing beat (audition,
   cover, callback, Saturdays).
8. **(3)** Three problems (any three): "irresponsible" is an inaccurate
   opinion — Marcus was at work and arranged coverage (par. 21, 7–8);
   "lazy" is a smuggled opinion about Darnell; "sweet" is a smuggled
   opinion; it omits the audition/callback/Saturdays beats. 1 pt each.
9. **(4)** Sample: (a) the audition — "had been saying it in the shower
   for three weeks" (par. 1); (b) protecting his mother from the conflict
   — "He had not told his mother about the schedule conflict" (par. 2).
   1 pt per motivation, 1 pt per quote+location.
10. **(4)** Words: "I got plans Thursday" (par. 9); "You think you're
    better than me now? Theater kid?" (par. 11). Actions: walks in at 4:15
    (par. 21); takes Saturdays back "regular" (par. 28). The gap: the
    words perform indifference; the actions show he was listening all
    along — care disguised as contempt until the cost of the disguise
    (losing Marcus) got too high. 1 pt dialogue+location, 1 pt action+
    location, 2 pts analysis.

**Reteach triggers:** Q1–Q4 wrong → reteach L1/L2 (evidence test). Q5–Q8
wrong → reteach L3 (theme vs. topic, summary rules). Q9–Q10 wrong →
reteach L5 (motivation mapping, action/word gap).

## Unit assessment

1. **(4)** Habit: "First it was because I didn't know what else to do at
   4:40" (par. 8) — the visits began as unexamined routine filling an
   empty hour. Deliberate remembrance: "Mostly I just sit. The sitting is
   the point, I think" (par. 11) — he now understands and chooses the
   ritual's meaning. 1 pt per quote+location, 1 pt per explanation.
   Accept "the bench felt like the one place I wasn't performing being
   fine" (par. 8) as the "deliberate" piece with a good defense.
2. **(4)** Strongest counter-line: "the bench felt like the one place I
   wasn't performing being fine" (par. 8). A stuck person doesn't diagnose
   the performance; Abernathy names exactly what the ritual does for him —
   honest, deliberate grief practice, not paralysis. Also acceptable:
   "You can't hurry a bench" (par. 11) as acceptance rather than stuckness.
   2 pts quote+location, 2 pts explanation.
3. **(3)** Strong = relevant/direct/sufficient; thorough = plural and
   distributed across the text. Yes — two pieces from different parts
   (par. 8 and par. 11). 2 pts definitions, 1 pt verdict with reason.
4. **(3)** Literal: grief and remembrance can't be rushed; sitting with
   loss takes the time it takes. Larger work: the sentence states the
   passage's theme in miniature — the bench scenes *enact* unhurried
   remembrance, and the line gives the reader permission to value slowness.
   1 pt literal, 2 pts larger work.
5. **(4)** Sample theme: "Grief becomes bearable when given a deliberate,
   repeatable form — ritual turns absence into company." Refining detail:
   Lena brushing the bench too (par. 12) — the first draft ("his private
   ritual") didn't account for the ritual *transferring*; the theme must
   include shareability. Or: "She was right about the month" (par. 11) —
   humor inside grief complicates a pure-sorrow draft. 2 pts theme, 2 pts
   refining detail.
6. **(6)** Sample (72 words): "After his wife's death, retired Mr.
   Abernathy walks daily to the commuter platform where he once met her,
   sitting on the same bench from 4:40 to 5:30. His daughter Lena,
   concerned, questions him; he explains the visits began as habit and
   became deliberate remembrance. On the fourth day Lena joins him
   unasked. They do the crossword together, and she brushes the bench as
   he does." Deduct 1 per opinion or missing beat (routine, Lena's
   questioning, the explanation, her joining).
7. **(8)** Must walk the passage in order with ≥2 brief located
   quotations and visible refinement logic. Sample spine: routine
   established as mysterious habit (par. 1: the precise times, the brushed
   bench) → the conversation reframes habit as chosen remembrance (par. 8:
   "I wasn't performing being fine") → Lena's unasked joining and mirrored
   bench-brushing (par. 12) refines the theme from private ritual to
   *transmissible* ritual. Grade the *arc*, not the exact theme wording.
8. **(5)** Sample: (a) concern for her father — "watched this from her car
   for three days" (par. 2); (b) respect for his autonomy — she comes
   "unasked" (par. 12) rather than staging an intervention, and accepts
   "You don't have to do this" with "I know... That's why I'm doing it"
   (par. 13–14): she refuses the obligation frame, keeping the act freely
   chosen. 1 pt per motivation, 1 pt per line+location, 1 pt for the pair
   showing conflict.
9. **(5)** Says: "keeping company with the idea" — minimizing, almost
   joking; doing: daily 4:40–5:30 attendance for two years, thermos and
   crossword, brushing the empty bench. The gap reveals protective
   understatement — naming the ritual's full emotional weight would make
   it fragile, so he deflects with humor while his actions show it is the
   most deliberate thing in his life. Full marks for connecting the gap to
   a *reason* (protection, reticence, fear of sentimentality), not just
   naming it.
10. **(8)** Grade: lead-in present (2), quotation accurate with correct
    ("The Platform," par. N) citation (2), commentary explaining what the
    quotation proves *beyond paraphrase* (4). A commentary that merely
    restates the quote earns at most 1 of the 4.

## Project scoring notes

- "Located precisely" = paragraph number (stories) or Letter/Chapter
  (Frankenstein chain). "Around the middle" is not precise.
- Evidence stages: *emergence* (first appearance of the theme's raw
  material), *complication* (a detail that troubles the first draft),
  *refinement* (the detail that forces the rewrite), *confirmation* (a
  late detail the final theme predicts). Accept adjacent-stage
  disagreements if defended.
- Sample skeptic's objections worth full marks: "The salt box is just a
  container — Maya projects meaning onto random objects" (answer must show
  the *actions*, not just the object, carry the theme); "Victor's warning
  proves he learned, so ambition isn't the theme" (answer must engage "yet
  another may succeed").
- Presentation: talk *from* the evidence, not *at* the pages. Reading
  aloud = Developing at best for the presentation component (fold into the
  narrative score).
