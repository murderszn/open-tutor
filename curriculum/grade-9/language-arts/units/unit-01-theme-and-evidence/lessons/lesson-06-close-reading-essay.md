# Lesson 06 — The Close-Reading Essay: Building a Case

**Goal (student-readable):** I can assemble a claim, a developed theme, and
thorough evidence into a 2–3 paragraph close-reading essay — and revise it
against a skeptic's objections.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, all unit texts
and charts · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L6](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

**Readiness check (5 min):** You have a theme (L3/L4), evidence (L2), and
characters (L5). What is still missing between those ingredients and a
finished essay? (Answer: *architecture* — an order that carries a skeptic
from doubt to conviction — and *revision*, which is where essays are
actually written.)

## The architecture: claim → development → evidence → commentary

A close-reading essay is not a book report and not a freewrite. Its job is
to **prove an interpretation**. The unit's standard shape:

1. **Claim paragraph (the theme, stated as an arguable sentence).** Open
   with the theme itself — not with biography, not with "In this essay I
   will." One sentence of context, then the claim.
2. **Development paragraph (how the theme emerges and is refined).** Walk
   the reader through the text in order, showing the theme changing —
   exactly your L4 chart, in prose. Each turn gets its forcing detail.
3. **Evidence-and-commentary paragraph (the case, made thorough).** The
   strongest 2–3 quotations, each introduced, cited, and *commented on* —
   commentary explains what the quotation proves that a paraphrase could
   not. End by answering the skeptic: what is the best objection to your
   reading, and why does your evidence survive it?

Two to three paragraphs total for this unit's essay; later units expand the
form.

## Worked example 1 — A skeleton, then flesh

**Skeleton** (theme: *Frankenstein* shows that ambition unmakes both the
ambitious and the innocent around them, because makers refuse responsibility
for what they make):

- P1 claim: ambition → irresponsibility → collateral damage.
- P2 development: Walton's heroic ambition (Letter 1) → Victor's horror at
  his own success (Ch. 5) → the creature's counter-testimony (Ch. 10, 16)
  → Victor's cracked final warning (Ch. 24).
- P3 evidence: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend" (Ch.
  10) — commentary: the creature supplies the causal sentence Victor never
  manages; "the beauty of the dream vanished" (Ch. 5) — commentary: Shelley
  re-prices Walton's dream vocabulary. Skeptic: "Victor warns Walton, so he
  learned responsibility." Answer: his warning doubts itself — "yet another
  may succeed" — which proves the theme rather than refuting it.

**Flesh** (opening of P2, modeled):

> The theme does not arrive all at once. It first appears as heroism:
> Walton prefers "glory to every enticement that wealth placed in my path"
> (Letter 1), and the reader is invited to admire him. Two hundred pages
> later the same vocabulary curdles — Victor confesses he "desired it with
> an ardour that far exceeded moderation," and "the beauty of the dream
> vanished" (Ch. 5). The reader who admired Walton must now distrust the
> admiration. Then the creature takes the witness stand and supplies what
> neither man can: a causal account — "I was benevolent and good; misery
> made me a fiend" (Ch. 10).

Notice: every quotation is introduced (not dropped), cited, and followed by
commentary that a paraphrase couldn't supply.

## Worked example 2 — Revising against the skeptic

First draft sentence: *"The salt box symbolizes memory."* The skeptic asks:
*Which memory? The box was empty for years — does it symbolize forgetting?*
Revision: *"The salt box tracks memory's second life: empty of salt for
years, it becomes an archive of recipes, and Maya's copied card turns
archive back into practice."* The skeptic's question didn't kill the claim;
it **specified** it. Teach the learner to thank the skeptic.

Revision checklist (use on every draft):

- [ ] Every quotation has a lead-in and a ("Title," par./ch.) citation.
- [ ] Every quotation is followed by commentary — never two quotations in a
      row.
- [ ] The theme sentence could be disagreed with (if not, it's a topic).
- [ ] One paragraph shows the theme *changing* across the text, not just
      existing in it.
- [ ] The best objection is named and answered.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Choose your essay text: "The Salt Box," the *Frankenstein* chain, or
   "The Audition." Write the skeleton (three bullet blocks as above).
2. The adult plays skeptic for five minutes: for each block, one hard
   question. Record the questions verbatim — they are your revision plan.

## Independent practice

1. Draft the essay (2–3 paragraphs, ~300–450 words). Use the revision
   checklist before you call it done.
2. Write the "skeptic's paragraph": the strongest case *against* your own
   theme, in 4–6 sentences, with at least one quotation. Then write two
   sentences on whether your essay survives it — and if not, what you'd
   change. (This paragraph is not submitted with the essay; it is submitted
   *to yourself* as a revision tool.)
3. Reading log: draft a theme sentence for your independent book and one
   skeptic's question about it.

## Application

Exchange essays with the adult. Each reader marks: the claim sentence, each
quotation's lead-in and citation, each commentary sentence, and the
objection-answer. Anything unmarked is missing. The writer revises exactly
those gaps — no wholesale rewriting.

## Exit check

Write your essay's claim sentence on an index card. Below it, the one
quotation you would keep if you could keep only one — and one sentence on
why it is irreplaceable.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the essay may be dictated; the adult scribes exactly
  what is said, including the citations, so the learner practices the
  *structure* without the transcription load.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the essay as labeled paragraph cards
  (CLAIM / DEVELOPMENT / EVIDENCE+COMMENTARY) that can be reordered before
  drafting.
- **Extension:** expand to five paragraphs by adding a counter-reading
  paragraph (the L4 responsibility-vs-ambition debate, fully argued) before
  the conclusion. This is the U06 argument-essay form in miniature.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners write the essay as *summary with a theme sentence taped on*.
Test: delete every plot sentence — does an argument remain? If the essay
collapses, it was a retelling. Also: commentary that merely restates the
quotation ("This shows that the creature was miserable") is not commentary;
commentary explains what the quotation *does* that nothing else in the
paragraph does.
