# Lesson 04 — Tracking a Theme Across a Full-Length Text

**Goal (student-readable):** I can trace how a theme emerges and is shaped
and refined by specific details across many chapters of a novel — not just
within one story — using an evidence chart.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, the Frankenstein
theme chain · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L4](../answer-key.md#lesson-04)

**Readiness check (5 min):** In "The Salt Box" your theme changed as you
read. Now imagine doing that across a 200-page novel, where the author has
months of story to develop an idea. What new problems appear? (Possible
answers: you forget early details; the theme might split into two; a
character might contradict your theme and be right.)

## Why novels need a chart, not just a memory

Short stories refine a theme in a few pages. Novels develop themes the way
rivers cut canyons — slowly, with switchbacks. Mary Shelley's
*Frankenstein* (1818) is a good training ground: it is in the public domain,
it argues with itself, and its theme about ambition does not sit still. You
will not read the whole novel in this unit; you will work from five
verified excerpts in chronological order, in
[the theme chain](../texts/frankenstein-theme-chain.md). The method is the
same one scholars use on the full book: **claim → detail → revised claim.**

## Worked example 1 — The first two excerpts

**Excerpt 1** (Letter 1, Walton): "I shall satiate my ardent curiosity with
the sight of a part of the world never before visited... My life might have
been passed in ease and luxury, but I preferred glory to every enticement
that wealth placed in my path."

- Candidate theme v1: *Ambitious people sacrifice comfort for glory, and the
  novel admires them for it.* (Note the risk: this is Walton describing
  *himself*. The reader has no second opinion yet.)
- What it adds: ambition enters as *aspiration* — curiosity, glory,
  discovery. The tone is heroic.

**Excerpt 2** (Chapter 5, Victor): "I had desired it with an ardour that far
exceeded moderation; but now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream
vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart."

- Theme v2: *Ambition pursued past moderation turns its own reward to
  horror; the glory Walton chases is the trap Victor fell into.*
- What forced the revision: the *same vocabulary* returns poisoned —
  "ardour" was Walton's fuel and is now Victor's indictment; "the beauty of
  the dream" names exactly what Walton is still dreaming. Shelley uses
  Walton's words against him before Walton ever meets Victor.

Notice the technique: track **repeated words across distance**. "Ardour,"
"glory," "dream" — the novel plants them early and re-prices them later.

## Worked example 2 — When a new voice refines the theme

**Excerpt 3** (Chapter 10, the creature): "I was benevolent and good;
misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous."

- Theme v3: *Ambition's damage is not only what it does to the ambitious —
  it creates victims who can name the mechanism better than the ambitious
  can.* The creature does what Victor never does: states a cause and
  effect plainly.
- What forced the revision: a *new speaker* with standing Victor lacks.
  Victor describes his feelings; the creature describes a *system*
  (benevolence → misery → fiend → possible return to virtue). The theme
  widens from "ambition hurts the ambitious" to "ambition makes makers
  irresponsible toward what they make."

This is the grade-9 move: the theme is not *found* in one passage. It is
**assembled** — each excerpt contributes a part, and some parts contradict
earlier ones until you reconcile them.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

Build the chart for excerpts 4 and 5:

| Excerpt | Key words/details | What it adds or changes | Revised theme (v4, then v5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 — Ch. 16, "I am malicious because I am miserable." | | | |
| 5 — Ch. 24, "Seek happiness in tranquillity and avoid ambition... Yet why do I say this? I have myself been blasted in these hopes, yet another may succeed." | | | |

For excerpt 5, wrestle with the crack in it: Victor warns against ambition
*and in the same breath* doubts his own warning ("yet another may
succeed"). Does that weaken the theme or complete it? There are defensible
answers on both sides — pick one and defend it with the words.

## Independent practice

1. Write a theme-development paragraph (8–12 sentences): state your final
   theme (v5), then walk through the chain showing how each excerpt shaped
   or refined it, quoting briefly at least three times with locations
   (Letter 1 / Ch. 5 / Ch. 10 / Ch. 16 / Ch. 24).
2. **Counter-reading:** a classmate claims the novel's real theme is not
   ambition but *responsibility* — that every disaster comes from someone
   refusing responsibility (Victor abandoning the creature, the creature
   choosing revenge, Walton nearly sacrificing his crew). Using the same
   five excerpts, write four sentences: the strongest evidence *for* this
   reading and the strongest evidence *against* it.
3. Reading log: find one repeated word or image in your independent book
   across two distant chapters. Note both locations and what changed
   between them.

## Application

The adult chooses one excerpt you have not charted closely. Cold-chart it:
15 minutes, no help — key words, what it adds, revised theme. Then compare
with your earlier chart: did the new excerpt confirm your v5 or force a
v6? Either outcome is a success if you can point at the words that caused
it.

## Exit check

One sentence: the difference between tracking a theme in a short story and
in a novel. (Acceptable core: in a novel the theme must survive *distance,
new voices, and contradiction* — and the chart is how you hold it all at
once.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** excerpts may be read aloud; the chart is the thinking,
  not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the chart as a physical timeline on a
  table — excerpt cards in order, revision cards clipped on with string to
  the words that forced them.
- **Extension:** read the full Chapter 10 confrontation (free at the
  Gutenberg link in the chain file) and add two more rows to your chart.
  Does the longer passage confirm or complicate your v5?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners treat each excerpt as a separate mini-theme ("excerpt 3 is about
revenge") instead of revising *one* running claim. Insist: there is one
theme column, and it gets rewritten, not replaced. Also watch for plot
summary creeping into the "what it adds" column — "the creature is angry"
is plot; "the creature gives ambition a *victim's vocabulary*" is theme
work.
