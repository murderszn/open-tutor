# Lesson 02 — "Strong and Thorough" Evidence

**Goal (student-readable):** I can judge whether evidence is strong
(relevant, direct, sufficient) and thorough (multiple pieces, spread across
the text), and I can weave quotations and paraphrases into my own sentences
with precise citations.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, "The Salt Box"
with your annotations · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Claim: *Maya's grandmother loved feeding
people.* Write down the single best line from "The Salt Box" that proves it.
Now write down a second, different line that also proves it. Notice: the
second one was harder to find. That difficulty is today's lesson.

## What "strong and thorough" means (RL.9-10.1)

Grade 8 asked for the *strongest* evidence. Grade 9 raises the bar twice:

- **Strong** evidence is *relevant* (it actually bears on the claim),
  *direct* (it needs little interpretive stretching), and *sufficient*
  (enough to carry the point, not just hint at it).
- **Thorough** evidence is *plural and distributed*: more than one piece,
  drawn from more than one part of the text. One perfect quotation is a
  sample; three quotations from the beginning, middle, and end are a case.

A claim with one strong quotation is *supported*. A claim with several
strong quotations from across the text is *proven to a skeptic*. This unit
builds the second kind.

## Worked example 1 — Ranking evidence for one claim

**Claim:** Maya feels guilty about the rolls she never learned to make.

Candidate evidence from "The Salt Box":

| # | Quotation | Strength | Thoroughness contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | "She was fourteen when she asked for the rolls. She was sixteen now." | Strong — directly measures the lost time | Middle of story |
| B | "That night she copied the rolls recipe onto a fresh card in her own handwriting... At the bottom she wrote: *Ask Mom to teach me before I stop asking.*" | Strongest — her action answers the guilt without naming it | End of story |
| C | "Maya stared at that one a long time." | Weak alone — staring could mean many things | Middle; needs a partner |
| D | "She had practiced copying when she was eight, filling notebook pages with looping *R*'s that never came out right." | Weak for *this* claim — it shows admiration/connection, not guilt | Beginning |

Ranking: **B > A > C > D** for this claim. B wins because it is an
*action* responding to the guilt — behavior is harder to fake than feeling.
Notice D is not bad evidence; it is evidence for a *different* claim (that
Maya admired her grandmother's handwriting). Strong is always relative to
the claim.

## Worked example 2 — Introducing evidence in a sentence

Evidence never drops into a paragraph unexplained. Three legal moves:

1. **Quoted with a lead-in:** Maya's guilt surfaces as action, not
   confession: the night after the move, she copies the rolls recipe "onto
   a fresh card in her own handwriting" and adds, "Ask Mom to teach me
   before I stop asking" ("The Salt Box," par. 22).
2. **Paraphrased with attribution:** Two years passed between Maya's request
   and the move — she was fourteen when she asked, sixteen when she finally
   sat on the kitchen floor ("The Salt Box," par. 11).
3. **Blended:** The card that stops her — "Maya asked for the rolls. Teach
   her before she stops asking" — is the only one in the box written *about*
   Maya rather than *for* a dish ("The Salt Box," par. 10).

Citation format for this unit: **("Title," paragraph number)**. Paragraph
numbers are counted from the first story paragraph. Precision matters: a
skeptic must be able to find your words in seconds.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

**Claim:** The salt box mattered to the family long before Maya opened it.

1. Find four candidate quotations. Rank them strongest to weakest and write
   one sentence for each explaining its rank.
2. Identify your weakest candidate: is it weak because it is irrelevant,
   indirect, or insufficient? Say which.
3. Write one paragraph that uses your two strongest pieces — one quoted,
   one paraphrased — with correct ("The Salt Box," par. N) citations.

## Independent practice

1. **Claim:** Maya's mother is grieving too, not just managing the move.
   Collect three pieces of evidence from three different parts of the story.
   Rank them and write the paragraph.
2. **Counter-exercise:** Find one quotation that *seems* to support the
   claim "Maya's brother does not care about the family" and explain why a
   careful reader would reject it as insufficient. (Hint: what does the soup
   scene show?)
3. Reading log: for today's pages, record one claim about your book and two
   pieces of evidence with locations (chapter/page).

## Application

Trade paragraphs with the adult. Each of you plays skeptic: underline the
evidence, then ask of each piece, "Could this support a *different* claim
instead?" If yes, the evidence is not yet strong enough — or the claim
needs narrowing. Revise once.

## Exit check

Write a two-sentence case: a claim about "The Salt Box" + your single
strongest quotation with a correct citation. Then add one sentence
explaining *why* that quotation is stronger than one other candidate you
considered and rejected.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** evidence-hunting may be done with the story read aloud
  in chunks; the learner calls "stop" when they hear a candidate line.
- **Alternate response mode:** rank evidence cards physically (strongest on
  top); dictate the ranking reasons.
- **Extension:** take one of your "rejected" quotations and write the
  *different* claim it actually supports well. This is how counter-readings
  are born — and Lesson 6 will use them.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Two common failures: (a) the "quote dump" — three quotations in a row with
no sentences explaining what each proves; (b) citing the *most emotional*
line instead of the *most relevant* one. Teach the test: cover the claim and
read only the quotation — can a stranger guess the claim? If not, the
evidence is decoration. Also watch paragraph-number accuracy; have the
learner count aloud once.
