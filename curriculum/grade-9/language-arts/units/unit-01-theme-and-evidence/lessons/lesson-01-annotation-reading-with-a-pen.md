# Lesson 01 — Reading with a Pen: Annotation, Observation, and Inference

**Goal (student-readable):** I can annotate a text with a purpose — marking
what it says outright, noting what it suggests, and questioning what I need
to check — and I can tell an observation from an inference.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, two highlighter
colors or two pen marks (underline vs. circle), the generated illustration ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L1](../answer-key.md#lesson-01)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Look at the illustration below for one full
minute without writing. Then cover it and write down five things you
*observed* and three things you *guessed*. We will sort out the difference
together.

## Observation is not inference

- An **observation** is what you can point to: *the girl is holding a wooden
  box; the counters are bare; light comes through a window.*
- An **inference** is a conclusion your brain builds from observations:
  *someone is moving out; the box matters to her; she found something
  unexpected.*

Both are useful. The danger is mistaking one for the other — treating a
guess as if the picture proved it. Close readers label which is which.

## Worked example 1 — Sorting observation from inference

Using the illustration above:

| Statement | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "There are yellowed cards on the floor around the girl." | Observation | You can point to them. |
| "The kitchen is being emptied because someone is moving." | Inference | Supported by the bare counters and moving boxes — but the picture never says so. |
| "The girl looks like she is reading carefully." | Borderline | Her posture and gaze are observable; "carefully" is your interpretation of them. A careful reader flags the interpretation part. |
| "The box used to hold salt." | Inference with weak support | Nothing in the picture says what the box held. (This is the kind of guess a good annotator marks with a question mark, not a period.) |

## Worked example 2 — The three-pen annotation system

Read the first four paragraphs of ["The Salt Box"](../texts/the-salt-box.md)
now. Annotate as you go with three marks:

1. **Underline** anything the text states outright (explicit).
2. **Circle** any detail that makes you infer something the text does not
   say (a suggestion).
3. **Margin mark `?`** anywhere you are unsure — a word, a motive, a
   connection.

Watch it done on paragraph 2:

> Maya was supposed to be sorting. That was the deal she had made with her
> mother: one box for keep, one box for donate, everything else in the black
> bags by the door. *(underline "one box for keep, one box for donate" —
> explicit rule)* Her mother was in the bedroom wrapping the good plates in
> newspaper. Her brother had taken the garage and disappeared into it with
> his phone. *(circle "disappeared into it with his phone" — suggests he is
> avoiding the work, but the text never says so)* So it was Maya and the
> kitchen, and the kitchen was winning. *(margin `?` — what does "the kitchen
> was winning" mean literally? It is figurative; note it and move on.)*

Notice: the annotator does not stop to resolve everything. Annotation is
*collecting evidence for later thinking*, not finishing the thinking now.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

Finish reading "The Salt Box" with the three-pen system. Then:

1. Pick your three strongest circled inferences. For each, write the
   observation(s) underneath it that support it.
2. Find one place where your first inference turned out wrong or incomplete
   after you finished the story. Mark it honestly — revising is the skill.
3. Write two margin `?` questions you still have. A good question points at
   a specific line.

## Independent practice

1. Return to the illustration. Write six observations and four inferences
   about it in two labeled columns. For each inference, name the observation
   it rests on.
2. Choose one inference from your illustration columns and argue *against*
   it: what is a different, also-plausible reading of the same observations?
3. In your reading log, record today's pages plus one annotated observation
   and one inference about your independent book.

## Application

The adult picks one paragraph of "The Salt Box" you have not annotated
closely (suggested: the soup-making scene). Annotate it alone, then explain
aloud: which of your inferences would you defend to a skeptic, and which
would you withdraw? Defend means pointing at words.

## Exit check

On an index card: one observation from the story, one inference, and the
exact words that support the inference. If you cannot point at words, it is
not ready.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the story aloud while the learner
  follows and marks; audiobook-style pacing is fine — the marks are the
  work, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** observations and inferences may be dictated
  and scribed, or sorted as written cards into two labeled piles.
- **Extension:** annotate the same passage twice — once for what Maya
  knows, once for what the *reader* knows that Maya does not (e.g., the
  reader sees the mother's grief before Maya names it). Compare the two
  sets of inferences.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat any reasonable guess as "what the text says." Press
the distinction physically: *point at the words.* If the finger lands on
nothing, it is an inference — valuable, but labeled. Also watch for
annotation as decoration: underlining everything is the same as
underlining nothing. The three-pen system only works if most of the page
stays unmarked.
