# Lesson 05 — Complex Characters: The People Who Carry the Theme

**Goal (student-readable):** I can analyze how a character with multiple or
conflicting motivations develops, how interactions reveal them, and how their
choices advance the plot or develop the theme.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, "The Audition"
· **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Think of someone you know who wanted two
incompatible things at once. Write both wants in one sentence each. Now:
which one did they choose, and did their *actions* match their *words*?
Characters work the same way — today you learn to read the gap.

## What makes a character "complex" (RL.9-10.3)

A simple character has one motivation and acts on it. A **complex**
character has **multiple or conflicting motivations**, changes (or
resists changing) under pressure, and reveals different faces to different
people. The grade-9 skill is not just naming traits ("Marcus is loyal") but
showing **how the conflict inside the character moves the story and feeds
the theme.**

Three tools:

1. **Motivation mapping:** list what the character wants — all of it, even
   the contradictory parts.
2. **The action/word gap:** compare what the character *says* with what the
   character *does*. The gap is where the truth lives.
3. **Interaction as revelation:** characters are revealed by how others act
   around them — who softens, who hardens, who changes because of them.

## Worked example 1 — Mapping Marcus

Read ["The Audition"](../texts/the-audition.md). Marcus's motivations:

| Want | Evidence (with paragraph) | Conflicts with |
|---|---|---|
| The audition — to be seen as more than the store kid | Three weeks of shower rehearsals (par. 1); running four blocks in work shoes (par. 26) | The Thursday shift |
| Keeping the family afloat — not adding to his mother's load | Never telling his mother about the conflict (par. 2); covering the register without complaint | The audition |
| His brother's respect — or at least not his contempt | The careful way he phrases the swap request; telling Darnell about the callback *first* (par. 27) | His resentment of Darnell |

Marcus is complex not because he is mysterious but because he is **pulled**.
The story's tension is structural: every scene tightens one want against
another until Darnell resolves it *for* him — which is itself a character
revelation about Darnell.

## Worked example 2 — Darnell, revealed sideways

Darnell never gets a viewpoint scene. Everything we know comes
sideways — through Marcus's eyes and Darnell's actions:

- **Words:** "I got plans Thursday." (par. 9) / "You think you're better than me
  now? Theater kid?" (par. 11) — defensive, prickly, performing not-caring.
- **Actions:** walks in at 4:15 (par. 21); picks up
  the pricing gun; asks where the apron is (par. 23); takes Saturdays back "regular"
  (par. 28).
- **The gap:** the words say *I don't care*; the actions say *I was
  listening the whole time*. The quiet closing of the bedroom door (par. 13)
  — "not slam, which was worse" — is the hinge: Marcus reads it as a
  decision, and he is right.

Darnell's arc in one line: *a brother who performs indifference until the
cost of indifference (losing Marcus's regard) exceeds the cost of showing
up.* He changes — or rather, he *stops pretending not to have changed*.

**Theme connection:** the story's theme might be *showing up is a choice
you make before you feel like it* — and that theme is carried almost
entirely by Darnell, the character with the least dialogue. Complex
characters don't just decorate themes; they *deliver* them.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Map **Ms. Okafor** or **Mr. Abernathy** (minor characters): what does
   each one want, what do they actually do, and what does the gap reveal?
   (Small roles can still be complex — or pointedly simple. Decide which.)
2. Find the story's **turning point** — the moment after which the outcome
   is decided. Defend your choice with one quotation. (Likely candidates:
   the quiet door close, Darnell walking into the store. Argue it out.)

## Independent practice

1. Choose one character from your independent reading book. Write a
   motivation map (at least three wants, with evidence locations) and one
   paragraph on how a conflict between two of those wants advanced the
   plot.
2. **Error analysis:** a classmate writes, "Marcus is irresponsible because
   he almost missed his shift." Write four sentences explaining what this
   reading gets wrong — using the action/word gap and at least two
   quotations with paragraph numbers.
3. Reading log: note one interaction in your book where a character was
   revealed by someone *else's* reaction to them.

## Application

Write the scene from **Darnell's point of view** (150–200 words): Tuesday
night, after Marcus asks him to cover Thursday. Stay consistent with every
known fact — the cereal, the door, the showing up. What does Darnell want
that he won't say aloud? (This is characterization as a *test*: if you
understand his motivations, you can write inside them without breaking
them.)

## Exit check

Name one conflicting-motivation pair for Marcus and one for a character in
your independent book. One sentence each.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the story may be read aloud with roles (narrator,
  Marcus, Darnell, others) — hearing the dialogue exposes the word/action
  gap faster than silent reading.
- **Alternate response mode:** build motivation maps as two-column cards
  (WANT / EVIDENCE) that can be physically rearranged as the story
  progresses.
- **Extension:** compare Darnell to the creature in *Frankenstein*
  (Excerpts 3–4): both are judged by appearances and both demand to be
  *heard*. Where does the comparison hold, and where does it break? Write
  the breaking point carefully — false equivalences are the enemy here.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners list adjectives ("brave, loyal, determined") instead of
motivations. Adjectives describe; motivations *explain*. Force every trait
claim into the form "he wants X, shown when he does Y." Also: learners
treat the first impression of a character as final. Teach re-reading
Darnell's early scenes *after* knowing the ending — complex characters
reward the second pass, and noticing that is the lesson.
