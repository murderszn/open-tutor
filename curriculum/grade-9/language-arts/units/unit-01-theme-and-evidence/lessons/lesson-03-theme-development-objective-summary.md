# Lesson 03 — Theme Is a Claim: Development and Objective Summary

**Goal (student-readable):** I can state a theme as a full-sentence claim,
show how specific details shape and refine it across a text, and write an
objective summary that leaves my opinions out.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, "The Salt Box,"
the informational passage · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (5 min):** In one or two words, what is "The Salt Box"
*about*? Now state what it *claims* about that subject in one full
sentence. The second task is the unit's central skill — notice how it
forces you to commit.

## Topic names; theme claims (RL.9-10.2)

- A **topic** is a word or two: *memory, family, grief, home.*
- A **theme** is what the text claims about the topic, in a complete
  sentence: *A home is not a place but the people and rituals we carry out
  of it.*

Two tests from the grade-8 unit still apply, sharpened: (1) if it is not a
complete sentence, it is a topic; (2) it must be supportable by the *whole*
text. Grade 9 adds a third test: **the theme must be able to change as you
read.** A first-draft theme is a hypothesis. Details confirm it, complicate
it, or force you to rewrite it.

## Worked example 1 — A theme under revision

Watch a reader's theme for "The Salt Box" evolve:

- **After paragraph 4** (the empty box described): *"Old objects hold
  memories."* Plausible but thin — it could describe any attic story.
- **After paragraph 10** (the cards, including "Teach her before she stops
  asking"): *"Family love is passed down through small rituals — and the
  rituals can fail."* Sharper: the box was *empty of salt for years*, and
  the rolls were never taught. The detail about the unworn path — the
  grandmother's regret — refines "holds memories" into something with loss
  in it.
- **After paragraph 22** (Maya copies the recipe, writes her own card):
  *"What makes a home is not the place but the practices we choose to
  carry forward — memory only survives if someone re-enacts it."* The final
  action forces the rewrite: Maya doesn't just remember; she *restarts* the
  ritual, imperfectly, in her own handwriting. The theme now includes
  agency, not just nostalgia.

Each revision was forced by a *specific detail* — the empty box, the
never-taught rolls, the copied card. That is what RL.9-10.2 means by
analyzing "how it emerges and is shaped and refined by specific details."

## Worked example 2 — Objective summary vs. retelling

**Retelling** (fails — opinions and trivia smuggled in):

> *Maya finds her grandma's recipe cards while cleaning the kitchen, which
> is sad because her grandma is old now. She decides to learn the rolls,
> which is sweet. The soup wasn't very good but they ate it anyway.*

Problems: "sad," "sweet" are judgments; "the soup wasn't very good" is a
detail that doesn't serve the arc; no structure.

**Objective summary** (passes — no opinions, complete arc, ~70 words):

> *While clearing out her grandmother's kitchen after a move, sixteen-year-
> old Maya finds a salt box full of recipe cards in her grandmother's
> handwriting, including one noting that Maya once asked to learn the rolls
> and was never taught. Maya and her mother cook the potato soup together.
> That night Maya copies the rolls recipe in her own handwriting and adds a
> note to ask her mother to teach her.*

Check a summary three ways: (1) no adjectives of approval or disapproval;
(2) a stranger could follow the plot; (3) nothing important is missing and
nothing trivial is included.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Draft a theme for "The Salt Box" in one sentence. Then deliberately try
   to *break* it: find one detail in the story your theme doesn't account
   for. Revise the theme to account for it. Repeat once more.
2. Write an objective summary of "The Salt Box" (60–90 words). Trade with
   the adult; each of you hunts the other's summary for one smuggled
   opinion or one missing story beat. Fix and re-trade.

## Independent practice

1. Read ["What Memory Research Says About the Past We Remember"](../texts/memory-and-the-past.md).
   State its **central idea** (the informational-text cousin of theme) in
   one sentence, then list the three pieces of evidence the passage uses —
   with paragraph numbers — and note which one is strongest and why.
2. Write an objective summary of the passage (50–70 words). Then write one
   sentence explaining how its central idea *connects to* "The Salt Box"
   without claiming either text proves the other.
3. Reading log: today's entry must include one sentence of objective
   summary of what you read — no opinions allowed.

## Application

Take your revised theme from guided practice and test it against the
*informational* passage: does your literary theme about carried-forward
rituals survive contact with the research claim that memory is
reconstructed, not recorded? Write a paragraph: where the two agree, where
they tension, and what that tension teaches you about the limits of each
kind of text. (This is the move Lesson 6's essay will require.)

## Exit check

On an index card: your current theme sentence for "The Salt Box" + the one
detail that most refined it + its paragraph number.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** theme-drafting can be done orally — the adult records
  each version verbatim so the learner can see the revisions accumulate.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the theme as a "claim ladder" on index
  cards, one card per revision, each clipped to the detail card that forced
  it.
- **Extension:** write a theme for the story from the *mother's* point of
  view — what claim would she make about the same events? Compare: can one
  story support two true themes? Defend your answer with details.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners write morals ("you should appreciate your grandparents") instead
of themes. A moral tells the reader what to do; a theme claims what the
*text shows*. If the sentence starts with "you should," send it back.
Also: learners confuse "objective" with "boring" and strip summaries of all
causation. Objectivity bans opinions, not explanations of why things
happen.
