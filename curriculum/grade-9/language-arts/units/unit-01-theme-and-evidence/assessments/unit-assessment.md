## The passage: "The Platform"

*An original story written for this assessment (~430 words).*

Every weekday at 4:40, Mr. Abernathy — retired now, the hardware store sold
to his nephew — walked to the commuter platform at the end of Marlowe
Street and sat on the second bench from the stairs. He brought a thermos
and the crossword. The 4:52 came and went. The 5:07 came and went. At 5:30
he stood, brushed the bench though nothing was on it, and walked home.

His daughter Lena, home for the holidays, watched this from her car for
three days before she asked.

"Waiting for someone, Dad?"

"Waiting's a strong word," he said. "Keeping company with the idea."

She laughed, then didn't. "Mom's been gone two years."

"I know how long."

"Is this — are you okay?"

He poured thermos coffee into the spare cup he always brought and handed it
to her. "Your mother took the 5:07 for thirty-one years. Same bench. She'd
grade papers while she waited for the connection. I used to meet her here
on Fridays with takeout. After she — after, I kept coming. First it was
because I didn't know what else to do at 4:40. Then it was because the
bench felt like the one place I wasn't performing being fine."

Lena sat down. The 5:07 pulled in, sighed, left.

"Do you talk to her?" Lena asked quietly. "Here?"

"Sometimes I tell her about the store. She always said I'd sell it to the
nephew and regret it within a month. She was right about the month." He
smiled. "Mostly I just sit. The sitting is the point, I think. You can't
hurry a bench."

On the fourth day Lena came with him, unasked, with two thermoses. They
did the crossword together and got seven wrong. When the 5:30 passed they
both stood, and he brushed the bench, and she found herself brushing it
too, though nothing was on it.

"You don't have to do this," he said.

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm doing it."

## Questions

### A. Evidence (14 pts)

1. (4 pts) **Claim:** *Mr. Abernathy's platform visits began as habit and
   became deliberate remembrance.* Quote two lines from two different parts
   of the passage that together prove this claim — one for "habit," one
   for "deliberate remembrance" — with paragraph numbers, and write one
   sentence for each explaining what it proves.
2. (4 pts) A classmate claims the passage shows Mr. Abernathy is "stuck and
   can't move on." Quote the single strongest line *against* this reading
   and explain in two sentences why it undermines the claim.
3. (3 pts) What is the difference between strong and thorough evidence?
   Does your answer to question 1 use thorough evidence? Explain in two
   sentences.
4. (3 pts) The line "You can't hurry a bench" is figurative. State in one
   sentence what it means literally in this scene, and in one more sentence
   what work it does for the passage's larger meaning.

### B. Theme and summary (18 pts)

5. (4 pts) State the passage's theme as one full sentence. Then name the
   single detail that most *refined* your theme as you read — the detail
   your first draft didn't account for.
6. (6 pts) Write an objective summary of the passage (60–80 words). It must
   contain no opinions and no missing story beats.
7. (8 pts) Write a theme-development paragraph (8–10 sentences): walk
   through the passage in order — the opening routine, the conversation
   with Lena, the final scene — showing how specific details shape and
   refine the theme. Quote briefly at least twice with paragraph numbers.

### C. Character (10 pts)

8. (5 pts) Name two conflicting motivations Lena has in the final scene.
   For each, cite one line (with paragraph number).
9. (5 pts) Mr. Abernathy says, "Waiting's a strong word... Keeping company
   with the idea." In 4–5 sentences, analyze the gap between what he *says*
   here and what he *does* across the passage. What does the gap reveal?

### D. Integration (8 pts)

10. (8 pts) Choose your best quotation from any answer above. Rewrite it
    as a fully integrated evidence sentence: a lead-in of your own, the
    quotation, a ("The Platform," par. N) citation, and at least one
    sentence of commentary explaining what the quotation proves that a
    paraphrase could not.
