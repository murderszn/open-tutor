# "Night Train"

*An original short story written for this unit (2026). Used in Lessons 1,
2, 3, and 6 for plot-structure mapping, character analysis, point-of-view
study, and author-choice comparison.*

---

The conductor was already at her row before Maya could decide whether
to lie.

"Ticket," he said. Not unkindly. He had the tired politeness of a man
who had asked for a thousand tickets and expected a thousand small
disappointments.

Maya handed it over. Her fingers left a damp crescent on the paper. In
her coat pocket, the compass knocked once against her phone — a small,
brass, certain sound — and she folded her hands to keep them still.

The conductor punched the ticket and moved on. Maya exhaled through her
nose and watched the black window turn her reflection back at her: a
girl of sixteen trying to look like somebody going somewhere on
purpose.

She had been going somewhere on purpose, once. That morning.

---

That morning the house had smelled like rain coming. Maya had let
herself in with the key her uncle kept under the ceramic frog — the key
everybody in the family knew about and nobody mentioned, because
mentioning it would mean admitting that Uncle Ray locked the good
silver drawer but not the back door.

The desk was in the study. It had been her grandfather's desk before it
was her uncle's desk, and everybody said so every time they walked
past it, the way people repeat a fact to keep it from sliding away.
Her grandfather had been dead eleven months. The compass had been in
the desk's top drawer for all eleven of them, in the little felt bag,
and every time Maya visited, she checked that it was still there. It
always was. That was the problem. Nobody was doing anything with it.

"He said it was mine." Maya said it out loud to the empty study,
because saying it out loud made it truer. "At the lake, the summer
before. He said, 'This'll be yours one day, navigator.'"

Uncle Ray had laughed when she told him. Not cruelly — the tired
laugh of a man dividing a dead father's things among people who all
loved him differently. "He said a lot of things that summer, kiddo.
The lawyer has the list. The compass stays with the desk."

So Maya had done the thing she had been rehearsing for three weeks in
the shower and on the bus: she had taken the felt bag, put it in her
coat pocket, and walked out through the back door, past the ceramic
frog, into the rain that had finally decided to come.

She told herself it was not stealing if it had been promised. She told
herself this all the way to the station, and then she stopped telling
herself things, because the telling was starting to sound like someone
else talking.

---

Across the aisle, an old man was unwrapping a sandwich with the slow
ceremony of someone performing a play for an audience of one. He caught
her watching and held up half of it in offering. Maya shook her head.

"You look like you're carrying the weight of the world, young lady,"
he said. "I've carried it. It's mostly sandwiches."

Maya almost smiled. "I'm fine."

"Sure," he said. "Everybody on the night train is fine. That's the
rule." He ate with neat, deliberate bites. "I found a wallet once. On
this very line, twenty years ago. Leather, fat with cash. A man's whole
week in there." He wiped his mouth. "I turned it in at the next
station. The clerk looked at me like I'd grown a second head."

"Why'd you do it?" Maya asked, before she could stop herself.

The old man considered. "Because I'd have known, every time I spent a
dollar of it, exactly what kind of man I was. Some prices are too high
even when nobody's charging." He folded the wax paper into a perfect
square. "What's in the pocket?"

Maya's hand flew to her coat before she could stop it. "Nothing."

"Mm." The old man did not look away. "The nothingest nothing I've ever
seen."

The train slowed. Through the window, lights smeared past — a town
Maya didn't know, platform lamps, a closed ticket booth. Her stop was
two towns further: her mother's house, where she had said she would be
all weekend, where Uncle Ray would call tomorrow looking for a compass
that would not be in the drawer.

The compass knocked against her phone again, small and brass and
certain. *Navigator*, her grandfather had called her. He had taught
her to read it on the dock at the lake, his big hand steadying hers,
the needle shivering and settling, shivering and settling, until it
chose north and held it.

Maya stood up. Her legs had gone strange and light, as if the train
had been carrying more of her weight than she knew.

"Miss your stop?" the conductor called from the far end of the car.

"No," Maya said. "This is mine." It wasn't. But the town had a post
office, and the post office would be open in the morning, and a padded
envelope and a stamp cost less than the lie she had been rehearsing.

She stepped onto the platform into air that smelled of wet gravel and
diesel. Behind her, the old man raised his folded wax-paper square in
a small salute. The train pulled away, taking her reflection with it,
and Maya stood in the lamplight holding the nothingest nothing she
had ever carried, finally deciding what kind of person was going to
carry it home.

## About this text

"Night Train" was written for Unit 02 of the grade-9 language arts
track. It opens *in medias res* (on the train, after the theft),
flashes back to the theft itself, and returns to the present for the
climax (the decision at the unplanned stop) and a brief resolution.
Maya's motivations conflict throughout: loyalty to her grandfather's
memory and the "promise" pull her toward keeping the compass, while
guilt, her uncle's fairness, and the old man's quiet example pull her
toward returning it. The old man functions as a **foil** — a character
whose opposite choice throws Maya's choice into relief. Details planted
early (the damp ticket, the knocking compass, the ceramic frog, the
needle "shivering and settling") pay off later, inviting discussion of
foreshadowing and motif. The story is told in **third-person limited**,
staying close to Maya's thoughts while occasionally withholding what
she will not admit to herself.
