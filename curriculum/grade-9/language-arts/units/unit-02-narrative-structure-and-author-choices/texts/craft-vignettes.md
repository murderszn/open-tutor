# Craft Vignettes

*Short original passages written for this unit (2026). Each vignette
isolates one author choice so learners can compare versions of the same
scene. Used in Lessons 3 (point of view), 4 (pacing), and 5 (word
choice). All three describe the same fictional event — a teenager named
June waiting in a school stairwell after hours — so that differences in
effect can only come from the author's choices, not the events.*

---

## Vignette A — Point of view: three tellings of the stairwell

**Version 1 — First person (June narrates).** *Used in Lesson 3.*

The stairwell door clicked shut behind me, and then it was just the
buzz of the lights and my own breathing. I told myself I was waiting
for Priya, which was true, and I told myself I wasn't hiding, which
was the kind of true that needs saying twice. My backpack was too
heavy. Everything I owned that mattered was in it, which is a stupid
thing to think at 4:40 on a Tuesday, but there it was. Footsteps on
the landing above — not Priya's, too heavy — and I pressed back
against the wall where the shadow was thickest. Whoever it was kept
going. I stayed where I was anyway. Just in case. Just until I knew.

**Version 2 — Third-person limited (a narrator stays close to June).**
*Used in Lesson 3.*

The stairwell door clicked shut behind June, and then it was just the
buzz of the lights and her own breathing. She told herself she was
waiting for Priya, which was true, and she told herself she wasn't
hiding, which was the kind of true that needs saying twice. Her
backpack was too heavy. Footsteps sounded on the landing above — not
Priya's, too heavy — and June pressed back against the wall where the
shadow was thickest. Whoever it was kept going. She stayed where she
was anyway.

**Version 3 — First person, unreliable.** *Used in Lesson 3. Read
after Versions 1–2 and compare what changed.*

The stairwell door clicked shut behind me. Perfect. Nobody uses these
stairs after four, which I knew because I'd checked, three days
running, writing down the times in my notebook like a scientist. Priya
said she'd meet me, but Priya says a lot of things. My backpack was
exactly as heavy as it needed to be. I heard footsteps on the landing
above and I didn't move — not because I was scared, I am never scared
in stairwells — but because moving would have ruined the plan. The
plan was excellent. The plan had no flaws. Whoever it was kept going,
which proved the plan was working. I stayed exactly where I had
planned to stay.

*Lesson 3's question: Version 3's narrator insists everything is fine,
but the details argue otherwise — the notebook of stairwell times, the
protests ("I am never scared"), the plan that is never explained. A
narrator can be wrong about their own story. Your job is to notice.*

## Vignette B — Pacing: "The last hundred meters"

*One scene, written so the sentence length itself does work. Used in
Lesson 4. Read it aloud; the adult taps the table on each period.*

June saw the bus at the end of the block. The doors were closing.

She ran.

Her backpack slammed her spine with every step. The driver looked up.
Looked at her. Looked away. The doors sighed shut. The bus pulled from
the curb, and June stopped in the middle of the crosswalk with her
hands on her knees, breathing in great ragged pulls of exhaust and
cold air, watching the taillights shrink into traffic. It had been the
last bus. The next one came in forty minutes, and the sky was already
going the color of a bruise, and her phone was at three percent, and
Priya was not answering, and the wind found every gap in her jacket
like it had been given a map.

*Lesson 4's question: the first six sentences average just over five words;
the last sentence runs to 46 words — by far the longest in the scene.
What does the acceleration — then the long, piling sentence — do to
your body as a reader? Pacing is time control: the author decides how
fast the story's clock ticks.*

## Vignette C — Word choice: two kitchens

*The same kitchen described twice. Nothing in the room changes — only
the diction. Used in Lesson 5.*

**Version 1.**

The kitchen glowed. The soup pot muttered to itself on the stove, and
steam drew soft curtains across the window. June's grandmother moved
between the counter and the table the way she had moved for forty
years, unhurried, humming under her breath, setting down a chipped mug
that June had loved since she was small enough to hold it with both
hands.

**Version 2.**

The kitchen reeked. The soup pot sputtered on the stove, and grease
filmed the window. June's grandmother shuffled between the counter and
the table the way she had shuffled for forty years, tuneless, muttering
under her breath, setting down a cracked mug that June had been stuck
drinking from since she was small.

*Lesson 5's question: list the changed words in pairs
(glowed/reeked, muttered/sputtered, humming/muttering, chipped/cracked,
loved/stuck). For each pair, name the denotation both words share and
the connotation that differs. Then answer: which kitchen would you
rather eat in — and did the author tell you that, or only make you
feel it? That gap is cumulative impact: no single word decides the
tone, but twenty of them, all leaning the same way, do.*
