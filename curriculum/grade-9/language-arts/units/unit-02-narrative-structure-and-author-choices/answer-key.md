## Practice A — plot mapping

**A1.** "Night Train" — two timelines:

*Story order (as told):* exposition — the train, the conductor, the
compass in her pocket ("The conductor was already at her row before
Maya could decide whether to lie") → flashback: inciting incident —
the theft from Uncle Ray's desk ("she had taken the felt bag… and
walked out through the back door") → rising action — the conversation
with the old man (the wallet story raises the moral stakes) →
climax — "No… This is mine" (the decision to get off at the wrong
stop) → falling action — the platform, the old man's salute, the
train pulling away → resolution — "finally deciding what kind of
person was going to carry it home."

*Chronological order:* the theft (inciting incident) → the train ride
and conversation (rising action) → the decision (climax) → the
platform (falling action/resolution).

**A2.** The flashback begins at "That morning the house had smelled
like rain coming" and ends at "…the telling was starting to sound
like someone else talking." Before it, the reader knows only that
Maya is on a train with a ticket, a compass, damp hands, and a lie
she hasn't decided on — so the flashback lands harder: we already
suspect her, and the theft confirms the suspicion while supplying the
motive (the "promise").

**A3.** Monkey's Paw arc from excerpts: A = exposition (household,
chess, Morris's arrival); B = inciting incident (the paw changes
hands) bleeding into rising action (the wishes are discussed, the
warnings pile up); C = climax (the knocking, the third wish) and
resolution (the empty road). Missing stages: the full rising action
(Herbert's wish for £200, his death, the second wish — summarized in
the adult background note) and the falling action (the moments
between the wish and the silence). Accept any one-sentence
reconstruction that places Herbert's death in rising action and the
wish's aftermath in falling action.

**A4.** The conductor's arrival is not the inciting incident because
the inciting incident is the event that *sets the central conflict in
motion* — the theft, which creates the Maya-vs.-herself conflict the
whole story turns on. The conductor's arrival is rising action: it
applies pressure (will she be caught lying?) inside a conflict that
already exists.

## Practice B — foreshadowing hunt + reorder rewrite

**B1.** Accept any three well-located pairs; strongest set:
- "Her fingers left a damp crescent on the paper" (nervous hands) →
  payoff: she has stolen the compass and fears discovery.
- "the compass knocked once against her phone — a small, brass,
  certain sound" → payoff: the compass as moral weight that will not
  stay quiet.
- "trying to look like somebody going somewhere on purpose" / "She
  had been going somewhere on purpose, once" → payoff: she abandons
  her plan and gets off at the wrong stop.
- Also accept: the ceramic frog key (→ she lets herself in); the old
  man's wallet story (→ her decision to return the compass).

**B2.** Motif trace: (1) "the compass knocked once against her phone
— a small, brass, certain sound" — at first, a physical detail, the
object asserting itself; (2) "The compass knocked against her phone
again, small and brass and certain" — now moral pressure, interrupting
her conversation; (3) "the needle shivering and settling, shivering
and settling, until it chose north and held it" — the compass becomes
the story's thesis image: choosing a direction and holding it. By the
third appearance it means *moral orientation*, not navigation.

**B3.** Accept any coherent 8–12 sentence chronological rewrite.
Predicted effects — look for: (a) mystery *lost*: the reader knows
about the theft from sentence one, so the train scene's tension
("whether to lie") deflates; (b) sympathy *shifted*: we understand
her motive earlier, which may increase sympathy sooner but removes
the slow reveal; (c) final-line punch *weakened or relocated*: the
story now ends on the train (or wherever the writer puts the
platform), and the "deciding what kind of person" line must do
different work. Full credit for any specific gain/loss pair tied to
exact effects.

## Practice C — two openings, one story

**C1.** Chart — Opening 1: knows = June is in handcuffs, the field
trip is over before it began; wants = what did she do?; feels =
shock, tension, curiosity. Opening 2: knows = June prepared
carefully, her mother packed a granola bar; wants = what will happen
at the museum?; feels = warmth, calm, fondness.

**C2.** Mystery: Opening 1 — "The handcuffs were already cold on
June's wrists before she understood." Sympathy: Opening 2 — "laid
out her clothes, and set two alarms" or "tucked a granola bar into
the front pocket."

**C3.** Accept either verdict if defended with quotes. Strongest
mystery-story answer: Opening 1 — the story promises *what happened*,
and the cold handcuffs deliver the hook. Strongest character-study
answer: Opening 2 — knowing the careful girl first makes the
handcuffs land harder (dramatic irony: we know what she had to lose).
Full credit requires quoting both openings.

**C4.** Accept any 2–3 sentence flash-forward, e.g.: "Three weeks
later, June couldn't look at a granola bar without feeling sick. But
on the morning of the field trip, she was happy." It promises the
reader a *fall*: we read to learn how happiness curdles — a
different contract from mystery (what happened?) or sympathy (poor
June).

## Practice D — character-motivation charts

**D1–D2. Maya's chart (three motivations minimum):**

| Motivation | Evidence | Complication |
|---|---|---|
| Keep the compass — grandfather "promised" it | "He said, 'This'll be yours one day, navigator.'" | Uncle Ray: "The lawyer has the list. The compass stays with the desk." Taking it = stealing. |
| Honor the promise / be the navigator | "She told herself it was not stealing if it had been promised." | "…the telling was starting to sound like someone else talking" — she stops believing her own story. |
| Be the kind of person who does the right thing | The old man: "Some prices are too high even when nobody's charging." | She has already taken it; returning means confessing. |

**Morris's chart (two minimum):**

| Motivation | Evidence | Complication |
|---|---|---|
| Be rid of the paw / stop its mischief | Throws it on the fire; "Better let it burn"; "It has caused enough mischief already." | White wants it — and Morris explains how to use it anyway. |
| Warn them / clear his conscience | "I warn you of the consequences"; "don't blame me for what happens." | He still hands it over; the warning is a substitute for refusing. |

**D3.** Maya's hardest collision: the promise vs. the guilt — resolving
it means giving up either her grandfather's memory-as-she-wants-it or
her self-image as an honest person. Morris's: warning vs. handing
over — resolving it would require destroying the paw himself or
refusing White outright; he does neither, so he keeps the guilt and
loses the paw.

**D4.** Morris is **static** — the stronger answer. His "I don't
know," said twice, shows a man who has not resolved anything; he
begins and ends the exchange frightened, evasive, and unwilling to
take responsibility. ("Dynamic" argued from his shift from storyteller
to warning-giver earns partial credit, but nothing in him *changes*.)

## Practice E — two conflict analyses

**E1.** Maya's conflict is **both**. Internal: "She told herself it
was not stealing if it had been promised" vs. "the telling was
starting to sound like someone else talking." External: Uncle Ray
("The compass stays with the desk"), the conductor, the old man's
example. Resolved on the platform: the *external* decision (get off,
mail it back). Still open: the internal one — "finally deciding what
kind of person was going to carry it home" is a process, not a
verdict.

**E2.** The Whites: **external** (husband vs. wife; both vs. whatever
is at the door) braided with **internal** (his love for her vs. his
terror; her maternal drive vs. reality). Starkest lines: "It's my
boy; it's Herbert!" (Mrs. White) vs. "For God's sake don't let it in"
(Mr. White). The horror comes from the *interaction*: two fully
sympathetic motives collide, so the reader dreads both outcomes —
the door opening and the door staying shut.

**E3.** The old man is Maya's **foil**: he found a wallet "fat with
cash" on this very line and "turned it in at the next station"; Maya
took the compass and is carrying it away. His line — "Because I'd
have known, every time I spent a dollar of it, exactly what kind of
man I was. Some prices are too high even when nobody's charging." —
is the standard Maya answers when she gets off at the wrong stop.
His presence converts the reader's judgment from *will she get
caught?* to *what kind of person will she be?*

## Practice F — character-arc timelines

**F1. Maya:** (a) the study — "it was not stealing if it had been
promised" (rationalization intact); (b) the train — "the telling was
starting to sound like someone else talking" (rationalization
cracking); (c) the platform — "finally deciding what kind of person
was going to carry it home" (rationalization abandoned, choice made).
**Verdict: dynamic.** What changed is her *understanding* first and
her *situation* second — she stops defending the theft to herself
before she acts to undo it.

**F2. Herbert:** (a) chess — teasing, "cleverly" needling his father;
(b) "Well, why don't you have three, sir?" — still joking, treating
the paw as a lark; (c) absent in Excerpt C — dead, the cost of the
joke. **Verdict: static.** Dramatic things happen *to* Herbert, but
he exhibits no internal change; his function is catalytic. (A
learner who argues "we can't judge — too little text" shows good
critical caution; grant near-full credit if the reasoning is explicit.)

**F3. Mrs. White:** (a) "knitting placidly by the fire" — the calm
center of the household; (b) "persisted" about the wishes — curiosity
overriding Morris's visible distress; (c) "struggling mechanically,"
"It's Herbert!", "a long loud wail of disappointment and misery" —
grief has unmade her composure. **Verdict: dynamic** — the unit's
clearest arc in the excerpts. Her understanding shatters and her
nature (placid → frantic) transforms.

**F4.** Most changed: Mrs. White. But "most changed" ≠ "most
important": Mr. White makes the wishes that drive the entire plot
while changing less than his wife does. Importance is a question of
*function* (who moves the plot, who carries the theme), not of arc
size. Full credit for any defended distinction between change and
importance with quotes.

## Practice G — POV rewrite + reliability audit

**G1–G2.** Score the rewrites on: (1) events preserved — no new plot;
(2) G1 stays inside the old man's head (his wallet memory, his
reading of Maya); (3) G2 (omniscient) contains **two** marked spots
of impossible knowledge — e.g., the conductor's thoughts three cars
away, or the old man's unspoken memory *and* Maya's unspoken thought
in the same passage. Sample omniscient beats: "The conductor, two
cars back, was thinking about his dinner" / "The old man did not say
that the wallet had belonged to a man who never thanked him."

**G3. Reliability audit — Vignette A, Version 3** (four required;
accept any four well-argued):

| Narrator's claim | Contradicting detail | What the detail suggests |
|---|---|---|
| "waiting for Priya" / everything is fine | "writing down the times in my notebook like a scientist" — three days running | Surveillance, not casual waiting; obsession or a stakeout |
| "I am never scared in stairwells" | The denial itself, unprompted | Protest-too-much; fear is exactly what's present |
| "The plan was excellent. The plan had no flaws." | Repetition as self-persuasion; the plan is never explained | Doubt; the narrator is convincing themselves |
| "waiting for Priya" | "Priya says a lot of things" | Priya may not be coming; the stated reason is shaky |
| "exactly as heavy as it needed to be" | Evasion about the backpack's contents | Something is being hidden — from the reader, or from the narrator |

**G4.** Gained by the old man's first-person: his backstory
authority, dramatic irony (the reader knows what Maya did; he only
suspects), his judgment as the story's moral center. Lost: Maya's
interiority — "the telling was starting to sound like someone else
talking" only works from inside her head; the final line's ambiguity
("what kind of person") would flatten into his verdict instead of her
becoming. Full credit for one specific scene whose meaning changes
(e.g., the wallet story becomes exposition rather than pressure).

## Practice H — pacing analysis + flashback placement

**H1. Sentence map — Vignette B** (word counts):

| # | Words | # | Words |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 9 | 7 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 | 8 | 4 |
| 3 | 2 | 9 | 37 |
| 4 | 8 | 10 | 6 |
| 5 | 4 | 11 | 46 |
| 6 | 3 | | |

Effect: sentences 1–8 are staccato — discrete panicked perceptions,
a sprint the reader's body enacts (short sentences = fast story
clock). Sentences 9 and 11 pile everything up at once: the aftermath
arrives as a single breathless heap — missed bus, bruise-colored sky,
3% phone, no Priya, the wind. The acceleration-then-pileup makes the
reader *feel* effort followed by defeat. (Note: the two long
sentences are 37 and 46 words — "very long" relative to the 2–9 word
run before them; the contrast, not the absolute count, does the work.)

**H2.** Accept any precisely located alternative placement with a
gained/lost argument. The author's placement serves **mystery** first
(what did she do? — the train scene makes us ask) and **sympathy**
second (the flashback supplies the motive before we judge her).
Strongest verdict: the author's placement is right for a story about
*judgment* — we suspect her, then understand her, then watch her
judge herself. A chronological telling would trade mystery for
context and land softer.

**H3. Sounds in Excerpt C, in order:** a loud knock → "another
knock, and another" → the chain rattling back → the bottom bolt drawn
"slowly and stiffly" → the scraping chair → the creaking bolt coming
"slowly back" → the knocking ceasing "suddenly." Why scarier unheard:
the reader's imagination builds a worse thing than any description
could supply; sound implies *proximity* without giving the mind an
image to dismiss or domesticate; each sound is a countdown beat, and
a countdown needs no monster — only an arrival.

**H4.** Adult approves the casebook claim (one sentence, arguable,
about an author choice — see [project.md](project.md)).

## Practice I — connotation sets + tone revision

**I1. Pair table** (six minimum):

| V1 word | Shared denotation | V2 word | Connotation difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| glowed | gave off light/quality | reeked | foul smell; disgust |
| muttered | low indistinct sound | sputtered | erratic, failing, angry spitting |
| humming | making music under breath | muttering | complaining, tuneless |
| unhurried | slow pace | shuffled | aged, reluctant, worn down |
| chipped | damaged edge | cracked | broken, ruined |
| loved | felt affection for | stuck (with) | resented obligation |

Weakest single swap: "chipped/cracked" or "unhurried/shuffled" — each
still conveys age/wear on its own. What this proves: **tone is
cumulative** — no one word carries it; twenty words leaning the same
way do. Cover five of six pairs and the tone wobbles but survives;
cover all six and it flips.

**I2. Morris's tags:** "his blotchy face whitened" — fear made
physical, contradicting his casual soldier talk; "his glass tapped
against his strong teeth" — the body betraying nerves ("strong"
teeth vs. tapping = the brave soldier is frightened); "said the
soldier, solemnly" — the joking fireside tale is over, ritual
gravity; "said his friend, doggedly" — stubbornness, a man who will
not be moved off his warning. Pile-up effect: dread gathers around
Morris before anyone names the danger — the reader learns to fear
the paw through his body before understanding why.

**I3. Sample revisions** (accept any consistent pair with a change log):
- *Warm:* "The corridor glowed, and the lamps hummed. A door stood
  open at the far end, spilling light. Someone had left a chair
  waiting in the middle of the floor. June wandered toward the door."
  (hallway→corridor, long→—, lights were on→glowed/hummed,
  stood open→stood open spilling light, left→waiting, walked→wandered)
- *Menacing:* "The hallway stretched, and the lights buzzed. A door
  gaped at the far end. Someone had left a chair blocking the middle
  of the floor. June edged toward the door."
  (long→stretched, on→buzzed, stood open→gaped, left→blocking,
  walked→edged)
- Which is harder: accept either with reasoning — menacing is often
  easier (English has a deep bench of threat words); warm is harder
  because warmth without sentimentality takes restraint.

**I4.** "Fusillade" is a battlefield term (simultaneous gunfire). It
imports *violence and coordination* — the knocks arrive like a
volley, an attack, not a visitor. "Many knocks" is mere quantity;
"fusillade" makes the house sound besieged and the knocker sound
inhuman in its persistence.

## Quiz — week-09 (30 pts)

**1. (2)** Exposition → inciting incident → rising action → climax →
falling action → resolution. (1 pt for 4–5 in order; 0.5 for all six
unordered.)

**2. (3)** The choice is ***in medias res*** (opening in the middle
of the action). Gain: immediate mystery/tension — the reader must
read on to learn what June did. Loss: context and sympathy arrive
late — we don't know June yet, so we feel shock before we feel for
her. (1.5 pts per half; the label alone earns 1.)

**3. (2)** **Exposition** — in story order, the train scene introduces
Maya, the situation, and the object (the compass) before we know the
backstory. (Accept "rising action" for half credit *only* if defended
as pressure-building toward the decision — but exposition is the
stronger label for where the scene sits in the telling.)

**4. (3)** **Disagree.** A flashback isn't decoration or mere
backstory — it's *re-timing for effect*. The Night Train flashback
is placed after the train scene so the reader first wonders what Maya
did (mystery), then learns why (the "promise"), which recolors every
detail already seen (the damp ticket, the knocking compass). Backstory
explains; a flashback *recontextualizes*.

**5. (3)** A **foil** is a character whose contrasting choices or
qualities throw the protagonist's into relief. In "Night Train" the
old man is Maya's foil: he found a wallet "fat with cash" and "turned
it in at the next station," while Maya took the compass — his choice
reveals the choice in front of her. (1 pt definition, 1 pt
identification, 1 pt the revelation.)

**6. (2)** Any two: (a) wants to be rid of the paw / believes it is
dangerous ("Better let it burn," "It has caused enough mischief
already"); (b) wants to hand off responsibility while keeping his
conscience clean ("don't blame me for what happens" + giving the
instructions anyway); (c) wants to warn vs. wants to be rid of the
burden of knowing alone. 1 pt each.

**7. (3)** **Dynamic** is the stronger position: "knitting placidly
by the fire" (A) vs. "struggling mechanically… 'It's Herbert!'" and
"a long loud wail of disappointment and misery" (C) — grief unmakes
her composure across the excerpts. Requires **two** quoted details
from **different** excerpts. (A well-argued "static — she is
consistently maternal; only circumstances change" earns 2/3; it
misreads transformation as situation, but the reasoning is real.)

**8. (2)** First person — cannot directly show other characters'
unspoken thoughts (limited to the narrator's knowledge). Third-person
limited — cannot show what the focal character doesn't perceive or
know. Third-person omniscient — cannot easily withhold interiority,
so surprise from hidden motives is harder to earn (its cost is the
loss of mystery). Accept any reasonable "cannot" per POV.

**9. (4)** Any two: the stairwell-times notebook (surveillance, not
casual waiting); "I am never scared" (unprompted denial = fear);
"The plan was excellent… no flaws" (repetition = self-persuasion,
doubt); "Priya says a lot of things" (Priya may not be coming);
"exactly as heavy as it needed to be" (evasion about the backpack).
2 pts per detail+suggestion.

**10. (3)** The short sentences enact the sprint — discrete, panicked
perceptions, a fast story clock the reader's body feels. The long
final sentence piles every defeat at once (last bus, bruise sky, 3%
phone, no Priya, the wind) — the aftermath arriving as one
breathless heap. The pattern *is* the meaning: effort, then collapse.

**11. (3)** Withholding is scarier than showing because the reader's
imagination builds a worse thing than any description could fix in
place; sound implies proximity (it's *at the door*) without giving
the mind an image to dismiss; and a shown monster can disappoint —
an unshown one cannot. Full credit for any two of these mechanisms
tied to the excerpt's sounds.

## Unit assessment (50 pts)

### Task 1 — "The Blue Envelope" (20 pts)

**a. (5)** The **inciting incident** is June finding and opening the
blue envelope (paragraphs 1–2 — the letter enters her hands and her
life). Paragraph 1 sits in **exposition** in story order: we meet
June, the library, the object, before we know the backstory. The *in
medias res* opening gains immediate intrigue — "The envelope was
already open in June's hands before she remembered deciding to open
it" makes the reader ask *whose letter? why her throat?* — versus a
chronological start (paragraph 2's shelving), which would build
context first but arrive at the hook a page later. Loss: we don't yet
know June or the sparrows, so sympathy lags behind curiosity.

**b. (5)** **Third-person limited**, staying close to June: we get
her interiority ("Her throat did something complicated"; "'Tired,'
which was true and was also the word she used when something else was
true") but no one else's. It lets the reader know June's thoughts
and rationalizations; it withholds the letter's intended recipient,
the rest of the letter's contents, and what Mrs. Abernathy actually
thinks ("You're quiet tonight, June" — we never learn if she
suspects).

**c. (5)** **Connotation.** Denotatively a held breath is just paused
breathing; connotatively it means suspense, anticipation, something
awaiting release — so the envelope feels tense, expectant, *alive*.
Tone: hushed and charged. One more connotative choice (any one with
explanation): "accusing nothing, asking nothing" (the envelope
personified as a silent moral presence); "with the care of someone
defusing something" (the letter as explosive/dangerous); "The lie sat
in her pocket… light as a sparrow and twice as alive" (the lie as a
living thing she now carries).

**d. (5)** Two motivations with evidence: (1) curiosity / recognition
— "For the girl who feeds the sparrows when she thinks no one is
watching" — "Nobody watched her feed the sparrows. That was the
entire point" (the letter seems *meant for her*); (2) duty/honesty —
"you turned them in, you didn't read them" / "I'll turn it in
tomorrow." **Curiosity wins**: she pockets the envelope ("Lost and
found, she told herself"). Cost: she now carries a lie — "The lie
sat in her pocket all the way home, light as a sparrow and twice as
alive" — and the reader understands "tomorrow" is a rationalization.

## Project notes — scoring the casebook (40 pts)

Annotated level guidance for [project.md](project.md)'s rubric:

- **Choice identification:** Excellent names choices with technical
  precision ("Jacobs stages the climax offstage through sound alone";
  "the 'Night Train' author withholds the inciting incident until a
  mid-story flashback"). Proficient names them plainly ("the ending
  you don't see"). Cap at Developing for "good description" / "nice
  words."
- **Evidence:** Excellent quotes are *load-bearing* — remove the
  quote and the claim collapses. Watch for decorative quoting (a
  quote restated, not used).
- **Mechanism:** Excellent shows the chain (choice → reader
  experience → effect), e.g.: "Because the knocks are only heard,
  the reader must imagine the knocker; because each reader imagines
  their own worst thing, the scene is scarier than any single
  description could be." Cap at Proficient for straw-man skeptics
  ("some might disagree, but they're wrong").
- **Rewrite + verdict:** Excellent changes *exactly one* choice and
  can name what the change cost. The verdict must argue
  break-vs.-wobble: "without the offstage climax the story wobbles
  but survives; without the paw's rules it breaks" — with reasons.

## Lesson 01

**Readiness check:** the events are identical; what changed is the
*order of telling* — starting with "The bus pulled away" leads with
the outcome (curiosity: *how did we get here?*), starting with "The
alarm didn't go off" leads with the cause (sympathy: *poor you*).

**Guided practice:**
1. Paragraph labels (accept reasonable variation): ¶1–2 (train,
   conductor, damp ticket) — exposition; ¶3 (flashback begins, "That
   morning…") through the theft — inciting incident inside a
   flashback; old-man conversation — rising action; "No… This is
   mine" — climax; platform/salute — falling action; final line —
   resolution.
2. Chronological list (8–10 events): grandfather dies → compass sits
   in desk 11 months → Maya hears the "promise" memory → Uncle Ray
   refuses → Maya takes the compass → train ride → conductor checks
   ticket → old man's wallet story → Maya decides → platform/salute/
   final reflection. Inciting incident: the theft. Defense: it is the
   event that creates the central conflict (keep vs. return); without
   it there is no story.
3. Three details gaining meaning: the damp crescent (nerves → guilt);
   the knocking compass (strange object → stolen moral weight);
   "going somewhere on purpose, once" (odd phrasing → she abandons
   her plan).
4. Accept any three-sentence loss analysis naming curiosity, tension,
   or sympathy with specifics.

**Independent practice:**
1. Plot arc: peak = "No… This is mine" (the decision). Why not the
   theft: the theft *starts* the conflict; the decision *resolves*
   it — climax is the turning point, not the most dramatic event.
2. "The telling was starting to sound like someone else talking"
   foreshadows the platform decision: her rationalization is
   collapsing, so the reader expects her to stop defending the theft.
3. Accept either verdict if argued with lines: the wallet story hits
   harder *because* of the in-medias-res opening — we already suspect
   Maya, so the old man's standard lands as personal pressure, not
   abstract advice. Chronological telling would make it a moral
   lecture before we care.
4. Reading log: any genuine in-medias-res example (many films: *Star
   Wars* opens mid-rebellion; *The Odyssey* opens with Telemachus).

**Application:** accept any three orders with predicted first-feelings;
the author's order (in medias res → flashback → present climax)
should be judged on mystery-first vs. sympathy-first grounds.

**Exit check:** inciting incident = the event that breaks the normal
world and starts the central conflict; here, the theft of the
compass. It appears *later* in the telling (flashback), not first —
because opening on the train creates the question ("why is she
nervous?") that the flashback answers.

## Lesson 02

**Readiness check:** any genuine example of surprising behavior +
hidden motive (e.g., "my usually stingy uncle tipped hugely — I
later learned he'd just gotten good news").

**Guided practice:**
1. Mr. White's chart — toward the paw: curiosity ("What was that you
   started telling me… about a monkey's paw"), desire ("Wish to be
   an emperor, father" — via Herbert; "eyeing him keenly"); away
   from it: Morris's visible fear ("his blotchy face whitened"),
   the warnings ("don't blame me for what happens"). Accept any
   quoted pair showing the pull both ways.
2. "The third was for death" dropped casually tells us Morris has
   *seen* a wish's price paid in full — the understatement is the
   evidence of trauma: a man who describes death as a transaction
   detail has stopped being able to describe it any other way.
3. Accept any of the three lines with a want-vs.-reveal paragraph,
   e.g. "Nothing" (wants him to think she's an ordinary passenger;
   her hand flying to her coat reveals she's hiding something).

**Independent practice:**
1. The old man is **static** — he arrives wise and leaves wise; his
   wallet story is already resolved. Quotes: "I turned it in at the
   next station" / "Some prices are too high even when nobody's
   charging." A static character matters as a *measure* — without
   him, Maya's choice has no visible standard.
2. Same: both torn between keeping and relinquishing a powerful
   object. Different: Maya's conflict is moral/internal (guilt vs.
   loyalty) while Morris's is fear vs. responsibility-shirking — or:
   Maya resolves hers (dynamic), Morris does not (static). Accept
   any quoted same/different pair.
3. Lying to another: "Nothing." (to the old man). Lying to herself:
   "it was not stealing if it had been promised." Self-lies are
   harder to spot because the narration reports them in her own
   reasonable voice; other-lies come with body-language tells.
4. Reading log: any two quoted conflicting motives.

**Application:** the barely-holding-together performance matches
better — "his blotchy face whitened," "his glass tapped against his
strong teeth," the doubled "I don't know" all signal a man
performing calm over panic. The calm performance would flatten the
dread the scene needs.

**Exit check:** Maya's two strongest: loyalty to the "promise"
("This'll be yours one day, navigator") vs. guilt/the honest choice
("the telling was starting to sound like someone else talking" /
the old man's "Some prices are too high…"). Her arc: from
rationalizing the theft to deciding what kind of person she'll be.

## Lesson 03

**Readiness check:** any genuine difference (position, loyalty,
attention) + the reason.

**Guided practice:**
1. Accept any sentence rewritten both ways with a two-sentence
   distance observation (e.g., "I told myself…" → "She told
   herself…": same facts, but "she" lets the reader *watch* the
   self-deception instead of *performing* it).
2. Fourth betrayal detail (beyond the lesson's three): "Priya says a
   lot of things" (undermines the stated reason for waiting);
   "exactly as heavy as it needed to be" (evasion about the
   backpack); "Nobody uses these stairs after four" (how would a
   casual waiter know that?).
3. First-person Maya: gained — rawer guilt, direct access to the
   rationalization; lost — the "telling was starting to sound like
   someone else talking" split (first person can't easily report
   its own voice as alien). The wallet scene would change most: we'd
   lose the narrator's cool observation of her body language.

**Independent practice:**
1. (Folded into Practice G — see Practice G key.)
2. Maya as narrator of her own life: claim "It was not stealing";
   contradicting details: the damp ticket, the folded hands, "the
   telling was starting to sound like someone else talking." Verdict:
   *conflicted, not unreliable* — the stronger answer, because the
   narration reports her self-deception honestly instead of hiding
   it from the reader. (A defended "unreliable" earns near-full
   credit; the distinction is the point.)
3. Close distance: "which was the kind of true that needs saying
   twice" (inside the exact shape of self-deception — intimacy).
   Far distance: "The stairwell door clicked shut behind June"
   (external report — authority, no interior). Effect: close =
   intimacy/complicity; far = cool observation.
4. Reading log: any POV identification with a quoted proof line.

**Application:** paragraph 1 — the English parlor's POV frames the
story; the fakir never speaks; his belief arrives via Morris's
fireside warning. Paragraph 2 — accept any concrete rewrite
showing what changes (e.g., the paw scene framed by the fakir's
warning as doctrine rather than exotic tale: the reader would know
the *why* of the spell but lose the Whites' skeptical distance that
makes the horror land).

**Exit check:** (a) narrative distance = how deep inside a
character's inner life the narrator stands; (b) any Version 3
detail + contradicted claim (e.g., the notebook vs. "casual
waiting"); (c) the English parlor's (Morris/the Whites'); the fakir
never speaks.

## Lesson 04

**Readiness check:** any honest answer naming which sentences sped
the reader up (the fragments) and which forced a slowdown (the
46-word pile-up).

**Guided practice:**
1. Flashback begins: "That morning the house had smelled like rain
   coming." Ends: "…the telling was starting to sound like someone
   else talking." Transition signals: the tense/time-marker shift
   ("That morning") opening it; the return to the train present
   ("Across the aisle, an old man was unwrapping a sandwich") closing
   it.
2. Accept any 3–4 sentence chronological opening + two concrete lost
   effects (e.g., loses the damp-ticket mystery; loses the compass
   as unexplained object).
3. Accept any three labeled sentences with per-moment effects (e.g.,
   "There was another knock, and another" — short: the sentence
   *is* the knocking, two beats; the bolt sentence — long: stretches
   the waiting; "The knocking ceased suddenly" — short: abrupt
   relief/dread).

**Independent practice:**
1. (Practice H — see Practice H key.)
2. Conductor scene map: sentences run mostly medium-long with short
   bursts ("Ticket," he said. / "She ran"—style beats); the author
   speeds up at the inspection moments (short dialogue, quick
   beats) and slows for Maya's interiority. Accept any careful map
   tied to story events.
3. Reading log: any scene (moment-by-moment, dialogue/sensory) vs.
   summary (time compressed) pair with a why-it-matters explanation.

**Application:** the five-short-sentence version loses the pile-up —
the defeats arrive as a list instead of a heap, so the reader
processes them one by one rather than feeling them all at once. It
gains clarity and punch per fact. An author wants the short version
when each fact deserves its own beat (e.g., a countdown, a set of
instructions).

**Exit check:** any one pacing choice + exact words + named effect on
speed or feeling.

## Lesson 05

**Readiness check:** any *house/home* association pairs (e.g., house →
building, neutral; home → warmth, belonging); the difference lives
in the word's connotation, not the building.

**Guided practice:**
1. Two more words (any two): *grave* (denotation: serious; adds
   funeral weight); *hush* (denotation: silence; adds reverent
   fear); *presumptuous* (denotation: overstepping; adds generational
   judgment — "middle age… regard presumptuous youth"); *keenly*
   (denotation: sharply; adds predatory interest); *mischief*
   (denotation: trouble; understatement that minimizes horror —
   itself a tonal choice).
2. Accept any three rewrites with the tonal words underlined (e.g.,
   warm: "The dog woofed happily at the mail carrier"; hostile:
   "The dog snarled at the mail carrier").
3. Practice I started: any five-word same-lean set (e.g., for
   "dread": *lurking, oppressive, hollow, brittle, smothering*).

**Independent practice:**
1. (Practice I — see Practice I key.)
2. *moved/shuffled*: shared denotation — changed location at walking
   pace. *Moved* (V1) connotes purpose, ease, familiarity; *shuffled*
   (V2) connotes age, reluctance, worn-downness. It feeds V2's
   pile-up: every verb in V2 drags.
3. Reading log: any five same-lean words + named tone; the
   counter-word (if any) creates texture — a single warm word in a
   cold paragraph can signal irony, memory, or loss.

**Application:** accept any two rewrites (trustworthy vs.
threatening) with unchanged facts; the blind test should succeed —
if not, the pile-up needs more leaning words.

**Exit check:** any pair + shared denotation + differing connotation;
tone needs a pile-up because one word can be dismissed as a
fluke — twenty leaning words create a pattern the reader feels as
atmosphere, not accident.

## Lesson 06

**Readiness check:** any honest seed sentence + one reason (this
becomes thesis raw material; no wrong answer).

**Guided practice:**
1. (Practice J begun — see Practice J key.) Working thesis must name
   two *choices* (not topics) and be arguable.
2. Skeptic drill: the thesis survives when every "so what?" is
   answered with words on the page, not with restated claims.
3. Block vs. point-by-point with a two-sentence justification; the
   other structure serves better when the thesis's logic flips
   (e.g., point-by-point wins for same-choice comparisons; block
   wins when each text needs full context first).

**Independent practice:**
1. (Practice J skeletons — see Practice J key.)
2. Body paragraph check: claim → short attributed quote →
   explanation (longest part). If the explanation isn't the longest
   part, the paragraph is quoting, not analyzing.
3. Skeptic paragraph: strongest objection + evidence-based answer.
   If unanswerable, the thesis — not the paragraph — needs work.
4. Quote audit: every quote short, attributed, explained; lone
   quotes cut or completed.

**Application:** timed rehearsal — the cost is usually completeness
(transitions, second quote); the plan gives structure under
pressure (thesis + skeletons = no blank-page panic). Two honest
sentences earn full credit.

**Exit check:** working thesis + chosen structure + one-sentence
justification tying structure to thesis logic.
