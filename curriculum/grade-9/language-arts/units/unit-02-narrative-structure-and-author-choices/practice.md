# Practice Tasks — Unit 02 (Grade 9 Language Arts)

its session, goal, and success criterion. Solutions and scoring notes

adult reviews written practice the same day it is done.

## Practice A — Session 2: plot mapping

**Goal:** I can separate story order from chronological order and label
each stage of a plot arc.
**Success:** two completed plot maps with every stage labeled and
located by paragraph or line.

1. Draw two parallel timelines for "Night Train." On the top line,
   place the events in **story order** (the order the author tells
   them); on the bottom line, the same events in **chronological
   order**. Label each event with one of: exposition, inciting
   incident, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution. (Hint:
   the theft is the inciting incident, but it is not told first —
   where does it sit on each line?)
2. Mark on the story-order line exactly where the flashback begins and
   ends. In one sentence, state what the reader knows *before* the
   flashback that makes the flashback land harder.
3. Sketch a plot arc (rising line to a peak, falling line after) for
   the three "Monkey's Paw" excerpts taken together (A → B → C).
   Place each excerpt on the arc and label which plot stage each
   excerpt mostly represents. Then answer: which plot stages are
   *missing* from the excerpts, and what must happen in them (one
   sentence each — you know the story's shape even from excerpts)?
4. **Error analysis:** a classmate's map labels the train conductor's
   arrival as the inciting incident of "Night Train." Explain in 2–3
   sentences why that label is wrong and which stage the conductor's
   arrival actually belongs to.

## Practice B — Session 3: foreshadowing hunt + reorder rewrite

**Goal:** I can find planted details and explain how changing event
order changes effect.
**Success:** three foreshadowing pairs identified with line references;
one rewritten scene with a predicted-effect paragraph.

1. Find **three** details planted early in "Night Train" that pay off
   later (foreshadowing). For each, give: the early detail (quoted or
   located), the later payoff (quoted or located), and one sentence on
   what the early detail makes the reader feel *before* they know why.
2. One planted detail is a **motif** (it repeats and gathers meaning):
   the compass knocking in her pocket / the needle "shivering and
   settling." Trace its three appearances. What does the compass
   *mean* by the third appearance that it did not mean at the first?
3. **Rewrite:** retell the story's opening as if it began
   chronologically — start with "That morning the house had smelled
   like rain coming" and write the train scene as the ending, in
   8–12 sentences. Then write a paragraph predicting how the
   chronological version changes (a) mystery, (b) sympathy for Maya,
   and (c) the punch of the final line. Be specific: which exact
   effect is gained and which is lost?

## Practice C — Session 5: two openings, one story

**Goal:** I can compare two structural choices for the same material
and argue which serves the story better.
**Success:** a comparison chart plus a verdict paragraph with quoted
evidence from both openings.

Read the two original openings below — same character, same situation,
different author choices.

**Opening 1 (in medias res):**
> The handcuffs were already cold on June's wrists before she
> understood that the field trip was over.

**Opening 2 (chronological exposition):**
> June had been looking forward to the museum field trip for three
> weeks. She had packed her bag the night before, laid out her
> clothes, and set two alarms. Her mother had signed the permission
> slip in blue ink and tucked a granola bar into the front pocket.

1. Complete a chart with three rows — *What the reader knows after the
   first two sentences*, *What the reader wants to know*, *What the
   reader feels* — and one column per opening.
2. Which opening creates more **mystery**? Which creates more
   **sympathy**? Quote one phrase from each opening that does the
   work you are naming.
3. **Verdict paragraph:** if the story is a mystery about *what June
   did*, which opening serves it better, and why? If the story is
   about *why a careful girl made one careless choice*, does your
   answer change? (There is no single right answer; there is a
   well-defended one. Your paragraph must quote both openings.)
4. Write a third opening — a **flash-forward** (one image from the
   story's ending, then "Three weeks earlier…") — in 2–3 sentences.
   What does the flash-forward version promise the reader that the
   other two do not?

## Practice D — Session 7: character-motivation charts

**Goal:** I can chart a complex character's conflicting motivations
with quoted evidence.
**Success:** two completed charts; each motivation has at least one
quote and a one-sentence explanation of the conflict it creates.

Copy this chart twice — once for **Maya** ("Night Train"), once for
**Sergeant-Major Morris** ("Monkey's Paw," Excerpt B):

| What the character wants (motivation) | Evidence (quote + location) | What blocks or complicates it |
|---|---|---|
| | | |
| | | |

1. For Maya, chart **at least three** motivations (include the
   grandfather's "promise" and at least one that pulls the opposite
   way).
2. For Morris, chart **at least two** (use "I don't know," said twice;
   throwing the paw on the fire; warning White "solemnly" while handing
   the instructions over anyway).
3. Under each chart, write 2–3 sentences: which two motivations collide
   hardest, and what would the character have to give up to resolve
   the collision?
4. **Explain your reasoning:** is Morris a *static* or *dynamic*
   character in Excerpt B? Defend your answer with one quote. (Note:
   "dynamic" does not mean "active" — it means the character changes.)

## Practice E — Session 8: two conflict analyses

**Goal:** I can distinguish internal from external conflict and show
how conflict reveals character.
**Success:** two analyses, each naming the conflict type, quoting two
pieces of evidence, and connecting the conflict to a character change
or revelation.

1. **Maya's central conflict:** name it as internal, external, or both.
   Quote two passages — one showing the conflict inside her, one
   showing it between her and another person or circumstance. Then
   explain: which conflict is resolved on the platform, and which one
   is still open in the final paragraph? (Hint: "finally deciding
   what kind of person was going to carry it home.")
2. **The Whites' conflict in Excerpt C:** Mr. White physically holds
   his wife back from the door. Name the conflict type(s). Quote the
   two lines that show each character's motivation most starkly
   ("It's my boy; it's Herbert!" vs. "For God's sake don't let it
   in"). Explain how their *interaction* — not either character
   alone — creates the scene's horror.
3. **Foil paragraph:** explain how the old man on the train functions
   as a **foil** to Maya. Name the choice he made (the wallet), the
   choice Maya is facing, and the exact line of his that Maya seems to
   be answering when she gets off at the wrong stop. A foil is not an
   opposite for decoration — say what his presence *does* to the
   reader's judgment of Maya.

## Practice F — Session 10: character-arc timelines

**Goal:** I can trace a character across a text and judge static vs.
dynamic with evidence.
**Success:** three timelines with at least three dated points each and
a defended static/dynamic verdict per character.

1. Build a timeline for **Maya** with at least three points: (a) the
   study, (b) the train conversation, (c) the platform. At each
   point, note what she believes about the compass ("not stealing if
   it had been promised" → ? → ?). Verdict: static or dynamic? What
   *exactly* changed — her situation, her understanding, or both?
2. Build a timeline for **Herbert White** across Excerpts A–C (the
   chess game → "why don't you have three, sir?" → his absence in
   Excerpt C). Verdict with evidence.
3. Build a timeline for **Mrs. White** across Excerpts A–C (knitting
   "placidly" → "persisted" about the wishes → the knocking scene).
   Verdict with evidence. (This is the hardest one — argue it
   carefully.)
4. **Explain your reasoning:** which character changed the *most*,
   and does "most changed" always mean "most important"? One
   paragraph, with quotes.

## Practice G — Session 13: POV rewrite + reliability audit

**Goal:** I can rewrite a scene in a different point of view and audit
a narrator's reliability.
**Success:** two rewrites of the same scene (8–12 sentences each) plus
a labeled audit of the unreliable narrator.

1. Take the train-conversation scene from "Night Train" (the old man
   and the sandwich). Rewrite it in **first person as the old man**
   narrating. You must keep every event the same — only the
   *teller* changes. (8–12 sentences.)
2. Now rewrite the same scene in **third-person omniscient** — a
   narrator who can report what *both* Maya and the old man are
   thinking. Mark the two places where your narrator knows something
   neither character could know.
3. **Reliability audit:** return to Vignette A, Version 3 (the
   unreliable stairwell narrator). List **four** details that
   contradict the narrator's claim that everything is fine and the
   plan is excellent. For each, write what the detail suggests vs.
   what the narrator claims — two columns.
4. One paragraph: the original "Night Train" is third-person limited,
   staying close to Maya. What would be *gained* and what would be
   *lost* if it were told by the old man in first person instead?
   Name one specific scene whose meaning would change.

## Practice H — Session 14: pacing analysis + flashback placement

**Goal:** I can show how sentence length and scene placement control a
story's clock.
**Success:** a sentence-length map of Vignette B, a flashback analysis
with a defended alternative, and a project topic choice.

1. **Sentence map:** copy out Vignette B ("The last hundred meters")
   and number its sentences. Record each sentence's word count in a
   two-column table (sentence # → words). Then write a paragraph:
   where does the scene accelerate, where does it stall, and what is
   the *effect* of the 46-word final sentence after the run of short ones?
   (Read it aloud first — the adult taps each period.)
2. **Flashback placement:** "Night Train" places the theft flashback
   *after* the train opening. Propose an alternative placement (one
   paragraph earlier or later — be exact: "after the sentence…")
   and argue in 3–4 sentences what your placement gains and loses
   versus the author's. Then give the final verdict: was the author's
   placement the right call? (Defend with the word *mystery* or the
   word *sympathy* — pick the one the placement actually serves.)
3. **Offstage analysis:** in "Monkey's Paw" Excerpt C, Jacobs never
   shows what is at the door. List the **sounds** in the excerpt in
   order (knock, chain, bolt, scraping chair…). In 2–3 sentences,
   explain why *hearing* the climax is more frightening than *seeing*
   it would be — what does the reader's imagination do that a
   description could not?
4. **Project launch:** choose your Author's Choices Casebook topic
   (see [project.md](project.md)) and write the one-sentence claim
   you will defend. The adult approves it before session 16.

## Practice I — Session 17: connotation sets + tone revision

**Goal:** I can analyze cumulative word choice and revise for a target
tone.
**Success:** two connotation analyses plus one tone revision with a
change log.

1. From Vignette C, build the full pair table (at least **six**
   pairs: glowed/reeked, muttered/sputtered, humming/muttering,
   chipped/cracked, loved/stuck, unhurried/shuffled …). Columns:
   *Version 1 word → shared denotation → Version 2 word →
   connotation difference*. Then answer: which *single* pair, if
   swapped alone, would change its sentence's feeling least — and what
   does that prove about **cumulative** impact?
2. From "Monkey's Paw" Excerpt B, collect **four** dialogue tags or
   body-language phrases (e.g., "his blotchy face whitened," "his
   glass tapped against his strong teeth," "said the soldier,
   solemnly," "said his friend, doggedly"). For each: what does the
   tag *add* that the spoken words alone do not? What tone does the
   pile-up create around Morris?
3. **Tone revision:** take this neutral paragraph and revise it
   **twice** — once toward a warm tone, once toward a menacing tone —
   changing *only* word choice (no new events):
   > The hallway was long and the lights were on. A door stood open
   > at the far end. Someone had left a chair in the middle of the
   > floor. June walked toward the door.
   Keep a change log: list every word you swapped, in pairs
   (neutral → warm / neutral → menacing). Then write 2–3 sentences:
   which version was harder to write, and why?
4. **Technical meaning check:** the word "fusillade" in Excerpt C ("A
   perfect fusillade of knocks") is a *technical* term (a simultaneous
   discharge of firearms). Why does Jacobs borrow a battlefield word
   for knocks on a door? What does the technical meaning add that
   "many knocks" would not?

## Practice J — Session 18: comparative-analysis essay studio

**Goal:** I can plan and draft a comparative analysis of two author
choices across two texts.
**Success:** an approved thesis, two paragraph skeletons with quoted
evidence, and a full draft revised once against the skeptic test.

This studio prepares the unit assessment (session 19): a timed
comparative analysis of two author choices across two texts. Today you
plan and draft; the assessment gives you a new passage and the clock.

1. **Thesis workshop.** Draft a thesis that names **two author
   choices** (one per text, or two choices in one text compared with
   one in another) and makes an **arguable claim** about what each
   choice does. Use one of these frames, then make it yours:
   - "Although both Jacobs and the 'Night Train' author withhold
     information, Jacobs withholds ___ to create ___, while the
     'Night Train' author withholds ___ to create ___."
   - "The authors' different ___ choices — ___ in [Text A] and ___
     in [Text B] — produce opposite effects: ___ versus ___."
   A thesis that only says "both authors make choices" is not
   arguable. The adult approves your thesis before you draft.
2. **Paragraph skeletons.** For each of your two body paragraphs,
   build the skeleton before writing sentences:
   - Claim (one sentence: what this paragraph proves)
   - Evidence (two quotes, each with location)
   - Explanation (2–3 sentences: *how* the choice creates its effect
     — name the mechanism: withholding, ordering, diction pile-up,
     distance…)
   - Skeptic sentence (one sentence answering "that's just your
     opinion" — point at the words that force your reading)
3. **Draft** the essay (introduction + two body paragraphs +
   conclusion, ~400–550 words). You may use your U01 evidence
   sandwich: claim → quote → explanation of why *this* quote.
4. **Skeptic revision.** Trade drafts with the adult (or re-read your
   own with a skeptic's pen). For each body paragraph, the skeptic
   writes one margin note beginning "But couldn't it be that…?"
   Revise so that each paragraph *answers* its skeptic note — with
   evidence, not with louder adjectives.
5. **Self-check against the rubric** ([project.md](project.md) uses
   the same criteria the assessment is scored with): claim, evidence,
   explanation of mechanism, organization, conventions. Mark the one
   criterion your draft meets least well and write the single revision
   you will make first in the assessment.

**Reading log (all four weeks):** continue the daily 20–30 minute
independent reading log begun in U01. Each week the adult checks: pages
read, one observation and one inference about the independent book,
and — new in U02 — one **author choice** noticed in the wild (a
flashback, a POV switch, a word that set the tone), with the page
number where you found it.
