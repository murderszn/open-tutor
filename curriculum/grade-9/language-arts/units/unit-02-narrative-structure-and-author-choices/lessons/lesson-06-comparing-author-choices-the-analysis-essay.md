# Lesson 06 — Comparing Author Choices: The Comparative Analysis Essay

**Goal (student-readable):** I can build a comparative analysis essay —
an arguable thesis about author choices in two texts, organized
point-by-point or in blocks, with quoted evidence explained for its
effect — and I can answer the skeptic who says "that's just your
opinion."
**Time:** ~50 minutes (Session 18, week 10) · **Materials:** this page,
notebook, pen, ["Night Train"](../texts/night-train.md),
[Monkey's Paw Excerpts A–C](../texts/monkeys-paw-excerpts.md),
[Vignette A](../texts/craft-vignettes.md) ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L6](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write one sentence: which ending stayed with
you more — the knocking at the Whites' door, or Maya alone on the
platform? Add one reason. You are about to turn that sentence into the
seed of an essay.

## What a comparative analysis essay argues

A comparative analysis essay does not summarize two texts. It makes an
**arguable claim about author choices across two texts** — a claim a
reasonable reader could disagree with, defended with evidence. Weak
version: "Both Jacobs and the 'Night Train' author make structural
choices." (True of every author who ever lived — not arguable, not
interesting.) Strong version: "Both authors build their climaxes by
withholding something from the reader, but they withhold opposite
things, and each kind of withholding moves the ending's work into the
reader's head in a different way." That can be argued *against* — which
is exactly what makes it worth arguing *for*.

The claim must be about **choices**, not topics. "Both stories are
about guilt" compares topics. "Both authors delay the revelation of
guilt, but one delays it with a flashback and the other with an
offstage climax" compares choices. Choices are things the author *did*;
topics are things the story is *about*. Essays live in the first
category.

## Two structures: block vs. point-by-point

Once you have two texts and a claim, you need an order. There are two:

- **Block structure:** everything about Text A, then everything about
  Text B, then the comparison. Best when each text's choice needs full
  context before it can be compared — for example, explaining Jacobs's
  whole offstage-climax machinery before setting it beside Maya's
  platform scene.
- **Point-by-point structure:** alternate by point — Choice 1 in both
  texts, then Choice 2 in both texts. Best when you are tracking the
  *same* choice across both texts, so the reader can watch the
  comparison happen in real time.

Neither is "correct." The question is which one lets your particular
claim land harder. Name your structure in your plan and justify it in
one sentence.

## The evidence sandwich

Every body paragraph follows the same architecture:

1. **Claim about the choice** (topic sentence): what the author did,
   in this text, at this moment.
2. **Short quote**: the exact words — a clause or a sentence, never a
   paragraph. Attribute it.
3. **Explanation of the effect**: what the choice *does to the reader*.
   Not "this shows the author is good at pacing" — *what happens in
   your body or your judgment as you read those words*.

The explanation is the meat. A dropped-in quote with no explanation is
not evidence; it is decoration. And the explanation must stay on the
*choice*, not drift into plot summary: the paragraph is about what
Jacobs *withheld*, not about what happened to the Whites.

## The skeptic move

From Unit 01: every analysis has a reader leaning back with crossed
arms saying, "That's just your opinion." The **skeptic move** answers
that reader — not with louder asserting, but with *words on the page
plus reasoning anyone could check*. "The tone is dreadful because I
felt dread" loses to the skeptic. "The tone is dreadful because Jacobs
chooses *whitened* over *paled*, *solemnly* over *seriously*, and
*doggedly* over *stubbornly* — each a heavier, graver word — and
anyone can verify those choices on the page" wins. The skeptic is not
your enemy; the skeptic is your quality control.

## Worked example 1 — Model thesis + body paragraph: what each author withholds

**Model thesis:** Both Jacobs and the "Night Train" author build their
climaxes by withholding something from the reader — but Jacobs
withholds the *sight* of the horror while the "Night Train" author
withholds the *completion* of the choice, and each kind of withholding
moves the ending's work into the reader's head in a different way.

**Model body paragraph (point-by-point, first point):** Jacobs stages
his climax entirely offstage: the reader gets knocks, bolts, and a
scraping chair, but never the thing at the door. "There was another
knock, and another" (Jacobs, "The Monkey's Paw," Excerpt C) gives the
reader sound in six words and image in zero — the short, doubled
sentence *is* the knocking, and there is deliberately nothing to see.
The effect is outsourcing: because Jacobs refuses to describe the
horror, the reader's imagination builds it, and a self-built monster
is always worse than a described one. The "Night Train" author
withholds the opposite thing. We *see* everything — Maya on the
platform, the lamplight, the train pulling away — but the story ends
before the choice is completed: she stands "finally deciding what kind
of person was going to carry it home." The mailing of the compass, the
actual deed, happens off the page, in the reader's future. Where
Jacobs makes the reader supply the *image*, the "Night Train" author
makes the reader supply the *judgment*: we must decide what kind of
person she is, because the story will not decide for us. Same device
family — strategic withholding — opposite effects: one moves fear into
the reader's imagination, the other moves moral judgment into the
reader's hands.

Notice the paragraph's skeleton: claim about the choice → short quote
→ effect on the reader → second text, same skeleton → the comparison
sentence that answers "so what." Nothing in it summarizes the plot
beyond what the analysis needs.

## Worked example 2 — Model paragraph: narrative distance and trust

A second paragraph needs a *different* pairing — a new choice, not a
restatement. Try narrative distance: how close the narrator stands to
the character, and what the reader is therefore allowed to trust.

**Model paragraph:** "Night Train" is told in **third-person limited** —
the narrator stays outside Maya but close to her thoughts, reporting
her rationalizations honestly: "She told herself it was not stealing
if it had been promised." The narration does not lie to us; it shows
us Maya lying to *herself*, which means we can trust the events while
distrusting her reasoning — the author splits our trust in two, and
the essay's work is judging the gap. Vignette A, Version 3, does the
opposite with a first-person **unreliable narrator**: June insists "I
am never scared in stairwells" and calls her plan "excellent," but the
details betray her — the notebook of stairwell times, the protests that
protest too much. Here the reader cannot trust the voice at all and
must build the truth *against* the narrator, from leaked evidence. Both
authors limit what the reader knows — that is the shared choice — but
the limits produce opposite reading jobs: "Night Train" asks you to
judge a character you can see clearly; the vignette asks you to
detect a truth your guide is hiding. Same device family (controlled
information), opposite effects (judgment vs. suspicion).

A paragraph like this earns its place because it compares *choices*
(limits on information) and their *effects* (what the reader must do),
not because it mentions two texts.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Begin **Practice J** (essay planning studio): draft a working thesis
   about author choices in two of this unit's texts, then skeleton two
   body paragraphs — each skeleton is three lines: choice-claim, quote
   (with attribution), effect on the reader.
2. Trade thesis drafts with a partner. One of you plays the skeptic:
   ask "so what?" and "prove it with words on the page" until the
   thesis survives three rounds. Then switch roles.
3. Choose block or point-by-point for your plan and justify the choice
   in two sentences. When would the *other* structure serve your thesis
   better?

## Independent practice

1. Finish **Practice J**: complete both paragraph skeletons.
2. Write one full body paragraph from a skeleton — a real paragraph,
   not bullets. Check it against the sandwich: claim → short quote →
   explanation of effect. Highlight the explanation sentences; they
   should be the longest part.
3. Write your skeptic paragraph: the strongest objection a skeptic
   could raise against your thesis ("that's just your opinion because
   ___"), then your answer, built from words on the page. If you
   cannot answer it, your thesis needs revising — that is the move
   working as designed.
4. Audit your quotes: each must be short, attributed, and followed by
   explanation. Any quote sitting alone, unexplained, gets cut or
   completed.

## Application

Timed rehearsal for the unit assessment (Session 19: timed comparative
analysis). Set a 15-minute timer. Using only your Practice J plan —
no new reading — draft your introduction and thesis. When the timer
ends, stop mid-sentence if you must, and note exactly where you were.
Write two sentences: what the time pressure cost you, and what your
plan gave you that you would not have had without it.

## Exit check

On an index card: your working thesis in one sentence, the structure
you chose (block or point-by-point), and one sentence on why that
structure serves this thesis. If the "why" is hard, the structure
choice is not finished.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read both model paragraphs aloud
  while the learner tracks the sandwich parts with three pen marks
  (claim / quote / effect) before writing their own.
- **Alternate response mode:** paragraph skeletons may be built as
  labeled cards (one card per sandwich layer) and physically stacked;
  the essay plan may be dictated and scribed.
- **Extension:** write the *other* structure's version of your first
  body paragraph — if you chose point-by-point, draft it as a block
  paragraph — and compare which serves your thesis better in a short
  written verdict. Real editors do exactly this.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The most common failure is summary wearing an essay costume: two plot
retellings stapled together with "similarly" between them. Enforce the
choice-level thesis early — if the thesis could be true of the *topics*,
it is not about *choices*. Also watch for the dropped quote: learners
insert a quotation and move on as if the quote explains itself. The
rule is physical: no quote may touch another sentence without
explanation in between. Finally, "both authors use author choices" is
not a thesis; it is a tautology. If the claim cannot be disagreed
with, it cannot be defended — send it back.
