# Lesson 05 — Word Choice and Cumulative Tone: The Pile-Up Effect

**Goal (student-readable):** I can tell denotation from connotation, name
figurative and technical meanings of words, and explain how many small
word choices pile up to create a tone — no single word does it alone.
**Time:** ~50 minutes (Session 16, week 10) · **Materials:** this page,
notebook, pen, [Vignette C](../texts/craft-vignettes.md),
[Monkey's Paw Excerpt B](../texts/monkeys-paw-excerpts.md) ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write the words *house* and *home*. Under
each, list three feelings or associations it gives you. Same building —
different charge. Share one pair with a partner: where does the
difference live, in the building or in the word?

## Denotation and connotation

Every word carries two loads. The **denotation** is the dictionary
meaning — what the word points to in the world. The **connotation** is
everything the word drags along with it: feelings, judgments, memories,
social signals. *Cheap* and *inexpensive* share a denotation (low
price). Their connotations split: *cheap* sneers (poor quality, cutting
corners), *inexpensive* smiles (a good deal). Same price tag — opposite
verdicts.

Authors choose words for the second load. When Jacobs wants you to feel
dread around Sergeant-Major Morris, he does not write "Morris was
scary." He picks words whose connotations do the scaring for him — and
he picks a lot of them.

## Three kinds of meaning (RL.9-10.4)

The standard names three layers to check in any word or phrase:

- **Figurative meaning:** what the words suggest beyond the literal. In
  Vignette C, "steam drew soft curtains across the window" is
  figurative — steam is not literally sewing curtains. The figure
  tells you the steam is gentle, domestic, sheltering.
- **Connotative meaning:** the associations and feelings, as above.
  *Reeked* does not just mean "smelled strongly"; it means "smelled
  strongly *and I disapprove*."
- **Technical meaning:** the specialized, field-specific sense. A
  "fusillade" (Excerpt C) is technically a military term for rapid
  gunfire — Jacobs borrows it to make knocks sound like an attack.
  Technical meanings import a whole world with one word.

Close readers check all three before deciding what a word is doing.

## Worked example 1 — Two kitchens, same room: Vignette C

Vignette C describes the same kitchen twice. Nothing in the room
changes — only the **diction** (the author's word choices). Pair the
changed words, name what they share, and name what splits them:

| Pair | Shared denotation | Version 1 connotation | Version 2 connotation |
|---|---|---|---|
| glowed / reeked | The kitchen filled the senses strongly | Warm light; welcoming, alive | Bad smell; repulsive, wrong |
| muttered / sputtered (the pot) | Low, intermittent sound from the stove | Like quiet talk to itself — companionable, almost alive | Angry spitting — malfunctioning, unpleasant |
| humming / muttering (grandmother) | Quiet sound under her breath | A tune; contentment, ease | Tuneless complaint; bitterness |
| chipped / cracked (the mug) | Damaged but still usable | Worn with love; history, endearment | Ruined; untrustworthy, about to fail |
| loved / stuck (with the mug) | Kept it for a long time | Affection; a choice | Trapped; no choice, resentment |

Now the real lesson: the **cumulative impact**. No single pair decides
the tone. *Chipped* alone would not make a kitchen warm; *cracked*
alone would not make one cold. But count the lean across a whole
version. Version 1 piles *glowed, muttered, soft, unhurried, humming,
chipped, loved* — seven or more warm-leaning words in four sentences.
Version 2 piles *reeked, sputtered, filmed, shuffled, tuneless,
muttering, cracked, stuck* — eight cold-leaning words in the same four
sentences.

That pile-up is the tone. The author never writes "this kitchen is
cozy" or "this kitchen is miserable." She never has to. Twenty words
all leaning the same way do what one announcing word cannot — they make
you *feel* the verdict instead of being told it. Ask the lesson's
question honestly: which kitchen would you rather eat in? The author
told you nothing. She only made you feel it. That gap — between what
is stated and what is felt — is cumulative impact.

## Worked example 2 — What Morris's body tells you: Excerpt B

In Excerpt B, Jacobs builds dread around Sergeant-Major Morris almost
entirely through diction — dialogue tags and body language. Morris
barely explains himself; his *words about his body* do it:

| Choice (Jacobs, "The Monkey's Paw," Excerpt B) | Literal (denotation) | Connotation — what it makes you feel about Morris |
|---|---|---|
| "his blotchy face whitened" | His mottled face lost color | *Blotchy* already suggests unhealthy, uneasy skin; *whitened* is blood draining — fear the body admits before the mouth does. He is frightened, and trying not to show it. |
| "his glass tapped against his strong teeth" | His glass hit his teeth as he drank | *Tapped* is a trembling hand made audible. *Strong* teeth is ironic — the one strong thing left in him is involuntary. He is shaken to the body. |
| "said the soldier, solemnly" | He spoke in a formal, serious way | *Solemnly* belongs to judges and funerals. It turns a fireside warning into a verdict: this is not a story, it is a sentence. |
| "said his friend, doggedly" | He spoke stubbornly, refusing to move | *Doggedly* sounds like its consonants — clamped down, teeth in. Morris will not be argued with because he *knows*. |

None of these words means "afraid." Together, they saturate the scene
in gravity and dread — the **tone** (the author's attitude toward the
subject, felt by the reader) goes grave the way a room goes dark: not
all at once, but word by word until you notice the light is gone.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. In Excerpt B, find two more connotation-carrying words not in the
   table above (candidates: *grave*, *hush*, *presumptuous*, *keenly*,
   *mischief*). For each: name the shared denotation with a neutral
   synonym, then name what the author's word adds.
2. Tone-revision warm-up: take the neutral sentence "The dog barked at
   the mail carrier." Rewrite it three times — once warm, once neutral,
   once hostile — changing only word choices, not the events. Underline
   the words doing the tonal work in each version.
3. Begin **Practice I** (connotation sets + tone revision task): build
   one connotation set of five words (all leaning the same way) for a
   tone of your choice, and check with a partner that all five lean
   together.

## Independent practice

1. Finish **Practice I** (connotation sets + tone revision task).
2. Back to Vignette C: find one changed pair *not* in the worked-example
   table (*moved/shuffled*). Analyze it the same way — shared
   denotation, differing connotation — and explain in two sentences how
   it contributes to its version's pile-up.
3. In your independent book, find a paragraph with a clear tone. List
   five words or phrases all leaning the same way, and name the tone
   they build together. Then find the one word in the paragraph that
   leans the *other* way, if any — what does that single counter-word
   do?

## Application

The adult gives you a neutral sentence (suggested: "The principal
announced the new schedule in the auditorium."). Rewrite it twice with
different connotation sets — once to make the principal sound
trustworthy, once to make the principal sound threatening — without
changing the facts. A partner reads both versions blind and names the
intended tone of each. If the partner cannot tell, your pile-up was too
thin: add more leaning words.

## Exit check

On an index card: one word pair from today (either the author's or your
own), the denotation both words share, and the connotation that differs.
Then one sentence: why does tone need a pile-up instead of a single
word?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read both versions of Vignette C
  aloud back-to-back while the learner just listens for the *feeling*
  shift; analysis of individual words comes second.
- **Alternate response mode:** connotation work may be done as word
  cards sorted into "warm / cold / neutral" piles, or as a two-column
  chart dictated and scribed.
- **Extension:** take one paragraph of "Night Train" and rewrite it twice
  with opposing connotation sets — once making Maya sound sympathetic,
  once making her sound guilty — changing only diction. Then write a
  paragraph on which version is "truer" to the story, and what that
  says about how diction steers judgment.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat connotation as "the word's opinion" and stop at
one word: "reeked is negative, so the tone is negative." Press the
cumulative question: *would the tone survive if you changed only that
one word back?* Usually yes — which proves the tone lives in the
pile-up, not the word. Also watch for figurative/connotative confusion:
"soft curtains" of steam is figurative (a comparison), while *reeked*
is connotative (an attitude). Both shape tone, but they are different
tools — name which one you are looking at.
