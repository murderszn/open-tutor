# Lesson 01 — Plot Structure and Event Order

**Goal (student-readable):** I can name the six parts of a plot arc
(exposition, inciting incident, rising action, climax, falling action,
resolution), identify *in medias res* and nonlinear order, and explain why
the author arranged events the way they did.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, "Night Train"
text, colored pencils for plot-mapping · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L1](../answer-key.md#lesson-01)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Tell this story twice, out loud, in under a
minute each time: *You wake up late, you miss the bus, you run, the bus
leaves anyway.* First tell it starting with "The bus pulled away."
Second tell it starting with "The alarm didn't go off." What changed —
the events, or something else? (Answer: the events are identical; what
changed is the *order you heard them in*, and that changes what you feel
first.)

## Plot is not the same as story

The **story** is what happens. The **plot** is the order the author tells
it in — and the author's order is a choice, not an accident.

Most plots follow a six-part **plot arc**:

- **Exposition:** the setup — characters, setting, the normal world.
- **Inciting incident:** the event that breaks the normal world and starts
  the story's real conflict. Everything after this is a reaction to it.
- **Rising action:** the complications — events that raise the stakes and
  tighten the conflict.
- **Climax:** the turning point — the moment of highest tension, where the
  conflict gets decided (not always loudly; sometimes it is a quiet
  choice).
- **Falling action:** the immediate consequences of the climax.
- **Resolution:** the new normal — what settles, what remains unsettled.

Authors often refuse to tell events chronologically (in the order they
happened). **In medias res** ("in the middle of things") opens mid-action
and fills in the past later, usually through **flashback** — a scene that
returns to an earlier time. A **nonlinear** plot jumps around in time on
purpose; a **chronological** plot moves straight forward. **Foreshadowing**
is a hint planted early about what comes later: a detail that seems small
now and turns out to be a promise.

When you map a plot, always ask two questions: *What is the story order?*
and *What is the chronological order?* The gap between the two answers is
where the author's craft lives.

## Worked example 1 — Mapping "Night Train": story order vs. chronological order

"Night Train" opens *in medias res*. Watch how the telling order differs
from the happening order:

| Plot part | Story order (how the author tells it) | Chronological order (how it happened) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening / exposition | ① On the train, the conductor checks Maya's ticket; the compass knocks in her pocket | ① Eleven months ago: grandfather dies; compass sits in the desk drawer |
| Flashback (inciting incident + rising action) | ② *Cut back to this morning:* Maya takes the compass from the study and boards the train | ② This morning: Maya lets herself in with the key under the ceramic frog, takes the compass, walks out into the rain |
| Rising action continues | ③ Back in the present: the old man offers half a sandwich, tells the wallet story, asks "What's in the pocket?" | ③ Same as story order — train, old man, wallet story |
| Climax | ④ Maya stands up at the unplanned stop and says "This is mine" — the decision to mail the compass back | ④ Same as story order — the decision |
| Resolution | ⑤ The platform; the old man's salute; "finally deciding what kind of person was going to carry it home" | ⑤ Same as story order |

The inciting incident — the theft — is shown **second**, not first. Why?
Because opening on the train, with a conductor approaching and a damp
fingerprint on a ticket, makes you feel Maya's *guilt before you know her
crime*. The flashback then arrives as the answer to a question you are
already asking: *why is she so nervous?* If the story opened chronologically
with eleven months of a compass sitting in a drawer, that question — the
engine of your curiosity — would never start.

Now spot the **foreshadowing**: the compass "knocked once against her
phone — a small, brass, certain sound" in paragraph one, before you know
what it is. The author plants an object you cannot explain yet, so that
when the theft is revealed, you feel the click of a piece falling into
place. That click is the payoff foreshadowing exists to create.

## Worked example 2 — Reading the illustration backward

Look only at the illustration (cover the story). The conductor is
approaching down the aisle; Maya holds the compass up to the window; her
hand has left dampness on things; she sits alone at night. From this image
*alone*, infer what the unseen **inciting incident** must have been.

Step by step:

1. **What is she holding, and why is it strange?** A small brass compass,
   held up in the lamplight like something precious. People do not study
   compasses on night trains for navigation — she is looking *at* it, not
   with it. It is a symbol, or a secret.
2. **What does her body say?** She sits alone, still, tense enough that her
   hand was sweating on the ticket. The approaching conductor means
   inspection — and inspection frightens people who are carrying something
   they should not have.
3. **Inference:** something happened *before* this scene — she took or was
   given something she is not sure she is allowed to have, and now she is
   riding somewhere to decide what to do about it.

Now check against the story: the inciting incident is the theft of the
compass that morning. The image got you there honestly — a guilty person
carrying a precious object on a train at night almost always means a
wrongful taking followed by a decision. Notice what the image *cannot* tell
you: that her grandfather promised it to her, that her uncle said no. The
illustration gives you the inciting incident's *shape*; the story gives you
its *moral weight*. An author places the inciting incident offstage — or
in a flashback — precisely to make you do this kind of detective work.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Label each paragraph of "Night Train"'s first six paragraphs with one
   of the six plot parts. You will find that several paragraphs share one
   label — that is normal; plot parts are stretches, not sentences.
2. Write the story's events as a numbered chronological list (aim for
   8–10 events). Then circle the one event you would call the inciting
   incident and defend your choice in two sentences. (For **Practice A** —
   plot mapping — bring both lists.)
3. For **Practice B** (foreshadowing hunt): list three details from the
   opening train scene that gain new meaning after the flashback. For each,
   write what it seemed to mean *before* you knew about the theft and what
   it means *after*.
4. Prompt for the **Practice B** rewrite: pick one flashback scene and
   rewrite it as a chronological opening. What is lost? Write three
   sentences explaining exactly which reader experience the reorder
   destroyed (curiosity? tension? sympathy?).

## Independent practice

1. Draw a plot arc for "Night Train": a horizontal line for time, a curve
   rising to a peak at the climax and falling after. Label the peak with
   the decision at the unplanned stop — in your own words, why is *that*
   the climax and not the theft?
2. Find the foreshadowing in the last paragraph of the flashback: "the
   telling was starting to sound like someone else talking." What future
   event does this hint at? (Two to three sentences.)
3. Explain in a paragraph: if the author had told the story chronologically
   (theft first, train second), would the old man's wallet story hit the
   reader as hard? Why or why not? Point at specific lines.
4. In your reading log, note one story or film you know that opens *in
   medias res*. Name the opening moment and the incident it withholds.

## Application

With a partner, take the chronological list from Guided Practice #2 and
re-order it into three different telling orders: fully chronological,
*in medias res*, and fully nonlinear (three jumps minimum). For each,
write one sentence predicting what the reader would feel first. Agree on
which order is strongest for *this* story and why the author's actual
order matches or beats your choice.

## Exit check

On an index card: define **inciting incident** in your own words, and name
the inciting incident of "Night Train." Then answer: does the inciting
incident appear in the story's *first told* event or later? Why did the
author choose that placement?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read "Night Train" aloud
  while the learner tracks events with numbered sticky notes, then moves
  the notes into chronological vs. story order — the reorder is the work,
  not the reading.
- **Alternate response mode:** plot maps may be drawn as a comic strip of
  6–8 panels instead of written paragraphs; each panel must still be
  labeled with its plot-arc part.
- **Extension:** rewrite the story's first two paragraphs so the compass
  theft is revealed immediately in chronological order. Compare the two
  openings line by line and write a paragraph judging which is stronger
  and why, using the terms *inciting incident*, *foreshadowing*, and
  *rising action*.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often label the *opening scene* as the inciting incident ("the
conductor checks her ticket — that's what starts it!"). Press the
definition: the inciting incident is the event that *causes* the conflict,
not the first thing the author shows you. In a nonlinear story the two
are different events by design — that difference is the whole point of
the lesson. Also watch for "climax = the most exciting part": the climax
is the *decision point* (Maya standing up), even though the theft scene
is more dramatic. Excitement is not structure.
