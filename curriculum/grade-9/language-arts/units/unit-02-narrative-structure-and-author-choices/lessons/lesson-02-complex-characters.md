# Lesson 02 — Complex Characters

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain a complex character's
conflicting motivations with quoted evidence, describe how they change
(or refuse to change), and show how their interactions with other
characters reveal who they are.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, "Night
Train" text, "The Monkey's Paw" Excerpt B · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Think of someone you know — or a character
you love — who did something that surprised you: generous when they are
usually stingy, cruel when they are usually kind. In two sentences, name
the surprise and the hidden reason behind it. Real people contain more
than one motive. Today you learn to read characters the same way.

## A character is a bundle of conflicting motives

A flat character has one trait (the bully is cruel; the sidekick is
loyal). A **complex character** has multiple, often **conflicting
motivations** — wants that pull in opposite directions at the same time.
The tension between those wants is what makes them feel real.

Watch for three things when you analyze a character:

- **Conflicting motivations.** What does the character want, and what *else*
  do they want that contradicts it? The conflict between the wants is
  usually the story's real engine.
- **Character arc.** A **dynamic** character changes meaningfully by the
  end — in belief, behavior, or self-knowledge. A **static** character
  stays essentially the same (which can be a deliberate choice: a static
  character can be a rock the world breaks against, or a warning of what
  happens to people who never change).
- **Foil and interaction.** A **foil** is a character whose choices are the
  opposite of the main character's, thrown in to make the main character's
  choices visible by contrast. And a rule of thumb: characters are
  revealed by **interaction** — what they say and do to *each other*
  under pressure shows more than any description. Listen to dialogue like
  a detective: what does the character *want* the other person to think?

Evidence rule (from Unit 01, still in force): every claim about a motive
needs a quote. "Maya feels guilty" is a claim. "Her fingers left a damp
crescent on the paper" is the evidence that proves it — a body doing what
a mouth will not admit.

## Worked example 1 — Maya's conflicting motivations, with evidence

Maya is pulled two directions for the whole story. Map it:

| Motivation | Quoted evidence | What it pushes her toward |
|---|---|---|
| Loyalty to her grandfather's "promise" | "He said it was mine… At the lake, the summer before. He said, 'This'll be yours one day, navigator.'" | Keeping the compass — she believes it was promised to her |
| Her uncle's fairness | "'He said a lot of things that summer, kiddo. The lawyer has the list. The compass stays with the desk.'" | Returning it — the family system, the legal list, his tired (not cruel) laugh |
| Guilt | "Her fingers left a damp crescent on the paper." / "she folded her hands to keep them still" / "she told herself it was not stealing… and then she stopped telling herself things" | Returning it — her body confesses before her mouth does |
| The old man's example | "'Because I'd have known, every time I spent a dollar of it, exactly what kind of man I was.'" | Returning it — a lived model of the honest choice |
| Her grandfather's identity for her | "'Navigator,' her grandfather had called her." | This one cuts *both* ways: a navigator finds the right direction — is keeping the compass the right direction, or is returning it? |

Notice the last row. The strongest evidence of complexity is a motive
that does not pick a side: "navigator" means both *the one who was
promised the compass* and *the one who finds true north*. Maya's final
line — "finally deciding what kind of person was going to carry it home"
— is her **character arc** in one clause: she starts the story
rationalizing ("it was not stealing if it had been promised") and ends
it *deciding who she is*. She is **dynamic**: her belief about herself
changed. The old man is a **foil**: he found a wallet and turned it in,
which makes Maya's kept compass visible as a choice, not an accident —
and his wallet story is the exact mirror she has to answer.

## Worked example 2 — Sergeant-Major Morris: what "I don't know" reveals

Read Excerpt B's exchange again. Mr. White asks the question straight:
"If you could have another three wishes, would you have them?" Morris
answers: "I don't know," said the other. "I don't know."

Work through it the way you worked Maya:

1. **What he says:** He repeats himself — "I don't know" *twice*. A man
   who knows the answer says it once. The repetition is a man refusing to
   finish a sentence he is afraid of.
2. **What he does:** He takes the paw "and dangling it between his
   forefinger and thumb, suddenly threw it upon the fire." *Dangling* is
   contempt — the paw is trash to him, held at arm's length. *Suddenly*
   is panic — the contempt is a performance, and the panic breaks through
   it. Then: "Better let it burn," he says "solemnly."
3. **What he fears and wants, reconstructed:** He *wants* to be done with
   the paw (fancy, he calls his reason for keeping it — "Fancy, I
   suppose," which is what a man says when the true reason is
   unspeakable). He *fears* what the paw did to him and, worse, that he
   still wants the wishes: a man who was truly finished would not need to
   throw it on a fire to prove it. The doubled "I don't know" is the
   confession — he is afraid he would take the wishes again, and afraid
   of what they would cost him.
4. **Interaction as revelation:** notice he never tells the Whites what
   his wishes were ("I don't know what the first two were, but the third
   was for death. That's how I got the paw."). He warns them —
   "If you keep it, don't blame me for what happens" — and *still* lets
   White snatch it from the fire. His conflict plays out entirely in
   dialogue with the Whites: he wants to save them and wants to be rid
   of the burden, and the second want wins when he stops fighting White
   for the paw. That is characterization through interaction — no
   narrator told you any of this; the dialogue did.

Morris is arguably **static** — he arrives warned and leaves warning —
but his scene is the proof that a static character can still be complex:
his *motivations* war with each other even though his *behavior* does
not change.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Build a motivation chart for **Mr. White** in Excerpt B (use the same
   three columns as Worked example 1: motivation, quoted evidence, what it
   pushes him toward). Find at least one motive that pushes him *toward*
   taking the paw and one that pushes him *away*. (For **Practice D** —
   motivation charts — this is your second entry after Maya.)
2. In Excerpt B, Morris says the first man's third wish "was for death."
   Write two sentences: what does that single fact, dropped casually into
   dialogue, tell you about what Morris has *seen* — without him ever
   describing it?
3. For **Practice E** (conflict analyses): pick one line of Maya's dialogue
   with the old man ("I'm fine." / "Nothing." / "Why'd you do it?") and
   write a paragraph explaining what she *wants him to think* versus what
   the line actually *reveals*. Quote the line and at least one piece of
   body-language evidence from the same scene.

## Independent practice

1. Is the old man on the train dynamic or static? Defend your answer with
   two quotes. (Trick check: can a static character still matter to the
   story? Say why, in one sentence.)
2. Write a paragraph comparing Maya's and Morris's conflicts: both are
   torn between keeping and destroying/returning a powerful object. Name
   one way their conflicts are the *same* and one way they are
   *different*, each with a quote.
3. Find one line in "Night Train" where Maya lies to another character
   and one line where she lies to herself. Which is easier to spot? Why?
   (Two to three sentences.)
4. In your reading log, pick a character from your independent book and
   name two of their conflicting motivations with one quoted line each.

## Application

With a partner, stage Excerpt B's dialogue (Morris, Mr. White, Mrs.
White) and read it aloud twice: once with Morris played as calm and
controlled, once with Morris played as barely holding himself together.
Which performance matches the text better? Point at specific stage
directions in the prose — "his blotchy face whitened," "his glass tapped
against his strong teeth," "solemnly," "doggedly" — and argue it in a
paragraph.

## Exit check

On an index card: name Maya's two strongest conflicting motivations and
one quote for each. Then answer in one sentence: what changed in Maya
between the first paragraph and the last — that is, what is her arc?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read Excerpt B aloud with
  voices for each speaker while the learner underlines Morris's lines
  only; the motivation chart is then built from the underlined lines
  alone — narrowing the evidence is the support, not simplifying the
  thinking.
- **Alternate response mode:** motivation charts may be drawn as a
  tug-of-war (two arrows pulling the character's name in opposite
  directions, quotes written on each rope) instead of a table.
- **Extension:** write the scene where Uncle Ray discovers the compass
  is gone, from Uncle Ray's point of view, in 150–200 words. Give him at
  least two conflicting motivations (anger at the theft vs. understanding
  of why she took it), and include one line of dialogue that reveals both
  at once. Then write two sentences explaining which choice makes him a
  foil for Maya — and which makes him sympathetic to her.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often reduce a character to one adjective ("Maya is guilty,"
"Morris is scared") and stop. The lesson's target misconception is
**single-motive reading**: the belief that a character wants one thing.
When a learner offers one motive, ask: "And what does she want that
fights *against* that?" until the chart has genuine tension. Also watch
for confusing *static* with *flat*: Morris does not change, but he is
not simple — stasis can itself be the tragedy (a man who learned nothing
he can use). Flat means one-dimensional; static means unchanging; they
are different claims and need different evidence.
