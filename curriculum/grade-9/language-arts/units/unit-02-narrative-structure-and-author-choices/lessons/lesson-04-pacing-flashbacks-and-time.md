## The story clock: pacing

**Pacing** is how fast or slow a story *feels* — not how many pages it has,
but how quickly the story's clock seems to tick while you read. Authors
control the clock with two main tools.

**Tool 1: Sentence length.** Short sentences move fast. They hit like
footsteps — one thing, then the next, no room to rest. Long sentences
move slow, or they pile: clause after clause, each one adding weight,
until the reader feels the load. Neither speed is "better." A chase
scene in long sentences would feel wrong; a moment of grief in two-word
sentences would feel heartless.

**Tool 2: Scene vs. summary.** A **scene** shows events moment by moment,
in real time — dialogue, action, sensory detail. A **summary** compresses
time: weeks pass in a sentence. Scenes slow the clock down (the reader
lives inside the moment); summaries speed it up (the reader skims across
time). An author switching between them is choosing what deserves your
full attention and what can be hurried past.

The analysis question is always the same: *what does this speed do to
the reader?* Pacing is a body-level choice — it works on your pulse
before it works on your brain.

## Worked example 1 — Mapping the sprint: Vignette B

Vignette B was written so the sentence length itself does work. Count the
words in each sentence and watch what happens:

| # | Sentence (opening words) | Words | What the length does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "June saw the bus at the end of the block." | 10 | Setup — a normal, orienting sentence. |
| 2 | "The doors were closing." | 4 | The clock starts ticking. Shorter = faster. |
| 3 | "She ran." | 2 | Two words. The shortest sentence in the scene = the fastest moment. |
| 4 | "Her backpack slammed her spine with every step." | 8 | Slightly longer — one full stride of detail. |
| 5 | "The driver looked up." | 4 | Back to staccato. |
| 6 | "Looked at her." | 3 | Fragments now — grammar itself is running. |
| 7 | "Looked away." | 2 | The door slams in two words. |
| 8 | "The doors sighed shut." | 4 | The failure, in four flat words. |
| 9 | "The bus pulled from the curb, and June stopped…" | 37 | The sprint ends; the sentence stretches. |
| 10 | "It had been the last bus." | 6 | A short, bleak verdict. |
| 11 | "The next one came in forty minutes, and the sky was already going the color of a bruise, and her phone was at three percent…" | 46 | The longest sentence in the scene, by far — and it *piles*. |

Notice the shape. The first six sentences average just over five words —
the chase is told in gasps. Then sentence 9 (37 words) lands like the
moment she stops running, and the final sentence (46 words) does the
opposite of the chase: it *accumulates*. Each "and" adds another bad
fact — forty minutes, bruise-colored sky, dying phone, no answer from
Priya, wind through the jacket. The author could have written five short
sentences. Instead she wrote one long one, so the reader feels all the
bad news *at once*, the way June does: not a list, a pile.

That is pacing as time control. The sprint takes eight sentences and
maybe ten seconds of story time; the aftermath takes one sentence and
feels longer. The author decided how fast your body moved through it.

## Flashback is a tool, not decoration

A **flashback** is a scene from an earlier time inserted into the
present-tense story. Some stories open even earlier in the disruption:
**in medias res** — Latin for "in the middle of things" — means starting
after the trouble has begun, then filling in the backstory later.
"Night Train" does this: it opens on the train, *after* the theft, and
only then flashes back to the theft itself.

A flashback is never decoration. It always has a job. The most common
job is **motivation reveal**: the author withholds *why* a character acts
until the reader is already invested, so the explanation lands as
understanding instead of as an excuse. If you learn a character stole
something on page one, you judge her. If you sit beside her for two
pages first — her damp ticket, her guilt, the compass knocking in her
pocket — and *then* learn what she did, you still judge her, but now
you also understand her. That is the difference between a character and
a case file.

## Worked example 2 — The climax that never happens onstage: Excerpt C

Jacobs stages the entire climax of "The Monkey's Paw" **offstage** — the
horror is never shown. The reader gets sound and reaction only. Watch how
sentence length does the work:

| Quote (Jacobs, "The Monkey's Paw," Excerpt C) | Length | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| "There was another knock, and another." | Short (6 words) | Two stressed beats, like the knocking itself. The sentence *is* the sound. |
| "He heard the chain rattle back and the bottom bolt drawn slowly and stiffly from the socket." | Long | Slow verbs — *rattle*, *drawn slowly and stiffly* — stretch the seconds. The long sentence makes you wait, the way he waits. |
| "A perfect fusillade of knocks reverberated through the house, and he heard the scraping of a chair as his wife put it down in the passage against the door." | Long, compound | Two panicked actions chained with "and." The sentence races because he is racing — paw in one hand, wife at the door. |
| "He heard the creaking of the bolt as it came slowly back, and at the same moment he found the monkey's paw, and frantically breathed his third and last wish." | Longest of the passage | Three "and"-clauses run two timelines at once: her hands on the bolt, his on the paw. The wish has to win the race *inside one sentence*. |
| "The knocking ceased suddenly, although the echoes of it were still in the house." | Short | The stop is as abrupt as the sentence. Relief — and dread, because echoes remain. |
| "The street lamp flickering opposite shone on a quiet and deserted road." | Short, plain | After all that noise, plainness. The horror stays offstage forever: we never see what knocked. |

The technique: **withhold the visual, amplify the sound.** Jacobs knows
that whatever he describes will be less frightening than whatever *you*
imagine. So he gives your imagination raw material — knocks, bolts, a
scraping chair, a wail — and lets it build the monster. The short
sentences punch; the long sentences delay. Both serve the same choice:
never show it.

## The counterfactual test: "Night Train" in chronological order

Try the test good readers run on every structural choice: *what would be
lost if the author had done it the plain way?*

If "Night Train" opened with the theft — Maya letting herself in with
the frog key, taking the compass, walking out into the rain — the train
scenes would have no engine. The conductor's "Ticket," the damp crescent
on the paper, the compass knocking in her pocket: all of it would be
*explained before you felt it*. You would read the train as a girl
traveling with a stolen compass, and you would have already decided what
she is.

Instead, the author opens in medias res and flashes back. On the train,
you notice the guilt before you know the crime. The flashback then does
its job — motivation reveal: the grandfather's promise ("This'll be
yours one day, navigator"), the uncle's tired fairness, the three weeks
of rehearsing in the shower. By the time you learn she stole it, you
have already sat beside her and heard the old man's wallet story. You
judge her *and* understand her. Chronological order would give you the
judgment without the understanding — a simpler story, and a worse one.

**Rule of thumb:** if moving a scene earlier or later would cost the
reader nothing, the placement was decoration. If it would cost mystery,
sympathy, or suspense, the placement was craft.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. In "Night Train," find the exact sentence where the flashback begins
   and the exact sentence where it ends. Mark both. What single word or
   phrase signals each transition?
2. Rewrite the story's opening in chronological order in three or four of
   your own sentences (start with the frog key, end with the conductor).
   Then list two specific effects the real opening has that your version
   loses. Be concrete — name the lost lines.
3. In Excerpt C, label three sentences of your choice "short" or "long"
   and write one sentence for each explaining what that speed does to
   the reader at that exact moment.

## Independent practice

1. Complete **Practice H** (pacing analysis + flashback placement task).
2. Sentence-length map: take the conductor scene in "Night Train" (from
   "The conductor was already at her row" through "keep them still").
   Count the words in each sentence and sketch the shape — short, long,
   short? Where does the author speed up, and what is happening in the
   story at that spot?
3. In your independent book, find one passage that is mostly **scene**
   and one that is mostly **summary**. Quote one line from each and
   explain in two sentences why the author slowed down for the first
   and sped up for the second.

## Application

Take the final sentence of Vignette B (the 46-word pile-up) and rewrite
it as five short sentences, keeping every fact. Read both versions aloud.
Then write a paragraph: what did the short-sentence version lose, and
what (if anything) did it gain? When would an author *want* the short
version?

## Exit check

On an index card: one pacing choice you noticed today (sentence length,
scene vs. summary, flashback placement, or offstage staging), the exact
words, and what it did to your reading speed or your feelings as you
read. If you cannot name the effect, the observation is not finished.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read Vignette B and Excerpt C aloud
  while the learner taps on each period; the body hears pacing before
  the eyes analyze it.
- **Alternate response mode:** instead of a word-count table, draw the
  "story clock" — a line graph where sentence length is height. Peaks
  and valleys show pacing visually; label what happens in the story at
  each peak.
- **Extension:** take one paragraph of "Night Train" and rewrite it twice
  — once to make it feel twice as fast, once to make it feel twice as
  slow — changing only sentence length and scene/summary balance, not
  the events. Compare the two with a partner.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often equate pacing with "fast" and call any slow passage
boring. Press the function question: *slow for what?* Jacobs's long bolt
sentences are slow on purpose — the dread is in the waiting. Also watch
for the flashback-as-summary error: a flashback is a *scene* from the
past, not a paragraph of backstory explanation. If it has no dialogue,
no sensory detail, no moment-by-moment action, it is exposition wearing
a flashback costume.
