# Lesson 03 — Point of View and Narrative Distance

**Goal (student-readable):** I can identify first person, third-person
limited, and third-person omniscient; explain how narrative distance and
unreliability change what the reader knows; and analyze how a story's
cultural point of view is filtered through its narrator.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, Craft
Vignette A (all three versions), "The Monkey's Paw" adult background
note · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (5 min):** You and a friend both witness the same
argument in the hallway. Later, you each tell it. Will your versions be
identical? In two sentences, name one thing that would differ — and
*why* it would differ. (Answer: your position, your loyalties, what you
were paying attention to. The teller is always part of the tale.)

## Who is telling, and how close are they standing?

**Point of view** is the vantage point the story is told from — and it
decides what the reader is *allowed* to know.

- **First person:** the narrator is a character in the story ("I"). You
  get their thoughts, their feelings, their blind spots — and nothing
  they do not know. *Limit: you can never see inside anyone else's head.*
- **Third-person limited:** a narrator outside the story ("she," "he")
  who stays close to **one** character's thoughts and perceptions. You
  get depth on that character and the same blind spots about everyone
  else. ("Night Train" is told this way: you are inside Maya's head but
  never the conductor's.)
- **Third-person omniscient:** a narrator outside the story who can enter
  *any* character's mind and report what no character could know. More
  knowledge — but less intimacy, because the narrator is everywhere and
  therefore close to no one.

**Narrative distance** is how close the narrator stands to a character's
inner life — not just *whose* head you are in, but *how deep*. A narrator
can report "June was scared" (far) or "June told herself she wasn't
hiding, which was the kind of true that needs saying twice" (close —
you are hearing the exact shape of her self-deception). Close distance
creates intimacy; far distance creates authority or coldness. Authors
slide the distance in and out on purpose.

An **unreliable narrator** is a narrator whose telling you cannot fully
trust — they lie, misremember, misunderstand, or see the world through a
distortion they cannot name. You detect unreliability the way you detect
a lie in real life: the *details* contradict the *claims*. When a
narrator says "I am never scared" and then describes pressing into the
darkest shadow "just in case," the details are telling the truth the
narrator will not.

Finally, **cultural point of view**: every narrator carries the
assumptions of their world. When a story is set in one culture but its
driving events come from another, ask *whose* perspective frames the
events — and who never gets to speak for themselves.

## Worked example 1 — Vignette A, Version 1 vs. Version 2: what changes, what stays

Both versions describe the same stairwell, the same June, the same
Tuesday at 4:40. List exactly what changes and what stays identical:

| | Version 1 (first person) | Version 2 (third-person limited) |
|---|---|---|
| Pronouns | "I," "me," "my" | "June," "she," "her" |
| Access to thoughts | Direct: "I told myself I was waiting for Priya" | Reported: "She told herself she was waiting for Priya" — the narrator *reports* the thought, June does not speak it |
| Knowledge limit | Cannot know anything June does not know | Same limit — still only June's perceptions (the footsteps are "not Priya's, too heavy" — *June's* judgment, not a fact) |
| Distance | Closest possible — you *are* June | A half-step back — you are *with* June, watching her think |

What stays identical: every event, every perception, every limit. Both
versions know only what June knows; both stop at the same unanswered
questions (*who was on the landing?*).

Conclusion: **POV controls pronouns and distance, not information** —
at least between these two. The shift from "I" to "she" costs almost no
facts and gains a small but real distance: Version 1 lets you *be* the
girl telling herself she is not hiding; Version 2 lets you *watch* a girl
telling herself she is not hiding — and watching someone deceive
themselves is, for a reader, often more revealing than being them. That
is an author choice: intimacy versus observation, and neither is
automatically better.

## Worked example 2 — Vignette A, Version 3: catching the unreliable narrator

Version 3 is also first person — but June is now someone you should not
trust. The narrator's *claims* and the *details* fight each other. Catch
the three betrayals:

1. **The notebook of stairwell times.** She claims this was casual
   waiting ("Perfect. Nobody uses these stairs after four"). But she
   "checked, three days running, writing down the times in my notebook
   like a scientist." Three days of logged observations is not casual —
   it is surveillance. The detail exposes the claim as a lie she is
   telling herself.
2. **"I am never scared in stairwells."** The protest is too loud. She
   raises the topic of fear only to deny it, unprompted — and she has
   positioned herself "where the shadow was thickest" and stayed "just
   in case" in Versions 1–2. A person who is never scared does not need
   to announce it mid-paragraph. Denials this emphatic are confessions
   wearing a costume.
3. **The unexplained plan.** "The plan was excellent. The plan had no
   flaws." But the plan is never described — not its goal, not its
   steps, not its reason. A real plan can be explained; a fantasy cannot
   survive being said out loud. The vaguer the praise ("excellent," "no
   flaws"), the emptier the plan. She is narrating confidence to cover
   its absence.

The method, generalized: **trust details over claims.** When a narrator
tells you what to think ("not scared," "excellent plan"), check it
against what they *show* you doing (hiding in shadows, keeping
notebooks). The gap between claim and detail is where unreliability
lives — and finding it is close reading at full power.

## Worked example 3 — Cultural point of view: the fakir who never speaks

Read the adult background note in the Monkey's Paw excerpts file. The
story's engine is a worldview: an Indian fakir's belief that fate rules
human lives and that "those who interfered with it did so to their
sorrow." That belief *drives the entire plot* — every wish, every
consequence.

But the fakir never appears. He never speaks. His perspective arrives
**filtered through Sergeant-Major Morris**, a British soldier, telling a
fireside tale to an English family: "twenty-one years in India" becomes
adventure background, and the holy man's philosophy is reduced to a
warning Morris half-remembers.

Ask the RL.9-10.6 question: *whose* cultural point of view frames this
story? The answer is that the reader only ever gets the English
parlor's view of the Indian belief — exoticized ("spellbound"), distant,
delivered secondhand. Jacobs's author choice is to keep the fakir
offstage, which means the reader can feel the *power* of the belief
(the paw works) while never hearing the *believer*. That filtering is
not an accident of plotting; it is a point-of-view decision about whose
knowledge counts as knowledge. Whether you find that choice effective,
unfair, or simply of its time, you are now analyzing point of view at
the cultural level — not just *who tells*, but *whose world the telling
belongs to*.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Copy one sentence from Vignette A Version 1 and rewrite it in
   third-person limited (Version 2 style). Then copy one sentence from
   Version 2 and rewrite it in first person. Read both aloud. In two
   sentences, describe what the pronoun swap did to the *distance* — not
   the facts.
2. Find one more detail in Version 3 (beyond the three in Worked example
   2) that contradicts the narrator's confident claims. Write the claim,
   the detail, and one sentence explaining the contradiction.
3. Discussion: could "Night Train" be told in first person by Maya? What
   would be gained and what would be lost? Name one specific scene that
   would change most, and say why. (Two to three sentences.)

## Independent practice

1. For **Practice G** (POV rewrite tasks): take the train-conversation
   scene from "Night Train" (the old man and the sandwich) and rewrite
   it in **first person as the old man** narrating — keeping every event
   the same, only the teller changes (8–12 sentences). Then rewrite the
   same scene in **third-person omniscient**, marking the two places
   where your narrator knows something neither character could know.
   Finally, write a paragraph: what did each rewrite gain and cost
   versus the original third-person limited?
2. For **Practice G** (second task — reliability audit): reread the
   flashback in "Night Train" and audit Maya as a narrator of her own
   life. Find one claim she makes about herself ("It was not stealing")
   and the details that argue against it. Is she unreliable, or just
   conflicted? Defend your answer with two quotes — there is a real
   distinction, and the line between them is the point.
3. Write three sentences explaining narrative distance using Vignette A:
   give one example of *close* distance and one of *far* distance from
   the versions, and say what effect each distance creates.
4. In your reading log, identify the POV of your independent book (first,
   third-limited, or omniscient) with one quoted line as proof. If you
   suspect unreliability, name the detail that made you suspicious.

## Application

Take the adult background note's question — *whose cultural point of view
frames "The Monkey's Paw"?* — and answer it in a full paragraph with
evidence. Then add a second paragraph: imagine one scene (the paw
changing hands, or the final knocking) rewritten so the fakir's
perspective frames it instead of the English parlor's. Describe in
concrete terms what would change — what the reader would now know, and
what they would lose. This is analysis, not fan fiction: every choice
you describe must connect to a point-of-view term from this lesson.

## Exit check

On an index card: (a) define **narrative distance** in your own words;
(b) name one detail from Vignette A Version 3 that proves the narrator
is unreliable, and the claim it contradicts; (c) in one sentence, say
whose cultural point of view frames "The Monkey's Paw" and who never
speaks.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read the three Vignette A
  versions aloud in sequence while the learner follows with three
  different colored pencils, underlining only what is *different* between
  versions — the visible color pattern becomes the Version 1 vs. 2
  comparison.
- **Alternate response mode:** the reliability audit may be done as a
  two-column "Claims vs. Details" chart instead of prose; the chart is
  complete when every claim row has a contradicting detail row with a
  line reference.
- **Extension:** rewrite the opening paragraph of "Night Train" in
  third-person omniscient — enter the conductor's head for two sentences
  (what does *he* think of this nervous girl?). Then write a paragraph
  arguing whether the story is stronger limited or omniscient, using the
  terms *distance*, *dramatic irony* (the reader knowing what a character
  does not), and *tension*.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The target misconception is **"first person = honest, third person =
objective."** Learners assume an "I" narrator tells the truth and a
"she/he" narrator is neutral. Version 3 demolishes the first half —
first person is the *easiest* POV to lie in, because there is no outside
check. Press the second half too: third-person limited is not objective;
it is one character's subjectivity with the pronouns changed (Worked
example 1 proves the knowledge limit is identical). Objectivity is not
a POV; it is a fantasy. Also watch for learners labeling *any* mistaken
character "unreliable" — unreliability is a property of the *telling*,
not of being wrong about one fact. Maya misjudges herself, but the
narration reports her misjudgment honestly; June in Version 3 misleads
the reader. That is the line.
