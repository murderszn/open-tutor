# Lesson 06 — The Full Editing Pass

**Goal (student-readable):** I can complete a full editing pass
over my own writing, treating every change as a meaning
decision and documenting it.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
one piece of your own U01–U03 writing, Practice J ·
**Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L6](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

**Readiness check (5 min):** What is the difference between
revising and editing? Answer used in this unit: revising
changes what is said and how it is structured (claims, order,
evidence); editing changes how sentences say it (clauses,
phrases, punctuation, word choice, format). This lesson is
about editing; it assumes the piece's ideas have already been
revised, and it refuses to use editing as an excuse to avoid a
revision the piece still needs.

## The editing pass, in order

Work through a piece in passes, one concern at a time. A single
read that tries to catch everything catches nothing reliably.

**Pass 1 — Completeness (clauses).** Read each sentence and
ask: does it have an independent clause? Mark every fragment
and every comma splice or run-on. Repair them before anything
else, because a sentence that is not a sentence cannot be
styled. Use Lesson 1's repairs; when two repairs are possible,
choose the one that keeps the emphasis you intended.

**Pass 2 — Structure (phrases).** Underline every opening
phrase. For each, ask: who or what does it modify, and is that
the subject of the sentence? Repair dangling and misplaced
modifiers (Lesson 2). Then ask the variety question: are three
sentences in a row built the same way? If so, rebuild one with
a phrase — participial, prepositional, or absolute — and check
that the rebuild did not change the meaning.

**Pass 3 — Punctuation (semicolons).** Find every place two
independent clauses sit side by side. For each, defend the
join: period, comma + conjunction, or semicolon (Lesson 3)?
For every semicolon, write in the margin why the two clauses
belong together. If you cannot defend it in one sentence, use
a period. Find every "however," "therefore," "moreover," and
"consequently" and check its punctuation pair.

**Pass 4 — Words (vocabulary).** Circle every vague word
("nice," "good," "things," "stuff," "very") and every word-bank
word used in this unit. For each vague word, either replace it
with a precise word or cut it. For each word you are unsure
about, run Lesson 4's four strategies and the substitution
test. Check every figure of speech: what would be lost if it
were said plainly? If the answer is "nothing," cut it or keep
it knowingly.

**Pass 5 — Style and format (register, MLA).** Read the piece
aloud. Does the register fit its audience and purpose (Lesson
5)? Is the voice consistent, or does it drift between formal
and informal? Is the parallelism clean? Then format: heading,
centered title, header, double spacing, paragraph indents,
in-text citations, Works Cited page with hanging indents.

**Pass 6 — The revision log.** List every change you made,
grouped by pass, in this form: *original → revised → why
(meaning decision, not "sounded better")*. Example: "Original:
'The lamp burned all night, the boats found the harbor.'
Revised: 'The lamp burned all night; the boats found the
harbor.' Why: the two clauses are one idea — the light's
effect — and the semicolon claims that closeness; a period
would have separated cause from effect." A change you cannot
explain is a change you should reconsider.

## Worked example 1 — One paragraph through all six passes

Original (a learner's draft, invented for this lesson):

> The keeper climbed the tower, she was tired. Because the
> fog was thick. She lit the lamp, however the boats could not
> see it, the light was weak. The work was hard, the pay was
> fair, the food was plain.

**Pass 1:** "The keeper climbed the tower, she was tired" is a
comma splice. "Because the fog was thick" is a fragment.
Repair: "The keeper climbed the tower, although she was
tired." (comma + subordinating structure: one independent
clause, one dependent clause — a complex sentence) and attach
the fragment: "Because the fog was thick, she lit the lamp."

**Pass 2:** Opening phrase check — none yet. Add variety
without changing meaning: "Tired, the keeper climbed the
tower." Now "tired" is a participial adjective modifying
"keeper," and the sentence is shorter. Keep it only if the
tiredness matters less than the climbing; here it does, since
the paragraph is about the work, not the tiredness.

**Pass 3:** "She lit the lamp, however the boats could not see
it" needs a semicolon before "however" and a comma after it:
"She lit the lamp; however, the boats could not see it."
"The work was hard, the pay was fair, the food is plain" is a
triple comma splice; the three clauses are parallel judgments,
so semicolons are defended: "The work was hard; the pay was
fair; the food was plain."

**Pass 4:** "weak" is vague: weak in what way? "The light was
dim behind the fog" is precise. "Hard" survives, because the
paragraph earns it with the climb and the fog.

**Pass 5:** Register: this is a narrative paragraph, not a
logbook, so the first person and the feelings stay. Format:
no citations are involved, so no Works Cited is invented.

**Pass 6 (edited paragraph + log excerpt):**

> Tired, the keeper climbed the tower. Because the fog was
> thick, she lit the lamp; however, the boats could not see
> it — the light was dim behind the fog. The work was hard;
> the pay was fair; the food was plain.

Log entries (abridged): comma splice → comma + subordination
(keeps tiredness subordinate to climbing); fragment attached
to its main clause; "however" given its semicolon–comma pair;
triple splice repaired with defended semicolons; "weak" →
"dim behind the fog" (precise, and it explains *why* the boats
could not see it).

## Worked example 2 — A change that should not be made

Original: "The boats turned toward the sound."

Tempting edit: "The boats, which had been lost in the fog for
hours, turned toward the sound, which was the bell."

That edit adds information the writer may not know (were the
boats lost for hours? was the sound the bell — or could it
have been the foghorn?). Editing never invents facts. If the
information is true and the writer wants it, it belongs in a
revision, with evidence, not smuggled in as a relative clause
during an editing pass. The honest edit here is no edit at all.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Take the edited paragraph in Worked example 1 and argue
   against one of its changes: is there a change you would
   reverse, and what meaning would the reversal restore?
   Bring the argument to Practice J.
2. Choose a paragraph from your own U01–U03 writing. Run
   Pass 1 only, and list every fragment, splice, and run-on
   you find — do not fix them yet.
3. Exchange Pass 1 lists with the adult (or read yours aloud):
   did you miss any error the adult found, or find any the
   adult missed? The disagreements are the teaching.

## Independent practice

1. Run Passes 2 and 3 over the same paragraph. List every
   phrase repair and every semicolon decision, with a
   one-sentence defense for each semicolon.
2. Run Passes 4 and 5 over the same paragraph. List every word
   replaced or cut, with the reason, and confirm the MLA
   formatting (or note honestly that the piece has no sources
   and therefore needs no Works Cited page — do not invent
   one).
3. Write the full revision log for the paragraph (Pass 6) and
   choose the three changes you are proudest of. For each,
   write two sentences explaining why the revised sentence is
   stronger than the original.

## Application

Complete the Style Portfolio's Part 3 (see
[`project.md`](../project.md)): the editing pass over your own
earlier piece, with its revision log, is the portfolio's
centerpiece. The portfolio is scored on whether each change is
defended as a meaning decision — not on how many changes you
made. A portfolio with five well-defended changes outscores a
portfolio with twenty unexplained ones.

## Exit check

On an index card: list the six editing passes in order, and
for Pass 3 write the one-sentence defense test for a
semicolon. (Answer: passes — completeness, structure,
punctuation, words, style/format, revision log. The test: "I
can say, in one sentence, why these two clauses belong
together.")

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read the piece
  aloud while the learner follows and marks errors, so
  listening catches what silent reading misses — especially
  run-ons, which the ear hears as breathlessness.
- **Alternate response mode:** the revision log may be
  dictated, recorded, or drawn as before/after sentence pairs
  with arrows labeled by pass number.
- **Extension:** edit a paragraph from a public-domain text
  (not your own) using the six passes, and write a note on
  which passes changed the author's meaning — and whether
  those changes were improvements or intrusions.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat editing as "fixing mistakes the teacher
would mark" rather than as meaning decisions, and they over-
edit: changing every sentence, including sound ones, to prove
they worked. The revision log is the antidote — it forces a
reason per change, and a reasonless change is visible at once.
Also watch for editing that invents facts (Worked example 2)
and for MLA formatting applied to a piece with no sources,
which invents a Works Cited page instead of honestly omitting
one.
