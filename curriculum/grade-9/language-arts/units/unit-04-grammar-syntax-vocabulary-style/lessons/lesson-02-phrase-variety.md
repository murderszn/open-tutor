# Lesson 02 — Phrase Variety: Participial, Prepositional, Absolute

**Goal (student-readable):** I can identify and use noun, verb,
adjectival, adverbial, participial, prepositional, and absolute
phrases to add variety and specific meaning to my writing.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
sentence bank · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

**Readiness check (5 min):** A phrase has no subject–verb pair of
its own; a clause has one. Sort these into phrase or clause:
(a) "in the early morning" (b) "when the fog thickened"
(c) "tired and salt-stained" (d) "the keeper lit the lamp."
Answers: (a) phrase, (b) clause, (c) phrase, (d) clause. If (b)
tempts you, remember: "when" plus subject "fog" plus verb
"thickened" is a clause, even though it cannot stand alone.

## Phrases are the sentence's movable parts

A **phrase** is a group of related words without a subject–verb
pair that can stand as a clause. Phrases do jobs inside clauses:
they act as subjects, objects, modifiers, and connectors. The
standard lists seven kinds for grades 9–10; this lesson teaches
all seven, with the most weight on the three that are new at this
level.

- **Noun phrase:** a noun plus its modifiers, doing a noun's job:
  "The old brass lamp" (subject), "the top shelf" (object of a
  preposition). The head noun is the word the rest describes.
- **Verb phrase:** a main verb plus its helpers: "had not been
  lit," "was ringing."
- **Adjectival phrase:** modifies a noun: "the lamp **on the top
  shelf**," "a light **visible for miles**."
- **Adverbial phrase:** modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb —
  telling when, where, how, or why: "at dawn," "with steady
  hands," "in the early morning."
- **Participial phrase:** begins with a **participle** — a verb
  form used as an adjective (present participles end in -ing;
  past participles often end in -ed, -en, or take an irregular
  form). It modifies a noun: "**Tired and salt-stained**, the crew
  secured the boat." The phrase describes the crew. "**Hoping for
  clear weather**, Mara checked the sky at dawn." The phrase
  describes Mara.
- **Prepositional phrase:** begins with a preposition (in, on,
  at, with, from, to, before, after, under) and ends with its
  object: "**In the early morning**, the keeper climbed the
  tower." "**With steady hands**, the keeper worked." A
  prepositional phrase can be adjectival ("the lamp on the top
  shelf") or adverbial ("climbed in the early morning") — again,
  the job, not the first word, decides the label.
- **Absolute phrase:** a noun plus a participle (or an adjective
  or prepositional phrase) that modifies the *whole* sentence
  rather than one word, and is set off by a comma: "**The wind
  gone**, the sea lay flat." "**Her hands steady**, Mara
  threaded the wick." Test: an absolute phrase cannot be moved
  into the middle of the clause as a modifier of one noun
  without changing the meaning — it comments on the whole
  situation.

## Worked example 1 — One flat sentence, four phrase expansions

Flat sentence: "The crew secured the boat."

1. **Participial (front):** "Tired and salt-stained, the crew
   secured the boat." The opening phrase gives the crew's state
   before the action, in two words instead of a whole clause.
2. **Prepositional (front, adverbial):** "In the early morning,
   the crew secured the boat." Time is set before the action.
3. **Absolute (front):** "The tide turned, the crew secured the
   boat." — *No.* That is a comma splice, not an absolute
   phrase, because "the tide turned" has a subject and a verb.
   The honest absolute version is: "The tide having turned, the
   crew secured the boat." The noun "tide" plus the participle
   "having turned" has no tensed verb of its own.
4. **Adjectival (after the noun):** "The crew secured the boat
   with the torn sail." The phrase tells you *which* boat — the
   information a relative clause would give, in fewer words.

Notice the economy: each phrase replaces a clause the writer
might otherwise have needed ("because they were tired,"
"when the morning came," "after the tide had turned"). Variety
is not showing off; it is choosing the smallest structure that
carries the meaning.

## Worked example 2 — Two modifier errors and their repairs

**Dangling modifier:** "Climbing the tower, the lamp came into
view." The participial phrase "climbing the tower" needs a noun
to modify, and the subject of the main clause is "the lamp" —
lamps do not climb towers. Repair by giving the phrase its true
subject: "Climbing the tower, the keeper saw the lamp." Or turn
the phrase into a clause: "As the keeper climbed the tower, the
lamp came into view."

**Misplaced modifier:** "The keeper saw the boat with the
telescope." Did the boat have the telescope, or did the keeper
use it? The phrase sits at the end, so it attaches to the nearest
noun, "boat." Repair by moving it next to what it modifies:
"With the telescope, the keeper saw the boat." Placement is
meaning: a modifier belongs beside the word it modifies.

The test for both errors is the same question: *who or what is
doing the action in the opening phrase, and is that the subject
of the sentence?* If the answer is "no one in this sentence,"
the modifier dangles.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Label every phrase in Set 2 of the sentence bank by kind
   before Practice D, and mark which word each phrase modifies.
2. Take Passage A and expand three of its eight sentences with
   a participial, a prepositional, and an absolute phrase
   respectively — one phrase kind per sentence, labeled.
3. Fix these two sentences and explain the error in each:
   (a) "Running to the tower, the bell rang." (b) "Mara found
   the logbook on the shelf with the torn cover."

## Independent practice

1. Rewrite "The keeper wrote in the logbook" four ways, adding
   in turn: a participial phrase, a prepositional phrase, an
   absolute phrase, and an adjectival phrase. Label each.
2. Explain, in a paragraph, why "Tired and salt-stained, the
   crew secured the boat" is stronger than "The crew was tired
   and salt-stained, and the crew secured the boat." Name what
   the phrase version gains (economy, emphasis on the action)
   and what it risks (a dangling modifier if the subject
   changes).
3. Write a six-sentence paragraph about preparing a meal or a
   workspace that uses at least one participial, one
   prepositional, and one absolute phrase. Underline each phrase
   and label its kind in the margin.

## Application

Take the flat paragraph in Passage A and rewrite it as a single
paragraph of four to six sentences that uses at least one phrase
of each kind taught in this lesson (noun, verb, adjectival,
adverbial, participial, prepositional, absolute). After the
paragraph, list each phrase you used, its kind, and the word or
clause it modifies. A paragraph that uses all seven kinds but
loses a fact from Passage A has failed the task — meaning first,
variety second.

## Exit check

On an index card: (1) define **absolute phrase** in your own
words and give an original example; (2) repair "Walking home,
the rain started" and name the error; (3) state one difference
between a participial phrase and an adverbial clause.
(Answers: (2) dangling modifier — e.g., "Walking home, Mara
was caught by the rain"; (3) a participial phrase has no
subject–verb pair, while an adverbial clause has both.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read Set 2 aloud
  while the learner physically moves phrase cards (one phrase
  per card) next to the noun each phrase modifies.
- **Alternate response mode:** phrase labels may be color-coded
  (one color per kind) instead of written out in full.
- **Extension:** find a sentence in your current reading that
  opens with an absolute phrase, copy it, and explain what the
  sentence would lose if the absolute phrase were deleted.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often label any -ing phrase a participial phrase, even
when the -ing word is the main verb ("The crew was securing the
boat" — "was securing" is a verb phrase, not a participial
phrase). The test: can you remove the phrase and still have a
complete sentence? If yes, it is a modifying phrase; if no, it is
part of the verb. Also watch for absolute phrases mislabeled as
comma splices: "The wind gone, the sea lay flat" has no verb in
the first part ("gone" is a participle, not a tensed verb), so it
is not two independent clauses.
