# Lesson 04 — Vocabulary Systems and Figures of Speech

**Goal (student-readable):** I can figure out an unknown word's
meaning using context, word-change patterns, reference materials,
and verification, and I can interpret euphemism and oxymoron in
context.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
sentence bank, a dictionary (print or free digital) ·
**Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L4](../answer-key.md#lesson-04)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Without a dictionary, what does
"meticulous" most likely mean in this sentence, and which words
are your clues? "The keeper's log was meticulous: every lamp
check, tide time, and visitor was recorded to the minute."
Answer: very careful about details — the clues are the colon
(whatever follows explains the word), "every," and "to the
minute." The colon is doing vocabulary work before any dictionary
is opened.

## Four strategies, used in order — and then checked

The standard names four strategies (L.9-10.4.a–d). A strong
reader uses them flexibly, not in a rigid order, and always
verifies at the end.

**1. Context (L.9-10.4.a).** Use the overall meaning of the
sentence, paragraph, or text, and the word's position or
function, as a clue. Context clues come in kinds: definition
("a meticulous log: every check recorded to the minute"),
example ("she checked the lamp, the oil, and the bell" after
"anticipate"), contrast ("the precise time was 6:42, not 'about
seven'"), and cause–effect ("one loose bolt had a serious
consequence: the shutter jammed"). No single clue is proof; two
clues pointing the same way are much stronger than one.

**2. Word-change patterns (L.9-10.4.b).** Identify and correctly
use patterns of word changes that indicate different meanings or
parts of speech. The standard's own examples are *analyze,
analysis, analytical* and *advocate, advocacy*. This unit's bank
extends the pattern: precise (adjective) → precision (noun);
revise (verb) → revision (noun); transform (verb) →
transformation (noun); evaluate (verb) → evaluation (noun);
derive (verb) → derivation (noun). Knowing one family member
often unlocks the others: if you know "advocate" (argue in
favor), "advocacy" cannot mean "opposition" — the pattern
preserves the root's meaning while changing its job in the
sentence. Beware of false friends: "sustain" and "sustainable"
share a root but "sustainable" has grown a specialized sense
(able to continue without depleting resources) that "sustain"
alone does not carry.

**3. Reference materials (L.9-10.4.c).** Consult general and
specialized reference materials — dictionaries, glossaries,
thesauruses, print or digital — to find pronunciation, precise
meaning, part of speech, or etymology. Read a dictionary entry
like a small report, not a single word: check the part of
speech first (is this "advocate" the verb or the noun?), then
the numbered senses (which sense fits this sentence?), then
the example. A thesaurus gives related words, not definitions —
use it to refine a word you already roughly understand, never
to define a word you do not.

**4. Verification (L.9-10.4.d).** Verify the preliminary
determination — by checking the inferred meaning in context or
in a dictionary. The test is substitution: put your guessed
meaning back into the sentence. "The keeper's log was *very
careful about details*: every check recorded to the minute."
It fits, and it explains "every" and "to the minute." If the
substitution sounds wrong, the guess is wrong, however confident
it felt.

## Worked example 1 — Four strategies on one word

Sentence: "Before she trusted the new chart, Mara evaluated it,
checking its date, its source, and its measurements against the
old chart."

1. **Context:** "checking its date, its source, and its
   measurements" — she is judging the chart, not merely reading
   it. The clause after the comma explains the verb.
2. **Word-change pattern:** "evaluate" → "evaluation." You may
   know "evaluation" from school ("course evaluation" — a
   judgment). The verb must be the act of making that judgment.
3. **Reference:** a dictionary entry for "evaluate" (verb)
   gives, in its first sense, "to judge or determine the
   significance, worth, or condition of." That matches the
   context, not a competing sense like "to calculate."
4. **Verification:** substitute: "Mara *judged* it, checking
   its date, its source, and its measurements." The sentence
   still makes sense, and the checking now has a purpose. The
   meaning is settled: to judge with reasons.

## Worked example 2 — Figures of speech in context

A **figure of speech** uses words in a way that is not literal
in order to create an effect. Two are named in the standard
(L.9-10.5.a):

- **Euphemism:** a mild or indirect word used in place of one
  that is harsh or blunt. "The old keeper *passed away* last
  winter" softens "died"; a report that a station was
  "downsized" softens "staff were dismissed." The role of a
  euphemism is not to deceive the reader about the facts — the
  reader knows what happened — but to change the emotional
  register: gentler, more formal, or more distant. A writer
  who uses a euphemism unknowingly, in a report that needs
  plain facts, has chosen the wrong register (Lesson 5).
- **Oxymoron:** two contradictory terms placed together, so
  that the contradiction itself says something true. "After the
  storm, a *deafening silence* filled the tower." Silence
  cannot literally deafen; the phrase says the quiet was so
  complete, after so much noise, that it felt loud. "The keeper
  felt a *bittersweet* pride as she handed over the keys":
  pride and sorrow at once, neither canceling the other. The
  role of an oxymoron is to hold two truths that a single plain
  word would flatten.

The analysis question for any figure of speech is always the
same: *what would be lost if the writer had said it plainly?*
If the answer is "nothing," the figure is decoration. If the
answer names a feeling, a tension, or a register shift, the
figure is doing work.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. For vocabulary sentences 2, 3, 7, and 9 in the sentence
   bank, name the context-clue kind (definition, example,
   contrast, cause–effect) and write the guessed meaning before
   checking a dictionary.
2. Build the word families for analyze, advocate, precise,
   revise, and transform: give the noun, verb, and adjective
   form of each where one exists, and mark any form whose
   meaning has shifted (like "sustainable").
3. Read these two sentences and name the figure and its role:
   (a) "The committee said the program would be 'sunsetted'
   rather than closed." (b) "It was an open secret that the
   tower would close."

## Independent practice

1. Choose three words from the word bank you did not know
   before this unit. For each, write: the sentence that
   introduced it, your context-based guess, the dictionary
   sense that fits, and the substitution test in one sentence.
2. Write two original sentences: one using a euphemism
   deliberately and well (and a note saying what plain word it
   replaces and why the softer word is right there), and one
   using an oxymoron deliberately and well (with a note saying
   what two truths it holds together).
3. Explain in a paragraph why "verify" is a separate strategy
   rather than a repeat of "consult a reference": give a case
   where the dictionary alone could mislead you (for example,
   a word with several senses, or a specialized sense in a
   technical context) and show how the substitution test
   catches the error.

## Application

Write a 150–200-word paragraph about a keeper handing a station
over to a successor. Use at least four word-bank words accurately
(underline them), one euphemism, and one oxymoron, each labeled
in the margin with its role in one sentence. The paragraph must
still read as a coherent scene, not as a list of required words
— a word used inaccurately counts against the paragraph even if
all the required items are present.

## Exit check

On an index card: (1) name the four vocabulary strategies in
your own words; (2) give the noun form of "precise" and the verb
form of "analysis"; (3) identify the figure in "a cruel
kindness" and state its role in one sentence. (Answers:
(2) precision; analyze. (3) oxymoron — it claims an act was
kind in intent and cruel in effect at the same time.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may pre-teach the
  word-bank words orally with gestures or images before the
  written work, and allow dictionary use in the learner's home
  language where available.
- **Alternate response mode:** vocabulary work may be done as
  a word map (word in the center; context clue, family, guessed
  meaning, verified meaning around it) instead of sentences.
- **Extension:** research one word's etymology in a dictionary
  (for example, "derive" from Latin *derivare*, "to lead a
  stream from its source") and explain how the origin still
  shows in the modern meaning.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat the first dictionary sense as the meaning,
even when the sentence needs the third. Teach sense selection
explicitly: part of speech first, then fit to context, then
substitution. Also watch for euphemisms being read as lies:
a euphemism is a register choice, and its honesty is judged by
whether the reader can still recover the plain fact — "passed
away" passes that test; a euphemism that hides who acted
("mistakes were made") is a different, and weaker, move.
