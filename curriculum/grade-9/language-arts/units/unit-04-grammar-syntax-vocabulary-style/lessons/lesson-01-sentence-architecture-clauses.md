# Lesson 01 — Sentence Architecture: Clauses and Sentence Types

**Goal (student-readable):** I can identify independent and
dependent clauses (noun, relative, adverbial), name the four
sentence types, and repair fragments and run-ons.

and how do you know? (a) "The keeper lit the lamp." (b) "Because

is not there. (c) has no verb at all.

## A clause is a subject plus a verb

A **clause** is a group of words with a subject and a verb. There
are two kinds, and the difference is whether the clause can stand
alone as a sentence.

- An **independent clause** can stand alone: "The keeper lit the
  lamp." Subject: keeper. Verb: lit.
- A **dependent clause** has a subject and a verb but cannot stand
  alone, because a word at its start makes it depend on another
  clause: "because the tide turned" (subject: tide; verb: turned —
  but "because" leaves the thought unfinished: because the tide
  turned… *what happened?*).

Dependent clauses come in three useful kinds, named by the job they
do in the sentence:

- A **noun clause** does a noun's job — often as the object of a
  verb: "The keeper knew **that the storm was coming**." The whole
  clause is what she knew. Test: replace the clause with "it" —
  "The keeper knew it" still works.
- A **relative clause** describes a noun, and usually starts with
  a **relative pronoun** (who, whom, whose, which, that): "The
  lamp, **which had burned all night**, guided the boat home."
  The clause tells you more about "the lamp." Commas matter here:
  "which" clauses that add extra information take commas; a
  restrictive "that" clause that identifies *which* one ("The boat
  **that carried no lantern** passed unseen") takes no commas,
  because without it you would not know which boat.
- An **adverbial clause** answers when, why, where, or under what
  condition, and starts with a **subordinating conjunction**
  (because, when, while, although, if, after, before, since,
  unless): "**Because the tide turned**, the crew secured the
  boat." Put the adverbial clause first and a comma follows it;
  put it after the main clause and usually no comma is needed:
  "The crew secured the boat because the tide turned."

## Four sentence types

Count independent and dependent clauses, and every sentence falls
into one of four types:

- **Simple:** one independent clause, no dependent clause. "The
  gulls circled the rocks." (A simple sentence can still have a
  compound subject or verb, and phrases — "simple" describes the
  clause count, not the length.)
- **Compound:** two or more independent clauses, joined by a comma
  plus a coordinating conjunction (and, but, for, nor, or, so, yet)
  or by a semicolon (Lesson 3). "The gulls circled the rocks, and
  the keeper watched from the tower."
- **Complex:** one independent clause plus one or more dependent
  clauses. "When the fog thickened, the keeper lit the lamp."
- **Compound-complex:** two or more independent clauses plus one or
  more dependent clauses. "When the fog thickened, the keeper lit
  the lamp, and the boats turned toward the light."

## Worked example 1 — Taking a sentence apart

Sentence: "When the fog thickened, the keeper lit the lamp, and
the boats turned toward the light."

1. Find the verbs: thickened, lit, turned. Three verbs — but are
   there three clauses? Yes: each verb has its own subject (fog,
   keeper, boats).
2. "When the fog thickened" — subject fog, verb thickened, starts
   with "when." Dependent adverbial clause (tells when).
3. "the keeper lit the lamp" — subject keeper, verb lit. It could
   stand alone. Independent clause.
4. "the boats turned toward the light" — subject boats, verb
   turned. It could stand alone. Independent clause, joined by
   ", and."
5. Count: 2 independent + 1 dependent = **compound-complex**.

The comma after "thickened" is there because the dependent clause
comes first. Move it to the end — "The keeper lit the lamp, and
the boats turned toward the light, when the fog thickened" — and
the sentence is still grammatical but clumsier: the timing now
dangles at the end instead of setting the scene.

## Worked example 2 — Three repairs, three different errors

**Fragment:** "Because the tide turned." The repair depends on
what the writer meant. Attach it to a main clause: "Because the
tide turned, the crew secured the boat." Or drop "because": "The
tide turned." Never "fix" a fragment by adding a period somewhere
else; ask what thought is missing.

**Comma splice:** "The lamp burned all night, the boats found the
harbor." Two independent clauses joined by only a comma. Three
honest repairs, each with a different feel: a period ("The lamp
burned all night. The boats found the harbor."); a comma plus
"and" ("The lamp burned all night, and the boats found the
harbor."); a semicolon ("The lamp burned all night; the boats
found the harbor." — Lesson 3 explains why this one claims the
two thoughts belong together).

**Run-on (fused sentence):** "The keeper rang the bell the boats
turned toward the sound." No punctuation at all between two
independent clauses. Repair the same three ways; the period is the
safest first choice when you are unsure.

A common misconception: a long sentence is not automatically a
run-on, and a short sentence is not automatically a fragment.
Length is not the test. The test is always: how many independent
clauses, and how are they joined?

## Independent practice

1. Combine these three short sentences into one compound-complex
   sentence, then into one complex sentence, and explain what
   each version emphasizes: "The fog thickened. The keeper lit the
   lamp. The boats turned toward the light."
2. Repair this paragraph (two fragments, one comma splice — find
   them before you fix them): "Mara climbed the tower. Because
   she wanted to see the harbor. The fog was thick, she could see
   nothing. She waited."
3. Write a paragraph of five sentences about a job you know well
   (real or imagined) that uses at least one sentence of each of
   the four types. Label the type of each sentence in the margin.

## Application

Take Passage A and rewrite it as exactly three sentences — no
more, no fewer — without dropping any of its eight facts (keeper

## Exit check

On an index card: define **dependent clause** in your own words,
give one example of each dependent-clause kind (noun, relative,
adverbial), and name the sentence type of "Although the bell
rang, the boats kept their course, and the keeper logged the
hour." (Answer: compound-complex — "although" clause is dependent;
"the boats kept their course" and "the keeper logged the hour"
are independent.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read the lesson
  examples aloud while the learner marks subjects with one color
  and verbs with another; the clause count then becomes visible
  before any labeling.
- **Alternate response mode:** clause labels may be given as a
  diagram (boxes for clauses, arrows for "depends on") instead of
  a written paragraph.
- **Extension:** find a compound-complex sentence in your current
  reading, diagram its clauses, and rewrite it as two sentences.
  Which version is clearer, and what, if anything, did the split
  lose?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often call any clause starting with "that" a relative
clause. "That the storm was coming" after "knew" is a noun clause
(it is what she knew); "that carried no lantern" after "boat" is
a relative clause (it identifies the boat). The test is the job,
not the first word. Also watch for "and" as a false signal: "The
keeper lit the lamp and logged the hour" is a simple sentence
(one subject, compound verb), not a compound sentence — there is
only one independent clause.
