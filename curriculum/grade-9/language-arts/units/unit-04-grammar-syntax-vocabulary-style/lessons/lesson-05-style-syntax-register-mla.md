# Lesson 05 — Style: Syntax Choices, Register, and MLA Basics

**Goal (student-readable):** I can choose sentence shapes,
register, and voice for meaning and style, and format edited
work to MLA guidelines.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
Passage A · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Which sentence is stronger, and
why? (a) "The lamp was lit by the keeper at dusk, and the boats
were guided by the light." (b) "At dusk the keeper lit the
lamp, and its light guided the boats." Answer: (b) — the active
voice names who acted, the fronted time phrase sets the scene,
and the second clause's subject ("its light") picks up the
first clause's object, so the sentence moves forward instead
of circling.

## Language functions differently in different contexts

The standard's Knowledge of Language strand (L.9-10.3) asks the
writer to apply knowledge of language to understand how language
functions in different contexts, to make effective choices for
meaning or style, and to comprehend more fully when reading.
Three choices carry most of the style work in this unit.

**1. Sentence length and shape as emphasis.** A long,
cumulative sentence builds pressure: "She checked the wick,
trimmed the flame, logged the hour, and climbed to the gallery
to watch the fog thin over the rocks." Then a short sentence
lands the decision: "She stayed." Readers feel a short sentence
after a long one the way they feel a door closing — the shape
is the emphasis. A paragraph of all-long sentences is tiring;
a paragraph of all-short sentences is choppy (Passage A). Style
is the *pattern* of lengths, not any one length.

**2. Parallel structure.** Items in a series should share a
grammatical shape: "She checked the wick, trimmed the flame,
and logged the hour" (three verbs, same form). The faulty
version — "She checked the wick, trimming the flame, and the
hour was logged" — forces the reader to re-parse at every item.
Parallelism is not fussiness; it is a promise to the reader
that items in a list are equals.

**3. Register and voice.** **Register** is the level of
formality a situation calls for. A logbook entry, a letter to
a friend, and a text message are three registers, and the same
fact wears different words in each:

- Text message: "lamp's out again, ugh, fixing it now"
- Letter to a friend: "The lamp went out again last night —
  I was up half the night fixing it."
- Logbook: "Lamp extinguished 02:14. Wick replaced. Lamp
  relit 02:40. Cause: oil feed blocked."

None is "better" in the abstract; each is right or wrong for
its audience and purpose. A logbook written like a text message
fails its job (a future keeper cannot trust it); a letter to a
friend written like a logbook fails its job too (it withholds
the person). **Active voice** ("the keeper lit the lamp")
names the actor and is usually clearer; **passive voice**
("the lamp was lit at dusk") is right when the actor is
unknown, unimportant, or deliberately backgrounded ("The tower
was built in 1890" — the builder's name is not the point of
that sentence). Passive voice is a tool, not a fault; a
paragraph of nothing but passive sentences is a fault, because
it hides every actor at once.

## Worked example 1 — The same paragraph in two registers

**Informal (letter to a friend):**

> You won't believe the night I had. The lamp went out right
> in the middle of the fog, and I had to climb up there in the
> dark to fix it. I was so tired afterward I just sat on the
> gallery and watched the boats go by.

**Formal (logbook / report):**

> At 02:14 during heavy fog, the lamp was extinguished. The
> keeper ascended to the lamp room, replaced the wick, and
> relit the lamp at 02:40. Visibility remained poor; the bell
> was rung at fifteen-minute intervals until 04:00.

What changed, step by step? Contractions and exclamations
gone; vague time ("in the middle of") replaced by exact time
("02:14"); the narrator's feelings ("so tired") replaced by
observable actions and measurements; the passive voice used
where the procedure, not the person, is the record. What did
*not* change: the facts. Register changes how facts are
dressed, never which facts are told. A register change that
quietly drops or invents a fact is not style — it is error.

## Worked example 2 — Editing for MLA (L.9-10.3.a)

A **style manual** gives a discipline's agreed rules for
formatting and citing sources. For school writing in this
track, the manual is the *MLA Handbook* (9th edition), as
summarized in Purdue OWL's MLA guide. The rules a grade-9
writer must actually apply are few, and they are checked in
the project and in U06/U07:

- **First page:** your name, instructor's name, course, and
  date in the upper left; a centered title (not bold, not
  underlined, not in quotation marks unless it quotes another
  work); a header with your last name and the page number in
  the upper right.
- **Body:** double-spaced throughout; one-inch margins;
  readable 12-point font; first line of each paragraph indented
  half an inch.
- **In-text citations:** author–page style — the author's name
  in the sentence or in parentheses, with the page number in
  parentheses: "Jacobs stages the climax offstage (42)." If no
  author is listed, use a shortened title.
- **Works Cited:** a separate page at the end, titled "Works
  Cited" (centered, not italicized); entries in alphabetical
  order by the author's last name; double-spaced, with a
  hanging indent (first line flush left, later lines indented
  half an inch); every entry ends with a period.

This unit does not reproduce the MLA Handbook, which is a
copyrighted book. It teaches the rules above, and the Resource
Pack points to Purdue OWL for the full guide and examples.
When a rule in this unit and a rule in OWL appear to conflict,
OWL — and the adult — decide.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Rewrite the informal paragraph in Worked example 1 as a
   logbook entry, then rewrite the formal paragraph as a
   letter to a friend. Compare the two rewrites with the
   originals: which facts survived in each, and which facts
   (if any) did a register change tempt you to drop?
2. Repair the parallelism in: "The keeper's duties were
   checking the wick, to trim the flame, and log the hour."
   Then explain, in one sentence, why the repaired version is
   easier to read.
3. Decide active or passive for each sentence, and justify
   it: (a) "The tower was built in 1890." (b) "Mistakes were
   made in the log." (c) "Mara signed the log at midnight."

## Independent practice

1. Take Passage A and rewrite it twice: once as a logbook
   entry (formal register, exact times invented only if you
   label them as invented — better: keep the facts and change
   the wording, not the facts), and once as a letter to a
   friend. Each version must keep all eight facts.
2. Write a paragraph of six sentences about a routine task
   that uses: one long cumulative sentence, one short sentence
   for emphasis, one parallel series, and one deliberate
   passive sentence. Label each device in the margin.
3. Format a one-page essay opening to MLA rules: heading,
   centered title, header, double spacing, indented paragraph,
   and one author–page in-text citation for a source you name
   in a two-entry Works Cited list (use real sources you have
   actually consulted, or clearly label the entries as practice
   entries).

## Application

Choose one paragraph from your own U01–U03 writing. Rewrite it
in a different register (formal to informal, or informal to
formal), keeping every fact. Then write a 150-word note
answering: what did the register change cost, what did it
gain, and which register serves this paragraph's actual
purpose better? Bring the paragraph and the note to Lesson 6.

## Exit check

On an index card: (1) state one situation where passive voice
is the right choice; (2) repair "She liked hiking, to swim,
and biking" for parallelism; (3) name three MLA first-page
requirements. (Answers: (1) actor unknown/unimportant, e.g.,
"The tower was built in 1890"; (2) "She liked hiking,
swimming, and biking"; (3) any three of: name/instructor/
course/date block, centered title, last-name-plus-page-number
header, one-inch margins, double spacing.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read the two
  registers in Worked example 1 aloud in contrasting voices
  (conversational vs. report-flat) so the register difference
  is audible before it is analyzed.
- **Alternate response mode:** register rewrites may be
  dictated and transcribed by the adult; the style decisions,
  not the handwriting, are the assessed work.
- **Extension:** take a paragraph from a public-domain text
  and rewrite it in the opposite register, then argue whether
  the original register was a choice or a constraint of its
  time and genre.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often equate "formal" with "long words" and "passive
voice." Formal writing is precise and audience-aware, not
padded: "The lamp went out at 02:14" is formal and short.
Watch also for MLA treated as decoration: the heading, header,
and Works Cited exist so a reader can *find* the writer and
the sources. A perfectly formatted page that cites a source
never consulted is a citation failure, not a formatting
success.
