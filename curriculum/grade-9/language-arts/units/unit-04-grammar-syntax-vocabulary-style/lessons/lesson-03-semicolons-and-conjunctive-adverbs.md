# Lesson 03 — Semicolons and Conjunctive Adverbs

**Goal (student-readable):** I can use a semicolon to link
closely related independent clauses, punctuate conjunctive
adverbs correctly, and use semicolons in a complex list.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
Passage B · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Punctuate this pair three ways —
with a period, with a comma plus "and," and with a semicolon:
"The fog lifted / the boats returned." Then say, in one
sentence, what the semicolon version claims that the period
version does not. Answer: the semicolon version claims the two
thoughts belong together as one idea; the period version leaves
them as two separate statements.

## The semicolon's one job (and its second job)

A **semicolon** (;) links two or more closely related
**independent clauses** — the standard's exact wording
(L.9-10.2.a). That is its main job. A period would also be
grammatical; the semicolon adds a claim: *these two thoughts are
one thought, in two parts.*

- "The fog lifted; the boats returned." Both sides could stand
  alone. The semicolon says the return follows from the lifting
  closely enough to keep them in one sentence.
- "The fog lifted. The boats returned." Also correct, and
  weaker: the connection is left for the reader to infer.

A semicolon never joins an independent clause to a dependent
clause ("The fog lifted; because the boats returned" is wrong),
and never joins a clause to a phrase ("The fog lifted; the boats"
is not a sentence). Both sides must be able to stand alone.

Its second job is in a **complex list**: when list items
themselves contain commas, semicolons separate the items so the
reader can tell where one item ends and the next begins: "The
crew visited Portland, Maine; Salem, Massachusetts; and
Newport, Rhode Island." With commas only, "Portland, Maine,
Salem, Massachusetts" would read as four places, not three.

## Conjunctive adverbs: the semicolon–comma pair

A **conjunctive adverb** is a word or phrase that connects
independent clauses while showing the relationship between
them: however (contrast), therefore and consequently (result),
moreover and furthermore (addition), otherwise (condition),
nevertheless (concession), meanwhile (time). When a conjunctive
adverb joins two independent clauses, the punctuation is a
fixed pair: **semicolon before it, comma after it**.

- "The fog lifted; however, the sea stayed rough."
- "The lamp was lit; therefore, the boats could find the harbor."
- "The keeper logged the hour; moreover, she noted the wind."

Move the conjunctive adverb and the punctuation moves with the
clause boundary, not with the word: "The fog lifted; the sea,
however, stayed rough." Here "however" sits inside the second
clause, so the semicolon still marks the boundary between the
two clauses, and commas set "however" off inside its clause.

Two traps:

1. **The comma splice in disguise:** "The fog lifted, however,
   the sea stayed rough." "However" is not a coordinating
   conjunction, so a comma alone cannot join the clauses. This
   is the single most common semicolon error in grade-9 writing,
   and Practice G hunts it in Passage B.
2. **The conjunctive adverb that is not joining anything:**
   "However, the sea stayed rough." At the start of a sentence,
   "however" is an ordinary adverb, and a comma after it is all
   that is needed. The semicolon appears only when the adverb
   stands *between* two independent clauses.

Contrast with coordinating conjunctions (and, but, for, nor,
or, so, yet): those take a **comma** before them, not a
semicolon — "The fog lifted, but the sea stayed rough." And
contrast with subordinating conjunctions (because, when,
although): those make a clause dependent, so no semicolon is
involved at all.

## Worked example 1 — Choosing among period, comma-and, and semicolon

Original pair: "The keeper rang the bell. The boats turned
toward the sound."

- **Period:** two facts, equal weight, no claimed connection
  beyond sequence. Use it when the facts are genuinely separate,
  or when either clause is long and complex.
- **Comma + and:** "The keeper rang the bell, and the boats
  turned toward the sound." The connection is sequence plus
  addition — the second event follows the first.
- **Semicolon:** "The keeper rang the bell; the boats turned
  toward the sound." The connection is tighter: the turning is
  presented as the direct counterpart of the ringing, almost its
  echo. A careful writer chooses the semicolon here only if
  that tight pairing is the point.

Now a pair where the semicolon is the *wrong* choice:
"The keeper rang the bell; the next morning, she wrote a
letter." The two thoughts are not closely related in content —
a bell and a letter the next day. A period is honest; a
semicolon would pretend to a connection the sentence does not
have.

## Worked example 2 — Repairing a paragraph

Faulty paragraph: "The work is steady, the pay is fair, the
food is plain. I will write again soon, I promise to send the
photograph."

Step by step:

1. "The work is steady, the pay is fair, the food is plain."
   Three independent clauses joined by commas — a double comma
   splice. Repair with semicolons only if the three are meant
   as one balanced list of judgments: "The work is steady; the
   pay is fair; the food is plain." That works, because the
   three clauses are parallel and closely related. Or use
   periods for three separate judgments.
2. "I will write again soon, I promise to send the
   photograph." Two independent clauses, one comma. Repair:
   "I will write again soon, and I promise to send the
   photograph." Or a period. A semicolon would also be
   grammatical but would claim a closeness ("writing again"
   and "promising a photograph") that is thinner than the
   first example's.

Rule of thumb to take into Lesson 6: use a semicolon when you
can defend, in one sentence, *why* the two clauses belong
together. If you cannot, use a period.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. In Passage B, find every comma splice and every
   semicolon-related error before Practice G. Do not fix them
   yet — listing is the skill being practiced here.
2. Combine each pair below with a semicolon and a suitable
   conjunctive adverb, then read the result aloud and check
   that both sides still stand alone: (a) "The oil ran low /
   the lamp stayed lit." (b) "The chart was old / Mara checked
   it against the new survey." (c) "The bell was loud / the
   fog swallowed the sound."
3. Write a three-item complex list about a journey, a pantry,
   or a toolkit, in which at least two items contain commas
   (city + state, item + detail), and punctuate it with
   semicolons.

## Independent practice

1. Punctuate: "The tide turned however the crew stayed at the
   dock." Explain the two marks you added and why.
2. Decide period, comma + conjunction, or semicolon for each
   pair, and justify each choice in one sentence:
   (a) "Mara trimmed the wick / the flame steadied."
   (b) "The storm passed / the damage remained."
   (c) "The log was complete / the inspector signed it."
3. Correct this paragraph, naming each error as you fix it:
   "The lamp burned all night, the boats found the harbor,
   however, one boat turned back, it had lost its lantern."

## Application

Write a paragraph of five sentences about a task done carefully
(cooking, repairing, rehearsing, keeping a record) that uses
exactly one semicolon between independent clauses, exactly one
semicolon with a conjunctive adverb, and exactly one complex
list with semicolons. Label each semicolon in the margin with
its job. A paragraph with a fourth semicolon, or with a
semicolon joining a dependent clause, has missed the target —
precision is the point.

## Exit check

On an index card: punctuate "The fog lifted therefore the
boats returned" correctly, and punctuate "The crew visited
Austin, Texas, Boston, Massachusetts, and Dover, Delaware" as
a list. (Answers: "The fog lifted; therefore, the boats
returned." and "The crew visited Austin, Texas; Boston,
Massachusetts; and Dover, Delaware.")

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read pairs aloud
  with a long pause for a period, a short pause for a comma,
  and a medium pause for a semicolon, so the punctuation's
  "weight" is audible before it is written.
- **Alternate response mode:** semicolon decisions may be
  shown by cutting printed clauses apart and joining them with
  period, comma-and, or semicolon cards, then defending the
  choice orally.
- **Extension:** find three semicolons in your current reading
  (or explain, honestly, that your reading uses none), copy
  each sentence, and state for each whether a period would
  have weakened it — and why.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat the semicolon as a "fancy comma" or a
"strong comma." It is neither: it only joins independent
clauses, and it claims a close relationship. Watch also for
semicolons before "because" or "when" clauses, and for the
belief that a long list needs semicolons even when its items
contain no commas — it does not.
