# Answer Key — Unit 04 (Grade 9 Language Arts)

**For the adult only.** Do not hand this file to the learner.
Every practice, quiz, and assessment item is solved here; open
responses give a sample answer and the criteria for accepting
variations. A variation that meets the stated criteria scores
full marks even if its wording differs from the sample.

## Lesson 01

- **Readiness:** (a) complete; (b) fragment (dependent clause
  alone); (c) fragment (no verb).
- **Guided 2:** Passage A has eight independent clauses, one
  per sentence.
- **Independent 1 (sample):** complex — "When the fog
  thickened, the keeper lit the lamp, and the boats turned
  toward the light" is actually compound-complex; the honest
  complex version is "When the fog thickened, the keeper lit
  the lamp." The compound-complex version foregrounds the
  boats' response as an equal event; the complex version
  foregrounds the keeper's action with the fog as timing.
  Accept any pair that correctly distinguishes the two types
  and names what each foregrounds.
- **Independent 2:** fragments — "Because she wanted to see
  the harbor." Comma splice — "The fog was thick, she could
  see nothing." Repairs: "Mara climbed the tower because she
  wanted to see the harbor." and "The fog was thick; she could
  see nothing." (or a period).
- **Exit check:** compound-complex: "although" clause
  dependent; "the boats kept their course" and "the keeper
  logged the hour" independent.

## Lesson 02

- **Readiness:** (a) phrase, (b) clause, (c) phrase, (d) clause.
- **Guided 3:** (a) dangling — "Running to the tower, the bell
  rang" repaired as "Running to the tower, Mara heard the
  bell." (b) misplaced — the torn cover belongs to the
  logbook: "Mara found the logbook with the torn cover on the
  shelf."
- **Exit check:** (1) absolute phrase: noun + participle
  modifying the whole sentence, e.g., "The tide having turned,
  the boats left." (2) dangling modifier — "Walking home,
  Mara was caught by the rain." (3) a participial phrase has
  no subject–verb pair; an adverbial clause has both.

## Lesson 03

- **Readiness:** period / comma + and / semicolon versions as
  in the lesson; the semicolon claims the thoughts belong
  together.
- **Guided 2 (sample):** (a) "The oil ran low; nevertheless,
  the lamp stayed lit." (b) "The chart was old; however, Mara
  checked it against the new survey." (c) "The bell was loud;
  nevertheless, the fog swallowed the sound." Any conjunctive
  adverb whose logic fits scores; "therefore" in (a) or (c)
  does not fit and is marked wrong on logic, not punctuation.
- **Independent 1:** "The tide turned; however, the crew stayed
  at the dock." Semicolon before "however," comma after it.
- **Independent 2:** (a) semicolon or period — the steadying
  follows directly from the trimming; (b) period — passing
  and remaining damage are separate facts; (c) semicolon —
  signing follows from completeness as one judged event. Accept
  any choice defended in one sentence that names the
  relationship; reject a semicolon defended only as "sounds
  better."
- **Independent 3:** "The lamp burned all night; the boats
  found the harbor. However, one boat turned back; it had lost
  its lantern." Errors: comma splice (lamp/boats), comma
  splice disguised by "however" (harbor/turned back — needs
  period + "However," or a semicolon), comma splice (turned
  back/lost lantern).
- **Exit check:** "The fog lifted; therefore, the boats
  returned." and "The crew visited Austin, Texas; Boston,
  Massachusetts; and Dover, Delaware."

## Lesson 04

- **Readiness:** meticulous = very careful about details;
  clues: colon, "every," "to the minute."
- **Guided 3:** (a) euphemism — "sunsetted" softens "closed/
  ended"; role: distance/formality, not deception about the
  fact. (b) oxymoron — "open secret": known yet
  unacknowledged; role: holds both truths at once.
- **Exit check:** four strategies in own words; precision;
  analyze; oxymoron "cruel kindness" — kind in intent, cruel
  in effect at the same time.

## Lesson 05

- **Readiness:** (b) is stronger: active voice names the
  actor; fronted time sets the scene; "its light" picks up
  the first clause's object.
- **Guided 2:** "The keeper's duties were checking the wick,
  trimming the flame, and logging the hour." (parallel gerunds).
- **Guided 3:** (a) passive justified — builder unknown/
  unimportant, date is the point. (b) passive unjustified as
  written — it hides who made the mistakes; prefer active
  unless the actor is genuinely unknown. (c) active correct —
  Mara is the actor and the log's authority depends on naming
  her.
- **Exit check:** (1) actor unknown/unimportant; (2) "She
  liked hiking, swimming, and biking"; (3) any three MLA
  first-page requirements from the lesson.

## Lesson 06

- **Readiness:** revising changes what is said and how it is
  structured; editing changes how sentences say it.
- **Exit check:** passes in order — completeness, structure,
  punctuation, words, style/format, revision log. Semicolon
  test: "I can say, in one sentence, why these two clauses
  belong together."

## Practice A

1. Simple — IC only.
2. Complex — IC "The keeper knew" + noun clause "that the
   storm was coming" (object of "knew").
3. Complex — IC "The lamp guided the boat home" + relative
   clause "which had burned all night" (nonrestrictive,
   commas; describes "lamp").
4. Complex — adverbial clause "Because the tide turned" +
   IC "the crew secured the boat."
5. Compound — IC "The gulls circled the rocks" + IC "the
   keeper watched from the tower" (comma + and).
6. Compound-complex — adverbial "When the fog thickened" +
   IC "the keeper lit the lamp" + IC "the boats turned toward
   the light."
7. Complex — IC "The boat passed unseen" + relative clause
   "that carried no lantern" (restrictive, no commas;
   identifies which boat).
8. Complex — IC "Mara wrote" + adverbial clause "while the
   tide rose."

## Practice B (sample)

1. "When the keeper climbed the tower, she found the lamp
   cold." — complex (climb + cold).
2. "She trimmed the wick and lit the lamp." — simple
   (compound verb; wick + lighting).
3. "Because the fog was thick, the boats could not see the
   light." — complex (fog + boats' blindness).
4. "She rang the bell, and the boats turned toward the
   sound." — compound (bell + turning).

Explanation sample: the climb and the fog are put in
dependent clauses because they are the conditions for the
main actions (finding the lamp cold, the boats' blindness);
the trimming/lighting and the ringing/turning stay in main
clauses because they are the keeper's decisive actions.
Accept any rewrite that keeps all eight facts and whose
explanation names a foreground/background reason.

## Practice C

1. Fragment — "Because the tide turned, the crew secured the
   boat." (or "The tide turned.")
2. Comma splice — "The lamp burned all night; the boats found
   the harbor." (period or ", and" also correct).
3. Run-on (fused) — "The keeper rang the bell. The boats
   turned toward the sound."
4. Fragment — "Mara climbed the tower because she wanted to
   see the harbor."
5. Comma splice — "The fog was thick; she could see nothing."
6. Fragment — "She was running to the tower." (Any repair
   giving the phrase a subject and verb scores.)
7. Triple comma splice — "The log was complete; the inspector
   signed it; the crew celebrated." (periods also correct).
8. Comma splice — "The boats turned toward the sound because
   they had heard the bell." (semicolon version also correct).

## Practice D

1. Participial phrase — modifies "crew."
2. Prepositional phrase (adverbial) — modifies "climbed"
   (when).
3. Absolute phrase — modifies the whole sentence (the sea's
   flatness follows from the wind's absence).
4. Prepositional phrase (adjectival) — modifies "lamp."
5. Participial phrase — modifies "Mara."
6. Prepositional phrase (adverbial) — modifies "worked" (how).
7. Infinitive phrase, used adverbially (purpose) — modifies
   "climbed." Note: this is an extension beyond the seven
   kinds in L.9-10.1.b; accept "adverbial phrase" with the
   purpose explanation.
8. Participial phrase — modifies "logbook."

## Practice E

1. Sample: participial — "Pausing to check the time, the
   keeper wrote in the logbook." Prepositional — "In the
   early morning, the keeper wrote in the logbook." Absolute
   — "The lamp lit, the keeper wrote in the logbook."
2. Dangling modifier — "Climbing the tower, the keeper saw
   the lamp."
3. Misplaced modifier — "Mara found the logbook with the torn
   cover on the shelf."
4. Dangling modifier — "Walking home, Mara was caught by the
   rain."
5. Sample paragraph (all facts kept): "Tired and salt-stained,
   the keeper climbed the tower in the early morning. The lamp
   cold, she trimmed the wick and lit the lamp. Because the
   fog was thick, the boats could not see the light. She rang
   the bell, and the boats turned toward the sound." Phrases:
   "Tired and salt-stained" (participial, modifies keeper);
   "in the early morning" (prepositional, modifies climbed);
   "The lamp cold" (absolute, modifies the whole sentence's
   situation). Accept any version keeping all facts with
   correctly labeled phrases.
6. Sample paragraph: the phrase version is stronger because
   it puts the crew's state in the background (a phrase) and
   the action in the foreground (the main clause), in fewer
   words; the two-clause version repeats "the crew" and gives
   equal weight to state and action. Its risk: if the subject
   changes ("Tired and salt-stained, the boat was secured"),
   the phrase dangles.

## Practice G — Corrected "A Letter Home"

> Dear Ana,
>
> I arrived at the station on Monday. The road was washed out,
> so I walked the last two miles. The keeper met me at the
> gate, and she showed me the logbook and the lamp room.
> Because the fog was thick, I stayed on the tower until
> midnight. I logged every passing light; however, I missed
> the small fishing boat that passed without a lantern.
>
> The work is steady; the pay is fair; the food is plain. I
> will write again soon, and I promise to send the photograph
> of the tower at dusk.
>
> Yours, Mara

Errors fixed, in order: (1) comma splice — "I arrived…,
the road was washed out" → two sentences; (2) comma splice —
"met me…, she showed" → comma + "and"; (3) fragment —
"Because the fog was thick." attached to "I stayed…";
(4) comma splice + conjunctive-adverb pair — "I stayed…,
I logged…, however, I missed…" → period, then "I logged…;
however, I missed…"; (5) triple comma splice — work/pay/food
→ defended semicolons (three parallel judgments); (6) comma
splice — "I will write…, I promise…" → comma + "and." No
fact was changed.

## Practice H

1. Sentence 2 (anticipate): example clue — checking lamp, oil,
   bell before the clouds → expect and prepare. Sentence 3
   (consequence): cause–effect clue — loose bolt → shutter
   jammed → a result. Sentence 7 (advocate): example clue —
   arguing patiently in every report → argue in favor.
   Sentence 9 (sustain): definition clue — "keeping it burning
   without a break" → keep going over time.
2. Precision (noun of precise); analyze (verb of analysis);
   advocacy (noun of advocate). Meanings stay the same across
   these forms; the part of speech, not the root meaning,
   changes.
3. (a) Euphemism — "sunsetted" replaces "closed/ended";
   role: softening/distance. (b) Oxymoron — "open secret";
   role: holds known-yet-unacknowledged together. (c)
   Oxymoron — "deafening silence"; role: quiet so complete
   after noise that it feels loud.

## Practice I

1. Sample logbook version (all eight facts): "Tower ascended.
   Lamp cold. Wick trimmed. Lamp lit. Fog thick; boats unable
   to see light. Bell rung. Boats turned toward sound."
   Sample letter version: "You should have seen my morning —
   I climbed the tower to find the lamp cold, so I trimmed
   the wick and lit it, but the fog was so thick the boats
   couldn't see the light, so I rang the bell and they turned
   toward the sound." Accept any pair keeping all eight facts;
   the logbook's telegraphic style is correct for its
   register, not a fragment error, because each entry is a
   conventional log shorthand — the adult should note this
   explicitly so the learner does not learn that log style
   equals sentence fragments in prose.
2. Parallelism: "The keeper's duties were checking the wick,
   trimming the flame, and logging the hour."
3. (a) Passive justified — builder unknown/unimportant.
   (b) Passive unjustified as written — hides the actor;
   prefer active ("Mara made mistakes in the log" only if
   that fact is known; otherwise name the unknown honestly).
   (c) Active correct — Mara is the actor.
4. Sample paragraph: "On Sunday mornings, I make soup by
   washing the vegetables, peeling the carrots, and slicing
   the onions into the pot (parallel series), and while the
   pot simmers, I set the table, wipe the counters, and fold
   the napkins (long cumulative sentence). The soup is served
   at noon (deliberate passive: the server is not the point;
   the time is). It is ready." Devices labeled in the margin;
   accept any paragraph meeting all four requirements.

## Practice J

No fixed answers: the learner's own writing is the material.
The adult checks: Pass 1 errors all found; phrase repairs give
the phrase its true subject; every semicolon has a
one-sentence closeness defense (a semicolon without one is
changed to a period); every replaced word has a reason;
MLA formatting is confirmed or honestly noted as not
applicable; the log groups changes by pass; the three
highlighted changes each have a two-sentence meaning defense.

## Formative quiz — Week 17 (20 points)

**Part A (8):** 1. Simple — IC only. 2. Complex — IC "Mara
knew" + noun clause "that the bell had rung." 3. Complex —
adverbial "Although the fog was thick" + IC "the boats kept
their course." 4. Compound — IC + IC. 5. Complex — IC "The
chart was out of date" + relative "that Mara trusted"
(restrictive, no commas). 6. Compound-complex — adverbial
"When the tide rose" + IC "the crew secured the boat" + IC
"the keeper logged the hour." 7. Simple — "Tired" is a
participial adjective, not a clause; one IC. 8. Complex — IC
"The sea lay flat" + adverbial "because the wind had gone."

**Part B (8, 2 each):** 1. Comma splice — "The work is steady;
the pay is fair." 2. Fragment — "The storm passed." (Attaching
it requires a main clause the writer must supply; do not
invent one for the learner.) 3. Comma splice disguised by
"however" — "The lamp was lit; however, the boats could not
see it." 4. Run-on (fused) — "The keeper wrote the log, and
the crew checked the lamps."

**Part C (4):** sample paragraph — "Mara was meticulous about
the storm check: she listed every lamp, rope, and bell, and
recorded each one to the minute. She tried to anticipate the
worst gust, so she secured the shutters before the clouds
arrived." Clues: for "meticulous," the colon + "every… to
the minute" (definition clue); for "anticipate," securing the
shutters before the clouds (example clue). Score: 1 point per
accurately used word, 1 point per correctly named clue kind.
Reteach if total < 16.

## Unit assessment (30 points)

**Part A (6):** 1. Simple — participial "hoping for a clear
night" modifies "apprentice"; one IC. 2. Complex — relative
"which had cracked in the winter" (nonrestrictive, commas)
modifies "lens"; one IC. 3. Compound-complex — adverbial
"After the storm passed" + IC "the crew repaired the gallery"
+ IC "the keeper wrote the report." 4. Complex — noun clause
"that the bell had rung twice" (object of "stated"). 5.
Simple — absolute "The tide having turned" modifies the whole
sentence; one IC "the boats left the harbor." 6. Simple —
one IC with a compound verb (checked, trimmed, logged).

**Part B (8, 2 each):** 1. Complex — "Because the fog lifted,
the boats returned." (or "The boats returned because the fog
lifted.") 2. Compound-complex — "When the fog thickened, the
keeper lit the lamp, and the boats turned toward the light."
3. Dangling modifier — "Repairing the lamp, Mara spread the
tools across the table." (Any named repairer scores; the tools
did not repair the lamp.) 4. Semicolon + conjunctive adverb —
"The chart was old; nevertheless, Mara trusted it." ("However"
also scores; "therefore" does not — it claims the oldness is
a reason to trust, reversing the logic.)

**Part C (8):** sample paragraph — "Mara revised the draft,
replacing vague sentences with precise times and names. The
change transformed a flat report into a clear record, and one
short note clarified the one term readers kept misreading."
Strategies (sample; accept any correctly applied strategy):
"revise" — context (replacing vague sentences shows the act);
"precise" — word-change pattern (precise → precision, same
root meaning); "transform" — verification by substitution
("changed completely" fits the sentence). Score: 1 point per
accurately used word (3), 1 point per correctly applied
strategy with what it shows (3), 2 points for a coherent
paragraph in which all three words are accurate in context.

**Part D (8, 2 per fix):** (1) Fragment — "The editor opened
the manuscript because she wanted to check the citations."
(2) Comma splice — "She found an error, and she corrected it
with a red pen." (3) Misplaced modifier — "On the telephone,
she praised the draft with enthusiasm." (4) Vague word —
"The draft was accurate and clearly organized." (Accept any
precise evaluative replacement that does not invent a new
factual claim about the draft's contents; "good" alone, or a
replacement that changes the facts, scores 0 for that fix.)
Each fix: 1 point for the correct repair, 1 point for a
meaning-based reason (e.g., for fix 1: the "because" clause
needs its main clause; for fix 4: "good" judges nothing
specific, while "accurate and clearly organized" names what
is being praised).

## Project notes

- **Excellent architecture sample:** "The sentence 'When the
  fog thickened, the keeper lit the lamp, and the boats turned
  toward the light' is compound-complex: an adverbial clause
  (when…), then two independent clauses joined by ', and.'"
  Every clause named, type correct.
- **Proficient architecture sample:** same sentence with the
  adverbial clause named but the second independent clause
  missed (called "part of the first clause"). One mislabel —
  cap at Proficient.
- **Developing mechanism sample:** "The long sentence feels
  dramatic." Effect asserted, no chain from structure to
  effect — cap at Developing for that criterion.
- **Excellent mechanism sample:** "Putting the fog first
  (dependent clause) makes the weather the condition for
  everything that follows; the two main clauses then give
  equal weight to the keeper's action and the boats'
  response, so the sentence's shape says: condition → action
  → consequence."
- **Excellent log sample:** "Original: 'The lamp burned all
  night, the boats found the harbor.' Revised: 'The lamp
  burned all night; the boats found the harbor.' Why: the two
  clauses are one idea — the light's effect — and the
  semicolon claims that closeness; a period would have
  separated cause from effect."
