## Teaching

News rarely arrives as one account. It arrives as many accounts
of the same subject in different media — print, video, data —
and RI.9-10.7 asks which details each account *emphasizes*.
SL.9-10.2 adds: integrate those accounts while judging each
source's credibility and accuracy. SL.9-10.3 adds: evaluate a
speaker's reasoning, evidence, and rhetoric, including
fallacies and exaggerated or distorted evidence.

The core skill is **source tracing**: for every claim, ask
*where did this account get it?* A claim repeated by three
outlets that all cite one announcement is **one root, three
repeats** — not three confirmations. An **independent
observation** is different: someone who saw, measured, or
recorded something themselves. Independence is about the
*root*, not the number of pages. Two more habits complete the
kit: **reading laterally** (leaving a page to check its source
elsewhere, instead of reading deeper into the same page) and
**going upstream** (following a claim back to its original
document — the council notice, the dated record — before
judging it).

Two cautions. First, dependence does not prove falsehood: the
council notice may be entirely true even though three outlets
repeat it. Second, an independent observation proves only what
was observed: seeing a fence proves a fence, not the state of
the water, the inspection, or the pavilion.

## Worked example 1 — Tracing A → B → C

Account A (council notice, 8:00) is the root: "inspection is
finished and the pavilion is ready." Account B (Gazette, 9:30)
says so "according to the council's morning notice" and admits
it "has not independently inspected" — B adds no new root.
Account C (blog, 11:00) cites "the Gazette reports" — C's root
is B, whose root is A. So the inspection claim has **one
root and three pages**, not three inspections. A careful
rewrite of C's headline would be: "Council says pavilion
inspected and ready, Gazette repeats notice." That sentence is
less exciting and more true — the exact trade media literacy
trains you to notice.

## Worked example 2 — Evaluating the speaker in E

The TV anchor (E, 6:00 p.m.) does three things a strong
speaker does: states both sides fairly ("The council says
inspected and ready; one vendor says the gate was fenced at
noon"), names what is missing ("have not yet received" the
dated inspection record), and states what would settle it ("a
reporter can verify the gate in person"). The reasoning is
sound because it *admits its own limits*: it does not claim
the pavilion is unsafe (D proves only a fence and a sign), and
it does not claim the council is lying (A may be true about an
inspection that happened elsewhere or earlier). The speaker's
one weakness is proportion: the segment gives the conflict
more airtime than the missing record, which can leave viewers
feeling the story is "disputed" when it is actually just
*unverified*. Evaluation means naming both the strength and
that cost.

## Guided practice (with an adult or partner)

1. Draw the source map for A–D: arrows from each account to
   its source. Which accounts share a root, and which stands
   alone?
2. Account D's observer "did not test anything." List exactly
   what D proves and exactly what it does not prove.
3. In F, the Gazette emphasizes August (480 shoppers) and the
   blog emphasizes growth (18 → 26 vendors). What does each
   emphasis hide, and what does the TV report correctly add?

## Independent practice

1. Rewrite account C's post (two sentences) so it no longer
   implies an independent inspection. Keep it lively; do not
   make it dull on purpose.
2. Suppose account A later corrects itself: "We meant the
   inspection is *scheduled* for next week." Which claims in
   B and C must be updated, and which part of D does *not*
   change? Explain in three sentences.
3. Name one *safe, legal* verification step a journalist
   could take next (for example, requesting the dated
   inspection record). State what it would establish and what
   it would not establish.
4. Write a 50–70-word correction notice for the blog that
   corrects the record without inventing an inspection result.

## Applied task

Choose any real (adult-selected) news topic you follow and
sketch its source map from memory: one root, its repeats, and
one genuinely independent check you could name. If you cannot
name an independent check, write what one would look like.
Bring the sketch to the discussion session.

## Exit check

Answer in two sentences: why is "three outlets reported it" a
weak reason to believe a claim, and what would make it strong?
Hand it in.

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** read accounts A–D aloud in time order;
  learners may build the source map with cards and arrows
  instead of writing.
- **Alternate response:** the correction notice may be
  dictated; the source map may be drawn or spoken.
- **Extension:** find a real (adult-selected) story where two
  outlets genuinely *do* have independent roots, and explain
  how you know — agreement alone is not the test; separate
  roots are.
- **Misconception:** "the vendor disagrees, so the council
  lied." Insist on the narrower true statement: the sources
  *conflict about immediate availability*, and the inspection
  itself is unverified, not disproven.
