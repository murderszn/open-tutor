# Lesson 3 — Delineating and Evaluating Arguments

**Goal:** I can delineate an argument's claims, assess whether
its reasoning is valid and its evidence relevant and sufficient,
and identify false statements and fallacious reasoning.
(RI.9-10.8)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** "The Fallacy Set"
([texts](../texts/fallacy-set.md)), notebook.
**Readiness check (5 min):** "Everyone loves the new bus
route, so it must be good." What is wrong with that sentence
as a reason? (It proves popularity, not goodness — a preview
of today's fallacy work.)

## Teaching

**Delineate** means to draw the argument's outline: claim,
reasons, evidence, and any counterclaim the author admits.
**Evaluate** means to test that outline with three questions:

1. **Valid?** If the reasons were true, would the conclusion
   follow? (A valid argument can still have false premises.)
2. **Relevant?** Does the evidence actually bear on *this*
   claim, or on a different one?
3. **Sufficient?** Is there *enough* evidence — enough cases,
   a representative sample, a strong enough link — to carry a
   claim of this size?

Common fallacies — reasoning moves that fail one of those
tests — have names worth learning:

- **Ad hominem:** attacking the person instead of the claim.
- **False dilemma:** pretending only two options exist.
- **Slippery slope:** claiming one step must cause a chain of
  disasters, without evidence for the chain.
- **Hasty generalization:** drawing a wide conclusion from one
  or two cases.
- **Appeal to popularity:** treating "many people believe it"
  as proof it is true.
- **Straw man:** refuting a weakened version of the other side.

Naming a fallacy is not the whole job. The evaluation must say
*what would fix it*: what evidence, qualification, or narrower
claim would make the argument sound.

## Worked example 1 — S2 is sound (and why that judgment is limited)

S2's claim: a twice-morning, twice-evening bus would serve
riders at lower cost per trip than the dial-a-ride van. Reasons:
214 residents took county-college classes last year; 68 percent
of surveyed riders would use a direct bus twice a week. This
passes all three tests *as far as it goes*: the reasoning is
valid (ridership + willingness → viable route), the evidence is
relevant (it is about these residents and this route), and it is
fairly sufficient for a *proposal* — though not for a final
budget, which would also need cost figures and survey details.
"Sound" here means "good enough to proceed with," not "proven
forever." A strong evaluation always states the claim's size
before judging its support.

## Worked example 2 — S4 is a false dilemma

"Either we fund the bus route or we admit we do not care about
young people's futures. There is no middle position." The claim
smuggles in a second, undefended claim: that funding *this*
route is the only way to care about young people. But a town
could care and still choose a different route, a van subsidy,
or a college shuttle partnership — middle positions exist, and
the sentence's own insistence ("There is no middle position")
is the tell. The fix is a narrower claim: "Funding this route
is one strong way to support young commuters; here is why it
beats the alternatives." That version can be argued with
evidence; the original can only be shouted.

## Independent practice

Read these two new snippets (written for this lesson; same
fictional town) and evaluate each:

**N1.** "The library's late-hours trial drew 40 extra visitors
on its first night, so late hours are obviously a permanent
success."

**N2.** "Late hours cost the library about $180 per evening in
staffing, based on the trial's payroll records. Against that,
the trial's study rooms served an average of 35 students per
evening. Whether $180 is worth 35 study seats is a judgment
the town must make, but it is a judgment we can now make with
numbers."

1. Delineate N1: claim, reason, evidence. Name its flaw and
   the test it fails (validity, relevance, or sufficiency).
2. Delineate N2: claim, reason, evidence. Does it pass all
   three tests? Where does it honestly stop short?
3. Rewrite N1 as a sound claim of the right size.
4. Which is more persuasive, N1 or N2, and why is
   "persuasive" not the same as "sound"?

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** read the fallacy set aloud; learners
  may sort snippets on cards (sound / flawed) before writing.
- **Alternate response:** evaluations may be spoken, or drawn
  as claim–reason–evidence diagrams with the failed test
  circled.
- **Extension:** find a fallacy in an advertisement or
  opinion piece the adult selects, and write the fix.
- **Misconception:** a fallacy label is not a verdict on the
  conclusion. A conclusion can be true and its argument still
  fallacious — evaluation judges the *reasoning*, not the
  side. Insist on that distinction in every answer.
