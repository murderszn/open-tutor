# Lesson 6 — The Evaluation Essay: Judging an Argument Fairly

**Goal:** I can write an evaluation essay that states a precise
claim, treats counterclaims fairly, supports reasons with
evidence, and keeps a formal, objective tone. (W.9-10.1,
W.9-10.9)
**Time:** ~50 minutes + studio session · **Materials:**
"Two Editorials, One Empty Lot" or the library editorial,
notebook, essay planner.
**Readiness check (5 min):** Write one sentence judging
Editorial A or B: is its argument strong or weak *overall*?
Then add one sentence admitting the strongest point on the
other side. Keep both — the essay needs them both.

## Teaching

An **evaluation essay** does not pick a side and cheer. It
judges an argument the way Lesson 3 taught you to: delineate
it, test validity, relevance, and sufficiency, name fallacies
fairly, and then *conclude* — with reasons — how strong the
argument is and what would strengthen it.

Structure (W.9-10.1):

1. **Introduction:** the text, its question, and your precise
   claim — not "Editorial A is good" but "Editorial A's
   argument is strong on cost and belonging, but its evidence
   for demand is thinner than its tone suggests."
2. **Body — strengths:** one paragraph per strength, each
   built as an **evidence sandwich**: your point, a quotation,
   and your explanation of what the quotation proves.
3. **Body — counterclaim, treated fairly:** the strongest
   objection, stated in its best form, with evidence *for* it,
   before your response. A counterclaim you weaken on purpose
   is a straw man, and readers can tell.
4. **Body — weaknesses:** fallacies, insufficient evidence, or
   unanswered objections, each named precisely (not "weak
   evidence" but "one trial, four weeks, no comparison group").
5. **Conclusion:** your overall judgment, the one change that
   would most strengthen the argument, and why the question
   still matters.

Tone stays formal and objective: no slang, no insults, no
cheering. "The author overstates" beats "the author lies";
"the evidence does not carry a claim this large" beats "this
is garbage." Precision is politeness in this genre.

## Worked example 1 — A precise thesis

Weak thesis: "Editorial B is convincing." (About what? In
what way? By what test?) Strong thesis: "Editorial B's garden
argument is strongest where it can point to a list — the forty
names waiting for plots — and weakest where it must predict the
future, since it offers no evidence that plot fees will cover
costs in year two." Notice what the strong version does: it
names the text, splits the judgment (strong here, weak there),
and previews the evidence. A reader can disagree with it
*specifically* — and that is the point.

## Worked example 2 — A fair counterclaim paragraph

Suppose you are evaluating Editorial A (skate park) and you
favor it. The fair counterclaim paragraph does not begin "Some
people whine about noise." It begins: "The strongest objection
is the neighbors' concern about noise and late-evening use,
which Editorial A answers only with a comparison to soccer —
a comparison that works for scraped knees but not for sound
after dark." Then the response: "That gap matters, but it does
not defeat the argument, because the town could answer it with
posted hours — a fix the editorial should have offered
itself." The pattern: **steelman, concede what is true,
answer what remains, name the fix.**

## Guided practice (with an adult or partner)

1. Turn this weak thesis strong: "The library editorial is
   persuasive." (Name the text's question, split the judgment,
   preview one strength and one weakness.)
2. Write one evidence sandwich for a strength of either
   editorial: point, quotation, explanation.
3. Write the steelman version of one objection to your chosen
   editorial — in its best form, not its weakest.

## Independent practice

1. Draft an introduction (thesis + roadmap) evaluating either
   editorial. 120–160 words.
2. Draft one body paragraph: a strength, built as an evidence
   sandwich.
3. Draft one body paragraph: a fair counterclaim and your
   response, using the steelman pattern.
4. Draft a conclusion: overall judgment + the single change
   that would most strengthen the argument.

## Applied task (essay studio, session 18)

Expand the four drafts into a complete evaluation essay of
600–800 words on one editorial (or, for strong writers, a
comparison of both). Use the project rubric's "Evaluation
essay" row as your checklist: precise claim, fair
counterclaim, quoted evidence, formal tone, named fix. Bring
the draft to the studio for adult feedback before the unit
assessment.

## Exit check

Read your thesis aloud. Can a reader disagree with it
*specifically*? If not, revise one clause now and hand the
thesis in.

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Writing access:** the adult may scribe dictated drafts;
   essay planners (thesis / strengths / counterclaim /
   weaknesses / fix) may be drawn as boxes.
- **Alternate response:** learners may record the essay as
  a spoken evaluation (5–7 minutes) with the same structure,
  if writing is the barrier rather than the thinking.
- **Extension:** evaluate *both* editorials in one essay and
  conclude which argument is stronger overall, with a
  head-to-head paragraph on the same criterion (for example,
  evidence quality).
- **Misconception:** "fair" does not mean "neutral" or "both
  sides are equal." A fair essay can conclude one argument is
  much stronger — fairness is in how the other side is
  *stated*, not in the verdict.
