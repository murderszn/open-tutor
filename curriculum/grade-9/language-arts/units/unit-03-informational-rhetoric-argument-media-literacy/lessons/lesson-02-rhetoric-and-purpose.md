# Lesson 2 — Rhetoric and Purpose: How Authors Move Readers

**Goal:** I can determine an author's point of view and purpose
and analyze how rhetoric — appeals, repetition, framing, and
word choice — advances them. (RI.9-10.4, RI.9-10.6)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** "Two Editorials, One
Empty Lot" ([texts](../texts/two-editorials.md)), notebook.
**Readiness check (5 min):** Read Editorial A's first paragraph.
Who is the author speaking *for*, and what does the author want
the town to *do*? One sentence each.

## Teaching

**Point of view** is the author's position on the question;
**purpose** is what the author wants the reader to think, feel,
or do. Rhetoric is the set of tools used to advance them:

- **Appeals:** *logos* (reason and evidence), *ethos*
  (credibility and character), *pathos* (emotion and values).
  Most strong writing mixes all three; the question is the
  balance, not the presence of any one.
- **Repetition:** repeating a word or structure so it sticks
  ("Pour the concrete," "Plant it") — emphasis, not filler.
- **Framing:** choosing which facts lead and which words
  describe them. "Teenagers gather in parking lots" frames the
  same scene very differently from "teenagers have nowhere to
  go."
- **Word choice and tone:** connotation (the feeling a word
  carries) and cumulative impact — the effect of many small
  choices adding up. Calling skaters "athletes" and a garden a
  "teacher" are not decorations; they are arguments.

A fair rhetoric analysis does three things: names the author's
purpose, names the tools, and judges whether the tools serve
the purpose honestly. Praising or blaming an author for having a
point of view is not analysis — every editorial has one.

## Worked example 1 — Appeals in Editorial A, ¶1

"Willow Creek's teenagers have nowhere to go. That is not a
complaint; it is a fact you can check any afternoon." The
purpose is already visible: the author speaks *for* teenagers
and wants the town to build a skate park. The tools: **logos**
(the checkable claim about booked courts, admission fees, and
the 5 p.m. youth-center closing), **ethos** (the plain,
check-it-yourself voice — "you can check"), and **pathos** in
the final turn ("We created this problem"). That last sentence
is the paragraph's engine: it converts a fact about teenagers
into shared responsibility for adults. Note the framing move
inside it: adults "complaining" about parking lots becomes
adults *causing* the parking-lot gatherings.

## Worked example 2 — Same lot, opposite framing

Both editorials describe the same empty lot, but their diction
builds different worlds. Editorial A calls it a place teenagers
are excluded from ("guests," "theirs, in public, in daylight").
Editorial B calls it "a weedy rectangle" that "could feed this
town." A's repeated word is *belonging* (belong, guests,
theirs); B's is *growth* (grows, seasons, plant). Neither author
lies about the lot — the lot is empty either way. The rhetoric
lesson is that **word choice selects which truth about the lot
the reader feels first**. A fair analysis names both framings
and asks what each framing hides: A never mentions who pays for
concrete; B never mentions that gardens need months before
anyone eats.

## Guided practice (with an adult or partner)

1. In Editorial B, ¶2, name one logos appeal and one pathos
   appeal. Quote the words that carry each.
2. Find one repetition in each editorial. What does each
   repetition emphasize?
3. Editorial A, ¶3 answers the noise/scrapes objection. Which
   appeal leads that paragraph, and does it fairly answer the
   objection or change the subject?

## Independent practice

1. Write a rhetoric inventory for Editorial A: purpose (one
   sentence), then one example each of logos, ethos, pathos,
   repetition, and framing, each with a short quotation.
2. Do the same inventory for Editorial B.
3. Compare the two closings ("Pour the concrete" / "Plant it,
   and watch what grows"). Which closing is more pathos-driven,
   and what value does each closing appeal to?
4. Choose one paragraph from either editorial and rewrite it
   in the *other* editorial's voice (A's bluntness or B's
   patience). What changes besides the words?

## Applied task

Write a genre-contrast paragraph (120–150 words): how would
Editorial A's argument sound if rewritten as a neutral town
report — same facts, no appeals, no framing words? Then state
in one sentence what the rhetoric was doing that the report
cannot do.

## Exit check

Name one rhetorical tool from today's lesson and one sentence
explaining how it advances its author's purpose. Use a
quotation. Hand it in.

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** read both editorials aloud in full
  before the inventories; learners may annotate appeals with
  colored marks (one color per appeal) instead of writing
  lists.
- **Alternate response:** inventories may be spoken and
  recorded, or drawn as a three-column table.
- **Extension:** find one place where either editorial's
  rhetoric overreaches its evidence, and name the overreach
  precisely (a claim bigger than its support).
- **Misconception:** "rhetoric" does not mean "dishonest
  tricks." Insist learners judge *how well* tools serve a
  purpose, not whether tools are used at all.
