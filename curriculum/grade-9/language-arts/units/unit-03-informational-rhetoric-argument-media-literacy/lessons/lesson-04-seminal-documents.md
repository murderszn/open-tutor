# Lesson 4 — Seminal Documents: Old Words, Living Themes

**Goal:** I can analyze a seminal U.S. document and trace how
it addresses themes that a modern text takes up again.
(RI.9-10.9, RI.9-10.2)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** "The Gettysburg
Address — and a Modern Response"
([texts](../texts/gettysburg-address-and-response.md)), notebook.
**Readiness check (5 min):** What does "dedicate" mean when you
dedicate a building? What else can a person dedicate — and to
whom? Two sentences.

## Teaching

A **seminal document** is a text that shaped how later writers
think — not because it is old, but because its ideas keep
getting reused. RI.9-10.9 asks you to analyze such documents
*including how they address related themes and concepts*:
what idea does the old text plant, and how does a later text
take it up, change it, or answer it?

Lincoln's Gettysburg Address (November 19, 1863) is short —
about 270 words — and it performs a reversal. The occasion is a
cemetery dedication, and the audience expects Lincoln to
dedicate the ground. Instead he argues the ground is *already*
consecrated by the dead, so the only dedication left belongs
to the living: "to be dedicated here to the unfinished work."
The speech's central idea is not about the cemetery at all. It
is about a transfer — dedication moves from ceremony to duty,
from the dead to the living, from the past to the future.

Reading a seminal document well means three moves: (1) find
its central idea in its own terms; (2) name the rhetorical
move that carries it (here, repetition with reversal: "we can
not dedicate — we can not consecrate — we can not hallow");
(3) trace the theme into a later text and say precisely what
changed — the setting, the stakes, or the audience.

## Worked example 1 — The reversal in Lincoln's third paragraph

"We can not dedicate — we can not consecrate — we can not
hallow — this ground." Three verbs, three denials, one
sentence. The repetition builds an expectation (surely the
third verb will turn positive) and then breaks it: the brave
men "have consecrated it, far above our poor power to add or
detract." The rhetorical effect is humility with authority —
Lincoln refuses the ceremonial role and claims a harder one.
Then the pivot: "It is for us the living, rather, to be
dedicated here." The word *dedicated* returns, but its subject
has changed from *the ground* to *us*. That single change of
subject is the speech's whole argument in miniature.

## Worked example 2 — How the modern response takes up the theme

The fictional response opens with the same reversal: "I did
not come here to dedicate anything. The people buried here did
that already." It then transfers Lincoln's theme into ordinary
places: "a pantry, a classroom, a town meeting where someone's
voice is missing." What changes? The *scale* (battlefield to
food pantry) and the *proof* (Lincoln points to the dead; the
speaker points to a grandmother's thirty years of volunteering).
What stays? The structure: ceremony is refused, and dedication
is handed to the living as unfinished work. A strong comparison
names both the continuity and the change — "same theme, new
evidence" is a summary, not an analysis, until the evidence is
quoted.

## Guided practice (with an adult or partner)

1. Find Lincoln's sentence that states the transfer most
   directly. Quote it and name its subject and verb.
2. The response says a memorial "can trick you." What trick,
   exactly — and how does Lincoln's speech refuse the same
   trick?
3. Compare the two closings (Lincoln's "shall not perish from
   the earth" / the response's "one piece of it before this
   year ends"). Which is broader, which is more concrete, and
   what does each choice cost?

## Independent practice

1. Write a theme-tracing paragraph: how does the idea of
   "unfinished work" develop from Lincoln's "unfinished work
   which they who fought here have thus far so nobly advanced"
   to the response's "your turn"? Quote both texts.
2. Lincoln's speech never names slavery directly, yet it
   redefines the war as a test of equality ("all men are
   created equal"). In two sentences, explain how the
   opening line does that work without naming the institution.
3. The response's speaker promises "something harder" than
   remembering. What is it, and is that promise a fair
   extension of Lincoln's argument or a change of subject?
   Defend your answer with a quotation.
4. Write an objective summary of the Gettysburg Address in
   60–80 words (no modern language, no opinion).

## Applied task

Write a four-sentence modern response of your own to one
Lincoln sentence (not the whole speech): keep his theme, move
it to a place you know (a team, a family, a neighborhood), and
end with one concrete piece of "unfinished work." Label your
piece as your own writing, not a historical document.

## Exit check

One sentence: what theme do Lincoln and the modern response
share, and one quoted phrase from each that proves it? Hand
it in.

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** read the Address aloud slowly, twice;
  the adult may gloss "consecrate," "hallow," "devotion," and
  "four score" (a score is twenty years: 87 years before 1863
  is 1776).
- **Alternate response:** theme traces may be drawn as two
  columns (Lincoln / response) with arrows between matching
  phrases.
- **Extension:** compare Lincoln's "government of the people,
  by the people, for the people" with the response's "the
  government we keep alive by showing up for it" — what does
  each version assume about who keeps a government alive?
- **Misconception:** "old text, modern text" is not
  "then vs. now." Insist learners show the *mechanism* of
  influence: quotation, echo, reversal, or answer.
- **Sensitive content note:** the Address concerns the Civil
  War and its dead. The adult frames it as a cemetery
  dedication speech, not a battle description; no graphic
  content appears in either text.
