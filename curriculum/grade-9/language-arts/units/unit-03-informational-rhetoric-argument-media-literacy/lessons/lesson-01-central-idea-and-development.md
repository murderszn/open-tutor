# Lesson 1 — Central Idea: How an Author Unfolds It

**Goal:** I can determine a text's central idea, trace how it
develops paragraph by paragraph, and write an objective summary
of it. (RI.9-10.2, RI.9-10.3, RI.9-10.5)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** "Keep the Library Open
Late" ([texts](../texts/library-hours-editorial.md)), notebook.
**Readiness check (5 min):** In one sentence, what is the
library editorial *about*, and in one sentence, what does it
*want*? (Topic ≠ idea: the topic is library hours; the idea is a
claim about them. Keep both sentences for the exit check.)

## Teaching

A **central idea** is the author's main claim — the point the
whole text exists to make. It is not the topic. "Library hours"
is a topic; "the library should stay open until nine on
weeknights" is a central idea, because it can be argued with and
supported.

Authors rarely state a central idea once and stop. They **unfold**
it: they introduce it, then develop it by adding reasons,
answering objections, offering evidence, and restating it more
strongly. RI.9-10.3 asks you to follow that order — *which* point
comes first, *how* each point is introduced, and *what connections*
the author draws between them. RI.9-10.5 zooms in further: which
exact sentences or paragraphs do the developing work?

A useful tool is a **claim-tracing map**: one row per paragraph,
with three columns — *what the paragraph says*, *what job it
does* (introduces, supports, answers an objection, restates),
and *what it connects to*. An **objective summary** then
reports the central idea and its main supports in your own
words, without your opinion and without the author's persuasive
language.

## Worked example 1 — Tracing ¶1–¶2

¶1's job is to **introduce the problem and imply the idea**:
the 6 p.m. closing decides who can use a public building, so the
current hours are unfair. Notice the author does not yet say
"open until nine" — the idea is *emerging*. ¶2 then **states
the proposal and defends its cost**: open until nine; the extra
cost is small because heat, light, and security are already paid
for by evening classes. The connection matters: ¶2 answers the
cost objection *before* the reader can raise it, which lets ¶3
spend its energy on the "trouble" objection instead. That order —
problem, proposal, cost defense, trouble defense, evidence,
identity, closing appeal — is the author's unfolding plan, not
an accident.

## Worked example 2 — How ¶4 develops the idea

¶4 does a different job from ¶2: it offers **evidence from a
trial**. "Visits rose by a third, and the number of students
finishing homework in the study rooms doubled" during a
four-week late-hours trial. Then comes the reasoning sentence:
"A building that is used more is a building the town is more
likely to keep funding. Use creates support." That sentence is
the paragraph's real work — it turns a statistic into a reason
to act. A careful reader asks two questions here: *is the
evidence relevant* (yes — it is about late hours in this very
library) and *is it sufficient* (partly — four weeks is short,
and the trial may have drawn curious first-timers). Lesson 3
will teach you to press that second question properly. For now,
notice the move: evidence, then the reasoning that connects
evidence to idea.

## Guided practice (with an adult or partner)

1. Map ¶3: what does it say, what job does it do, and what
   does it connect to? (Hint: it answers an objection with a
   reversal — closed buildings cause the very problem cited.)
2. Map ¶5: what job does redefining the library ("not a
   warehouse for books") do for the central idea?
3. Map ¶6: which earlier paragraphs does the closing appeal
   echo, and what new move does "a natural law" make?

## Independent practice

1. Write a claim-tracing map for all six paragraphs (one row
   each: says / job / connects to).
2. In two sentences, explain why the author puts the cost
   defense (¶2) *before* the trouble defense (¶3). What would
   change if the order were reversed?
3. Which paragraph does the most developing work for the
   central idea — ¶2, ¶4, or ¶5? Defend your choice with a
   quotation.
4. Write an objective summary of the editorial in 80–110
   words. No opinion words ("convincing," "weak"), no
   persuasive language copied from the author.

## Applied task

Choose any paragraph and rewrite it so its job is visible in its
first sentence (for example, a ¶3 opening of "The trouble
objection gets the causation backwards: …"). Then explain in
one sentence whether your rewrite strengthened or weakened the
paragraph's connection to the central idea.

## Exit check

Return to your readiness sentences. Revise the "what does it
want" sentence into a full central-idea statement, and name
the paragraph that develops it most. Hand both in.

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** read ¶1–¶2 aloud together before
  independent mapping; the map template (says / job / connects
  to) may be drawn as three columns on paper.
- **Alternate response:** learners may dictate their map rows
  or draw the map as a chain of arrows between paragraph
  numbers.
- **Extension:** find one sentence in ¶5 whose removal would
  least damage the central idea, and one whose removal would
  most damage it; justify both.
- **Misconception:** learners often write the topic ("library
  hours") as the central idea. Insist on a claim that can be
  argued with — if it cannot be disagreed with, it is not an
  idea yet.

  model maps, the summary, and acceptable variation.
