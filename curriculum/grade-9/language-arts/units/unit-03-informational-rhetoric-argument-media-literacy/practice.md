# Practice — Unit 03 (Grade 9 Language Arts)

Each session names its task and its success criterion. The adult
reviews the session's work the same day. Full solutions are in
[answer-key.md](answer-key.md).

## Practice A — Session 2: claim-tracing maps

Using "Keep the Library Open Late":

1. Complete a claim-tracing map for ¶3, ¶5, and ¶6 (says / job /
   connects to).
2. In two sentences, state the editorial's central idea and name
   the paragraph that introduces it most directly.
3. Which paragraph could be removed with the least damage to
   the central idea? Defend your choice in two sentences.

*Success: every row names a job the paragraph actually does;
the central-idea sentence is a claim, not a topic.*

## Practice B — Session 3: objective summaries

1. Write an objective summary (80–110 words) of Editorial B
   ("Plant the Garden").
2. Circle or list three persuasive words or phrases from
   Editorial B that you deliberately left out of your summary,
   and say why each is persuasive rather than factual.
3. Swap summaries with a partner (or the adult). Does the
   summary let a stranger state the editorial's central idea
   and two supports? Revise one sentence from the feedback.

*Success: the summary reports the idea and supports without
opinion words and without copying the author's persuasive
language.*

## Practice C — Session 5: the hardest-working paragraph

1. Write a paragraph (120–150 words) arguing which paragraph of
   the library editorial does the most developing work for the
   central idea. Quote the paragraph's key sentence.
2. Write a second paragraph arguing for a different paragraph
   (the "skeptic move" from U01: argue against yourself).
3. Conclude in one sentence which case is stronger and why.

*Success: both paragraphs quote evidence; the conclusion
follows from the comparison, not from preference.*

## Practice D — Session 7: rhetoric inventory

Complete a rhetoric inventory for Editorial B ("Plant the
Garden"): purpose (one sentence), then one example each of
logos, ethos, pathos, repetition, and framing, each with a
short quotation and a note on what it advances.

*Success: every entry quotes the text; the purpose sentence
names both the position and the action wanted.*

## Practice E — Session 8: genre-contrast paragraph

1. Rewrite Editorial B, ¶1 as a neutral town report: same
   facts (lot, garden proposal, who benefits), no appeals, no
   framing words, no value language.
2. In two sentences, state what the rhetoric in the original
   was doing that the report cannot do.
3. Name one fact the report version makes *clearer* than the
   editorial does.

*Success: the report contains no persuasive language; the
two-sentence note names a real rhetorical function.*

## Practice F — Session 10: paired-editorial comparison

Write a comparison (200–250 words) of Editorials A and B on one
shared criterion — **evidence quality**. Which editorial's
evidence is stronger, where is each editorial's evidence
thinnest, and what single piece of evidence would most help the
weaker side? Quote both editorials.

*Success: the comparison judges both sides by the same
criterion; the "single piece of evidence" is specific, not
"more facts."*

## Practice G — Session 13: fallacy classification

Classify S1, S3, S5, S6, and S8 from the Fallacy Set
([texts/fallacy-set.md](texts/fallacy-set.md)):

1. S1 — name the flaw, the test it fails, and the fix.
2. S3 — name the flaw, the test it fails, and the fix.
3. S5 — name the flaw, the test it fails, and the fix.
4. S6 — name the flaw, the test it fails, and the fix.
5. S8 — name the flaw, the test it fails, and the fix.
6. Rewrite one flawed snippet (your choice) as a sound claim of
   the right size, in no more than three sentences.

*Success: every classification names the fallacy, not just
"bad reasoning"; every fix narrows the claim or supplies the
missing evidence type.*

## Practice H — Session 14: theme chart

1. Draw a two-column theme chart (Lincoln / modern response)
   with four rows: occasion, central idea, key repeated
   phrase, closing call. Fill every cell with a quotation or
   close paraphrase.
2. In three sentences, state what changed between the two
   texts and what stayed the same.
3. Write an objective summary (60–80 words) of the modern
   response.

*Success: every chart cell is text-based; the change/stay
sentences name mechanism (setting, scale, proof), not just
"old vs. new."*

## Practice I — Session 17: full source audit

Using "Three Reports, One Announcement":

1. Draw the complete source map for A–F: arrows from each
   account to its root; mark each account "root," "repeat,"
   or "independent."
2. Write the TV anchor's next-day update (60–90 words) for
   each of two cases: (a) the dated inspection record arrives
   and confirms an inspection; (b) no record arrives and the
   gate is still fenced. Neither update may invent facts.
3. Build the emphasis table for F: for the Gazette, the blog,
   and the TV report, name the detail emphasized and the
   detail hidden or corrected.
4. Evaluate the TV speaker (E) in four sentences: one
   strength, one weakness, one piece of missing evidence, and
   an overall judgment.

*Success: the map shows one root for the inspection claim; both
updates stay inside what is known; the emphasis table names
both the emphasis and the cost.*

## Practice J — Session 18: essay studio

1. Expand Lesson 6's four drafts into a full evaluation essay
   (600–800 words) on one editorial.
2. Run the studio checklist on your own draft: precise claim?
   fair counterclaim? quoted evidence in every body paragraph?
   formal tone? named fix in the conclusion? Mark each item
   pass / revise.
3. Revise the two weakest checklist items and submit the
   revised draft for adult feedback.

*Success: the submitted draft passes all five checklist items;
revisions are visible (not just claimed).*
