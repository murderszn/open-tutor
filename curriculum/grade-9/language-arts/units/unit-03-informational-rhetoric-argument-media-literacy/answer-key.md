# Answer Key — Unit 03 (Grade 9 Language Arts)

**Teacher side only.** Every lesson task, practice session,
quiz item, assessment task, and project criterion is solved or
scored here. Accept any response that meets the stated
criterion, even if its wording differs from the models below.

## Lesson 1

**Readiness:** topic = library hours; what it wants = the
library should stay open until nine on weeknights.

**Guided:** (1) ¶3's job is answering an objection by
reversal: the trouble cited (loud teenagers) happened because
the building was closed; it connects to ¶2's proposal by
defending it against its second expected objection. (2) ¶5's
job is redefinition: by showing the library is a study room,
computer lab, and tutoring space — not a book warehouse — it
widens the proposal's beneficiaries beyond readers. (3) ¶6
echoes ¶1 (who the hours serve) and ¶2 (a choice, not a law);
its new move is framing 6 p.m. as a dated decision ("a town
that no longer exists") rather than a fact of nature.

**Independent:** (1) Model map — ¶1: problem introduced,
unfair access; job: introduce; connects to: the proposal in
¶2. ¶2: proposal + cost defense; job: state and defend;
connects to: ¶1's problem. ¶3: trouble objection answered by
reversal; job: answer objection; connects to: ¶2's proposal.
¶4: trial evidence (visits +33%, homework completions doubled);
job: support with evidence + reasoning ("Use creates
support"); connects to: ¶2's cost claim. ¶5: redefinition of
the library; job: widen beneficiaries; connects to: ¶1's
fairness claim. ¶6: closing appeal; job: restate + reframe;
connects to: ¶1 and ¶2. (2) Cost first because cost is the
objection most readers raise first and the easiest to settle
with arithmetic; reversed, the trouble defense would carry an
unsettled cost worry behind it. (3) Accept ¶2, ¶4, or ¶5 with
a quotation and a reason; strongest defensible answer is ¶4,
because it is the only paragraph offering evidence rather than
assertion. (4) Model summary (96 words): "The editorial
argues that Willow Creek's library should stay open until nine
on weeknights because its 6 p.m. closing excludes students and
workers. It claims the extra cost is small since evening
classes already require heat, light, and security; answers the
concern about trouble by arguing that closed buildings cause
parking-lot gatherings; cites a four-week trial in which visits
rose by a third and homework completions doubled; and defines
the library as a study, computer, and tutoring space for the
whole town."

**Applied:** accept any first-sentence job label that matches
the paragraph; the strength/weakness judgment must follow
from the rewrite's effect on connection, not taste.

**Exit:** central-idea statement = "The library should stay
open until nine on weeknights because its current hours
unfairly exclude the people who need it most." Any paragraph
with a reasoned defense is acceptable.

## Lesson 2

**Guided:** (1) Editorial B, ¶2 — logos: "the waiting list at
the county's only other garden already holds forty names"
(a countable list); pathos: "let residents do the work they
are already asking to do" (dignity, agency). (2) A repeats
belonging ("guests," "theirs"); B repeats growth ("grows,"
"seasons," "plant") — each repetition brands the lot with
its value. (3) Logos leads (sports-injury comparison), but it
partly changes the subject: scraped knees answer the injury
worry, not the noise worry.

**Independent:** (1) Editorial A inventory — purpose: to
persuade Willow Creek to build a skate park for its
teenagers. Logos: booked courts, admission fees, 5 p.m.
closing. Ethos: "you can check any afternoon." Pathos: "We
created this problem." Repetition: "nowhere to go" / "in
public, in daylight." Framing: teenagers as "neighbors" and
"guests." (2) Editorial B inventory — purpose: to persuade
the town to plant a community garden on the lot. Logos: forty
names on the waiting list; plot fees covering costs. Ethos:
the patient, practical voice ("unlock a gate"). Pathos:
"toddlers digging," "grandparents passing on knowledge."
Repetition: growth words. Framing: the lot as "a weedy
rectangle" that "could feed this town." (3) B's closing is
more pathos-driven (generations, seasons, growth); A's
closing appeals to belonging and fairness. (4) Accept any
rewrite that changes diction/tempo while keeping the facts;
the explanation must name what changed (for example, B's
patience becomes A's bluntness).

**Applied:** model report opening: "The empty lot behind the
old rail yard is proposed as a community garden site. The
proposal cites twenty family plots, a forty-name waiting list
at the county garden, and plot fees as the funding source."
Rhetoric's lost function: it cannot move a reader to care or
act — it reports without urging.

**Exit:** any tool + quotation + purpose link meeting the
criterion.

## Lesson 3

**Guided:** (1) S7 — claim: fund the route with a 9:30 p.m.
last bus. Reasons: ridership numbers; admitted counterclaim:
late-evening noise on a residential street. Admission
strengthens it because it shows the author weighed the real
cost instead of hiding it — credibility (ethos) rises.
(2) S6 — hasty generalization (fails sufficiency): one
student's dropout cannot carry a conclusion about bus routes.
Fix: a sample — for example, completion rates for bus riders
vs. non-riders across several towns. (3) S3 — the author
would need to argue that Alvarez's transport proposals are
unsound on their merits, not that her driving history
disqualifies her judgment.

**Independent:** (1) N1 — claim: late hours are a permanent
success. Reason/evidence: 40 extra visitors on night one.
Flaw: hasty generalization; fails sufficiency (one night,
novelty effect, no trend). (2) N2 — claim: the town can now
judge the trade-off with numbers. Evidence: $180/evening
staffing, 35 students/evening. Passes all three tests for a
*judgment* claim; it honestly stops short of deciding — it
supplies the terms, not the verdict. (3) Model rewrite: "The
trial's first late night drew 40 extra visitors. If that
level holds over a full month, late hours will have shown
real demand." (4) N2 is more sound; N1 is more persuasive
because certainty feels better than a judgment call —
persuasion is about effect, soundness about the
reasoning-evidence fit.

**Applied:** model S5 rewrite: "Approving one route creates a
precedent the council should manage: future requests should
face the same ridership test this route did. Without that
rule, each approval makes the next harder to refuse. The
council should adopt the test before approving the route."

**Exit:** any fallacy + example + one-sentence fix.

## Lesson 4

**Guided:** (1) "It is for us the living, rather, to be
dedicated here to the unfinished work." Subject: us, the
living; verb: to be dedicated. (2) The trick: treating a
memorial as finished business — carved, done, admired from a
distance. Lincoln refuses it by handing dedication to the
living as work; the response refuses it by promising action
instead of admiration. (3) Lincoln's closing is broader
(a nation's survival); the response's is more concrete (one
piece of work before year's end). Breadth inspires; concreteness
can be checked. Each costs what the other gains.

**Independent:** (1) Model paragraph: Lincoln's "unfinished
work" is the war's task of preserving a nation dedicated to
equality; the response hears it as "your turn" — the same
structure (work handed forward) with new content (pantry,
classroom, town meeting). (2) The opening dates the nation to
1776 and its proposition ("all men are created equal"), so
the war becomes a test of that proposition — slavery is the
unnamed institution whose fate the proposition decides.
(3) Accept either verdict with a quotation: fair extension
("the government we keep alive by showing up") or change of
subject (from national survival to personal volunteering) —
the defense, not the side, earns credit. (4) Model summary
(72 words): "Lincoln's Gettysburg Address, given at a cemetery
dedication in 1863, argues that the soldiers have already
consecrated the ground, so dedication now belongs to the
living. The living must carry forward the unfinished work of
preserving a nation dedicated to equality, resolving that the
dead shall not have died in vain and that government of the
people, by the people, for the people shall not perish."

**Applied:** any four-sentence response keeping Lincoln's
theme in a known place with one concrete task, labeled as the
learner's own writing.

**Exit:** shared theme = dedication as the living's work;
phrases: Lincoln's "unfinished work" and the response's "your
turn" (or equivalent quoted pair).

## Lesson 5

**Guided:** (1) Map: A is the root; B repeats A; C repeats B;
D stands alone (independent observation of a sign). (2) D
proves: at noon, a fence blocked the entrance and a sign read
NOT IN SERVICE. D does not prove: the inspection's status, the
water's safety, or the pavilion's condition inside. (3) The
Gazette's August emphasis hides that the figures describe last
year's open-air market, not the pavilion; the blog's growth
emphasis hides the small base (18 → 26). The TV report
correctly adds that none of the figures describe the new
pavilion.

**Independent:** (1) Model rewrite: "The Gazette repeats the
council's morning notice that the pavilion passed inspection.
The Gazette has not inspected the pavilion itself, and neither
have we — Saturday shoppers should check before traveling."
(2) If A corrects to "scheduled": B's and C's "inspected and
ready" claims must be updated or withdrawn; D's observation
(a fence and sign at noon) does not change, because it never
depended on A's claim — though its *interpretation* may.
(3) Accept any safe, legal step: requesting the dated
inspection record (establishes what the record says, not
whether it is authentic until verified); visiting the gate in
person (establishes current access, not inspection status).
(4) Model correction (62 words): "Correction: our earlier
post said the pavilion is inspected and ready. That wording
repeated the Gazette's report of a council notice; we have
not independently verified an inspection. A vendor reported a
fence and a NOT IN SERVICE sign at noon on Saturday. We do
not yet know the inspection's status and will update this
post when the council provides its dated record."

**Exit:** "Three outlets reported it" is weak because the
three may share one root; it becomes strong when each outlet
independently verified the claim by its own root.

## Lesson 6

**Guided:** (1) Model strong thesis: "The library editorial's
argument is strongest on fairness — its hours exclude exactly
the people a public building should serve — and weakest on
cost, since it never gives the staffing figure its own
argument calls for." (2) Evidence sandwich model: point —
Editorial B's waiting list is its strongest evidence;
quotation — "the waiting list at the county's only other
garden already holds forty names"; explanation — a list of
names is countable demand, not a prediction. (3) Steelman
model for Editorial A: "Neighbors reasonably worry about
noise after dark; a park used into the evening changes the
street's sound, and the editorial's soccer comparison does
not answer that specific worry."

**Independent/Applied:** score with the project rubric's essay
row: precise claim, fair counterclaim, quoted evidence in
every body paragraph, formal tone, named fix. The studio
checklist (Practice J) is the scoring tool.

**Exit:** a thesis is passable when a reader can disagree
with a specific clause of it.

## Practice

**A:** ¶3: says the trouble objection reverses causation;
job: answer objection; connects to: ¶2's proposal. ¶5: says
the library is study/computer/tutoring space; job: redefine
and widen beneficiaries; connects to: ¶1's fairness claim.
¶6: says 6 p.m. is a dated choice; job: restate + reframe;
connects to: ¶1 and ¶2. Least-damage removal: accept ¶5 or
¶6 with a reason (¶5 is the most common defensible answer —
the proposal survives without redefinition; ¶4 carries the
evidence).

**B:** summary must report the garden proposal, the waiting
list, plot-fee funding, and the patience/seasons argument
without opinion words. Persuasive words left out may include
"feed this town," "weedy rectangle," "lessons this town badly
needs" — each argues rather than reports.

**C:** accept either paragraph choice with a quotation; the
conclusion must follow from the two cases compared.

**D:** see Lesson 2 independent (1)–(2) models; any quoted
entry meeting the criterion passes.

**E:** model report: see Lesson 2 applied model. The report
makes the funding mechanism (plot fees, twenty plots) clearer
by stripping the seasonal metaphor.

**F:** evidence comparison — B's evidence is stronger
(countable waiting list, fee mechanism); A's is thinner (no
numbers for skaters or costs; "cheap" is asserted, not
priced). The single piece most helping A: a count of local
teenagers who would use a park, or a priced construction
estimate.

**G:** S1 — appeal to popularity; fails validity (popularity
does not entail goodness). Fix: evidence about outcomes.
S3 — ad hominem; fails relevance (driving-test history does
not bear on the route's merits). Fix: argue the proposal's
merits. S5 — slippery slope; fails sufficiency (no mechanism
or evidence for the chain). Fix: name the approval rule (see
Lesson 3 applied). S6 — hasty generalization; fails
sufficiency (n = 1). Fix: a sample with comparison. S8 —
appeal to popularity/silence (absence of objection is not
consent); fails validity. Fix: a survey or vote count.

**H:** chart rows — occasion: cemetery dedication / memorial
visit; central idea: dedication belongs to the living /
"your turn"; key phrase: "we can not dedicate" / "I did not
come here to dedicate anything"; closing call: government
"shall not perish from the earth" / "one piece of it before
this year ends." Change: scale, setting, proof. Same: the
transfer structure (ceremony refused, work handed forward).

**I:** map as Lesson 5 guided (1), with E marked "speaker:
fair summary, unverified claim" and F marked "data about last
year's market, not the pavilion." Updates: (a) record confirms
— update to "inspection confirmed by dated record; gate
status still to verify in person"; (b) no record, gate still
fenced — update to "inspection still unverified; gate still
fenced at last check." Neither invents an inspection result.
Emphasis table: Gazette → August 480, hides last-year scope;
blog → 18 → 26 growth, hides small base; TV → corrects scope,
emphasizes the missing record. Speaker evaluation: strength —
admits limits; weakness — conflict gets more airtime than the
missing record; missing evidence — the dated record or a
first-hand gate check; judgment — fair but incomplete.

**J:** score with the studio checklist; revisions must be
visible in the submitted draft.

## Formative quiz

1. Topic names a subject; central idea makes an arguable
   claim about it (example: topic "library hours" vs. idea
   "the library should stay open until nine"). (2 pts)
2. ¶3 answers the trouble objection by reversing causation
   (closure causes the gatherings); it defends ¶2's proposal
   and connects the objection to the solution. (3 pts)
3. Any appeal with quotation + explanation (for example,
   ethos: "you can check any afternoon" — invites the reader
   to verify, building credibility). (3 pts)
4. S1: appeal to popularity; fails validity. (3 pts)
5. S3: ad hominem; fails relevance. (3 pts)
6. S8: appeal to popularity (silence as consent); fails
   validity. (3 pts)
7. Quotation: "It is for us the living, rather, to be
   dedicated here to the unfinished work." Transfer: the
   subject of dedication changes from the ground to the
   living; the work changes from ceremony to duty. (4 pts)
8. Change: battlefield → pantry/classroom/town meeting;
   proof: dead soldiers → grandmother's volunteering. Same:
   dedication as the living's unfinished work. Must quote
   the response (for example, "your turn"). (4 pts)
9. One root (the council notice, A); B and C share it; D is
   independent. (4 pts)
10. Model: "Three pages repeat the council's inspection
    claim; they do not independently establish it." (2 pts)

## Unit assessment

**Part A:** (1) Central idea: Willow Creek High should start
at 8:30, not 7:30. Reasons: adolescent sleep biology (no
direct evidence cited — assertion); Brookfield's move (tardiness
−40%, calmer classes — one town, one semester); student
preference (9 in 10 prefer later start). Counterclaim admitted:
buses run later, after-school jobs suffer (answered as
"smaller than they look," with the bus fee still unpublished).
(2a) Brookfield: insufficient evidence — one neighboring town,
one semester, no control for other changes; it supports a
trial, not a district-wide conclusion. (2b) Closing: appeal to
popularity (fallacy) — 90 percent preference proves preference,
not that the policy is right, especially against the admitted
costs. (3) Fairness: the author admits real costs and names the
missing figure ("The district should publish that fee before
any vote") — that sentence most shows fairness. Still owed:
the fee itself, the number of affected jobs/shifts, and any
evidence on learning outcomes (not just tardiness).

**Part B:** score with the rubric in the assessment (claim 4,
strength 5, weakness 5, counterclaim 4, tone 2, conclusion with
named fix 4). Model overall claim: "The passage argues well
on fairness but weakly on evidence: it admits its costs
honestly, yet its two load-bearing supports — one town's
semester and one preference poll — cannot carry a
district-wide schedule change." Below 38 total: reteach
Lessons 3 and 6, reassess with a new passage.
