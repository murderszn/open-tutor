# A Case for Later Start Times

**An original editorial written for this unit's culminating
assessment. All people, places, and figures are fictional
practice material. The passage deliberately contains one
weak-evidence claim and one fallacious move; the assessment
asks learners to find and evaluate them.**

---

Willow Creek High should start at 8:30 instead of 7:30. The
reason is straightforward: teenagers' bodies are wired to fall
asleep late and wake late, so a 7:30 start forces students to
learn while half asleep. When the neighboring town of Brookfield
moved its start to 8:30, tardiness fell by 40 percent in one
semester and teachers reported calmer first-period classes. Our
students deserve the same chance.

Opponents say buses would run later and after-school jobs would
suffer. Those are real costs, and a serious plan must answer
them. But the costs are smaller than they look: most employers
already schedule student shifts around school hours, and the
bus company has said it could shift routes for a modest fee.
The district should publish that fee before any vote, so the
public can judge the trade-off with real numbers instead of
fears.

The deeper question is what school is for. If school exists to
prepare young people for adult life, then starting school at an
hour when adults are already at work teaches a useful lesson
about the real world. But that argument proves too much: by the
same logic, we would start school at 5 a.m. for students headed
for farm work, and at noon for students headed for night shifts.
A school schedule should follow learning, not imitate every
workplace at once.

Finally, consider the students themselves. In a survey last
spring, 9 out of 10 Willow Creek students said they would
prefer a later start. Critics dismiss student opinion, but
students are the people school is for, and their overwhelming
preference should settle the question. When 90 percent of the
people affected agree, further debate is just delay.

---

**Assessment note (teacher side):** the planted weaknesses are
(1) the Brookfield comparison, which is relevant but insufficient
on its own — one neighboring town, one semester, no control for
other changes — and (2) the closing move, which treats a
popularity vote (90 percent prefer) as settling an empirical
question — an appeal to popularity that ignores the costs the
author admits are real. Strong responses name both, credit the
author's fair treatment of the bus/job objection, and propose
what further evidence would be needed. See the answer key.
