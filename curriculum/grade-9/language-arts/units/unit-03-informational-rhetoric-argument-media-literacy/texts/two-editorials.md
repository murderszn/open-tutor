# Two Editorials, One Empty Lot

**Two original editorials written for this unit about the same
fictional decision: what Willow Creek should build on the empty lot
behind the old rail yard. All people, places, and figures are
fictional practice material.**

---

## Editorial A — "Build the Skate Park"

Willow Creek's teenagers have nowhere to go. That is not a complaint;
it is a fact you can check any afternoon. The basketball courts are
booked by leagues, the pool charges admission, and the youth center
closes at five. So teenagers gather in parking lots, and then adults
complain about teenagers gathering in parking lots. We created this
problem, and a skate park would solve it.

A skate park is cheap, tough, and open to everyone. Concrete does not
need watering, weeding, or a paid manager. Once it is built, it costs
almost nothing to run, and it is used from the first warm day of
spring to the last warm day of autumn. Compare that with any program
that needs staff, schedules, and sign-up sheets. A park is simply
there, every day, for anyone with wheels.

Some worry about noise and scraped knees. But noise is the sound of
young people outside instead of inside on screens, and every sport
carries scrapes. We do not ban soccer because players twist ankles.
We teach them to play well and we accept the risk. Skaters deserve
the same respect we give every other athlete in this town.

Most of all, a skate park says something about who belongs here.
Right now our town builds for drivers, shoppers, and homeowners, and
then wonders why its young people feel like guests. Pour the
concrete. Give teenagers a place that is theirs, in public, in
daylight, where adults can see them being exactly what they are:
neighbors.

## Editorial B — "Plant the Garden"

The empty lot behind the old rail yard could feed this town, and we
should let it. A community garden would turn a weedy rectangle into
rows of tomatoes, beans, and herbs grown by the people who eat them.
It would give seniors a reason to walk over in the morning, children
a place to learn where food comes from, and families a smaller
grocery bill. Nothing else we could build there does all three.

A garden is also the only option that pays for its own upkeep. Plot
fees from twenty families would cover water and tools, and the
waiting list at the county's only other garden already holds forty
names. Demand is not a guess; it is a list with names on it. The
town would not need to hire anyone or run a program. It would need
to unlock a gate and let residents do the work they are already
asking to do.

Critics call a garden slow and unexciting. They are right that
tomatoes take months, and that is exactly the point. A town that
only builds amusements teaches its children that everything worth
having arrives instantly. A garden teaches patience, seasons, and
shared work — lessons no ride or ramp can teach, and lessons this
town badly needs.

Finally, consider who a garden serves. A skate park serves one age
group with one interest. A garden serves toddlers digging in the
dirt, teenagers earning service hours, parents stretching a budget,
and grandparents passing on knowledge. One lot, every generation,
every season. Plant it, and watch what grows.

**About these texts:** written for Unit 03, Lesson 2 (rhetoric and
purpose) and Practice F (paired-editorial comparison). Both
editorials argue well; neither is presented as the "correct" view.
The lessons ask learners to name each author's purpose, appeals, and
framing — not to pick a winner.
