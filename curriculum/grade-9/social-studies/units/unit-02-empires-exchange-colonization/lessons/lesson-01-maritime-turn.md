# Lesson 01 — The Maritime Turn: Why Ships Crossed Oceans

**Unit 02 · Week 7, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain the motives and the means behind
Portuguese and Spanish oceanic expansion, and show how new sea routes joined
four continents by about 1550.

**Materials:** this lesson, blank world map, notebook/inquiry journal,
ruler, the unit's port-reconstruction image (or its text alternative).

**Readiness check (5 min):** On your blank map, draw the three Afro-Eurasian
connection systems from U01 (Silk Roads, Indian Ocean network, trans-Saharan
routes). Now draw one thing all three share: *they connect Afro-Eurasia, but
none reaches the Americas or crosses the Atlantic.* (That's the gap this
lesson closes: between 1450 and 1550, Europeans learned to sail the open
ocean — and the map of connection changed forever.)

## Part 1 — Motives: why go to sea at all? (explicit teaching, 15 min)

In 1450, Europeans were the poor cousins of the world economy. Pepper,
cloves, and cinnamon came to Europe through long chains of middlemen across
the Indian Ocean, the Red Sea, and the Mediterranean — and every link took a
cut. By the time pepper reached Venice, it could cost many times its price
at the Malabar Coast. The Portuguese and Spanish crowns wanted to cut out the
middlemen: sail around Africa or across the Atlantic and buy Asian goods at
the source.

Three motives pushed ships outward, and historians often remember them as
**God, gold, and glory**:

1. **Gold (wealth):** direct access to spices, silk, and — soon — American
   silver and sugar. The crown that found a sea route would control the
   profits.
2. **God (faith):** Catholic rulers and missionaries wanted to spread
   Christianity and find allies against Muslim powers. Prince Henry of
   Portugal (d. 1460) sponsored Atlantic voyages for both faith and profit.
3. **Glory (power):** new lands meant titles, prestige, and leverage against
   rival kingdoms. In 1494, Spain and Portugal signed the **Treaty of
   Tordesillas**, literally drawing a line down the Atlantic map and claiming
   everything on one side for Spain and the other for Portugal — as if the
   people already living there had no say.

**Worked example 1 — motive check:** Suppose a Portuguese captain in 1490
says, "I sail to find Christians in the east and spices for the crown."
Which motives is he naming? *God* (finding Christians/allies) and *gold*
(spices for profit). The third, glory, is usually implied: the crown that
backed him expected prestige and power from whatever he found. A common
error is to pick only one motive — real voyages usually stacked all three.

**Worked example 2 — reading a claim:** A textbook says, "Europeans explored
because they were curious." Use the three motives to test that claim:
*curiosity* is real but incomplete — the ships were expensive, and kings
funded them for profit, faith, and power. "Curiosity alone" hides who paid
and why. Good history asks *who benefits* when it evaluates motives.

## Part 2 — Means: how could small ships cross an ocean? (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Wanting to sail and being able to sail are different things. Four means made
the ocean crossing possible:

1. **The caravel** — a small, fast ship combining square sails (power with
   the wind behind) and triangular **lateen sails** (ability to sail closer
   to the wind). It could tack back up the Atlantic when bigger ships could
   not.
2. **Navigation instruments** — the **astrolabe** and quadrant let sailors
   estimate latitude from the sun and stars; improved **portolan charts**
   recorded coastlines and compass bearings from earlier voyages.
3. **Knowledge of winds** — Portuguese sailors learned the *volta do mar*
   ("turn of the sea"): sail far out into the Atlantic to catch westerly
   winds home, instead of fighting the wind along the African coast. The
   "triangle" was a wind circuit before it was ever a trade circuit.
4. **Gunpowder artillery** — ship-mounted cannon gave small European crews
   outsized force in coastal encounters, from Calicut to Tenochtitlan's lake.

The timeline matters: Bartolomeu **Dias** rounded the Cape of Good Hope in
**1488**; **Columbus** reached the Caribbean in **1492** (sailing for
Castile); Vasco da **Gama** reached Calicut, India, in **1498**. By about
1550, regular sea routes joined Europe, Africa, Asia, and the Americas —
four continents in one trading system for the first time.

## Part 3 — Observation: reading a port (guided, 10 min)

Open `../assets/atlantic-port-reconstruction.png` (or read the text alternative
below). **This image is an AI reconstruction, not a historical source** — it
helps you picture a working port, but it proves nothing about the past.

**Text alternative (contains everything needed for the questions):** The
scene shows a stone quay at dawn. Five large sailing ships with tall masts
and furled square sails lie at anchor offshore. Small rowboats ferry sacks
and barrels between ships and shore. On the quay, workers roll wooden
barrels and carry heavy sacks; large stacks of sacks, barrels, and bales
wait beside a wooden crane lifting a crate. Several well-dressed men stand
apart, talking — merchants or officials overseeing the work.

**Guided questions (answer in your journal):**

1. List five things you can see that show goods were being *moved*, not just
   stored. (Possible: rowboats ferrying sacks; workers rolling barrels; men
   carrying sacks on shoulders; the crane lifting a crate; stacked bales
   waiting by the water.)
2. What does the presence of *many ships at once* suggest about this port's
   role? (It was a hub — ships came from different places on schedules, the
   way airports work today.)
3. Whose work do you see, and whose work is missing? (You see dockworkers
   and merchants. You do not see the sailors' months at sea, the farmers who
   grew the goods, or the enslaved people whose labor filled many of those
   barrels — the image, like much of the record, shows the port, not the
   whole system.)

**Teacher note (misconceptions):** Learners often treat the image as
evidence ("this proves..."). Redirect: a reconstruction illustrates a
lesson; only period sources can support claims about the past. See
[teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice (10 min, with adult or answer key nearby)

1. A Spanish king funds a 1502 voyage "to claim new lands for the crown and
   bring the true faith to their peoples." Name the two motives stated, and
   the one implied.
2. Why was the lateen sail important for *returning* from West Africa?
   (Hint: which way does the wind blow along that coast?)
3. Order these events: Tordesillas, da Gama reaches Calicut, Dias rounds the
   Cape, Columbus reaches the Caribbean. Then write one sentence explaining
   what the order shows about how fast the "maritime turn" happened.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 3–4 sentences why "Europeans were curious" is an incomplete
   explanation for oceanic expansion. Name who paid for voyages and what
   they wanted back.
5. Draw a simple map: Europe, West Africa, the Atlantic, the Caribbean,
   India. Mark Dias's 1488 turning point, Columbus's 1492 crossing, and da
   Gama's 1498 arrival with arrows. Label each arrow with its year.

## Applied task

6. **Merchant's ledger, 1501:** You are a spice merchant in Lisbon. Write a
   5-sentence journal entry the week da Gama's ships return from Calicut.
   Mention at least two of the three motives and one of the means — and
   note one group of people whose labor made the cargo possible but who will
   not appear in your ledger.

## Exit check

7. In one sentence: what changed between 1450 and 1550 that joined four
   continents by sea? (Acceptable: Europeans learned to sail open-ocean
   wind circuits with caravels, lateen sails, and new navigation, so regular
   routes connected Europe, Africa, Asia, and the Americas.)
