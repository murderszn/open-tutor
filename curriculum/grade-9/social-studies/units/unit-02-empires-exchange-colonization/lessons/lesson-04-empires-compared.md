# Lesson 04 — Five Empires Compared, and What the Record Cannot Tell Us

**Unit 02 · Week 10, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can compare five early-modern empires on a
consistent frame, and explain whose voices the surviving record
underrepresents and why.

**Materials:** this lesson, comparison-table worksheet (in practice.md,
Set D), notebook/inquiry journal.

**Readiness check (5 min):** List two empires you already know from this
course (e.g., the Mexica Triple Alliance, the Inca, Spain, Portugal). For
each, answer: *where did its wealth come from?* (Tribute? trade taxes?
mines? plantations?) This lesson widens the lens: the "age of European
expansion" looked very different from Istanbul, Isfahan, Agra, and Beijing —
and in 1600, the European empires were not obviously the strongest players.

---

## Part 1 — Five empires on one frame (explicit teaching, 20 min)

Compare on four questions: **(a)** what legitimized rule? **(b)** how were
diverse peoples governed? **(c)** where did wealth come from? **(d)** how
did the empire connect to wider trade?

1. **Ottoman Empire** (c. 1299–1922; peak under Suleiman, r. 1520–1566).
   (a) The sultan as caliph — defender of Islam — plus military success.
   (b) The **millet** system: religious communities governed their own
   family law under imperial oversight; the **devshirme** levy recruited
   Christian youths into administration and the **janissary** infantry.
   (c) Taxes on land and trade, including the tolls on east–west routes
   through Constantinople (taken 1453). (d) The empire *was* the hinge of
   Afro-Eurasian trade — which is exactly why Europeans sought sea routes
   around it.

2. **Safavid Empire** (1501–1736, Persia). (a) Shah Ismail I founded it as a
   **Twelver Shi'a** state — faith as legitimacy against Sunni Ottoman and
   Uzbek neighbors. (b) Qizilbash tribal warriors balanced against a Persian
   bureaucracy; under Shah Abbas I (r. 1588–1629) a standing army and a new
   capital at Isfahan. (c) Silk — Persian silk flowed west to Ottoman and
   European markets. (d) Overland trade hub between Central Asia, India, and
   the Mediterranean.

3. **Mughal Empire** (1526–1857, South Asia). (a) Babur's 1526 victory at
   Panipat founded it; Akbar (r. 1556–1605) legitimized rule through
   **sulh-i kul** ("universal peace") — toleration of Hindu, Muslim, Jain,
   and Christian subjects. (b) A ranked bureaucracy (*mansabdars*) and
   revenue settlement over a mostly non-Muslim population. (c) Land revenue
   — the Mughals taxed one of the world's most productive agrarian
   economies. (d) Indian Ocean trade: textiles for silver, with European
   companies as junior partners at Mughal ports.

4. **Ming and Qing China** (Ming 1368–1644; Qing 1644–1912). (a) The
   Mandate of Heaven and a Confucian examination bureaucracy; the Qing, a
   Manchu conquest dynasty, legitimized itself by adopting Chinese
   institutions. (b) Direct bureaucratic rule over the world's largest
   population. (c) Agrarian taxes — increasingly demanded in **silver**,
   which pulled American silver across the Pacific via Manila. (d) After
   Zheng He's voyages ended (1433), China restricted official maritime
   trade — yet private Chinese merchants and silver demand still tied China
   to the world economy. Restraint, not isolation.

5. **European overseas empires** (Spanish and Portuguese, 1500s–1700s).
   (a) Papal donation and the Tordesillas line; later, "civilizing mission"
   claims. (b) Settler colonies, plantation zones, and trading-post
   networks — ruling at a distance through viceroys, companies, and forts.
   (c) American silver and plantation crops produced by coerced labor.
   (d) They *built* the Atlantic system — but in the Indian Ocean they
   mostly muscled into trade networks that Asian merchants had run for
   centuries.

**Worked example 1 — using the frame:** Question: "Which empire was
strongest in 1600?" A weak answer picks one ("the Spanish — they had the
most silver"). A frame-based answer compares: by *population and revenue*,
Ming China and the Mughals dwarfed Spain; by *oceanic reach*, Spain and
Portugal led; by *military land power*, the Ottomans. "Strongest" depends on
the measure — state your measure before your answer.

**Worked example 2 — continuity check:** The Ottomans taxed the trade
routes; the Spanish seized silver mines; the Mughals taxed farmland. Three
different wealth bases — but one shared logic: empires converted *control
over people and routes* into revenue. Exchange (trade) and empire (power)
were not opposites; empires grew by taxing exchange, and exchange grew along
imperial routes.

## Part 2 — The limits of the record (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Now the hard question: **who is missing?** The surviving written record of
1450–1750 was produced mostly by people with power, literacy, and archives:
imperial bureaucrats, merchants keeping ledgers, missionaries writing home,
ship captains filing logs. Three consequences (D2.His.8.9-12):

1. **Merchant ledgers count goods, not people.** A ledger tells you how
   many barrels left a port — not who loaded them, under what coercion, or
   what they thought about it.
2. **Missionary letters filter through conversion.** A missionary describes
   Indigenous beliefs to show why conversion is needed — useful evidence of
   *the missionary's* aims, weak evidence of Indigenous belief on its own
   terms.
3. **Indigenous and enslaved voices survive mostly mediated.** The
   *Florentine Codex* (Book 12) preserves Nahuatl accounts of the conquest —
   but recorded decades later, in a Franciscan friar's project, in a
   colonial context. Equiano's narrative is firsthand — but published for
   abolitionist purposes in London, and scholars debate details of his early
   life. Neither problem erases the source; both require the historian to
   state the limits out loud.

**The rule for this unit:** every source gets three questions — *what does
it show? what does it hide? what is it useful for?* A source that hides much
can still be useful, once you name what it hides.

## Guided practice (10 min)

1. Fill one row of the comparison table (practice.md Set D) for the empire
   of your choice: legitimacy, governance of diversity, wealth source,
   trade connection.
2. A merchant's ledger from 1620 lists "40 barrels sugar, 12 captives."
   State one thing it shows, one thing it hides, and one inquiry it is
   useful for.
3. Why is the Florentine Codex Book 12 valuable *and* limited as evidence
   about the conquest? (Two sentences.)

## Independent practice

4. Complete the comparison table for all five empires. Then answer: which
   two empires were most similar in *how they legitimized rule*, and which
   two were most different in *where wealth came from*? Justify each in two
   sentences.
5. Rank these by how directly they preserve an enslaved or Indigenous
   person's own perspective (most direct → least): a plantation owner's
   ledger, Equiano's published narrative, a missionary's letter describing
   a revolt. Justify the ranking.

## Applied task

6. **The archive's missing shelf:** Imagine you are building an archive of
   1450–1750 that fixes the record's gaps. Name three kinds of sources you
   would collect (e.g., oral histories, workers' songs, market women's
   accounts), and for each, state what it would show that ledgers and
   imperial decrees cannot.

## Exit check

7. In one sentence: why can't the surviving written record alone tell the
   full story of 1450–1750? (Acceptable: it was produced mostly by the
   powerful and literate, so it underrepresents Indigenous and enslaved
   voices; historians must read it against its grain and name its limits.)
