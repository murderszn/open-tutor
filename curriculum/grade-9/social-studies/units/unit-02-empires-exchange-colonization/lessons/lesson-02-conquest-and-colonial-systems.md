# Lesson 02 — Conquest and Colonial Systems in the Americas

**Unit 02 · Week 8, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain the falls of the Mexica and Inca
with multiple interacting causes, and describe how colonial labor systems
extracted wealth from land and people.

**Materials:** this lesson, timeline supplies, notebook/inquiry journal.

**Readiness check (5 min):** List the means from Lesson 01 that let
Europeans project force across an ocean (caravels, cannon, navigation).
Then answer: *if 600 Spaniards faced a city of 200,000, what besides weapons
could decide the outcome?* (Possible: disease, local allies, the defenders'
own politics, food and water. This lesson argues conquest was never "a few
men with guns beat an empire" — that story is itself a colonial myth.)

---

## Part 1 — Two conquests, many causes (explicit teaching, 20 min)

### The Mexica (1519–1521)

When Hernán Cortés landed on the Mexican coast in 1519, the Mexica (often
called Aztec) Triple Alliance ruled from the island city of Tenochtitlan —
one of the world's largest cities, perhaps 200,000+ people, fed by
chinampa gardens and supplied by tribute from subject peoples. In August
1521, Tenochtitlan fell. No single cause explains it; historians trace
**interacting** causes:

1. **Disease.** Smallpox arrived with Europeans in 1520 and tore through a
   population with no immunity — killing the Mexica ruler Cuitlahuac among
   countless others, and collapsing the city's defenses during the siege.
2. **Indigenous allies.** Peoples resentful of Mexica tribute and sacrifice
   demands — above all the Tlaxcalans — joined the Spanish with tens of
   thousands of warriors. The conquest was largely fought by Indigenous
   armies against an Indigenous empire, with a small Spanish core.
3. **Political capture.** Cortés seized the ruler Moctezuma II and tried to
   rule through him; when that collapsed, the Spanish cut the causeways and
   the aqueduct and starved the city during a months-long siege.
4. **Technology and animals** — steel swords, guns, and above all horses —
   mattered in open battle, but historians now treat them as the *smallest*
   of the four: without disease and allies, 600 Spaniards could not have
   taken Tenochtitlan.

### The Inca (1532–1572)

Francisco Pizarro reached the Andes in 1532 — at the worst possible moment
for the Inca. A **civil war** between the brothers Huáscar and Atahualpa had
just ended, leaving the empire's armies exhausted. At Cajamarca in November
1532, Pizarro's ~180 men captured Atahualpa during a meeting, demanded a
roomful of gold and silver as ransom — received it — and executed him
anyway in 1533. Inca resistance continued from the refuge of Vilcabamba
until 1572, forty years later.

**Worked example 1 — multiple causes:** A student says, "Guns beat the
Inca." Test it against the evidence: at Cajamarca the Spanish had perhaps
180 men against thousands. Guns helped, but the decisive factors were the
civil war that had already broken the empire's unity, the capture of a
single irreplaceable ruler, and disease arriving ahead of the Spaniards.
*"Guns beat the Inca"* is a single-cause story; the evidence supports a
**causal web**: disease → weakened empire → civil war → capture →
collapse. In your journal, sketch the web with arrows between causes.

**Worked example 2 — the myth of the easy conquest:** Spanish accounts
emphasized a handful of heroes because that story justified the conquest
("God favored the brave"). But the Tlaxcalan allies outnumbered the Spanish
by roughly a hundred to one at Tenochtitlan. Ask of any conquest story:
*who is missing from this telling, and why would the teller leave them
out?* (D2.His.4.9-12: perspectives are shaped by the teller's position.)

## Part 2 — Colonial systems: how wealth was extracted (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Conquest was the beginning; **systems** made it profitable for centuries.
Three labor systems organized colonial work:

1. **Encomienda.** The crown granted a colonist (*encomendero*) the right to
   demand labor and tribute from an Indigenous community — in theory in
   exchange for Christian instruction and "protection." In practice it was
   forced labor. The Dominican friar Bartolomé de las Casas, after witnessing
   the system's cruelty, wrote *A Short Account of the Destruction of the
   Indies* (1542) — and the crown issued the **New Laws of 1542** limiting
   encomiendas (colonists got most of them revoked by 1545).
2. **Repartimiento / mita.** Rotational labor drafts: communities had to
   send a share of men for mines and public works. At **Potosí** (silver
   discovered 1545), Viceroy Toledo formalized the mita in the 1570s;
   mercury amalgamation then multiplied output. The mountain's silver flowed
   to Spain and, via the Manila galleons (1565–1815), to China — whose tax
   system demanded silver. Death-toll estimates vary widely; the unit teaches
   the range (hundreds of thousands to several million), not a single number.
3. **Plantation slavery.** On Brazilian sugar plantations (*engenhos*) and
   Caribbean estates, enslaved Africans worked under gang-labor discipline
   to grow a single cash crop (**monoculture**) for European markets. The
   São Tomé sugar model was transplanted to Brazil; the profits funded more
   ships, more forts, more captives — a self-feeding system.

Above these systems sat the **casta** hierarchy — a legal-social ranking by
ancestry that decided who could hold office, marry whom, and pay what tax.
Empire was not just extraction; it was a ranked order of human worth,
enforced by law.

## Guided practice (10 min)

1. For the Mexica conquest, rank these four causes by importance and
   justify your ranking in two sentences: (a) disease, (b) Indigenous
   allies, (c) political capture and siege, (d) guns and horses.
2. Why did the Inca civil war matter *more* than Spanish guns at Cajamarca?
3. In one sentence each: what did the encomienda promise in theory, and
   what did it deliver in practice?

## Independent practice

4. Compare the Potosí mita and Brazilian plantation slavery: name one
   similarity in *how labor was organized* and one difference in *who the
   laborers were and how they got there*.
5. Explain why a silver coin minted in Mexico could end up paying taxes in
   China. (Trace: mine → Spanish treasury → Manila galleon → Chinese
   markets, where silver was in demand for tax payments.)

## Applied task

6. **Evidence card sort:** Make three cards — *Encomienda*, *Mita*,
   *Plantation slavery*. On each, write: (a) who worked, (b) who profited,
   (c) one source that could tell us about the workers' experience and one
   limit of that source. Bring the cards to the Week 8 S3 investigation.

## Exit check

7. Complete: "The Mexica and Inca were not defeated by ____ alone; the
   conquests resulted from ____." (Acceptable: "...by European weapons
   alone; ...from interacting causes — disease, Indigenous alliances and
   rivalries, political capture, and colonial systems that turned conquest
   into centuries of extraction.")
