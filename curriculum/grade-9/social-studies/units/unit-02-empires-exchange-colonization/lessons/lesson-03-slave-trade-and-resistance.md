# Lesson 03 — The Transatlantic Slave Trade as a System, and Resistance to It

**Unit 02 · Week 9, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain the scale, organization, and
economics of the transatlantic slave trade using the Slave Voyages data,
and describe how enslaved people resisted it.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal. **Adult notice:** this
lesson discusses the Middle Passage, forced labor, and violence — textual
sources only, no graphic imagery. The adult previews the Equiano excerpt,
frames discussion around evidence and human dignity, and offers a
reflection alternative if needed. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) §8.

**Readiness check (5 min):** From Lesson 02, recall one colonial labor
system and who it exploited. Now consider: *plantations needed ever more
workers as Indigenous populations collapsed from disease and overwork. Where
did the replacement labor come from, and who organized the supply?* (This

## Part 1 — Scale: the largest forced migration in history (explicit teaching, 15 min)

The authoritative reconstruction is the **Slave Voyages Trans-Atlantic Slave
Trade Database** (SlaveVoyages.org), built by an international scholarly
team from ship logs, port registers, insurance records, and account books
across four continents — now documenting about 36,000 voyages. Its
estimates for **1501–1866** (verified 2026-10-20):

- **~12.5 million people embarked in Africa** — put aboard slave ships.
- **~10.7 million disembarked in the Americas** — arrived alive to be sold.
- **~1.8 million died during the Middle Passage** — the ocean crossing
  itself (about 1 in 7 of those embarked).

Three things these numbers do and do not mean:

1. They are **estimates with margins of error** — careful ones, built from
   roughly four-fifths of voyages documented directly and the rest modeled.
2. They **exclude** deaths during capture raids, the march to the coast,
   imprisonment in coastal barracoons, and the deadly first years of
   plantation labor ("seasoning"). The true human toll is larger than 12.5
   million; no complete aggregate exists.
3. Before 1820, **four enslaved Africans crossed the Atlantic for every one
   European** — Africa, not Europe, repopulated the Americas after 1492.

Portuguese-flagged vessels carried the most captives (~5.8 million
embarked), overwhelmingly to Brazil; British vessels carried the next most
(~3.3 million). The trade peaked in the 1700s and was not fully suppressed
until the late 1800s.

**Worked example 1 — reading an estimate:** A student says, "12.5 million
people died in the slave trade." Correct the statement precisely: *12.5
million is the estimate of people **embarked**; about 10.7 million
disembarked and about 1.8 million died **during the crossing** — and the
figure excludes deaths before embarkation and after landing, so the total
toll is higher.* Precision about what a number counts is a historical skill.

**Worked example 2 — scale as system, not event:** Divide 12.5 million by
365 years: roughly 34,000 people per year, every year, for more than three
centuries — about a full ship's company every few days. That rhythm is the
point: this was not an eruption of cruelty but a **routine, insured,
capitalized business** — joint-stock companies (like England's Royal
African Company, chartered 1672), coastal forts and factories (Elmina,
built by the Portuguese in 1482), credit networks, and marine insurance.
The paperwork of business is exactly what lets historians measure it now.

## Part 2 — Resistance: every stage, every form (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Enslaved people resisted at every stage. Three forms:

1. **Day-to-day resistance** — the most common and least recorded:
   slowdowns, breaking tools, feigning illness, preserving African
   languages, religions, and foodways, maintaining families under a system
   designed to break them. Survival itself was resistance.
2. **Escape and marronage** — fleeing to form free communities
   (*maroons*, from Spanish *cimarrón*). The great **Quilombo dos Palmares**
   in Brazil sheltered thousands for most of the 1600s before its
   destruction in 1694; Jamaican Maroons fought the British to treaties in
   1739–40. Across the Americas, maroon communities endured for generations.
3. **Revolt** — from shipboard uprisings during the Middle Passage to
   large-scale rebellions: the **Pueblo Revolt of 1680** expelled the Spanish
   from New Mexico for twelve years; the **Stono Rebellion of 1739** in South
   Carolina saw dozens of enslaved people march for freedom before being
   crushed. Revolts were usually defeated — and still they kept happening,
   which tells you what the system felt like from inside.

**A note on the Week 9 S2 source:** you will read an excerpt from Olaudah
Equiano's *Interesting Narrative* (1789) — a formerly enslaved man's
account of the Middle Passage, in the public domain. Historians debate some
details of Equiano's early life (whether he was born in West Africa, as he
wrote, or in South Carolina). That debate does not erase the account's
value — but it is a good reminder of D2.His.8: even firsthand accounts have
limits, and good historians state them.

## Guided practice (10 min)

1. State the three Slave Voyages figures (embarked, disembarked, Middle
   Passage deaths) and the date range. What does each figure count?
2. Why does the 12.5-million figure **understate** the total human toll?
   Name two categories of death it excludes.
3. Match each resistance example to its form (day-to-day / escape / revolt):
   (a) Palmares, (b) tool-breaking, (c) Stono 1739, (d) Jamaican Maroon
   treaties 1739–40.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 3–4 sentences why historians call the slave trade a
   "capitalized business" rather than just "cruelty." Name two business
   institutions involved.
5. Choose one resistance form and argue in a paragraph that it was the most
   significant — then add one sentence conceding the strongest argument for
   a different form.

## Applied task

6. **Numbers with names:** The database records ~36,000 voyages but very
   few names of the enslaved. Write 6 sentences: three stating what the
   *numbers* let historians prove, and three stating what the numbers
   *cannot* tell us — and what kinds of sources could fill those gaps.

## Exit check

7. In one sentence: why did the slave trade persist for 365 years, and what
   does the persistence of revolts tell us? (Acceptable: it persisted
   because it was a profitable, organized, state-backed business; revolts
   persisted because enslaved people never accepted the system — resistance
   was constant, not exceptional.)
