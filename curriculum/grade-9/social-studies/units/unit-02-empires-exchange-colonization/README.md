# Unit 02 — Empires, Exchange, Colonization, and Resistance

**Grade 9 · Social Studies · Modern world history · Weeks 7–10 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-20 for issue #45; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Between about 1450 and 1750, the world became one connected system — but
connection was not the same as fairness. European ships learned to cross
oceans, and in their wake came the largest biological exchange in human
history, two conquests that ended Indigenous empires, a forced-labor economy
that made some places rich and destroyed others, and the largest forced
migration ever recorded. This unit follows that story honestly: the maritime
turn that made it possible, the exchange that reshaped diets and diseases on
every continent, the colonial systems built on conquest and enslaved labor,
the resistance people mounted at every step — and the uncomfortable fact that
the written record mostly preserves the winners' version.

The unit's inquiry question runs through all four weeks: **between 1450 and
1750, did exchange or empire change the world more — and whose story does the
surviving record tell?**

A note on sources: enslaved and Indigenous voices are underrepresented in the
surviving written record. This unit deliberately pairs the accounts that
survive with the limits of what they can tell us (C3 D2.His.8.9-12). Learner
readings are original passages or short public-domain excerpts; secondary
claims are summarized and linked, never copied.

**Sensitive content (adult preview required):** this unit covers conquest,
enslavement, forced labor, and colonial violence — documentary and textual
sources only, no graphic imagery. The adult gives advance notice before Week
9's Middle Passage and slave-trade sessions, previews every source, frames
discussion around evidence and human dignity, and offers an observation/
reflection alternative for any activity the learner finds overwhelming. See
[teacher-guide.md](teacher-guide.md) §8 and the track
[scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md) §6–§7.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain the maritime turn** — describe the motives (markets, faith,
   power) and means (ships, sails, navigation, artillery) behind Portuguese
   and Spanish oceanic expansion, and show how new sea routes joined four
   continents by about 1550. (D2.His.1.9-12)
2. **Trace the Columbian exchange** — map two-direction flows of crops,
   animals, disease, and silver, and explain demographic and economic effects
   with evidence, using hedged language where scholars debate the numbers.
   (D2.His.2, D2.His.14.9-12)
3. **Analyze conquest and colonial labor systems** — give multiple
   interacting causes for the falls of the Mexica and Inca, compare the
   encomienda, the mita, and plantation slavery from contrasting sources, and
   explain how colonial economies extracted wealth. (D2.His.4, D2.His.14.9-12)
4. **Explain the transatlantic slave trade as a system and analyze
   resistance** — state its scale and organization from the Slave Voyages
   data (12.5M embarked, 10.7M disembarked, 1501–1866, with the estimate's
   limits), describe its economics, and compare forms of resistance from
   day-to-day acts to revolts. (D2.His.4, D2.His.14.9-12; D3.1–D3.4.9-12)
5. **Compare early-modern empires and evaluate the limits of the record** —
   compare Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, Ming/Qing, and European overseas empires
   on a consistent frame, and detect whose voices surviving sources
   underrepresent and why. (D2.His.8.9-12; D2.His.16.9-12)

**Source notes (checked at authoring, 2026-10-20):**

- Slave-trade scale: Slave Voyages Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database
  (~36,000 voyages, 1501–1866): **~12.5 million embarked in Africa, ~10.7
  million disembarked in the Americas**, ~1.8 million died during the Middle
  Passage; the figure excludes deaths before embarkation or after landing.
  Consortium: Rice, Emory, Harvard, Omohundro Institute, UC campuses, NMAAHC.
  Verified via Rice/Oracle coverage and the database's Wikipedia summary,
  2026-10-20.
- Potosí: silver discovered 1545; mita labor draft formalized under Viceroy
  Toledo in the 1570s; mercury amalgamation sharply raised output after the
  1570s; by the 1600s the city rivaled Europe's largest. Death-toll estimates
  vary widely (hundreds of thousands to several million); the exact number is
  debated — the unit teaches the range, not a single figure.
- Indigenous population decline after 1492: estimates vary widely by region;
  many regions lost a large majority to disease, warfare, and exploitation
  within a century. The unit uses ranges and attributes them; no single
  "90%" figure is presented as settled fact.
- Equiano excerpt: *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah
  Equiano* (1789), public domain (Project Gutenberg eBook #15399). The
  teacher guide notes the scholarly debate about Equiano's birthplace as a
  D2.His.8 sourcing lesson.
- Conquest dates used: Tenochtitlan falls August 1521; Cajamarca November
  1532; New Laws 1542 (partially revoked 1545); Valladolid debate 1550–51;
  Pueblo Revolt 1680; Manila galleons 1565–1815; Dias rounds the Cape 1488;
  da Gama reaches Calicut 1498; Tordesillas 1494.
