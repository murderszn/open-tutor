## Lesson 01 — The Maritime Turn

**Guided practice**

1. Stated: *God* ("bring the true faith") and *glory* ("claim new lands
   for the crown"). Implied: *gold* — the crown expected the voyage to pay
   (and the ledger to show it).
2. The lateen (triangular) sail lets a ship sail closer to the wind. The
   prevailing winds along the West African coast blow from the north, so a
   square-rigged ship struggles to return northward; lateen sails (and the
   *volta do mar* — sailing far out to catch westerlies) made the return
   leg possible.
3. Order: Dias rounds the Cape (1488) → Columbus reaches the Caribbean
   (1492) → Tordesillas (1494) → da Gama reaches Calicut (1498). Sentence
   (acceptable): "In one decade, Europeans rounded Africa, crossed the
   Atlantic, divided the ocean between two crowns, and reached India — the
   'maritime turn' was a ten-year revolution, not a slow drift."

**Independent practice**

4. Acceptable: "Curiosity" names no funder and no payoff. Crowns and
   merchants paid for caravels, crews, and cannon because they wanted
   direct access to Asian goods (profit), converts and allies (faith), and
   prestige and power over rivals (glory). Curiosity may have motivated
   individuals, but it does not explain who financed the voyages or why.
5. Map check: Europe → (1488, around southern Africa) → Indian Ocean →
   Calicut 1498; Spain → (1492, west across the Atlantic) → Caribbean;
   Tordesillas line 1494 between them. Arrows labeled with years; accept
   reasonable placement.

**Applied task (6)** — sample: "The ships came in today from Calicut, and
the whole quay smells of pepper. His Majesty will be pleased — no more
Venetian middlemen taking their cut [gold], and the priests say these
voyages carry the faith eastward [God]. Our new lateen-rigged caravels made
the return possible where older ships failed [means]. The barrels are full,
but my ledger has no line for the hands that loaded them in Calicut — or
the ones who will unload them here." Accept any 5-sentence entry naming 2+
motives, 1+ means, and the missing labor.

**Exit check (7)** — acceptable: "Between 1450 and 1550, Europeans learned
to sail open-ocean wind circuits with caravels, lateen sails, and new
navigation instruments, so regular sea routes connected Europe, Africa,
Asia, and the Americas."

## Lesson 02 — Conquest and Colonial Systems

**Guided practice**

1. Sample ranking: 1. disease — it killed the ruler and collapsed the
   city's defense during the siege, an irreplaceable loss; 2. Indigenous
   allies — tens of thousands of Tlaxcalan and other warriors did most of
   the fighting; 3. political capture and siege — cutting causeways and the
   aqueduct starved the city; 4. guns and horses — decisive in open battle
   but useless without the other three. Accept any justified order that
   places guns/horses last or explains why a different order is defended.
2. The civil war between Huáscar and Atahualpa had already exhausted the
   Inca's armies and unity before Pizarro arrived; capturing one ruler then
   decapitated the empire's command. Guns helped ~180 men survive the
   encounter, but without the civil war and the capture, they could not
   have taken an empire.
3. Theory: the *encomendero* received labor/tribute in exchange for
   Christian instruction and "protection." Practice: forced labor and
   tribute extraction with little instruction or protection — which is why
   Las Casas's account helped prompt the New Laws of 1542.

**Independent practice**

4. Similarity: both organized coerced Indigenous labor for colonial
   production under legal cover (encomienda grants; mita drafts). Difference:
   the mita was a rotational state labor draft sending community members to
   mines (Potosí), while plantation slavery used permanently enslaved
   African laborers bought through the transatlantic trade for gang labor
   on monoculture estates. (Also acceptable: encomienda tied labor to a
   colonist's grant; mita tied it to community quotas.)
5. Acceptable: "Silver mined at Potosí/Zacatecas flowed to Spain as royal
   revenue; Spanish ships (the Manila galleons, 1565–1815) carried silver
   across the Pacific to China, where the Ming tax system demanded payment
   in silver — so American silver ended up settling Chinese tax bills."

**Applied task (6)** — card checks:
- *Encomienda:* workers — Indigenous communities under a grant; profit —
  encomendero/crown; source — Las Casas, *Short Account* (1542): shows
  cruelty, hides Indigenous voices (written by a Spaniard for the crown).
- *Mita:* workers — drafted community men; profit — mine owners/crown;
  source — mine-owner accounts or Toledo's ordinances: show organization,
  hide workers' experience (no worker-authored records survive in number).
- *Plantation slavery:* workers — enslaved Africans; profit — planters,
  merchants, European consumers; source — plantation inventories: show
  the system's economics, hide the enslaved as people (listed as property).

**Exit check (7)** — acceptable: "The Mexica and Inca were not defeated by
European weapons alone; the conquests resulted from interacting causes —
disease, Indigenous alliances and rivalries, political capture, and
colonial systems that turned conquest into centuries of extraction."

## Lesson 03 — The Slave Trade as a System; Resistance

**Guided practice**

1. Embarked ~12.5M (put aboard ships in Africa); disembarked ~10.7M
   (arrived alive in the Americas); ~1.8M died during the Middle Passage;
   range 1501–1866 (Slave Voyages database).
2. It excludes deaths during capture raids, the march to the coast,
   barracoon imprisonment, failed embarkations, and the deadly "seasoning"
   years after landing — so the total human toll is higher than 12.5M.
3. (a) Palmares — escape/marronage; (b) tool-breaking — day-to-day;
   (c) Stono 1739 — revolt; (d) Jamaican Maroon treaties 1739–40 —
   escape/marronage (negotiated freedom after war).

**Independent practice**

4. Acceptable: "It was run by joint-stock companies (e.g., the Royal
   African Company, 1672), financed with credit, protected by marine
   insurance, and supplied through coastal forts and factories — the same
   institutions as any large trade. Calling it a business matters because
   it explains persistence: profit, not just prejudice, kept it running
   for 365 years, and states taxed and chartered it."
5. Sample: "Day-to-day resistance was most significant because it was
   constant — millions of small acts of survival and cultural preservation
   across three centuries, while revolts were rare. Still, the strongest
   case for revolt is that only revolts forced the system to change its
   laws and defenses." Accept any argued position with a fair concession.

**Applied task (6)** — sample: "The numbers prove the trade's scale
(~12.5M embarked), its duration (365 years), and its business character
(tens of thousands of documented voyages). They cannot tell us what the
crossing felt like, what languages and families were broken, or how the
enslaved understood their own resistance — for that we need firsthand
accounts like Equiano's, oral traditions, and archaeology."

**Exit check (7)** — acceptable: "It persisted because it was a
profitable, organized, state-backed business; revolts persisted because
enslaved people never accepted the system — resistance was constant, not
exceptional."

## Lesson 04 — Five Empires; Limits of the Record

**Guided practice**

1–2. Row checks (sample Ottoman row): legitimacy — sultan as caliph +
   military success; diversity — millet system, devshirme/janissaries;
   wealth — land/trade taxes incl. Constantinople tolls; trade — hinge of
   Afro-Eurasian routes. Ledger: shows goods/quantities/prices; hides the
   people and coercion behind them; useful for reconstructing trade volumes
   and prices.
3. Valuable: preserves Nahuatl survivors' accounts of the siege — among the
   closest sources to an Indigenous perspective. Limited: recorded decades
   later, inside a Franciscan missionary project, in a colonial context —
   mediated, not a direct transcript.

**Independent practice**

4. Table check against Lesson 04 Part 1. Sample judgments: most similar
   legitimacy — Ottoman and Safavid (both fused Islamic faith with imperial
   rule; caliph vs. Shi'a shah); most different wealth — Mughal (agrarian
   land revenue) vs. Spanish/Portuguese (American silver + plantation
   crops). Accept other defended pairs (e.g., Ottoman/Safavid vs.
   Ming/Qing bureaucratic legitimacy).
5. Ranking: Equiano's narrative (firsthand, published by the author) >
   missionary's letter (eyewitness but filtered through conversion aims
   and written about others) > plantation ledger (counts people as
   property; preserves no perspective). Accept missionary/ledger swapped
   only with strong justification; the key point is the *reasoning* about
   mediation.

**Applied task (6)** — sample shelf: (1) transcribed oral histories of
descendant communities — show memory and meaning, which ledgers cannot;
(2) market women's or artisans' accounts — show the economy from below;
(3) workers' songs and religious practices — show cultural resistance and
daily life. Each names what decrees/ledgers omit.

**Exit check (7)** — acceptable: "The surviving written record was produced
mostly by the powerful and literate, so it underrepresents Indigenous and
enslaved voices; historians must read it against its grain and name its
limits."

## Practice Sets

**A1.** Accept five with one-sentence explanations each, e.g.: multiple
ships at anchor (scheduled hub traffic); rowboats ferrying sacks (ship-to-
shore transfer); workers rolling barrels (manual cargo handling); stacked
bales awaiting shipment (accumulation for export); the crane lifting a
crate (heavy-lift loading infrastructure).

**A2.** Sample (silver): originated in the Americas (Potosí 1545,
Zacatecas); traveled east to Europe and west via Manila to China;
receiving effect — European price inflation and state revenues, Chinese
tax monetization (economic); sending effect — mita labor deaths and
colonial extraction (economic + demographic). Both biological and
economic: silver is economic; smallpox/maize are biological (and then
economic). Accept any correctly traced item.

**A3.** Gets right: Europe gained wealth/foods; the Americas suffered
catastrophic demographic loss. Oversimplifies: treats continents as
uniform (Africa's losses, Asia's gains); ignores two-way effects (disease
and crops moved both directions; Europe also suffered epidemics debates).
Rewrite (sample): "The exchange enriched European states and diversified
diets worldwide, while Indigenous American societies suffered catastrophic
population collapse — but its effects varied by region and flowed in both
directions."

**A4.** Sample: "The triangle is useful because it shows the three-way
link between European goods, African captives, and American produce in one
glance. Its biggest limit is that most ships sailed bilateral routes, not
a three-leg circuit — the 'triangle' compresses many separate trades into
one shape. I will draw the real wind-and-route map and keep the triangle
only as a labeled inset marked 'simplified model.'"

**B1.** Account 1: Díaz, Spanish soldier, writing 1632 (decades later);
audience — Spanish readers/crown; purpose — glorify the conquest and his
own role. Account 2: Nahuatl collaborators with Sahagún, *Florentine
Codex* Book 12, recorded mid-1500s; audience — the Franciscan project
(and posterity); purpose — preserve Mexica memory of the catastrophe
within a missionary compilation.

**B2.** Agreement: Tenochtitlan fell after a siege involving hunger and
heavy death. Emphasis differences: Díaz emphasizes Spanish bravery and
divine favor, minimizing Indigenous allies; the Nahuatl accounts emphasize
suffering, disease, and loss, minimizing Spanish heroics.

**B3.** Siege experience → Account 2 (survivors' perspective on hunger,
disease, death). Spanish justification → Account 1 (written to glorify the
conquest for readers back home). One sentence each with the reason stated.

**B4.** Acceptance rule: score the reasoning, not the verdict. Exemplary:
acknowledges the perspective advantage *and* the mediation problem (decades
later, Franciscan project, colonial context), concludes that neither
account is "automatically" truthful and that corroboration decides.

**C1.** Three conditions with quotes, e.g.: the stench ("such a salute in
my nostrils as I had never experienced"); crowding ("so crowded that each
had scarcely room to turn himself"); heat and closeness ("the closeness of
the place, and the heat of the climate... almost suffocated us").

**C2.** Sample: "'Cargo' treats kidnapped people as *commodity* — goods to
be loaded, insured, and sold — which is exactly how the trade's paperwork
(and profits) worked."

**C3.** Strength: eyewitness detail no ledger can give (the sensory reality
of the hold). Limit: written decades later for London abolitionist
readers, shaped by its persuasive purpose; some biographical details are
debated by scholars.

**C4.** Sample: "Equiano's account gives one ship's human texture — what
the hold smelled and felt like. The database gives the system scale:
~12.5M embarked, ~1.8M dead in the crossing. The story without the numbers
is anecdote; the numbers without the story are abstraction."

**D1.** Sample: (a) mine-owner book — shows costs/output/profits; hides
workers' suffering (they appear as expenses); useful for the economics of
extraction. (b) Jesuit letter — shows missionary aims and observations;
hides Indigenous perspectives on their own terms; useful for European
intentions. (c) Mughal chronicle — shows court legitimation; hides
peasants and the taxed; useful for imperial ideology. (d) Company report —
shows investor profits and trade organization; hides the enslaved as
people; useful for the business structure of empire.

**D2.** Sample: "Missing: Indigenous, enslaved, and non-European voices —
11 of 12 sources are Europeans writing about others. The risk is a history
in which the colonized appear only as objects of European action, never as
agents with their own aims."

**D3.** Acceptance rule: must concede the concern, argue the loss, and
propose a safeguard. Sample: "Memory does shift and dating is hard, so
caution is warranted. But excluding oral sources silences the only
perspectives some peoples left — the Florentine Codex's Nahuatl accounts
exist because someone wrote down oral memory. The safeguard is
cross-checking: compare multiple tellings and anchor them to datable
events."

**Writing Workshops** — scored against the task requirements (chain
length, evidence per link, hedged language; two perspectives + cited
facts + source-difference sentence; both sides prepared + interaction
sentence; claim + 3 evidence + counterclaim + source-limit sentence).
Sample solutions follow the lesson models.

## Week-08 Quiz (20 pts)

**1. (4 pts)** a–ii, b–i, c–iv, d–iii. (1 pt each.)

**2. (3 pts)** Motives: God (faith/conversion, allies against Muslim
powers), gold (spices, silver, profit), glory (prestige, power over
rivals). Means (any two): caravel + lateen sails; astrolabe/quadrant +
portolan charts; wind knowledge (*volta do mar*); ship-mounted gunpowder
artillery. (1 pt motives set, 2 pts means.)

**3. (2 pts)** b (1488) → d (1492) → c (1494) → a (1498). (2 pts all
correct; 1 pt one swap.)

**4. (3 pts)** (b). Justification must name at least two interacting
causes beyond weapons (disease, Indigenous allies, capture/siege) and
reject single-cause explanations.

**5. (4 pts)** Americas → Afro-Eurasia (any two): maize, potatoes, tomatoes,
cacao, tobacco, cassava, peppers, peanuts. Afro-Eurasia → Americas (any
two): wheat, sugarcane, horses, cattle, pigs, smallpox, measles. Effect
(1 pt): e.g., potatoes/maize boosted Afro-Eurasian food supply and
population; smallpox devastated Indigenous populations; sugar created the
plantation complex.

**6. (4 pts)** (a) Encomienda: the crown granted a colonist the right to
demand labor/tribute from an Indigenous community, in theory for
Christianization and protection — in practice forced labor. (b) Mita: a
rotational state labor draft requiring communities to send men (e.g., to
Potosí's mines). Similarity (1 pt): both were legal systems of coerced
Indigenous labor that enriched colonists and the crown.

## Unit Assessment (40 pts)

**A1. (4 pts)** 2+ motives (God/gold/glory with content) + 2+ means
(ships/sails, instruments/charts, winds, artillery). Deduct 1 per missing
element.

**A2. (5 pts)** Diagram: 3+ correct items per arrow (2 pts); demographic
effect with hedged language (2 pts — e.g., "large shares of Indigenous
populations died from disease; scholars debate exact percentages"); one
economic effect (1 pt — e.g., silver flows, plantation profits, new crops
raising food output).

**A3. (5 pts)** Paragraph names 3+ interacting causes (civil war,
capture of Atahualpa, disease, Spanish technology/allies) and explains at
least one connection between them (e.g., the civil war left armies
exhausted, which made the capture decisive). Single-cause answers cap at 2.

**A4. (6 pts)** (a) ~12.5M embarked, ~10.7M disembarked, ~1.8M Middle
Passage deaths, 1501–1866 (2 pts — accept rounded figures with the range);
(b) excludes pre-embarkation deaths (raids, march, barracoons) and
post-landing "seasoning" deaths (2 pts); (c) any two: joint-stock
companies, marine insurance, coastal forts/factories, credit networks (1
pt); "capitalized business" sentence: profit/routine/state backing
explains 365-year persistence (1 pt).

**B1. (6 pts)** Per source (3 pts): maker + purpose (1), show/hide (1),
useful-for inquiry (1). Score the precision of the limits, not just their
presence.

**B2. (6 pts)** Two empires × two frame questions; one similarity + one
difference, each with a supporting fact (1.5 pts each). Facts must come
from Lesson 04 content.

**B3. (8 pts)** Claim stated (1); 2+ evidence pieces from the unit (3);
counterclaim acknowledged and answered (2); source-limit sentence (1);
organization within word count (1). Either side ("exchange" or "empire")
is acceptable — score the argument, not the conclusion.
