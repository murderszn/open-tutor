# Unit 05 — World Wars, Ideologies, Genocide, and Historical Evidence

**Grade 9 · Social Studies · Modern world history · Weeks 20–23 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-11-12 for issue #45; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Between 1914 and 1945 the world fought two wars bigger than any before
them — and between and after them, people argued over what kind of society
should replace the old order. This unit follows that arc honestly: the
First World War's tangled causes and its punishing settlement; the interwar
crisis, when liberalism, fascism, and communism each claimed to answer
economic collapse and broken democracies; the Second World War's global
scope and its home fronts; and the evidence for the Holocaust and other
genocides, studied the way historians study it — by asking what each kind
of evidence can and cannot show. The unit closes with the institutions the
world built after 1945 and the question of how societies remember.

The unit's inquiry question runs through all four weeks: **how did two
world wars and the ideologies between them change what people believed a
just society could be — and how do we know what really happened?**

A note on sources: casualty figures in this unit are always given as
ranges with their sources, never as single settled numbers — because
historians themselves disagree, and the disagreement is part of the
lesson (C3 D2.His.10.9-12). Learner readings are original passages or
short public-domain excerpts; secondary claims are summarized and linked,
never copied.

**Sensitive content (adult preview required):** this unit centers war and
genocide — mass killing, persecution, and displacement. All sources are
documentary and textual (no graphic imagery, no photographs of victims).
The adult gives advance notice before Week 22, previews every source,
frames discussion around evidence, responsibility, and remembrance, and
offers an observation/reflection alternative for any activity the learner
finds overwhelming. See [teacher-guide.md](teacher-guide.md) §8 and the
track [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md) §6–§7.

## Prerequisites

From U01–U04 and the course diagnostic: place centuries and decades on a
timeline, read a scaled map, classify sources by kind, infer maker/date/
audience, detect a source's limits, and write a claim supported by evidence
and a counterclaim. The adult confirms the learner can sustain ~20-minute
document sessions, previews all Week 22 sources, and gives advance notice
of the genocide sessions. No cross-grade references: these are described
skills, not links.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Analyze WWI's causes, course, and settlement** — explain how
   militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism interacted to
   produce war in 1914; describe trench warfare and the war's global
   reach; and evaluate the Treaty of Versailles' terms and their
   contested legacy. (D2.His.14.9-12; D2.His.1.9-12)
2. **Explain the interwar crisis and compare ideologies** — explain how
   economic collapse and weak democracies created openings for radical
   answers, and compare liberalism, fascism, and communism as historical
   actors understood them, with evidence and without present-day
   labels. (D2.His.2, D2.His.4, D2.His.14.9-12)
3. **Analyze WWII's global scope and home fronts** — trace the war across
   the European, Pacific, and home-front theaters; explain turning
   points; and describe how the war changed daily life, labor, and
   civil liberties for civilians. (D2.His.1, D2.His.14.9-12)
4. **Assess historical evidence about genocide** — define genocide using
   the 1948 UN Convention's terms; evaluate what documents, testimony,
   photographs, physical evidence, and statistics can and cannot show
   about the Holocaust and at least one other genocide; and explain why
   casualty figures are ranges. (D2.His.8, D2.His.10, D2.His.12.9-12;
   D3.1–D3.4.9-12)
5. **Evaluate postwar outcomes, institutions, and memory** — explain the
   purposes and limits of the United Nations, the Bretton Woods
   institutions, and the Nuremberg trials; and compare how different
   societies remember the wars, using evidence. (D2.His.5, D2.His.6,
   D2.His.16.9-12; D2.Civ.5, D2.Civ.13.9-12)

**Source notes (checked at authoring, 2026-11-12):**

- WWI deaths: Encyclopaedia Britannica — **~8.5 million soldiers died**
  of wounds or disease, and **perhaps as many as 13 million civilians**
  also died; total deaths are commonly given as **15–22 million**. The
  figures are estimates; no exact count exists.
- WWII deaths: scholarly consensus range **70–85 million total** (~3% of
  the 1940 world population); military deaths **~21–25 million**,
  civilian deaths **~50–55 million**. The National WWII Museum's
  Research Starters give ~15 million battle deaths and ~45 million
  civilian deaths, noting Chinese civilian deaths alone may be far higher.
  Soviet losses: ~26.6 million per Russian government figures.
- The Holocaust: United States Holocaust Memorial Museum — **six million
  European Jews murdered** by the Nazi German regime and its allies and
  collaborators, 1933–1945; millions of others (Roma, disabled people,
  Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, political opponents, Jehovah's
  Witnesses, gay men) were also murdered.
- Armenian genocide: 1915–1917 (continuing to 1923); most historians
  estimate **600,000–1.5 million** Armenians killed; the International
  Association of Genocide Scholars accepts ~1–1.5 million. The Turkish
  government disputes the term "genocide" — the unit teaches the
  scholarly consensus and the dispute, not one side's label alone.
- Rwandan genocide: April–July 1994, about **100 days**; an estimated
  **800,000–1,000,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu** killed (UN); the UN
  Independent Inquiry (1999) found the UN system as a whole failed to
  prevent or stop it.
- Treaty of Versailles: signed **28 June 1919**; Article 231 ("war guilt"
  clause); reparations fixed in 1921 at **132 billion gold marks**;
  Germany lost about 13% of its territory and all overseas colonies;
  the League of Nations was founded in 1920 and the United States never
  joined.
- United Nations: Charter signed 26 June 1945, in force **24 October
  1945**. Genocide Convention adopted **9 December 1948**; Universal
  Declaration of Human Rights adopted **10 December 1948**.
- Short quotations in the lessons are from public-domain texts: the
  Treaty of Versailles (1919), Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points (1918),
  and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). All other
  lesson passages are original prose written for this unit.
