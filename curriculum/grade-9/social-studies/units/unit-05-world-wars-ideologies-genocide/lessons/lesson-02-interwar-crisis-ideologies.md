# Lesson 02 — The Interwar Crisis: Three Ideologies, Three Answers

**Unit 05 · Week 21, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain how economic collapse and weak
democracies created an opening for radical answers, and compare
liberalism, fascism, and communism as the people of the time understood
them.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal, the unit's
timeline (`../assets/world-wars-timeline.svg`).

**Readiness check (5 min):** From Lesson 01, name one thing the Treaty
of Versailles did that Germans resented (Article 231, reparations of
132 billion gold marks, territorial losses). Now ask: *if a country
felt humiliated and then its economy collapsed, what kinds of leaders
might start to sound convincing?* That is this lesson's question.

---

## Part 1 — The crisis: collapse and weak democracies (explicit teaching, 15 min)

The 1920s began with hope — the League of Nations, new democracies in
Germany and Eastern Europe, a booming American economy — and then the
floor fell out. In **1923**, Germany's government printed money to pay
its bills and the currency collapsed into **hyperinflation**: prices
doubled in hours, savings became worthless, and ordinary people carried
cash in wheelbarrows. Germany recovered by the late 1920s — and then
the **Great Depression** hit. The U.S. stock market crashed in
**October 1929**; banks failed; world trade shrank; and by **1933**
about **one in four American workers** was unemployed. In Germany,
unemployment passed six million.

Democracies struggled to answer. In Germany's Weimar Republic,
governments changed constantly and no party could hold a majority.
Voters who had trusted parliaments began to listen to movements that
promised simple, forceful answers — and blamed someone else for the
crisis. Meanwhile the League of Nations proved it could not enforce
peace: when Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 and Italy invaded Ethiopia
in 1935, the League protested and did almost nothing.

**Worked example 1 — from crisis to choice:** A German shopkeeper in
1932 has lost her savings twice (1923, then the Depression) and her
son is unemployed. Three movements offer answers: liberals say fix the
economy with reforms and free trade; communists say the whole system is
rigged and must be replaced by workers' rule; fascists say the nation
must be purified and led by a strong leader. The shopkeeper's choice
depends on what she believes caused her ruin — and who she trusts to
fix it. The lesson: ideology is not an abstract label; it is an answer
to a lived crisis. A common error is to treat voters in 1932 as foolish
for choosing badly — they were choosing under conditions most of us
have never faced.

**Worked example 2 — reading a claim:** A textbook says, "The Great
Depression caused World War II." Test it: the Depression made radical
movements more attractive, but it did not force Germany to invade
Poland or Japan to attack China. Economic crisis was a *condition*;
leaders' decisions were the *cause*. Keep conditions and causes
separate — the same discipline as Lesson 01's trigger-vs.-cause
distinction.

## Part 2 — Three ideologies, as their supporters described them (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Compare the three on the same frame — who holds power, who owns
property, what the state's job is, and how dissent is treated. Use
their own terms; the unit does not use today's political labels for
them.

**Liberalism** — the system of the victorious democracies (Britain,
France, the United States): individual rights, representative
government, free markets, and the rule of law. Its supporters said
freedom and prosperity came from open debate, elections, and trade.
Its weakness in the 1930s, critics said, was that it could not deliver
jobs or order fast enough when the economy collapsed.

**Fascism** — first in **Mussolini's Italy (1922)**, then in **Hitler's
Germany (1933)**: ultranationalism, a single party, a glorified leader,
and the suppression of dissent. Fascists said the nation — defined by
blood and soil — came before the individual, and that democracy was
weak and divisive. In Germany, the Nazis added virulent
**antisemitism**: the claim that Jews were responsible for Germany's
defeats and troubles. Hitler became Chancellor on **30 January 1933**;
the **Enabling Act** (March 1933) let him rule by decree; the
**Nuremberg Laws** (1935) stripped German Jews of citizenship.

**Communism** — in the **Soviet Union**, built from the 1917 Russian
Revolution: the state owns the means of production, a single party
rules in the name of the workers, and class — not nation — is the
dividing line. Under **Stalin**, the USSR industrialized at brutal
speed through five-year plans and forced collectivization of farms,
and the **Great Purge (1936–38)** imprisoned or executed hundreds of
thousands of perceived enemies. Its supporters said it ended
exploitation; its critics said it replaced one tyranny with another.

None of the three was a single thing everywhere: Italian fascism and
German Nazism differed (race was central to Nazism, less so to Italian
fascism), and Soviet communism differed from what Marx had imagined.
The comparison matters because in the 1930s, real people chose among
these — and the choices shaped the next war.

## Part 3 — The road back to war (explicit teaching, 10 min)

Through the 1930s, the democracies tried **appeasement** — giving in
to Hitler's demands in the hope of avoiding war. Germany rearmed
(openly, from 1935), remilitarized the Rhineland (1936), annexed Austria
(1938), and took the Sudetenland from Czechoslovakia after the **Munich
Agreement** (September 1938), when Britain and France agreed to the
demand. In March 1939 Germany took the rest of Czechoslovakia — proving
appeasement had not bought peace. In August 1939 Germany and the Soviet
Union signed a **non-aggression pact**, secretly agreeing to divide
Poland between them. On **1 September 1939** Germany invaded Poland;
Britain and France declared war two days later. The Second World War
had begun.

**Teacher note (misconceptions):** Learners often assume fascism and
communism were "the same because both were dictatorships." Redirect:
they shared one-party rule and repression, but differed sharply in
their stated goals (nation vs. class), their economics (private property
kept under fascism; abolished under communism), and their enemies.
Comparing honestly means naming both similarities and differences. See
[teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice (10 min, with adult or answer key nearby)

1. In your own words, define liberalism, fascism, and communism using
   the comparison frame: who holds power, who owns property, how
   dissent is treated.
2. Give one fact from Part 1 that shows the League of Nations was weak,
   and one fact that shows a democracy struggling in the 1930s.
3. Order these events and say what each one shows about appeasement:
   Munich Agreement; Germany remilitarizes the Rhineland; Germany
   takes the rest of Czechoslovakia; Germany invades Poland.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 3–4 sentences why the Great Depression made radical
   movements more attractive, using the 1932 shopkeeper example or one
   of your own. Keep "condition" and "cause" separate.
5. Write two sentences that a fascist supporter in 1933 might have used
   to argue against liberalism, and two sentences a liberal might have
   used in reply. Label each sentence as a *claim* or a *fact*.

## Applied task

6. **Debate prep:** Your class will deliberate the question, "Why did
   different societies choose different answers in the 1930s?"
   Prepare a 5-sentence opening statement for one side — either
   "economic crisis decided it" or "leaders' choices decided it" —
   with two facts from this lesson, and one sentence anticipating the
   other side's best point.

## Exit check

7. In one sentence: what did liberalism, fascism, and communism each
   promise to people living through the Depression? (Acceptable: each
   promised a different route out of crisis — liberalism through rights,
   elections, and markets; fascism through national unity and a strong
   leader; communism through workers' rule and state ownership.)
