# Lesson 01 — The First World War: Causes, Course, and Settlement

**Unit 05 · Week 20, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain how four long-term causes
interacted with one trigger to start the First World War, describe how
the war was fought, and evaluate the Treaty of Versailles' terms.

**Materials:** this lesson, blank world map, notebook/inquiry journal,
the unit's timeline (`../assets/world-wars-timeline.svg`).

**Readiness check (5 min):** From U04, name two ways industrial
capitalism changed Europe by 1900 (factories, railways, steel, global
trade, rival empires competing for colonies). Now add one sentence: *a
continent that could build steel ships and railways could also build

## Part 1 — Four long-term causes and one trigger (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Historians usually explain 1914 with four interacting causes, remembered
as **MAIN** — and the key word is *interacting*. No single cause alone
started the war; together they turned a local crisis into a continental
one.

1. **Militarism:** the great powers built up armies and navies and let
   generals plan as if war were inevitable. Germany and Britain raced to
   build battleships; France and Russia expanded their armies. Military
   timetables — rigid plans that had to run on schedule once started —
   made leaders afraid to wait.
2. **Alliances:** Europe was split into two armed camps. The **Triple
   Alliance** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) faced the **Triple
   Entente** (France, Russia, Britain). An alliance meant a local fight
   could pull in the big powers — which is exactly what happened.
3. **Imperialism:** the powers competed for colonies and markets in
   Africa and Asia (see U04). Rivalry over Morocco nearly caused war in
   1905 and 1911. Each crisis left resentment behind.
4. **Nationalism:** pride in one's nation — and, in the multi-ethnic
   empires, the demand of subject peoples for their own states. Serbian
   nationalists wanted Bosnia, which Austria-Hungary had annexed in
   1908, to join Serbia instead.

The trigger came on **28 June 1914**, when Archduke **Franz Ferdinand**,
heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, was assassinated in Sarajevo by
**Gavrilo Princip**, a Bosnian Serb nationalist. The **July Crisis**
followed: Austria-Hungary, backed by Germany's "blank cheque" of
support, issued Serbia an ultimatum designed to be refused; Russia
mobilized to defend Serbia; Germany declared war on Russia (1 August)
and France (3 August) and invaded neutral Belgium; Britain declared war
on Germany (4 August). Within five weeks, a Balkan assassination had
become a European war — because the alliances and timetables did the
escalating.

**Worked example 1 — trigger vs. cause:** A textbook says, "The
assassination started WWI." Test that claim: if Princip had missed, would
Europe have stayed peaceful? Probably not for long — the alliances,
arms race, and rivalries were already in place, and a different crisis
could have lit the same powder. The assassination was the *trigger*;
MAIN were the *causes*. A common error is to name only the trigger and
stop. Good history asks what made the trigger explosive.

**Worked example 2 — reading a claim:** A student writes, "Germany
caused WWI." Evaluate it: Germany's "blank cheque" and its invasion of
Belgium mattered, but Austria-Hungary issued the ultimatum, Russia
mobilized first among the great powers, and France and Britain chose
war over negotiation too. Historians still debate the *balance* of
responsibility — which is different from saying one country alone caused
it. The Versailles treaty's Article 231 (see Part 3) blamed Germany
alone; that was a political decision, not a historical consensus.

## Part 2 — How the war was fought (explicit teaching, 15 min)

The war most people picture happened on the **Western Front** in France
and Belgium: after Germany's advance stalled at the Marne in September
1914, both sides dug **trenches** — and for years the line barely moved.
Machine guns, artillery, barbed wire, and poison gas made attacking
across open ground suicidal. At the **Somme** in 1916, the British army
suffered about 57,000 casualties on the first day alone. The war was
also fought on the Eastern Front (Germany and Austria-Hungary vs.
Russia), in the Ottoman Empire (Gallipoli, 1915–16), in Africa, and at
sea (the Battle of Jutland, 1916; German U-boats).

This was the first **total war**: whole economies and societies were
mobilized. Governments rationed food, censored news, drafted millions,
and put women into factories and farms in unprecedented numbers. New
technologies — the tank, the airplane, the submarine — arrived faster
than tactics could adapt. In 1917 two events changed the war: revolution
in Russia (which took Russia out of the war in 1918) and the **United
States' entry** in April 1917, after Germany resumed unrestricted
submarine warfare. The **armistice** came on **11 November 1918**.

The human cost was staggering, and the numbers are estimates: about
**8.5 million soldiers** died of wounds or disease, and **perhaps as
many as 13 million civilians** also died — from starvation, disease,
displacement, and massacres (Britannica). Total deaths are usually
given as **15–22 million**. No exact count exists; the unit always
gives ranges and names the source.

## Part 3 — The settlement: Versailles and its critics (explicit teaching, 10 min)

The **Treaty of Versailles**, signed **28 June 1919**, was written by
the victors — Britain, France, and the United States — while Germany was
not invited to negotiate. Its key terms:

- **Article 231**, the "war guilt" clause: *"The Allied and Associated
  Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany
  and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the
  Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been
  subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the
  aggression of Germany and her allies."*
- **Reparations:** Germany was ordered to pay for war damage; the sum
  was fixed in 1921 at **132 billion gold marks**.
- **Territory and arms:** Germany lost about 13% of its territory and
  all overseas colonies, and its army was limited to 100,000 men.
- **The League of Nations** was created to keep the peace — but the
  United States Senate refused to join, and the League had no army of
  its own.

President Woodrow Wilson had proposed a different vision in his
**Fourteen Points** (January 1918), including open diplomacy and
"a general association of nations" — but the final treaty was harsher
than his plan. Critics then and now argue the settlement punished
Germany without making Europe safer: Germans resented Article 231 and
the reparations, and the League proved too weak to stop aggression in
the 1930s. Others reply that France, which had suffered invasion twice
in fifty years, had reasons to demand security. The debate itself is
the lesson: settlements are arguments about the future, not just
punishments for the past.

**Teacher note (misconceptions):** Learners often treat Article 231 as
proof of who "really" caused the war. Redirect: a treaty clause is a
political claim made by winners — evidence for what the victors wanted,
not a historian's verdict. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice (10 min, with adult or answer key nearby)

1. Name the four MAIN causes and, for each, give one fact from this
   lesson that shows it at work in 1914.
2. Put these in order and label each as a *cause*, *trigger*, or
   *escalation*: the July Crisis ultimatum; the assassination of Franz
   Ferdinand; the alliance system; Germany's invasion of Belgium.
3. In two sentences, explain why the war became a stalemate on the
   Western Front. Name two technologies that made attacking so costly.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 3–4 sentences why "the assassination started WWI" is an
   incomplete explanation. Use the words *trigger* and *interacting
   causes*.
5. List three terms of the Treaty of Versailles and, for each, write one
   sentence about who benefited and who resented it.

## Applied task

6. **Editorial, 1919:** You are a newspaper editor in Paris in June
   1919. Write a 5-sentence editorial reacting to the Treaty of
   Versailles. Take a position — too harsh, too lenient, or about
   right — and support it with two facts from this lesson. Then add
   one sentence a German editor would write in reply.

## Exit check

7. In one sentence: what turned a Balkan assassination into a world
   war? (Acceptable: the alliance system and military timetables
   pulled the great powers in, so a local crisis escalated into a
   European war within five weeks.)
