# Lesson 04 — Evidence Lab: What Counts as Evidence of Genocide

**Unit 05 · Week 22, Session 2 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can define genocide, classify five kinds of
historical evidence, and explain what each kind can and cannot show
about the Holocaust and at least one other genocide.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal, the unit's
evidence-table image (`../assets/evidence-table-reconstruction.png`)
or its text alternative.

**Readiness check (5 min):** From Lesson 03, name the war's most
contested numbers (total deaths 70–85 million; civilian vs. military).
Now ask: *when a government tries to destroy a whole people, it also
tries to destroy the evidence. How do historians prove what happened
anyway?* That is this lesson's question.

**Sensitive-content note (read aloud by the adult before starting):**
This lesson is about mass murder — the Holocaust, the Armenian
genocide, and the Rwandan genocide. We use words and documents, not
graphic images. If anything feels overwhelming, you may switch to the
reflection alternative in the teacher guide (§8): write about why
societies build memorials instead of answering the evidence questions.
No one is graded on how they feel about this material.

## Part 1 — What "genocide" means (explicit teaching, 10 min)

The word **genocide** was coined in 1944 by Raphael Lemkin, a
Polish-Jewish lawyer whose family was murdered in the Holocaust. In
1948 the United Nations adopted the **Convention on the Prevention and
Punishment of the Crime of Genocide**, which defines genocide as acts
"committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national,
ethnical, racial or religious group." The Convention lists five acts
that count as genocide when done with that intent:

1. killing members of the group;
2. causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group;
3. deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated
   to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part;
4. imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group;
5. forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.

Two words do the work: **intent** and **group**. A war can kill many
people without being genocide; genocide is the *deliberate attempt to
destroy a people as a people*. That is why historians argue about
intent and evidence — and why this lesson is about evidence, not just
events.

**Worked example 1 — is it genocide?** A student asks whether the
bombing of a city in wartime is genocide. Apply the definition: was
there intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group
*as such*? Bombing a city to break an enemy's war effort is a war
crime if it targets civilians — but it is not genocide unless the
intent is to destroy the group itself. The distinction matters because
the law, the history, and the moral judgment all turn on intent. A
common error is to call every mass killing "genocide"; the word is
precise, and precision is a form of respect.

## Part 2 — Five kinds of evidence (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Historians prove genocide the way a court does: by stacking different
kinds of evidence until the picture is consistent. Each kind has
strengths and limits.

| Kind | What it can show | What it cannot show |
|---|---|---|
| **Documents** — orders, laws, railway schedules, census records | Intent and organization: who ordered what, when, and how it was carried out | What the documents omit — destroyed files, coded language, unwritten orders |
| **Testimony** — survivor accounts, perpetrator confessions, witness statements | Lived experience; details no document records; what it felt like | One person's view only; memory changes over time; trauma affects recall |
| **Photographs and film** — taken by perpetrators, liberators, or victims | That a place, person, or event existed at a moment in time | Why it happened; what happened before or after the frame; photographs can be staged or cropped |
| **Physical evidence** — camps, graves, ruins, artifacts | That killings happened at a scale and in a place; corroboration of documents | Intent by itself; the names of the dead |
| **Statistics** — population counts before and after, deportation lists | The scale of the loss; patterns across regions and time | Individual stories; statistics depend on who counted and how |

**Worked example 2 — one claim, three kinds of evidence:** The claim:
"Nazi Germany systematically murdered six million European Jews."
Documents: the Wannsee Conference minutes (January 1942) record a
meeting to organize the "Final Solution"; railway schedules show
trains to extermination camps; the Nuremberg Laws (1935) show the
legal steps that came first. Testimony: thousands of survivor accounts
describe ghettos, deportations, and camps in consistent detail.
Physical evidence: the camps themselves — Auschwitz-Birkenau,
Treblinka, Sobibor, Belzec, Chelmno, Majdanek — with gas chambers,
crematoria, and mass graves. Statistics: pre-war and post-war Jewish
population counts, compiled by the United States Holocaust Memorial
Museum, show a loss of about six million. No single kind proves the
claim alone; together, they leave no reasonable doubt — which is why
denial depends on attacking each kind separately and never facing them
together.

## Part 3 — Observation: the evidence table (guided, 10 min)

Open `../assets/evidence-table-reconstruction.png` (or read the text

historical source** — it shows what a historian's desk looks like, not
real evidence. It helps you practice classifying evidence kinds; it

**Text alternative (contains everything needed for the questions):**
The scene shows a wooden desk under a brass lamp. On the left, a stack
of aged papers tied with string. In the center, an open notebook with
blurred handwritten lines. Below it, a black-and-white photograph of a
city street with buildings on both sides and no people close enough to
identify. On the right, a folded paper map with simple coastline
outlines. Near the bottom, a small plain metal tag, a magnifying glass
with a brass rim, and a pencil.

**Guided questions (answer in your journal):**

1. Match each object on the desk to an evidence kind from Part 2:
   the tied papers, the notebook, the photograph, the map, the metal
   tag. (Possible: papers = documents; notebook = testimony or a
   diary; photograph = photographs/film; map = documents or physical
   context; tag = physical evidence.)
2. What can the photograph show, and what can it not show? (It shows
   a street existed at a moment in time. It cannot show why anyone was
   there, what happened before or after, or whose street it was.)
3. What is missing from this desk that a real genocide investigation
   would need? (Statistics — population counts; more testimony; and
   the historian's own questions. A desk of objects is not an argument
   until someone asks what they prove.)

## Part 4 — Two other genocides, same questions (explicit teaching, 10 min)

**The Armenian genocide (1915–1917, continuing to 1923).** During
WWI, the Ottoman government arrested Armenian leaders in
Constantinople on **24 April 1915**, then deported hundreds of
thousands of Armenians on death marches toward the Syrian desert.
Most historians estimate **600,000–1.5 million** Armenians were
killed; the International Association of Genocide Scholars accepts
about 1–1.5 million. Evidence: diplomatic reports from German and
American officials who watched it happen, survivor testimony,
photographs, and Ottoman records. The Turkish government disputes the
term "genocide" — the unit teaches the scholarly consensus and the
dispute, with the evidence for each position, not one side's label
alone.

**The Rwandan genocide (April–July 1994).** In about **100 days**,
Hutu extremists killed an estimated **800,000–1,000,000 Tutsi and
moderate Hutu** — most of them with machetes, house by house, after
President Habyarimana's plane was shot down on 6 April 1994. The
United Nations had peacekeepers in Rwanda (UNAMIR) under General
Roméo Dallaire, who warned of the coming killing in January 1994;
the UN Security Council did not authorize intervention, and the
killing stopped only when the Rwandan Patriotic Front took power in
July. The UN's own Independent Inquiry (1999) concluded the UN
system as a whole failed to prevent or stop it. Evidence: radio
broadcasts urging killing, UN cables, survivor testimony, and the
tribunal that later convicted the organizers (the International
Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda, 1994–2015).

**Teacher note (misconceptions):** Learners sometimes ask, "If the
numbers are ranges, how do we know anything?" Redirect: ranges are
honest — they show where counting is hard (destroyed records,
unmarked graves). A range with a named source is more trustworthy
than a false exact number. And the *fact* of each genocide is
established by converging evidence kinds, not by agreeing on one
death toll. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice (10 min, with adult or answer key nearby)

1. In your own words, state the two words that do the work in the
   genocide definition, and explain why each matters.
2. Give one example of each evidence kind for the Holocaust from Part
   2's worked example, and name one limit of each kind.
3. Using the evidence-table image (or its text alternative), explain
   why a photograph alone cannot prove a genocide happened.

## Independent practice

4. Compare the Armenian genocide and the Rwandan genocide in 4–5
   sentences: one similarity in *how* the killing was organized, one
   difference in *who stopped it* (or did not), and one sentence on
   what kind of evidence is strongest for each.
5. A website claims "the Holocaust never happened because no single
   document orders it." Write a 3-sentence reply using the
   evidence-kinds table: which kinds answer that claim, and why does
   the claim misunderstand how historians work?

## Applied task

6. **Evidence brief:** Choose one of the three genocides studied. Write
   a 6-sentence brief for a classmate: (a) what happened, in two
   sentences; (b) two kinds of evidence that support it, with one
   specific example each; (c) one limit of the evidence; (d) one
   question you would still ask. End with the death-toll range and its
   source.

## Exit check

7. In one sentence: why do historians stack different kinds of
   evidence instead of relying on one? (Acceptable: because each kind
   has limits, and only together — documents, testimony, photographs,
   physical evidence, and statistics — do they establish intent, scale,
   and what happened beyond reasonable doubt.)
