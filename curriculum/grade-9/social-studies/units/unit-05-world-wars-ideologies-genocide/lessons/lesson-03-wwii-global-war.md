# Lesson 03 — The Second World War: A Global War and Its Home Fronts

**Unit 05 · Week 22, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can trace the Second World War across its
European, Pacific, and home-front theaters, explain its turning points,
and describe how it changed daily life for civilians.

**Materials:** this lesson, blank world map, notebook/inquiry journal,
the unit's timeline (`../assets/world-wars-timeline.svg`).

**Readiness check (5 min):** From Lesson 02, name the event that started
WWII (Germany's invasion of Poland, 1 September 1939) and the two
alliances that formed (the **Axis**: Germany, Italy, Japan; the
**Allies**: Britain, France — later the Soviet Union, the United States,
and China). Now ask: *why did this war kill far more civilians than
soldiers — the opposite of most earlier wars?* That is this lesson's
question.

---

## Part 1 — The war's course: three theaters (explicit teaching, 15 min)

**Europe, 1939–41 — the Axis advance.** Germany's **Blitzkrieg**
("lightning war") — fast tanks, aircraft, and radio coordination —
overran Poland in weeks, then Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands,
Belgium, and France (June 1940). Britain stood alone after the
evacuation at Dunkirk and survived the **Battle of Britain** (1940),
when the Royal Air Force defeated the Luftwaffe's bombing campaign.
In **June 1941** Germany invaded the Soviet Union (**Operation
Barbarossa**), opening the war's largest front.

**The Pacific, 1937–45 — a war already underway.** Japan had invaded
China in 1937 and fought a brutal war there for years before the West
paid attention. On **7 December 1941** Japan attacked the U.S. naval
base at **Pearl Harbor**, Hawaii, bringing the United States into the
war. Japan then seized Southeast Asia and the western Pacific; the
United States and its allies fought back island by island from 1942.

**Turning points, 1942–43.** The Axis reached its farthest extent in
1942 — and then the tide turned: the Soviet victory at **Stalingrad**
(1942–43), the U.S. naval victory at **Midway** (June 1942), and the
Allied landings in North Africa and Italy. On **6 June 1944** (D-Day),
Allied forces landed in Normandy and opened a second front in western
Europe. Germany surrendered on **8 May 1945** (VE Day). In the Pacific,
the United States dropped atomic bombs on **Hiroshima (6 August)** and
**Nagasaki (9 August) 1945**; Japan surrendered on **2 September
1945**.

**Worked example 1 — why "turning point" is a judgment:** Stalingrad
is often called the turning point of the European war. Check the claim:
before Stalingrad, Germany was advancing; after it, Germany was mostly
retreating. The battle cost the Soviet Union and Germany hundreds of
thousands of soldiers each, and Germany never recovered the initiative.
A turning point is not the bloodiest battle — it is the battle after
which the *direction* of the war changes. A common error is to pick a
famous battle (D-Day) as the turning point when the direction had
already changed a year earlier.

**Worked example 2 — the war's scale in numbers:** Total deaths are
estimated at **70–85 million people** — about 3% of everyone alive in
1940. Military deaths were roughly **21–25 million**; civilian deaths
roughly **50–55 million**. The Soviet Union alone lost about **26.6
million** people. Notice what the numbers say: for the first time in a
great war, civilians were the majority of the dead — because of bombing,
blockade, famine, forced labor, and genocide. That is why the next
lesson studies evidence so carefully: the numbers are not just big,
they are contested, and how we count is part of the history.

## Part 2 — Home fronts: total war at home (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Total war reached into kitchens and workplaces. In Britain, the United
States, Germany, and the Soviet Union, governments **rationed** food
and fuel, ran propaganda campaigns, and moved millions of workers —
including women — into factories making weapons, ships, and planes.
In the United States, about six million women took factory jobs; the
poster figure "Rosie the Riveter" became a symbol of that shift (though
most women left factory work after the war). In the Soviet Union,
whole factories were dismantled and rebuilt east of the Urals, beyond
German bombers' reach.

Total war also meant governments suspending liberties. The starkest
American example is **Japanese internment**: after Pearl Harbor,
President Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 (February 1942), and
about **120,000 people of Japanese ancestry** — two-thirds of them
U.S. citizens — were removed from the West Coast and held in camps for
the war's duration. The U.S. government apologized and paid reparations
in 1988. In Britain, "enemy aliens" were interned; in Germany, the
regime's persecution of Jews (Lesson 04) was the extreme of the same
logic — a state deciding that some of its own people were the enemy.

**Worked example 3 — judging a home-front policy:** A student writes,
"Internment was necessary for security." Test the claim with evidence:
no Japanese American was ever convicted of espionage for Japan during
the war; the policy was based on ancestry, not individual suspicion;
and the government itself later called it a grave injustice. A
historian's job is not to excuse or condemn in one sentence, but to
weigh the claim against the evidence — and to say what the evidence
does and does not support.

## Part 3 — Why this war was different (explicit teaching, 10 min)

Three things set WWII apart from WWI: **scale** (fought on every
continent except Antarctica, with more than 100 million people in
uniform); **ideology** (the Axis powers fought to remake the world on
racial and imperial terms, and the Allies — imperfectly — fought to
defend a different order); and **civilians as targets** (strategic
bombing of cities — London, Dresden, Tokyo — the siege of Leningrad,
and the systematic murder of whole peoples). The war's end did not
settle the questions it raised: who was responsible, what justice
looked like, and how the world should prevent a third war. Those are
the subjects of Lessons 04 and 05.

**Teacher note (misconceptions):** Learners often picture WWII as
mostly D-Day and Pearl Harbor. Redirect: the largest front was in the
East (the Soviet Union suffered most of the war's military deaths),
China had been at war since 1937, and civilians — not soldiers — were
the majority of the dead. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice (10 min, with adult or answer key nearby)

1. On your blank map, mark and date four events: Germany invades
   Poland; Pearl Harbor; D-Day; Japan surrenders. Draw one arrow for
   each theater (Europe, Pacific, home front) and label what moved
   along it (armies, ships, workers/supplies).
2. In two sentences, explain why Stalingrad counts as a turning point
   and D-Day does not (in the same sense).
3. Name two home-front changes from Part 2 and, for each, say who was
   affected and how.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 3–4 sentences why WWII killed more civilians than
   soldiers. Use at least two of these: bombing, blockade/famine,
   forced labor, genocide.
5. Write a paragraph (4–5 sentences) evaluating the claim "internment
   was necessary for security." Use the evidence from Part 2 and end
   with what the evidence supports and what it does not.

## Applied task

6. **Home-front diary, 1943:** You are a 16-year-old in a city under
   rationing (choose London, Detroit, or Moscow). Write a 5-sentence
   diary entry for one week: what changed in your daily life, what
   your family talks about, and one question you have about the war
   that the news does not answer. Base every detail on this lesson —
   do not invent battles or technologies.

## Exit check

7. In one sentence: what made WWII different from WWI in its human
   cost? (Acceptable: it was fought worldwide, civilians were the
   majority of the dead, and the war targeted whole peoples — through
   bombing, famine, forced labor, and genocide.)
