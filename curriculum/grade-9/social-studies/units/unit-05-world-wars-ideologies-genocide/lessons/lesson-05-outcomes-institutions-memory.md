# Lesson 05 — After the Wars: Institutions, Justice, and Memory

**Unit 05 · Week 23, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain the purposes and limits of the
postwar institutions — the United Nations, the Bretton Woods system,
and the Nuremberg trials — and compare how different societies remember
the wars.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal, the unit's
timeline (`../assets/world-wars-timeline.svg`).

**Readiness check (5 min):** From Lesson 04, name the year the
Genocide Convention was adopted (1948) and the two words that define
genocide (intent, group). Now ask: *after a war that killed 70–85
million people, what would you build so it never happened again?*
The world tried three answers — and this lesson tests each one.

---

## Part 1 — Three institutions, three jobs (explicit teaching, 15 min)

**The United Nations (1945).** The UN Charter was signed in June 1945
and came into force on **24 October 1945**. Unlike the League, the UN
gave the great powers a permanent seat and a veto on its Security
Council — the price of getting them to join. Its jobs: keep the peace,
settle disputes by talk, and promote human rights. Its limit was built
in: when the great powers disagreed, the veto froze it — which is why
the UN could not stop the Rwandan genocide in 1994 (Lesson 04) and
could not prevent the Cold War from dividing the world.

**The Bretton Woods institutions (1944).** In July 1944, while the war
still raged, delegates from 44 countries met at Bretton Woods, New
Hampshire, and created the **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** and
the **World Bank**. Their job: stabilize currencies and rebuild ruined
economies so that the economic chaos of the 1930s — the conditions
Lesson 02 described — would not return. Their limit: they were built
for rebuilding Europe, and the countries that needed development most
had the least say in how they ran.

**The Nuremberg trials (1945–46).** For the first time, individual
leaders were tried for **crimes against humanity** and **war crimes**
by an international court. Twenty-two Nazi leaders were tried at
Nuremberg; twelve were sentenced to death. The trials established a
principle that still matters: "I was following orders" is not a
defense against crimes against humanity. Their limit: the victors ran
the court, and no Allied conduct (such as the bombing of Dresden or
the atomic bombs) was tried — which is why critics called it "victors'
justice" and supporters called it the best justice available.

**Worked example 1 — judging an institution:** A student says, "The
UN failed because Rwanda happened." Test it against its purpose: the
UN was designed to keep the peace *when the great powers agreed* —
and in 1994 they did not. Rwanda shows the UN's real limit (the veto
and the lack of a standing army), not that the idea of international
law was worthless. Compare with the Genocide Convention (1948) and the
Universal Declaration of Human Rights (10 December 1948): both were
written *because* of the Holocaust, and both set standards the world
still uses to judge governments — even when governments break them.
A common error is to judge an institution only by its failures; good
history weighs its purpose, its limits, and what changed because it
existed.

**Worked example 2 — reading a claim:** A textbook says, "Nuremberg
proved that justice follows every war." Test it: Nuremberg happened
because the Allies won completely and occupied Germany. In most wars
since, no such trials have followed — Rwanda's tribunal took years,
and many perpetrators are never tried at all. Nuremberg was a
precedent, not a pattern. The lesson: institutions are arguments in
progress, and their success is measured over decades.

## Part 2 — Memory: the same war, different stories (explicit teaching, 15 min)

The wars ended, but the arguing did not — it moved into textbooks,
memorials, and family stories. **Historical memory** is what a society
chooses to remember about its past, and it differs by place:

- In **Russia**, the war is the "Great Patriotic War," remembered
  above all for the Soviet Union's 26.6 million dead and the victory
  over fascism — and, in Soviet times, the story left out Stalin's
  pact with Hitler in 1939 and the purges.
- In **Germany**, memory moved from silence in the 1950s to public
  remembrance: memorials in Berlin, school curricula that teach the
  Holocaust in detail, and a national consensus that responsibility
  must be faced rather than denied.
- In **Japan**, the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki are
  central to memory, while the war in China and the atrocities of the
  Japanese military were long minimized in textbooks — a dispute that
  still affects Japan's relations with China and Korea.
- In the **United States**, the war is often remembered as the "Good
  War" — D-Day, the liberation of the camps, American sacrifice — a
  story that can leave out Japanese internment, segregation in the
  armed forces, and the civilian cost of the atomic bombs.

**Worked example 3 — comparing two textbook accounts:** Imagine two
textbooks describing the same event, the end of WWII. Textbook A (from
one country) writes, "Our forces liberated Europe and saved the world
from tyranny." Textbook B (from another) writes, "The war ended when
our army reached Berlin after losing 26 million people." Both sentences
can be true — and both leave things out. The historian's question is
not "which is true?" but "what does each one emphasize, what does each
omit, and why?" That is D2.His.6.9-12: the perspectives of history's
writers shape the histories they produce.

## Part 3 — The Cold War begins (explicit teaching, 10 min)

The war's end created a new rivalry. The United States and the Soviet
Union — wartime allies — emerged as superpowers with opposite systems,
and Europe was split between them. In March 1946 Winston Churchill
spoke of an **"iron curtain"** descending across Europe. In 1947 the
United States announced the **Truman Doctrine** (support for countries
resisting communism) and the **Marshall Plan** (aid to rebuild Western
Europe). Germany was divided into zones that became two states; Berlin
was divided too. The **Cold War** — a rivalry fought through allies,
proxy wars, and nuclear threat rather than direct battle between the
superpowers — would shape the next forty years, and it is the subject
of U06.

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted **10 December
1948**, opens with a sentence worth memorizing: *"All human beings
are born free and equal in dignity and rights."* It was written by
people who had just watched what happened when that sentence was
denied. Whether the world has lived up to it is a question for U08's
capstone — and for the learner's own civic life.

**Teacher note (misconceptions):** Learners often assume "memory" is
just "what happened." Redirect: memory is a *choice* — what a society
puts in textbooks and builds memorials for. Two societies can remember
the same war differently without either one lying; the work is to ask
what each memory includes, omits, and serves. See
[teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice (10 min, with adult or answer key nearby)

1. For each institution (UN, Bretton Woods, Nuremberg), write one
   sentence stating its purpose and one sentence stating a limit.
2. In two sentences, explain why the UN has a Security Council veto and
   why that veto is both the reason the UN exists and the reason it
   sometimes cannot act.
3. Choose two countries from Part 2 and write one sentence each on how
   their memory of WWII differs — and one sentence on what each memory
   leaves out.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 3–4 sentences why Nuremberg was a precedent rather than
   a pattern. Use the phrase "victors' justice" and say what it means.
5. Write a paragraph (4–5 sentences) answering: did WWII resolve the
   crises of the interwar years, or relocate them? Use one fact about
   the UN, one about the Cold War's origins, and one about memory.

## Applied task

6. **Memorial proposal:** Your town wants a memorial to the victims of
   the Second World War. Write a 5-sentence proposal: whom it
   remembers, what it shows (no graphic imagery), one sentence from
   the Universal Declaration of Human Rights it quotes, and one
   sentence explaining what it deliberately leaves out and why.

## Exit check

7. In one sentence: what did the postwar world build, and what was
   each thing's limit? (Acceptable: the UN to keep the peace — limited
   by the veto; the IMF/World Bank to stabilize economies — limited by
   who controlled them; Nuremberg to try war criminals — limited by
   being run by the victors.)
