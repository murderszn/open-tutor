# Unit 05 Answer Key

**For the guiding adult only — never beside a student question.** Every
lesson question, practice item, workshop, quiz question, and assessment
task is solved here independently and reconciled with the lessons. For
open responses, sample reasoning and acceptable variation are given;
defensible alternatives that cite evidence are accepted.

## Lesson 01 — WWI: causes, course, settlement

**1.** Militarism — Germany and Britain raced to build battleships;
generals' rigid timetables made leaders afraid to wait. Alliances —
the Triple Alliance faced the Triple Entente, so a local fight pulled
in the great powers. Imperialism — rivalry over colonies (e.g. Morocco
1905, 1911) left resentment. Nationalism — Serbian nationalists wanted
Bosnia to join Serbia rather than stay in Austria-Hungary.

**2.** Order: (1) the alliance system — *cause* (long-term structure);
(2) the assassination of Franz Ferdinand — *trigger* (28 June 1914);
(3) the July Crisis ultimatum — *escalation*; (4) Germany's invasion
of Belgium — *escalation* (brought Britain in).

**3.** After the Marne (September 1914) both sides dug trenches and
the line barely moved. Attacking across open ground was suicidal
because of machine guns and artillery (also: barbed wire, poison gas).
Two technologies: machine guns, artillery (barbed wire/gas accepted).

**4.** The assassination was the trigger, not the cause: the alliances,
arms race, and rivalries were already in place, and a different crisis
could have lit the same powder. The MAIN causes interacted — the
assassination alone, without the alliance system and timetables, would
have stayed a Balkan crisis.

**5.** Three terms, each with beneficiary/resentment: Article 231
(war guilt) — benefited France/Britain (justified reparations),
resented by Germany (blamed alone). Reparations, 132 billion gold
marks (1921) — benefited the victors, resented by Germany (crippling
burden). Territorial losses (~13% of territory, all colonies) and army
limited to 100,000 — benefited France's security, resented by Germany
(humiliation). (League of Nations also accepted.)

**6.** Editorial: accept any clear position with two facts from the
lesson (e.g. "too harsh — Article 231 blamed Germany alone and
reparations of 132 billion gold marks bred resentment") plus a
one-sentence German reply (e.g. "A German editor would reply that
Germany was not invited to negotiate and that the treaty punished
rather than reconciled").

**7.** The alliance system and military timetables pulled the great
powers in, so a local crisis escalated into a European war within
five weeks.

## Lesson 02 — Interwar crisis and ideologies

**1.** Liberalism — power through elected representative government;
private property; dissent protected by rights and free debate.
Fascism — power in a single party and glorified leader; private
property kept but controlled by the state; dissent suppressed.
Communism — power in a single party ruling in the name of the
workers; state ownership of the means of production; dissent
suppressed.

**2.** League weakness: Japan invaded Manchuria (1931) and Italy
invaded Ethiopia (1935) and the League protested but did almost
nothing. Democracy struggling: Germany's Weimar Republic changed
governments constantly and no party could hold a majority.

**3.** Order: Germany remilitarizes the Rhineland (1936); Munich
Agreement (September 1938); Germany takes the rest of Czechoslovakia
(March 1939); Germany invades Poland (1 September 1939). What each
shows: each concession was meant to buy peace and each time Hitler
demanded more — appeasement did not satisfy him; by March 1939 it was
clear the demands would not stop.

**4.** The Depression made radical movements attractive because
unemployment and lost savings destroyed trust in existing governments:
in the U.S. about one in four workers was unemployed by 1933; in
Germany unemployment passed six million. People facing ruin listened
to movements promising forceful answers. But the Depression was a
*condition*, not the *cause* of WWII — leaders' decisions (invading
Poland, attacking China) started the war.

**5.** Acceptable fascist claims: "Democracy is weak and divided; only
a strong leader can restore order and national pride." Liberal reply:
"Rights and elections protect people from leaders who promise order
and deliver tyranny; free debate corrects mistakes." Label each as
claim (value judgment) or fact (checkable statement) — e.g. "democracy
is weak" is a claim; "Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933" is a
fact.

**6.** Debate prep: accept either side with two facts (economic-crisis
side: 25% U.S. unemployment 1933; German unemployment over six
million; hyperinflation 1923. Leaders'-choices side: Hitler's decisions
to rearm, remilitarize, annex; Stalin's purges; Mussolini's invasion
of Ethiopia) plus one sentence anticipating the other side's best
point.

**7.** Each promised a different route out of crisis — liberalism
through rights, elections, and markets; fascism through national
unity and a strong leader; communism through workers' rule and state
ownership.

## Lesson 03 — WWII: global war and home fronts

**1.** Map: Poland (1 September 1939), Pearl Harbor (7 December
1941), Normandy/D-Day (6 June 1944), Japan surrenders (2 September
1945). Arrows: Europe (German advance east, 1941), Pacific (U.S.
island campaign, 1942–45), home front (workers and supplies moving to
factories/ports). Accept any correct placement with dates.

**2.** Stalingrad (1942–43) is a turning point because before it
Germany was advancing and after it Germany was mostly retreating —
the direction of the war changed. D-Day (1944) opened a second front
after the direction had already changed; it hastened victory but did
not reverse the war's direction.

**3.** Two home-front changes: (a) women into factories — about six
million U.S. women took factory jobs (Rosie the Riveter); most left
after the war. (b) Japanese internment — about 120,000 people of
Japanese ancestry removed from the West Coast and held in camps;
two-thirds were U.S. citizens. (Also accepted: rationing, Soviet
factory relocation.)

**4.** WWII killed more civilians than soldiers because the war
targeted whole societies: strategic bombing of cities (London,
Dresden, Tokyo), blockade and famine (e.g. the siege of Leningrad),
forced labor, and genocide. Total deaths 70–85 million, of which
~50–55 million were civilians vs. ~21–25 million military.

**5.** Evaluation: "necessary for security" is not supported — no
Japanese American was convicted of espionage for Japan during the war;
the policy was based on ancestry, not individual suspicion; the U.S.
government apologized and paid reparations in 1988. The evidence
supports that internment was driven by fear and prejudice, not by
proven security need; it does not show that every official acted from
prejudice — motives varied.

**6.** Diary: accept any 5-sentence entry consistent with the lesson
(rationing, factory work, air raids or news of the war, a question the
news does not answer). No invented battles or technologies; the city
chosen must match its real wartime conditions (London — bombing and
rationing; Detroit — factory work and migration; Moscow — rationing
and the war's approach).

**7.** It was fought worldwide, civilians were the majority of the
dead, and the war targeted whole peoples — through bombing, famine,
forced labor, and genocide.

## Lesson 04 — Evidence lab: genocide

**1.** Intent and group. Intent matters because genocide is defined by
the deliberate attempt to destroy a group as such — without intent, a
mass killing (however terrible) is not genocide under the Convention.
Group matters because the crime is against a national, ethnic, racial,
or religious group, not against individuals at random.

**2.** Documents — Wannsee minutes, Nuremberg Laws, railway schedules;
limit: destroyed files, coded language, unwritten orders. Testimony —
survivor accounts of ghettos, deportations, camps; limit: one person's
view, memory changes, trauma affects recall. Photographs/film — camp
photographs; limit: shows a moment, not why or what came before/after;
can be staged or cropped. Physical evidence — camps, gas chambers,
mass graves; limit: shows scale and place, not intent or names.
Statistics — pre/post-war population counts (six million Jews);
limit: depends on who counted and how.

**3.** A photograph shows a street existed at a moment in time; it
cannot show why anyone was there, what happened before or after, or
whose street it was. A single photograph cannot prove a genocide —
genocide requires intent and scale, which a photograph alone cannot
show.

**4.** Armenian genocide vs. Rwandan genocide: similarity — both were
organized by a government or ruling group against a targeted people
(deportation/death marches in 1915; house-to-house killing by militias
in 1994). Difference in who stopped it — the Armenian genocide was
not stopped by outside powers; the Rwandan genocide ended when the
Rwandan Patriotic Front took power, after the UN failed to intervene.
Strongest evidence: Armenian — diplomatic reports and survivor
testimony; Rwandan — UN cables, radio broadcasts, tribunal records.

**5.** Reply to the denial claim (3 sentences): the claim makes a
logical error — historians do not need a single order; intent is
established by converging evidence. Documents (Wannsee minutes,
railway schedules) and physical evidence (camps) answer it directly.
The claim misunderstands historical method: no single document proves
a complex event; documents, testimony, photographs, physical evidence,
and statistics together establish what happened beyond reasonable doubt.

**6.** Evidence brief: accept any brief that (a) states what happened
in two sentences, (b) gives two evidence kinds with one specific
example each, (c) states one limit, (d) asks one further question,
and ends with the death-toll range and source: Holocaust — six million
Jews (USHMM); Armenian — 600,000–1.5 million (historians; IAGS ~1–1.5
million); Rwandan — 800,000–1,000,000 (UN).

**7.** Because each kind has limits, and only together — documents,
testimony, photographs, physical evidence, and statistics — do they
establish intent, scale, and what happened beyond reasonable doubt.

## Lesson 05 — Institutions, memory, Cold War

**1.** UN — purpose: keep the peace and settle disputes by talk, and
promote human rights; limit: the Security Council veto can freeze it
when great powers disagree. Bretton Woods (IMF/World Bank) — purpose:
stabilize currencies and rebuild ruined economies; limit: built for
rebuilding Europe, with the least say for the countries needing
development most. Nuremberg — purpose: try leaders for war crimes and
crimes against humanity, establishing individual responsibility; limit:
run by the victors; no Allied conduct was tried.

**2.** The UN has a veto because the great powers would not join
without it — the League failed partly because the U.S. never joined.
The veto is both the reason the UN exists (the great powers are inside
it) and the reason it sometimes cannot act (any permanent member can
block action, as in Rwanda in 1994).

**3.** Accept any two countries from Part 2: e.g. Russia remembers the
"Great Patriotic War" and 26.6 million dead, omitting Stalin's 1939
pact with Hitler; Germany remembers through public remembrance and
Holocaust education, omitting the 1950s silence; Japan centers the
atomic bombings, omitting the war in China; the U.S. remembers the
"Good War," omitting internment and segregation.

**4.** Nuremberg was a precedent, not a pattern: it happened because
the Allies won completely and occupied Germany. In most wars since,
no such trials followed — Rwanda's tribunal took years, and many
perpetrators are never tried. "Victors' justice" means the winners ran
the court and their own conduct was not tried — which critics cite
against it and supporters answer by pointing to the principle it
established (individual responsibility; "following orders" is no defense).

**5.** Accept any paragraph using one UN fact, one Cold War-origins
fact, and one memory fact, taking either position with evidence:
e.g. "resolved" — the UN, the Genocide Convention, and the UDHR
created tools that did not exist in 1919; "relocated" — the Cold War
divided Europe, the veto froze the UN, and rival memories kept the
conflicts alive in new forms.

**6.** Memorial proposal: accept any 5-sentence proposal naming whom
it remembers, what it shows without graphic imagery, quoting one UDHR
sentence (e.g. Article 1: "All human beings are born free and equal
in dignity and rights."), and explaining one deliberate omission and
why.

**7.** The UN to keep the peace — limited by the veto; the IMF/World
Bank to stabilize economies — limited by who controlled them;
Nuremberg to try war criminals — limited by being run by the victors.

## Practice Set A — WWI causes and settlement

**A1.** Cause map: center "War in Europe, August 1914"; four branches
with one fact each (see Lesson 01, Q1) and one cross-arrow per branch
(e.g. alliances → militarism: timetables assumed allies would fight;
imperialism → nationalism: colonial rivalry fed national pride;
nationalism → alliances: Serbia's claims threatened Austria-Hungary,
Russia's ally). Hardest to stop: the alliance-timetable connection —
once Russia mobilized, Germany's plan required attacking France
through Belgium on schedule, so delay meant defeat.

**A2.** (a) Written by the victorious Allies (Britain, France, the
United States). (b) Germany had to accept it; Germany was not invited
to negotiate. (c) Germany accepts responsibility for causing all the
loss and damage of the war. (d) A German in 1919 would call it unfair
because Germany was blamed alone and was not at the table; a French
person in 1919 would call it just because France had been invaded
twice in fifty years and needed security and compensation.

**A3.** Order: assassination in Sarajevo (June 1914); Germany invades
Belgium (August 1914); Battle of the Somme (1916); United States
enters the war (April 1917); Russia leaves the war (1918,
Brest-Litovsk); armistice (11 November 1918); Treaty of Versailles
signed (28 June 1919); League of Nations founded (1920). Course:
U.S. entry; Russia's exit (either pair accepted with reasoning).
Settlement: Versailles; League of Nations.

**A4.** Errors: (1) "Germany started the war" is not the historical
consensus — responsibility was shared (Austria-Hungary's ultimatum,
Russia's mobilization, Germany's blank cheque and invasion of Belgium,
France's and Britain's choices). (2) "Fair because they should pay"
confuses blame with justice — the treaty also had to make Europe
safer, and critics argue it bred resentment instead. Fairer claim:
"The treaty made Germany pay and accept blame, but historians debate
whether that was fair or wise, since it punished Germany without
securing lasting peace."

## Practice Set B — Interwar crisis and ideologies

**B1.** Liberalism — power: elected representative government; property:
private; dissent: protected by rights and free debate; example:
Britain/France/United States, 1920s–30s. Fascism — power: single
party and glorified leader; property: private but state-controlled;
dissent: suppressed; example: Italy 1922, Germany 1933. Communism —
power: single party ruling in the name of the workers; property: state
ownership; dissent: suppressed; example: Soviet Union, 1920s–30s.

**B2.** (a) The 25% U.S. figure shows mass unemployment in the world's
largest economy; the six-million German figure shows a crisis deep
enough to destroy trust in the Weimar Republic. (b) The figures cannot
tell you how people experienced the Depression — hunger, humiliation,
what families did to survive, or whom they blamed.

**B3.** A 1932 poster is a *propaganda* source (a primary source:
printed ephemera). Maker: likely a political party or movement;
audience: voters; purpose: to persuade people that only a strong
leader can save the nation. It can prove what the movement promised
and how it appealed to voters; it cannot prove that a strong leader
would have saved the nation, or what most people believed.

**B4.** "Cowards" is a moral judgment, not an explanation. Better
reasons appeasement failed: (1) Hitler's demands kept growing — each
concession (Rhineland 1936, Munich 1938) was followed by a new demand,
so giving in did not satisfy him; (2) Britain and France were trying
to avoid another war like WWI and were not yet rearmed — fear of war
and unreadiness, not cowardice, shaped the policy.

## Practice Set C — WWII scope and home fronts

**C1.** Map: Poland, Britain, Stalingrad, Normandy, Pearl Harbor,
Hiroshima marked and dated. Arrows: German advance east (June 1941),
Allied landing in France (6 June 1944), Pacific island campaign
(1942–45). The Eastern Front cost the most lives — the Soviet Union
lost about 26.6 million people, the largest national toll, because the
war there was fought across inhabited land with siege, starvation, and
mass killing of civilians.

**C2.** WWI total deaths: 15–22 million (Britannica). WWII total
deaths: 70–85 million (scholarly consensus; National WWII Museum
gives ~60 million on a narrower count). WWII military deaths: ~21–25
million. WWII civilian deaths: ~50–55 million (National WWII Museum:
~45 million, noting Chinese deaths may be far higher). Soviet WWII
deaths: ~26.6 million (Russian government figures).

**C3.** Women in U.S. factories: about six million women took factory
jobs; most left after the war, though women's workforce participation
never returned to pre-war levels. Japanese internment: about 120,000
people of Japanese ancestry held in camps; the policy ended with the
war, and the government apologized and paid reparations in 1988.
Together they show total war reached into daily life and civil
liberties — it mobilized some groups and targeted others.

**C4.** Errors: (1) D-Day (June 1944) came after the war's direction
had already changed at Stalingrad and Midway (1942–43). (2) "Won"
ignores the Eastern Front, where the Soviet Union bore most of the
fighting and the dying, and the Pacific war. Fairer claim: "D-Day
opened a second front that hastened Germany's defeat in a war whose
direction had already turned in 1942–43."

## Practice Set D — Evidence of genocide

**D1.** (a) Wannsee minutes — documents; shows organization and
intent; cannot show unwritten orders or what was destroyed. (b)
Survivor's account — testimony; shows lived experience and details no
document records; cannot show more than one person's view, and memory
changes. (c) Photograph of a camp gate — photographs/film; shows the
place existed at a moment; cannot show why or what happened before or
after. (d) Census before/after — statistics; shows the scale of loss;
cannot show individual stories. (e) Railway timetable — documents;
shows organization and scale of deportations; cannot show intent by
itself (a timetable does not say why the trains ran).

**D2.** Logical error: demanding a single document misunderstands how
historians prove a complex event — intent is established by converging
evidence, not one order. Two kinds that answer it: documents (Wannsee
minutes, railway schedules) and physical evidence (the camps
themselves). "Intent" in the Convention means the deliberate aim to
destroy a group in whole or in part. The converging evidence
establishes the systematic murder of six million European Jews beyond
reasonable doubt.

**D3.** Armenian genocide — dates: 1915–1917 (to 1923); deaths:
600,000–1.5 million; targeted: Armenians of the Ottoman Empire; key
evidence: diplomatic reports (German/American officials) or survivor
testimony; stopped by: no outside power stopped it (it ended with the
war and the empire's collapse). Rwandan genocide — dates: April–July
1994 (~100 days); deaths: 800,000–1,000,000; targeted: Tutsi and
moderate Hutu; key evidence: UN cables/radio broadcasts or tribunal
records; stopped by: the Rwandan Patriotic Front taking power (the UN
did not intervene). Similarity: both were organized by a ruling group
against a targeted people. Difference: Rwanda's killing was stopped by
a military victory while the UN stood by; Armenia's was not stopped by
any intervention.

**D4.** Not true. (1) Ranges are honest where counting is hard
(destroyed records, unmarked graves) — a range with a named source is
more trustworthy than a false exact number. (2) The *fact* of a
genocide rests on converging kinds of evidence (documents, testimony,
physical evidence, statistics), not on agreeing on one death toll.

## Practice Set E — Institutions and memory

**E1.** UN — purpose: keep the peace and settle disputes by talk;
limit: the Security Council veto; mattered: it gave the world a forum
and a body of international law (Genocide Convention, UDHR). IMF/World
Bank — purpose: stabilize currencies and rebuild economies; limit:
built for Europe, least say for those needing development most;
mattered: financed postwar reconstruction. Nuremberg — purpose: try
leaders for war crimes and crimes against humanity; limit: victors'
justice; mattered: established individual responsibility and that
"following orders" is no defense.

**E2.** (a) Adopted 10 December 1948. (b) The Second World War (and the
Holocaust) had just ended. (c) "Dignity" adds inherent worth — rights
belong to people because they are human, not because a government
grants them; "free" alone could mean merely "not enslaved." (d) Any
group from the unit: European Jews under Nazi rule; Armenians in 1915;
Tutsi in 1994; Japanese Americans interned in the U.S.

**E3.** Textbook A emphasizes liberation and its own country's role;
it omits the costs borne by others (e.g. the Soviet 26.6 million dead,
civilian suffering). Textbook B emphasizes its own country's sacrifice;
it omits others' contributions (e.g. the Western Allies, the
liberation of the camps). D2.His.6.9-12 in plain words: the people who
write history shape it — ask what each writer emphasizes, omits, and
why, before deciding what to believe.

**E4.** Weak because (1) the UN was designed to work when the great
powers agreed — its veto is the price of their membership, and Rwanda
shows the limit of that design, not that the idea is worthless;
(2) international law still set standards used to judge governments —
the Genocide Convention and the UDHR, and later tribunals like the
ICTR, exist because of the postwar institutions.

## Writing Workshops

**W1.** Accept any 6–8 sentence argument with: a clear claim (the
assassination was the trigger, not the cause); three evidence pieces
(one each from militarism, alliances, nationalism/imperialism);
a counterclaim (e.g. "without the assassination there would have
been no war in 1914") and a reply (the crisis would likely have come
from another trigger, given the alliances and timetables); and a final
sentence distinguishing trigger from cause.

**W2.** Both sides prepared with two facts each (see Lesson 02, Q6).
Judgment: accept either side when the 5-sentence judgment names the
stronger side, gives a reason, and states what evidence would change
the learner's mind (e.g. "a case where a stable economy still chose
fascism would weaken the economic-crisis side").

**W3.** Accept any claim about responsibility with three cited
evidence pieces (one document, one testimony or statistic, one other
kind), a limits paragraph, and a sentence distinguishing leaders'
(ordered and organized), participants' (carried out), and bystanders'
(knew and did not act) responsibility. No graphic detail; adult
previews the draft.

**W4.** Accept any 8–10 sentence argument with a claim, three or more
evidence pieces from at least two lessons, a counterclaim with reply,
a source-limit note, and a closing sentence on U06 (e.g. "the Cold
War shows the crises were relocated into a new rivalry rather than
resolved").
