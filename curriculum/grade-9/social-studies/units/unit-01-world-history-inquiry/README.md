# Unit 01 — World-History Inquiry: Geography and Global Connections circa 1450

**Grade 9 · Social Studies · Modern world history · Weeks 3–6 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-13 for issue #45; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Before sustained global contact connected the whole planet, the world of about
1450 was already deeply connected — just not everywhere, and not equally. This
unit builds the course's core toolkit: reading maps and region data, comparing
political and economic systems across regions, classifying historical sources,
framing inquiry questions, and arguing from evidence. The learner tours the
major world regions circa 1450, traces the three great Afro-Eurasian connection
systems (the overland Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean maritime network, and the
trans-Saharan routes), studies the Americas and Oceania on their own terms, and
finishes by judging which connections mattered most — with evidence.

The unit deliberately resists the "isolated world before Europeans" story.
Afro-Eurasia circa 1450 was a dense web of trade, faith, and ideas; the
Americas and Oceania had their own large-scale societies and networks. The
inquiry question that runs through all four weeks is: **what connected people
circa 1450, and what did not — and how do we know?**

## Prerequisites

Entry skills from the course diagnostic (weeks 1–2): place centuries on a
timeline, read a scaled map (key, scale, projection note), classify sources by
kind, and write a claim supported by evidence. No cross-grade references:
these are described skills, not links. An adult confirms reading stamina for
full passages and previews all materials.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Locate and compare major world regions circa 1450** — name at least eight
   regions/polities on a labeled map and compare the political organization of
   any two using a consistent frame (who rules, how power passes, how the
   economy is organized). (D2.His.1.9-12)
2. **Explain the three Afro-Eurasian connection systems** — describe the Silk
   Roads, Indian Ocean maritime network, and trans-Saharan routes: what moved
   along each (goods, faiths, ideas, technologies), who moved it, and what
   physical systems (monsoons, deserts, steppe) made each possible.
   (D2.His.1, D2.His.2.9-12)
3. **Analyze the Americas and Oceania on their own terms** — describe the
   political and economic organization of the Mexica, Inca, Mississippian, and
   at least one Pacific society circa 1450 without measuring them against
   Afro-Eurasian societies, and evaluate what the word "isolated" hides and
   reveals. (D2.His.4, D2.His.8.9-12)
4. **Work with historical sources** — classify sources by kind; infer maker,
   date, place, and intended audience; detect one limitation or gap in the
   record for a stated inquiry. (D2.His.8, D2.His.10, D2.His.12.9-12;
   D3.1–D3.4.9-12)
5. **Argue from evidence about connection and change** — frame compelling and
   supporting questions, integrate evidence from multiple sources into a
   reasoned claim about which circa-1450 connection mattered most, and state
   the claim's limits. (D1.1–D1.5, D2.His.16, D4.1–D4.4.9-12)

## Essential question

What connected the world's peoples circa 1450 — and how do historians know,
given that the surviving record favors some voices over others?

## Standards and source notes

C3 Framework indicator codes are exact as verified in the track audit
(2026-10-07, socialstudies.org/standards/c3); descriptions are paraphrases —
check the authoritative framework for exact wording. **No state adoption,
accreditation, or alignment certification is claimed.** Regional facts below
use widely published reference ranges; every population figure is labeled an
estimate and ranges are given where sources differ. Modern country data from
`resources/un_countries.csv` is used only with its documented limits
(population/lat/lng columns empty; boundaries are modern, not 1450). The two
modeled traveler passages are original modern retellings of episodes from Ibn
Battuta's Rihla, labeled as such — not translations.
