## Practice Set A

**A1.** (1) Silk Roads (overland): e.g., Samarkand, Constantinople/
Venice, Chang'an/Beijing. (2) Indian Ocean maritime network: e.g.,
Calicut, Aden, Malacca, Guangzhou. (3) Trans-Saharan routes: e.g.,
Timbuktu, Sijilmasa, Cairo. Any two accurate nodes per system earn
credit.

**A2.** West Africa (Mali/Songhai region) and North Africa/Mediterranean
world. Southward: salt (from Saharan deposits like Taghaza/Taoudenni),
plus cloth and books. Northward: gold (from Bambuk/Bure fields), plus
kola nuts and enslaved people.

**A3.** Acceptable table (wording may vary):

| Frame | Ottoman lands | Mali/Songhai West Africa |
|---|---|---|
| Who rules | Sultan (Murad II in 1450), with military-administrative elite | Mansa (Mali, fading) / emerging Songhai ruler, with provincial governors |
| How power passes | Hereditary dynasty, legitimized by conquest and Islamic law | Hereditary kingship, legitimized by control of trade and Islamic scholarship |
| Economy | Taxation, tribute from Balkans/Anatolia, control of trade routes | Gold-salt trade taxation, agricultural tribute, trans-Saharan commerce |

**A4.** Because it was not one road: it was a web of overlapping caravan
routes, sea links, and branch paths across Central Asia, with many
nodes, alternate passes, and no single builder or authority.

**A5.** Any three of: Brunei 5765; Cambodia 181035; Indonesia 1904569;
Laos 236800; Malaysia 330803; Myanmar 676578; Philippines 342353;
Singapore 710; Thailand 513120; Timor-Leste 14874; Vietnam 331212
(area_km2, from `un_countries.csv`, "South-Eastern Asia"). Acceptable
sentence: modern state areas measure 21st-century countries, not 1450
polities like Majapahit or Ayutthaya, whose borders differed and are

**A6.** Errors: (1) the Silk Roads were not built at one date by one

constructed in 1450; (2) no single "Chinese government" built them —
many peoples and states maintained segments. Corrected: the Silk Roads
were long-used trade networks across Central Asia, active circa 1450
under many different rulers.

## Practice Set B

**B1.** Any three with routes, e.g.: Islam — trans-Saharan and Indian
Ocean networks; Buddhism — Silk Roads to China; papermaking — Silk Roads
westward; gunpowder knowledge — Silk Roads westward; the Black Death —
Silk Roads; numerals/astronomy — via Muslim scholars along trade cities.

**B2.** Acceptable chain (≥3 links): winter monsoon blows Asia→East
Africa → ships sail from India/Arabia to the Swahili coast in winter →
merchants must wait months for the summer reversal to return → demand for
warehouses, lodging, credit in ports like Kilwa and Aden → cosmopolitan
port economies.

**B3.** Kind: primary source (literary/panegyric). Maker: a court poet —
shaped by patronage, writes to praise. Audience/purpose: the court and
scholars; to honor, not to record fact. Limits: says nothing reliable
about ordinary people's lives, the trade's economics, or dissenting
views. Cannot answer: e.g., what Timbuktu's porters earned, or what
critics of the scholars thought.

**B4.** Five items with origins (any accurate five): dhows/lateen sails —
Arabia/Indian Ocean shipbuilding; pepper sacks — Malabar Coast, India;
silk bolts — China; porcelain jars — China; cotton bales — India;
camels/caravan — Sahara/Arabia; balance scales — Mediterranean merchant
practice. Cannot prove (any one): e.g., "this is what Calicut looked
like in 1450" — it is a modern reconstruction, not evidence of any real
port.

**B5.** The merchant network was more resilient: it was decentralized —
hundreds of private merchants, many ports, no single authority — so it
survived political upheaval. Zheng He's fleets stopped by imperial
decision after 1433; one ruler's choice ended the whole program.
Evidence: Lesson 02 contrast; Indian Ocean trade continued and grew
after 1450 without state fleets.

**B6.** The 17 Eastern Africa rows with capitals (from the CSV): Burundi–
Gitega, Comoros–Moroni, Djibouti–Djibouti, Eritrea–Asmara, Ethiopia–
Addis Ababa, Kenya–Nairobi, Madagascar–Antananarivo, Malawi–Lilongwe,
Mauritius–Port Louis, Mozambique–Maputo, Rwanda–Kigali, Seychelles–
Victoria, Somalia–Mogadishu, Tanzania–Dodoma, Uganda–Kampala, Zambia–
Lusaka, Zimbabwe–Harare. Suggests: many modern states along the western
Indian Ocean, several with coastal capitals/ports — consistent with a
maritime-facing region. Hides: 1450 had no such states; the coast was
Swahili city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi), and most listed capitals
are modern inland creations.

## Practice Set C

**C1.** Mexica: (a) tribute in goods/labor from subject cities on fixed
schedules; (b) pictographic codices and scribal records; (c) chinampa
intensive agriculture (maize, beans, squash). Inca: (a) mit'a rotational
labor tribute; (b) quipu knotted cords; (c) terrace agriculture
(potatoes, maize, quinoa).

**C2.** Cahokia was one city; Mississippian culture was a wider way of
life — mound towns, maize farming, and trade networks across the
Southeast/Midwest — that continued in many towns after Cahokia's
abandonment. A city's fall is not a culture's end.

**C3.** Any two: star compass (memorized rising/setting stars) —
replaced the magnetic compass; wave-pattern reading — replaced charts;
bird flight observation — replaced coastal piloting; double-hulled canoe
design — replaced single-hull deep-water ships.

**C4.** Credit any rewrite that keeps the facts and changes the frame.
Example: A-sentence "Tenochtitlan was a planned imperial metropolis"
→ B-frame: "Tenochtitlan's planned grandeur was built with tribute
extracted under threat of war from subject cities."

**C5.** Any two, labeled: (capability) Atlantic too wide for 1450s ships
with no reliable return wind route; no stepping-stone ports.
(motive/knowledge) no knowledge the Americas existed, so no investment
in the attempt.

**C6.** Error: size ≠ "advanced" — the argument smuggles in an undefined
yardstick and a single measure. Better: define criteria first (e.g.,
feeding the population, public works, record-keeping), then compare each
society on those criteria with evidence.

## Practice Set D

**D1.** Compelling: open, arguable, needs a whole inquiry (e.g., "Did
Indian Ocean trade benefit ordinary port residents or only merchants?").
Supporting: narrower, source-answerable (e.g., "What goods passed through
Malacca circa 1450?").

**D2.** Kind: primary source (court artwork). Maker: Ming court
painters — shaped by imperial patronage. Audience/purpose: the emperor
and court; to glorify the voyages. Limits: propaganda of state power;
shows ships as the court wished them seen, not the voyages' costs,
failures, or ordinary sailors' experience.

**D3.** Continuity: the monsoon sailing calendar still scheduled Indian
Ocean trade (cause: unchanging wind physics + accumulated navigational
knowledge). Change: Ming China withdrew from state voyaging after 1433
(cause: imperial policy shift and court politics), leaving trade to
private merchants.

**D4.** Most likely: court records or merchant accounts *mentioning*
workers in passing — but payrolls for casual dock labor were rarely
kept, and what was kept seldom survived; also, the workers themselves
were mostly non-literate. Any well-reasoned version earns credit.

**D5.** Credit any arguable one-sentence claim with an explicit
yardstick, e.g.: "The Indian Ocean network mattered most *for moving
the greatest variety of goods between the most regions*" — yardstick
stated.

**D6.** Any one region with its count (Asia 47, Europe 45, Africa 54,
Americas 35, Oceania 14 — from the CSV's `region` column). Acceptable
sentence: the count describes the modern UN-membership map; it tells
nothing about how many polities existed in 1450 or how they were
organized.

## Unit Assessment (80 pts)

**A1.** Source 1: primary (travel narrative, retold); maker — Ibn
Battuta, Muslim judge/traveler writing for a courtly audience;
limit — elite male lens, dictated years later, omits laborers/sailors.
Source 2: primary (business record); maker — Venetian merchant, written
for himself/partners; limit — one merchant's data only; no causes, no
labor, no context. (12)

**A2.** Together they connect the human and the economic: Source 1 shows
the *network's human density* (shells as money from Maldives to Mali;
people living by ocean trade), Source 2 shows *hard price mechanics*
(buy/sell margins, freight costs). Neither alone shows both how the
system felt and how it paid — together they suggest *why* it persisted:
profit moved people, and people moved goods. Credit any 3–4 sentences
making a genuine synthesis. (8)

**A3.** Neither source is about labor: the traveler notices markets and
elites, the ledger records prices — both are elite/commercial
viewpoints. A source type that could help: e.g., port tax or wage
records, guild regulations, court cases involving workers, or
archaeology of workers' housing — with the honest note that such
records rarely survive for 1450. (10)

**B1.** Score: 3 systems shaded and named (6), 6 nodes labeled (3), 2
goods per system with origins (3). Deduct for misplaced nodes or modern
anachronisms (e.g., labeling "India" as a 1450 state).

**B2.** Any three South-Eastern Asia rows with area_km2 (see A5 key).
Sentences: the data locates Malacca's *modern region* and shows the
area of today's states — useful for geography orientation; it cannot
show Malacca's 1450 importance, which came from its strait position and
port institutions, not from any modern country's size. (8)
