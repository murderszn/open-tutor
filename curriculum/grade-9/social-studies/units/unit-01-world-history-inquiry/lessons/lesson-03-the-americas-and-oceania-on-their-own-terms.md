# Lesson 03 — The Americas and Oceania on Their Own Terms, circa 1450

**Unit 01 · Week 5, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can describe major American and Pacific
societies circa 1450 accurately — and explain what the word "isolated"
hides and what it reveals.

**Materials:** Lesson 01 map, notebook. (Keep the Afro-Eurasian map in
view for contrast — but do not measure these societies against it.)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write everything you already know about any
one of these: the Aztecs, the Inca, Cahokia, Polynesian voyagers. Star
anything you are *unsure* is true. We will check the starred items today.

---

## Part 1 — Four societies, four solutions (explicit teaching, 15 min)

**The Mexica (Aztec), Valley of Mexico.** The Triple Alliance of
Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan (formed 1428) ruled an empire of
several million people (estimates vary). Tenochtitlan — founded 1325 on an
island in Lake Texcoco — may have held 200,000+ residents (estimates
vary), bigger than London or Seville at the time. **Chinampas** ("floating
gardens" — actually anchored raised beds) produced multiple harvests a
year. The empire ran on **tribute**: subject cities sent food, cloth,
warriors, and luxury goods on fixed schedules, recorded by scribes. The
great market at Tlatelolco drew tens of thousands daily, regulated by
market judges.

**The Inca, Andes Mountains.** Under Pachacuti (who began imperial
expansion around 1438), the Inca were building Tawantinsuyu ("land of the
four quarters"). There was no money and no alphabetic writing; instead the
state organized **mit'a** — rotational labor tribute: communities owed the
state weeks of work on roads, terraces, mines, and armies. **Quipu**
(knotted cords) recorded quantities and obligations; whether they also
recorded narratives is still debated. Some ~40,000 km of roads (estimates
vary) — with relay runners, storehouses, and suspension bridges — stitched
coast, highlands, and jungle together.

**The Mississippian world, North America.** The great city of Cahokia
(near present-day St. Louis) had peaked centuries earlier — perhaps
10,000–20,000 people around 1100 (estimates vary) — and was largely
abandoned by 1400. But Mississippian life continued: towns with platform
mounds, maize-beans-squash farming, and trade networks moving copper,
shell, and chert across the Southeast and Midwest. Circa 1450 this was a
world of towns and chiefdoms, not one empire — political organization
looked different, not lesser.

**The Pacific voyagers.** Polynesian navigators had settled the Pacific's
far islands centuries before 1450 using double-hulled canoes, star
compasses, wave patterns, and bird flight — arguably the greatest
navigational achievement in human history. Circa 1450: the Tu'i Tonga
chiefly system in Tonga, complex chiefdoms in Hawai'i, and Māori
settlements in Aotearoa (New Zealand, settled around 1300). These were
connected worlds — inter-island voyaging, tribute, and warfare — on an
oceanic scale Europeans had not yet imagined crossing.

> **The teaching point:** each society solved the same basic problems —
> feeding large populations, organizing labor, keeping records, moving
> goods — with different tools. Chinampas, mit'a, mound towns, and star
> navigation are four answers to shared human questions.

## Part 2 — Two descriptions, two frames (modeled perspective check, 10 min)

Historians' descriptions reveal the describer. Compare these two original
modeled descriptions of the same thing — Mexica tribute (both written for
this unit; neither is a historical source):

**Description A:** *"The Triple Alliance built an empire to rival Rome's:
provincial governors, standardized tribute quotas, and a capital whose
markets and aqueducts impressed even the alliance's enemies. Tenochtitlan
was a planned imperial metropolis."*

**Description B:** *"Subject cities paid tribute or faced war. Alliance
armies took captives for sacrifice, and the threat of violence kept the
grain flowing to Tenochtitlan. The empire's wealth was extracted, not
shared."*

**Modeled reasoning:** Both can be factually grounded — and both select.
A emphasizes administration and grandeur (a *state-building* frame,
implicitly comparing to Rome — a European yardstick). B emphasizes
coercion and extraction (a *power-and-violence* frame). Neither is
"biased" in the lying sense; each answers a different question. The
historian's move (D2.His.6.9-12): ask *what question is this writer
answering, and what would change if they asked a different one?* A fuller
account needs both frames — and, crucially, Nahua and other Indigenous
perspectives, which the surviving record underrepresents (D2.His.8.9-12).

## Worked example A — Why no regular contact? (map reasoning)

**Question:** Afro-Eurasian merchants crossed deserts and monsoon oceans.
Why was there no regular contact with the Americas circa 1450?

**Reasoning (modeled):** Three interacting reasons. (1) **Distance and
wind:** the Atlantic is ~5,000 km at its narrowest (far wider than the
monsoon hops across the Indian Ocean), and its wind systems did not offer
an easy return route with 1450s ship technology. (2) **No stepping
stones:** Indian Ocean trade hopped port to port; the Atlantic offered
almost none. (3) **No knowledge, no motive:** Afro-Eurasian merchants did
not know the Americas existed, so no one invested in the attempt. Contact
required *both* capability and reason — 1450 had neither. (This is
D2.His.14 thinking: multiple, interacting causes, not one.)

**Check:** the Pacific is even wider — which explains why Polynesian
contact with the Americas, if it happened at all, left no sustained
exchange.

## Worked example B — Reading "isolated" carefully

**Question:** A textbook calls the Americas circa 1450 "isolated." What
does that word hide, and what does it reveal?

**Reasoning (modeled):** *Reveals:* no sustained biological or commercial
exchange with Afro-Eurasia — true, and it matters (it is why later contact
would be so catastrophic: no shared immunities). *Hides:* everything
inside the Americas — the Inca road system, Mississippian trade, Mexica
tribute networks, Pacific voyaging. "Isolated" describes a *relationship to
Afro-Eurasia*, then smuggles in the suggestion that nothing much was
happening. The honest sentence: "The Americas had no regular contact with
Afro-Eurasia, while sustaining large-scale societies and networks of their
own." Precision beats slogans.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** society profiles may be read in "jigsaw" — each
  learner masters one society, then teaches it to the group.
- **Alternate response mode:** the "solution summary" may be a labeled
  diagram or a 60-second spoken brief.
- **Language:** define *tribute, chinampa, mit'a, quipu* with a sketch or
  example, not just a definition.

## Extension

Investigate one society this lesson skipped (Great Zimbabwe at its height,

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- "No writing = no history." Quipu, oral traditions, and archaeology are
  records; press learners who equate literacy with civilization.
- Learners may treat the Americas as "behind." The Tenochtitlan-vs-London
  size comparison usually corrects this fast — then ask *why* the
  comparison felt surprising (that surprise is the Eurocentric frame
  showing).
- Handle human sacrifice factually and briefly if asked: it was real,
  religiously meaningful to its practitioners, and not the whole story of
  Mexica life. Do not sensationalize; redirect to the lesson's focus on
  organization.
