# Lesson 04 — Change and Continuity, and the Inquiry Toolkit

**Unit 01 · Week 6, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain what changed and what continued
between 1200 and 1450, frame compelling and supporting questions, and build
an evidence-based argument about which connection mattered most.

**Materials:** notebook/inquiry journal, Lessons 01–03 notes, project draft
materials.

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write one thing that *changed* between 1200
and 1450 and one thing that *stayed the same*, anywhere in the world. Then
answer: *why do historians always ask about both?* (Because "everything
changed" and "nothing changed" are both always false — the real story is
the mix, and the mix is where explanations live.)

---

## Part 1 — What changed, what continued, 1200–1450 (explicit teaching, 12 min)

**Continuities** — the deep structures still running in 1450:

- The **monsoon calendar** still scheduled Indian Ocean trade, as it had
  for a thousand years. The **Sahara** still separated and connected West
  Africa to the Mediterranean. **Agrarian empires** — states taxing farmers
  — were still the world's dominant political form.
- **Faiths kept traveling** trade routes: Islam continued spreading through
  merchant and Sufi networks into West Africa, East Africa, and Southeast
  Asia.

**Changes** — what was different by 1450:

- **The Mongol moment had passed — but left tracks.** In the 1200s the
  Mongol Empire briefly united the Silk Roads under one political roof
  (the "Pax Mongolica"), making overland travel safer than ever before or
  since. By 1450 the empire was long fragmented — but its tracks remained:
  the routes, the cities, and the precedent that Eurasia *could* be
  crossed. The Ming dynasty (1368) had replaced Mongol rule in China and
  turned inward after Zheng He's voyages ended in 1433.
- **New gunpowder empires were rising.** The Ottomans (soon to take
  Constantinople, 1453), the Safavid order gathering in Persia, and the
  expanding Muscovy were all built partly on gunpowder weapons — a Chinese
  invention that had traveled west along the very networks this unit
  studies.
- **West Africa was shifting.** Mali, glorious under Mansa Musa a century
  earlier, was fading; Songhai was rising. The gold-salt trade continued —
  the *routes* were continuous, the *rulers* changed.
- **The Americas transformed independently.** The Mexica Triple Alliance
  (1428) and Inca expansion (from ~1438) were brand-new empires — the
  1400s were a century of state-building in the Americas with no
  Afro-Eurasian cause at all. (This is why "separate histories" is half
  true: connected systems *and* independent developments, simultaneously.)
- **Plague's long shadow.** The Black Death (1340s) killed on a scale that
  reshaped labor, wages, and faith across Afro-Eurasia — a change whose
  effects were still unfolding in 1450.

**The pattern to carry forward:** every unit in this course will ask the
change/continuity question. Events are the headlines; structures are the
story underneath.

## Part 2 — The inquiry toolkit: questions and sources (explicit teaching, 10 min)

**Compelling vs. supporting questions (C3 D1):**

- A **compelling question** is open, arguable, and worth a whole inquiry:
  *"Which connection mattered most to the world circa 1450?"* Reasonable
  historians could disagree — that is the point.
- A **supporting question** is narrower and answerable from sources:
  *"What goods passed through Malacca circa 1450?"* You need many
  supporting answers to build one compelling answer.

**The source lab habit:** for any source, run four checks —
**kind** (primary/secondary; what *type*: letter, ledger, travel account,
map, object), **maker** (who, and what shaped their view), **audience and
purpose** (who was it for; what was it trying to do), and **limits**
(what it cannot show; whose voices are missing). Then ask the D2.His.12
question: *what source would I need next?*

---

## Worked example A — Framing questions

**Topic:** the trans-Saharan gold-salt trade.

**Weak:** "What was traded?" (Too narrow — a fact lookup, not an inquiry.)
**Compelling:** "Did the gold-salt trade benefit West Africa, North
Africa, or both — and for whom within each?" (Arguable; needs evidence
about prices, who controlled routes, and ordinary people's lives.)
**Supporting questions that feed it:** (1) What were typical exchange
rates of gold for salt at Sijilmasa? (2) Who owned the caravans — and who
drove the camels? (3) What did Timbuktu's scholars write about the trade's
effects?

**Modeled reasoning:** notice how the supporting questions deliberately
chase *different voices* (owners vs. drivers vs. scholars) — because the
compelling question is really about *whose* benefit, and one perspective
cannot answer it.

## Worked example B — A mini-argument: which connection mattered most?

**Compelling question:** Which Afro-Eurasian connection mattered most
circa 1450?

**Modeled argument (claim + evidence + limits):**

> **Claim:** The Indian Ocean maritime network mattered most, because it
> moved the greatest *variety* of high-value goods between the most
> regions — and, unlike the overland Silk Roads, it was still growing in
> 1450.
>
> **Evidence 1:** The port cities — Calicut, Aden, Hormuz, Malacca,
> Guangzhou — handled goods from East Africa to China in a single
> system: pepper, porcelain, textiles, horses, ivory (Lesson 02; the port
> illustration's warehouses show the variety in one place).
>
> **Evidence 2:** It carried the most *people-types*: merchants,
> pilgrims, scholars, and sailors of a dozen faiths — the monsoon
> layover cities became the era's most cosmopolitan places (Ibn
> Battuta's eighteen months in the Maldives; Lesson 02).
>
> **Counterclaim and limit:** The Silk Roads moved the most
> *transformative ideas* (paper, printing, gunpowder — the technologies
> behind the rising gunpowder empires). And "mattered most" depends on
> the yardstick: by *lives fed*, the chinampa fields and monsoon rice
> harvests mattered more than any trade route. This argument holds for
> *connection*; it does not claim trade mattered more than farming.

**What to steal from this model:** a claim that can be disagreed with,
numbered evidence tied to sources, an honest counterclaim, and a stated
limit. Your project argument (project.md, Part 2) will be graded on
exactly these four moves.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners often write compelling questions that are secretly factual
  ("What year did..."). Test: *could two historians with the same sources
  disagree?* If no, it is a supporting question.
- The "which mattered most" argument tempts single-cause answers. The
  rubric rewards *yardstick honesty* (mattered most *for what?*) over
  confident overclaiming.
- In the source lab, learners may call the AI illustration "primary."
  Correct firmly: it is a modern reconstruction — useful for observation
  practice, worthless as evidence of 1450.
