# Lesson 01 — World Regions and the Three Great Connection Systems, circa 1450

**Unit 01 · Week 3, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can name the major world regions circa 1450, place
them on a map, and explain the three connection systems that tied Afro-Eurasia
together.

**Materials:** world wall map or printed blank world map, this lesson,
notebook/inquiry journal, colored pencils (3 colors).

**Readiness check (5 min):** On your blank map, label every continent you can
from memory. Then answer: *what is the difference between a continent and a
region?* (A continent is a large landmass; a region is an area people group
together because of shared geography, culture, politics, or economy — regions
are human choices, and different historians draw them differently.)

---

## Part 1 — The world circa 1450 in twelve regions (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Circa 1450 — about 575 years before this unit was written — no one had sailed
all the way around the world, and no regular contact existed between
Afro-Eurasia and the Americas. Yet Afro-Eurasia was one of the most connected
places in human history. Here are the major regions, west to east:

1. **Western Europe** — kingdoms recovering from the Hundred Years' War
   (ending 1453): England, France, the Iberian kingdoms (Castile, Aragon,
   Portugal), and the patchwork Holy Roman Empire. Mostly agricultural, with
   growing trade cities (Venice, Genoa) hungry for Asian goods.
2. **The Ottoman lands** — a rising Muslim empire in Anatolia and the
   Balkans. In 1450 it had *not yet* taken Constantinople (that happens in
   1453); its capital was Edirne. Sultan Murad II ruled; his son Mehmed II
   would soon change the map.
3. **Mamluk Egypt and Syria** — a sultanate ruled by a military elite, based
   in Cairo. It controlled the Red Sea route that carried Indian Ocean goods
   to the Mediterranean — and taxed them.
4. **Timurid Central Asia and Persia** — the empire of Timur (Tamerlane, died
   1405) had fragmented, but Samarkand remained a center of scholarship (its
   observatory was built in the 1420s). Caravan cities linked China to the
   Middle East.
5. **Delhi Sultanate (northern India)** — Muslim-ruled sultanate over a
   largely Hindu population; wealthy, urban, and plugged into both overland
   and ocean trade.
6. **Ming China** — under the Ming dynasty (founded 1368), with the capital
   moved to Beijing in 1421. Tens of millions of people (estimates vary widely,
   roughly 60–100 million), a civil-service bureaucracy chosen by
   examinations, and the world's most productive economy. A generation
   earlier, the admiral Zheng He had sailed huge state fleets across the
   Indian Ocean (1405–1433); by 1450 those voyages had stopped.
7. **Japan** — the Ashikaga shogunate (Muromachi period): a military
   government with a figurehead emperor, frequent regional warfare, and active
   trade with China and Korea.
8. **Southeast Asia** — a mosaic: the declining Majapahit on Java, Ayutthaya
   in Siam, Dai Viet, and the great port of Malacca (founded early 1400s),
   where the Indian Ocean and China Sea trades met.
9. **West Africa** — the Mali Empire past its peak (its golden age under
   Mansa Musa, c. 1312–1337, was a century earlier) and the Songhai state
   rising to the east. Timbuktu was a center of Islamic scholarship with
   libraries of manuscripts.
10. **East and Southern Africa** — Swahili coast city-states (Kilwa, Mombasa,
    Malindi): stone towns trading gold, ivory, and enslaved people for cloth,
    beads, porcelain, and silks across the Indian Ocean. Inland, Great
    Zimbabwe's stone-walled city was in decline but its trade networks
    persisted. Ethiopia, under Emperor Zar'a Ya'eqob (r. 1434–1468), was a
    Christian kingdom tied to Red Sea trade.
11. **The Mexica (Aztec) world** — in the Valley of Mexico, the Triple
    Alliance (formed 1428) of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan ruled an
    empire of several million people (estimates vary), organized around
    tribute, intensive chinampa agriculture, and a capital city larger than
    most European cities of the time.
12. **The Inca world** — in the Andes, the Inca state under Pachacuti (who
    began expanding the empire around 1438) was building Tawantinsuyu, soon to
    stretch over 4,000 km along the mountains, linked by ~40,000 km of roads
    (estimates vary) and administered through labor tribute and knotted-cord
    record-keeping (quipu).

Two more societies get full attention in Lesson 03: the Mississippian towns of
North America and the voyaging societies of the Pacific.

> **Note on dates:** "circa 1450" means *around* 1450. Some of the events
> above sit a decade or two on either side. Historians use round dates as
> anchors, then check what was actually true at that moment — that checking
> is part of this unit's work.

## Part 2 — The three connection systems (explicit teaching, 10 min)

Three systems moved goods, people, faiths, and ideas across Afro-Eurasia:

- **The Silk Roads (overland).** Not one road but a web of caravan routes
  across Central Asia linking China to the Mediterranean. Moved silk,
  porcelain, and paper east-to-west; horses, glass, and silver west-to-east —
  and Buddhism, Islam, printing, and gunpowder technology in both directions.
- **The Indian Ocean maritime network.** Sailed by dhows riding the
  **monsoon winds** — which blow one direction in summer and reverse in
  winter, so merchants timed voyages to the wind calendar. Linked East Africa,
  Arabia, India, Southeast Asia, and China. Moved pepper and spices, cotton
  textiles, porcelain, horses, and ivory through great port cities (Calicut,
  Aden, Hormuz, Malacca, Guangzhou).
- **The trans-Saharan routes.** Camel caravans crossed the Sahara linking
  West Africa to North Africa and the Mediterranean. The core exchange was
  West African **gold for Saharan and North African salt** (plus cloth,
  kola nuts, and books). Carried Islam deep into West Africa along with the
  traders and scholars.

What made each possible was physical geography used cleverly: steppe
grasslands feeding caravan animals, reversing winds, and camels' desert
endurance — plus human institutions (caravanserais, port laws, merchant
partnerships) that made long journeys survivable and profitable.

## Worked example A — Tracing one good: black pepper

**Question:** How did black pepper get from India to a table in Venice circa
1450?

**Reasoning (modeled):** Start at the origin: pepper vines grew on the
**Malabar Coast** of southwestern India. Local growers sold dried peppercorns
to merchants in **Calicut**, a great Indian Ocean port. There, Gujarati and
Arab merchants loaded it onto dhows sailing west with the winter monsoon to
**Aden** (southern Arabia). From Aden, smaller ships carried it up the Red
Sea to **Suez/Jeddah**, then camel caravans hauled it overland to
**Alexandria** or across to the Mediterranean. In Alexandria, **Venetian**
merchants bought it, shipped it across the Mediterranean to **Venice**, and
sold it to European distributors. At every handoff the price rose — pepper
that cost little in Calicut sold for many times more in Venice, which is why
merchants endured the risks.

**Check:** the same method works for any good — origin → ports/caravan
cities → handoffs → final market. Try it yourself below.

## Worked example B — Comparing political organization

**Question:** Compare how Ming China and the Mexica Triple Alliance were
ruled circa 1450.

**Reasoning (modeled):** Use one consistent frame — *who rules, how power
passes, how the economy is organized*:

| Frame | Ming China | Mexica Triple Alliance |
|---|---|---|
| Who rules | An emperor, advised by scholar-officials chosen through civil-service examinations | A huey tlatoani (great speaker) of Tenochtitlan, allied with Texcoco and Tlacopan |
| How power passes | Hereditary dynasty (Ming family), legitimized by bureaucracy and ritual | Hereditary rulers within allied city-states, legitimized by military success and tribute |
| Economy | Taxation in grain/silver, state-managed granaries, huge internal markets | Tribute in goods and labor from subject cities, intensive chinampa farming, great markets (Tlatelolco) |

**Conclusion:** both were large, complex states — but one ran on bureaucracy
and taxation, the other on alliance and tribute. "Advanced" is not one shape.

**Common error to avoid:** describing one system as "more developed" because
it resembles a modern state. Judge each by how it solved its own problems:
feeding millions, keeping order, and managing distance.

   Calicut, Malacca, Guangzhou, Timbuktu, Kilwa. *(Hint: use the region list
   above to place each node in its region.)*

   Chinese porcelain to Cairo; West African gold to Morocco; Central Asian
   horses to Delhi.

   West Africa** using the same three-row frame as Worked Example B.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners often equate "empire" with "European-style state." The Mexica and
  Inca comparisons exist to break that reflex; watch for language like
  "primitive" applied to tribute systems.
- Learners may treat the Silk Roads as a single paved highway. Reinforce
  "web of routes" with the map shading exercise.
- Population figures are estimates with wide ranges — if a learner asks for
  "the" number, that is the teaching moment: historians argue from
  incomplete evidence.
