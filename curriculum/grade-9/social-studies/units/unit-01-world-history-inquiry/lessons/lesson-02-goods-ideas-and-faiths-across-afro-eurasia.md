## Part 1 — More than merchandise (explicit teaching, 12 min)

The same routes that carried pepper and silk carried everything people
carry in their heads and habits:

- **Faiths.** Buddhism had traveled the Silk Roads to China centuries
  earlier. Islam spread along *both* the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean
  networks — carried by merchants, scholars, and Sufi teachers rather than
  armies — reaching West Africa, the Swahili coast, and eventually Indonesia.
  Christian communities (Nestorian and others) dotted Central Asia and
  China, carried east by missionaries and traders.
- **Technologies.** Papermaking moved west from China along the Silk Roads;
  gunpowder knowledge traveled westward too, transforming warfare by the
  1300s. The sternpost rudder, the compass, and lateen sails spread among
  shipbuilders around the Indian Ocean and Mediterranean. Crop transfers
  (citrus, sugar, cotton varieties) reshaped farming.
- **Knowledge.** Astronomy, mathematics (including the numerals we call
  "Arabic," which reached Europe via Muslim scholars), and medical texts
  circulated through translation centers like Baghdad's earlier House of
  Wisdom and the madrasas of Timbuktu.
- **Disease.** Connection has costs. The Black Death (bubonic plague) raced
  along these same networks in the 1340s, killing a huge share of
  Afro-Eurasia's population — possibly a third or more in the hardest-hit
  regions, though exact figures are debated. Trade routes do not choose what
  they carry.

**The key pattern:** networks are *content-neutral*. The monsoon winds that
carried pepper also carried pilgrims; the caravans that carried salt also
carried books. When you see a connection, always ask: *what else traveled
with it, wanted and unwanted?*

## Part 2 — Reading a traveler: Ibn Battuta (modeled source work, 13 min)

**The source:** Abu Abdullah Muhammad ibn Battuta (1304–1368/69), a
scholar from Tangier (in present-day Morocco), left home in 1325 and
traveled for about thirty years — across North Africa, the Middle East,
India (where he served as a judge under the Delhi sultan), Southeast Asia,
China, and back across the Sahara to Mali. He later dictated his account,
the *Rihla* ("journey"), to a scribe. Historians estimate he covered on the
order of 120,000 km — farther than any other pre-modern traveler we know
by name.

> **About the passages below:** they are original modern retellings of
> episodes from the Rihla, written for this unit — not translations. They
> keep the episodes historians cite most; the wording is ours.

**Modeled passage 1 — Delhi (adapted):** *"The sultan received me with
honor and appointed me judge of Delhi, with a generous salary paid in
grain and coin. I found the city's markets overflowing — silk from China,
horses from the steppe, pepper from the southern coast — and its scholars
arguing law late into the night. Yet the sultan's moods were sudden, and
men who displeased him vanished from court. Favor here was a high,
narrow bridge."*

**Modeled passage 2 — The Maldives (adapted):** *"On these low islands the
people live by the sea: they dry fish for trade, weave coir rope from
coconut fiber, and sell cowrie shells that serve as money as far away as
Mali. Their mosques are built of coral stone. I married into a judge's
family here and stayed eighteen months — the islands were orderly, and
their trade reached every shore of the ocean."*

**How a historian reads these (modeled reasoning):**

1. **Kind:** primary source — a travel narrative dictated by the traveler
   himself. (D3: gather and evaluate.)
2. **Maker/audience:** an educated Muslim judge writing for a courtly
   audience back home; he notices mosques, judges, markets — his own
   world's institutions.
3. **What it shows:** the *density* of connection — Chinese silk in Delhi,
   Maldivian shells used as currency in Mali. One man's life proves the
   network was real and human.
4. **Limits (D2.His.10):** one elite man's view; he exaggerates, moralizes,
   and barely mentions the laborers, sailors, and enslaved people who made
   his journeys possible. A traveler's account shows the roads, not everyone
   on them.

**Contrast — Zheng He:** a generation earlier (1405–1433), the Ming admiral
Zheng He led seven state-sponsored treasure fleets across the Indian Ocean
with hundreds of ships — diplomacy and display, not private trade. Then the
voyages stopped, by imperial decision. Two models of connection: the
**merchant network** (Ibn Battuta's world — private, persistent, many small
actors) and the **state expedition** (Zheng He — spectacular, fragile,
dependent on one ruler's will). The merchant network outlasted the fleets.

## Worked example A — Cause and effect with the monsoon calendar

**Question:** Why did Indian Ocean merchants spend months waiting in port
cities?

**Reasoning (modeled):** The monsoon winds reverse seasonally — roughly,
summer winds blow toward South Asia and winter winds blow toward Africa and
Arabia. A merchant sailing from Arabia to India would depart with one wind
and *must wait months* for the reversal to sail home. Therefore port cities
like Calicut, Aden, and Malacca developed warehouses, moneylenders, lodging,
and markets for waiting crews — the wind calendar *caused* the port city's
whole service economy. **Effect chain:** reversing winds → fixed sailing
seasons → long layovers → demand for storage, credit, food, worship →
cosmopolitan port cities with mosques, temples, and churches side by side.

**Error to avoid:** saying "the winds caused trade." The winds made *timing*
predictable; human demand for goods made trade happen. Physical geography
enables; people decide.

## Worked example B — Classifying a source

**Question:** A historian finds a 1450s Venetian merchant's account book
listing pepper purchases in Alexandria. Classify it and state one thing it
can and cannot show.

**Reasoning (modeled):** Kind: primary source (a business record).
Maker/audience: a Venetian merchant, written for himself/partners — not for
posterity, which makes it honest about prices. **Can show:** what one
merchant paid, when, and in what quantities — hard economic data.
**Cannot show:** the whole trade (one merchant's book is one thread of the
web); the labor behind the pepper; or *why* prices moved. For causes, you
need more sources — which is exactly what D2.His.12 asks you to do: let one
source's limits send you looking for the next.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners may romanticize Ibn Battuta as an objective reporter. Press the
  limits analysis: elite, male, Muslim-judge lens; dictated years later.
- "Islam spread by trade" needs nuance: traders carried it, but local
  rulers' conversions and Sufi teachers mattered too — avoid single-cause
  stories.
- The AI port image is seductive; enforce the (c) question so learners
  practice *not* treating illustrations as evidence.
