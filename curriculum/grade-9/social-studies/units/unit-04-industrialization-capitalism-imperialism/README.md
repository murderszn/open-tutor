# Unit 04 — Industrialization, Capitalism, Imperialism, and Social Change

**Grade 9 · Social Studies · Modern world history · Weeks 15–18 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-11-02 for issue #45; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Between about 1760 and 1914, the world reorganized itself around a new way
of making things — and a new way of taking them. This unit follows that
reorganization in four moves. First, why industrialization began in
Britain: coal, steam, textiles, and iron, and the factory system that
turned workers' time into a commodity. Second, what industrial capitalism
did to ordinary life: factory discipline, fast-growing cities, child
labor, and the first laws and unions that pushed back. Third, how
industrial demand helped drive the "new imperialism": European powers
carved up Africa and tightened control in Asia, writing rules for empire
at the Berlin Conference (1884–85) without inviting a single African
representative. Fourth, how people answered — with resistance,
nationalism, labor movements, women's suffrage campaigns, and competing
visions of what an economy is for.

The unit's inquiry question runs through all four weeks: **who paid for
industrial progress, who profited from it, and who got to decide?**

A note on sources: learner readings are original passages written for
this unit or short public-domain excerpts and paraphrases of documents
such as the Berlin Conference's General Act (1885). Secondary claims are
summarized and linked, never copied. Where historians debate a figure —
for example, exact colonial death tolls or the size of a fortune — the
unit teaches the range or the mechanism, not a single disputed number.
The Congo Free State's rubber regime is taught through the quota system,
the Force Publique, and the 1904 Casement Report's findings, without
graphic detail.

**Sensitive content (adult preview required):** this unit covers child
labor, harsh factory discipline, colonial violence (including the Congo
rubber regime), and famine under colonial policy — documentary and
textual sources only, no graphic imagery. The adult previews every Week
17 source, gives advance notice before the Congo sessions, frames
discussion around evidence and human dignity, and offers an
observation/reflection alternative for any activity the learner finds
overwhelming. See [teacher-guide.md](teacher-guide.md) §8 and the track
[scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md) §6–§7.

## Prerequisites

From U01–U03 and the course diagnostic: place centuries and decades on a
timeline, classify sources by kind, infer maker/date/audience/purpose,
compare two accounts of the same event, and write a claim supported by
evidence. The learner can already explain the rights-and-exclusion frame
from U03: this unit asks who the new industrial and imperial order
included, excluded, and exploited. The adult confirms the learner can
sustain ~20-minute document sessions and previews all Week 17 primary
sources. No cross-grade references: these are described skills, not links.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain why industrialization began in Britain and how it changed
   production** — name the coal, steam, textile, and iron developments;
   explain the factory system, the division of labor, and why Britain's
   combination of cheap coal, high wages, capital, and markets made
   machines profitable; and read industrial production data accurately.
   (D2.His.14.9-12; D2.Eco.3.9-12)
2. **Analyze industrial capitalism's effects on labor, cities, and
   daily life** — describe factory discipline, urban growth, and child
   labor from at least two perspectives (worker, owner, inspector,
   reformer); explain the Factory Acts of 1833 and 1847 and the rise of
   unions as responses with limits. (D2.His.2.9-12; D2.Eco.1.9-12)
3. **Connect industrial capitalism to the new imperialism** — explain
   economic, strategic, and ideological motives for late-19th-century
   empire; explain the Berlin Conference's General Act (1885), the
   effective-occupation rule, and the partition of Africa from roughly
   10% European control (c. 1870) to roughly 90% (by 1914); and describe
   one colony's experience under two empires. (D2.His.14.9-12;
   D2.Eco.8.9-12)
4. **Compare resistance, reform, and competing economic visions** —
   compare anti-colonial resistance, labor organization, women's
   suffrage campaigns, and nationalist movements; and distinguish
   liberal, socialist, and nationalist critiques of industrial empire.
   (D2.His.4.9-12; D2.Civ.14.9-12)
5. **Build a source-based argument about imperialism's causes** — weigh
   economic against political explanations using at least three sources
   of different kinds, acknowledge a counterclaim, and state what the
   sources cannot show. (D2.His.16.9-12; D3.1–D3.4.9-12; D4.1–D4.4.9-12)

**Source notes (checked at authoring, 2026-11-02):**

- Invention dates used: coke-fired blast furnace at Coalbrookdale,
  Abraham Darby, 1709; spinning jenny, James Hargreaves, 1764; water
  frame, Richard Arkwright, 1769; Watt's improved steam engine patented
  1769, in commercial use from 1776 (Boulton & Watt); power loom,
  Edmund Cartwright, 1785; puddling process, Henry Cort, 1784; Bessemer
  process for mass steel, 1856; Stockton & Darlington Railway, 1825;
  Liverpool & Manchester Railway, 1830.
- Pig-iron figures (British output, approximate, standard historical
  tables): ~17,000 tons in 1740; ~70,000 tons in 1788; ~250,000 tons in
  1805; ~650,000 tons in 1830; ~2.7 million tons in 1852. Coal output:
  just under 3 million tons in 1700; ~16 million tons in 1815; over 30
  million tons by 1830. Figures are rounded and vary slightly by
  source; the unit teaches the scale of growth, not false precision.
- Factory Acts: 1833 Act — no factory work under age 9 in textiles;
  ages 9–13 limited to 48 hours a week (8 hours a day) plus 2 hours of
  schooling a day; ages 13–18 limited to 12 hours a day; no night work
  under 18; a four-man inspectorate for roughly 4,000 mills. 1847 Ten
  Hours Act — 10-hour day for women and young persons in textile mills.
  Enforcement was weak and evasion common; the unit teaches the Acts
  as a beginning, not a solution.
- Berlin Conference: November 15, 1884 – February 26, 1885, convened by
  German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck; 14 nations attended (13 European
  powers plus the United States); no African leader was invited. The
  General Act (February 26, 1885) guaranteed free trade and free
  navigation on the Congo and Niger, and set the "effective
  occupation" rule for future coastal claims. European control of
  Africa rose from roughly 10% (c. 1870) to roughly 90% by 1914; Ethiopia
  and Liberia remained independent. Congo Free State: Leopold II's
  personal rule, 1885–1908, then annexed as the Belgian Congo.
- Other dates used: Luddite machine-breaking 1811–16; Chartist
  petitions 1838–48; *Communist Manifesto* (Marx and Engels), 1848;
  Indian Rebellion, 1857; Suez Canal opened 1869; Meiji Restoration,
  1868; First Sino-Japanese War, 1894–95; Battle of Adwa (Ethiopian
  victory over Italy), 1896; Boxer Rebellion, 1899–1901; Casement Report
  on the Congo, 1904. The short Marx quotation in Lesson 04 ("The
  history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class
  struggles") is from the public-domain 1848 *Manifesto*.
- The "factory inspector's report" and "mill owner's defense" in
  Lesson 02, and the "administrator's report" and "colonized
  intellectual's response" in Lesson 04, are **original model passages
  written for this unit**, labeled as such — they illustrate the kinds
  of arguments real inspectors, owners, administrators, and critics
  made, and must not be cited as real documents. Naoroji's "drain
  theory" and Hobson's *Imperialism* (1902) are summarized, not quoted.
