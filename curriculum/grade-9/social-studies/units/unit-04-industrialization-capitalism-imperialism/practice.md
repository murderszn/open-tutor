## Practice Set A — Week 15: Why Britain first

**A1. Pig-iron data table** (the text alternative to
`assets/british-pig-iron-production.svg`; figures are rounded
historical approximations — see the unit README source notes):

| Year | British pig-iron output (tons) |
|---|---|
| 1740 | 17,000 |
| 1788 | 70,000 |
| 1805 | 250,000 |
| 1830 | 650,000 |
| 1852 | 2,700,000 |

1. By what factor did output grow from 1740 to 1852? Show your division.
2. How many tons were added between 1805 and 1852? Between 1740 and
   1805?
3. In which interval was the *absolute* increase largest? Compare the
   factor increase for 1740–1788 with that for 1830–1852: the factors
   are nearly the same (about 4.1 vs. about 4.2) — what does it show
   that a similar factor can mean very different absolute changes?
4. Coal output was just under 3 million tons in 1700, about 16 million
   in 1815, and over 30 million by 1830. What does the coal series
   add to the iron story?

## Practice Set B — Week 16: Factory labor, cities, daily life

**B1. Image analysis.** Use the mill-town reconstruction
(`assets/industrial-mill-town-1840-reconstruction.png`) or its text
alternative in Lesson 02.

1. List five features visible in the image (or stated in the text
   alternative).
2. For each feature, write one inference it supports and one claim it
   does *not* prove.
3. Which feature best shows that the town was planned around industry?
   Defend your choice in two sentences.

**B2. Factory Act matching.** Match each provision to the abuse it
targeted. (Provisions: no work under 9; ages 9–13 max 48 hours/week;
2 hours of schooling a day for under-13s; no night work under 18;
four inspectors for ~4,000 mills.)

1. Children working 14-hour days from age 7.
2. Children who could neither read nor write after years in a mill.
3. Children working through the night on dangerous frames.
4. Laws that existed on paper but were ignored in most mills.

**B3. Two perspectives.** Read model passages A (inspector) and B
(owner) in Lesson 02. For each passage, answer: (a) What checkable
details does it give? (b) What does it claim that cannot be checked
from the passage alone? (c) Whose interest does its proposed solution
serve?

**B4. Argument workshop (Week 16, S4).** Write a short argument
answering: *Did industrialization improve ordinary lives?* Requirements:
a clear claim, at least two pieces of evidence, one counterclaim you
answer, and a time limit on your claim (e.g., "by 1850" vs. "by 1870").

## Practice Set C — Week 17: The new imperialism

**C1. General Act close read.** The provisions below are paraphrases
of the Berlin Conference's General Act (February 26, 1885). For each,
state in your own words what it required, and who benefited most.

1. Trade and navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers free to all
   nations.
2. The Congo basin recognized as the Congo Free State under Leopold
   II's personal sovereignty.
3. Future coastal claims require notification of other powers and
   effective occupation.

**C2. Partition arithmetic.** European control of Africa: roughly 10%
c. 1870, roughly 90% by 1914.

1. How many percentage points did control rise? Over about how many
   years?
2. Why is "control" a slippery word here? Name two things it might
   hide (e.g., resistance, thin administration inland).
3. Ethiopia and Liberia stayed independent. What does their exception
   prove about the claim that conquest was inevitable?

**C3. Demand-to-policy chain.** Build one chain of at least four
arrows, modeled on Lesson 03's cotton → Egypt example, using a
different commodity or route (rubber, palm oil, copper, the Suez
route, or a coaling station). After each arrow, write one test
question that could weaken it.

**C4. Cause/effect writing (Week 17, S4).** Write one paragraph
tracing one industrial demand to one imperial policy. Requirements:
name the demand, the policy, and the mechanism linking them; include
one non-economic motive that also mattered; end by stating what your
chain does *not* prove.
