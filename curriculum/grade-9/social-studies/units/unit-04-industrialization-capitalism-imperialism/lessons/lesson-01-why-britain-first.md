# Lesson 01 — Why Britain First: Coal, Steam, Textiles, and Iron

**Unit 04 · Week 15, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain four reasons industrialization
began in Britain, describe how coal, steam, textiles, and iron fit
together, and explain how a factory differs from cottage industry.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal, the unit's
pig-iron chart (`../assets/british-pig-iron-production.svg`) or its data
table in Practice Set A.

**Readiness check (5 min):** In U02 you saw empires move goods across
oceans — spices, silver, sugar, enslaved people. Write one sentence
answering: *before machines, what limited how much cloth or iron one
worker could make in a day?* (Answer: the worker's own hands and
strength, plus daylight and water or wind power. Machines broke that
limit — that is the whole story of this unit.)

## Part 1 — Four advantages that met in one island (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Industrialization did not begin in Britain because British people were
smarter or worked harder. It began there because several advantages met
in one place at one time, and each one made the others pay off.

**1. Cheap coal, close to iron.** By 1700 Britain already mined more coal
than the rest of Europe combined — just under 3 million tons a year.
Coal was cheap because it lay near the surface, near rivers and the
sea, and near iron ore. In 1709 Abraham Darby showed at Coalbrookdale
that coke — coal baked to drive off impurities — could smelt iron in a
blast furnace. Suddenly iron-making no longer depended on charcoal from
forests, which were running out. Fuel and metal sat side by side.

**2. High wages that made machines profitable.** British workers were
paid more than workers in most of Europe or Asia. That sounds like a
disadvantage — until you see it from an inventor's side. If labor is
expensive, a machine that replaces ten workers pays for itself quickly.
If labor is cheap, why bother building the machine? Historian Robert
Allen's argument, which this unit follows, is that Britain's
high-wage, cheap-energy combination is exactly what made inventing
machines the profitable thing to do.

**3. Capital, markets, and a government that protected property.**
Centuries of overseas trade — including the profits of the slave trade
and of colonial commerce taught in U02 — had put money in the hands of
merchants and landowners willing to invest it. Britain's growing
population bought cloth; its colonies bought cloth too and supplied raw
cotton, much of it grown by enslaved labor in the Americas. Parliament
protected property rights and enforced contracts, so an investor could
risk money on a mill and expect to keep the returns.

**4. An agricultural revolution that freed hands.** Better crop rotation,
enclosure of common fields, and new tools meant fewer farm workers could
feed more people. Villagers pushed off the land moved to towns looking
for wages. Factories did not have to go find workers; workers came to
them.

None of these four alone explains industrialization. Coal without
capital stays in the ground; machines without markets gather dust. The
unit's first thinking move is always: *look for the combination, not the
single cause.*

**Worked example 1 — testing a single-cause claim:** A textbook says,
"Britain industrialized because it had coal." Test it: France and
Germany also had coal, yet industrialized decades later. Coal was
necessary but not sufficient. A complete answer adds: coal *plus*
expensive labor (motive to mechanize), capital and markets (ability to
invest and sell), and a mobile workforce (hands to hire). When you
answer "why here first?" questions, name at least three interacting
causes, or your answer is incomplete.

**Worked example 2 — the cotton chain:** Follow one product. Raw cotton
arrives from plantations worked by enslaved people. A spinning jenny
(Hargreaves, 1764) lets one worker spin many threads at once; a water
frame (Arkwright, 1769) uses water power; a power loom (Cartwright,
1785) weaves by machine. Each invention creates a bottleneck somewhere
else — more thread than weavers can use, then more cloth than spinners
can supply — and each bottleneck invites the next invention. This is
why the unit calls industrialization a *system*, not a list of gadgets:
each part pulled the others forward.

## Part 2 — Steam, iron, and the factory system (explicit teaching, 15 min)

**Steam untied power from place.** Thomas Newcomen's engine (1712)
pumped water from mines but wasted fuel. James Watt's improved engine,
patented 1769 and sold by Boulton & Watt from 1776, used far less coal
and could drive rotary motion — it could turn wheels, not just pump.
Now a mill did not need a fast river; it needed coal, which could be
carried anywhere by canal and, after 1825–30, by railway. Steam also
drove the blast furnaces and rolling mills that made iron cheap, and
cheap iron made more steam engines, rails, and machines. Each loop
tightened the next.

**Iron made the machine age physical.** Henry Cort's puddling process
(1784) turned brittle pig iron into workable wrought iron; the Bessemer
process (1856) later made steel cheap in bulk. The numbers tell the
scale: British pig-iron output rose from about 17,000 tons in 1740 to
about 250,000 tons in 1805, about 650,000 tons in 1830, and about 2.7
million tons in 1852 — roughly 160 times the 1740 figure. Railways then
multiplied demand again: the Stockton & Darlington line opened in 1825,
the Liverpool & Manchester in 1830, and every mile of track ate coal,
iron, and steel.

**The factory system reorganized work itself.** In cottage industry, a
family worked at home, set its own hours, and owned or rented its tools.
In a factory, the owner owned the machines, the worker sold *time*,
and the clock ruled: shifts began at a bell, fines punished lateness,
and each worker performed one small step — the division of labor —
repeated all day. Output per worker soared. So did the employer's
control over the worker's day. That bargain — higher output, less
freedom — is the argument of Lesson 02.

**Worked example 3 — reading the pig-iron chart:** From 17,000 tons
(1740) to 2,700,000 tons (1852). Divide: 2,700,000 ÷ 17,000 ≈ 159. So
output grew about 160-fold. Now ask *when* the growth was fastest: from
1740 to 1805 it grew about 15-fold; from 1805 to 1852 about 11-fold — but
in absolute tons, the later period added far more (about 2.45 million
tons vs. about 233,000). A good data answer gives both the factor *and*
the absolute change, and says which question each answers: the factor
shows the *rate* of transformation; the absolute tons show the *scale*
of the new world.

**Worked example 4 — cottage vs. factory:** A weaver at home in 1750
decides when to start, stops to help with harvest, and is paid per
piece. A weaver in a mill in 1820 arrives at 6 a.m. to the bell, tends
two power looms for 12–14 hours, and is fined for talking. Same skill,
different system. The difference is not the loom — it is who owns the
machine, who sets the hours, and who keeps the profit. That is what
"industrial capitalism" means in this unit: private owners, wage labor,
and production for sale in markets.

## Guided practice (10 min)

With the adult or a partner, answer aloud or in notes:

1. Name Britain's four advantages from Part 1, and for each, say what
   would have been missing without it.
2. Why did high wages *help* mechanization? (Say it in one sentence
   using the word "profitable.")
3. Use the pig-iron figures: how many tons were added between 1830 and
   1852? (2,700,000 − 650,000 = 2,050,000 tons.)
4. A classmate says, "Watt invented the steam engine." Correct the
   claim precisely: what did Watt improve, and why did it matter?

## Exit check (3 min)

In your own words, without looking: what is the factory system, and why
did it need steam? (Answer sketch: owners' machines + wage workers +
clock discipline; steam freed mills from rivers so factories could sit
near coal, labor, and markets.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult pre-teaches *coke, smelt,
  division of labor, capital* before the lesson; the pig-iron data table
  in Practice Set A is the text alternative to the chart.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may answer the applied task
  orally and have the adult scribe it, then read it back for correction.
- **Extension:** research one invention's inventor as a person — for
  example, Arkwright the barber-turned-mill-owner — and explain what his
  career shows about who could become a capitalist in this period.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- "Inventions caused the Industrial Revolution by themselves." Correct
  to: inventions were *adopted* because wages, energy prices, capital,
  and markets made them pay. Invention without adoption changes nothing.
- "Workers all got richer immediately." Real wages rose only slowly and
  unevenly; Lesson 02 handles this with evidence.
- "Steam engines were invented by Watt." Newcomen (1712) came first;
  Watt made the engine efficient and rotary — the improvement that
  mattered for factories.
