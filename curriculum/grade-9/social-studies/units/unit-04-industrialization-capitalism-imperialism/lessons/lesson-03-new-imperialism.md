# Lesson 03 — The New Imperialism: Motives, Berlin, and Partition

**Unit 04 · Week 17, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain four motives for
late-19th-century empire, describe what the Berlin Conference decided,
and explain how Africa went from roughly 10% to roughly 90% European
control in about forty years.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal, a blank outline
map of Africa (the adult prints one; no borders are pre-drawn).

## Part 1 — Four motives, one scramble (explicit teaching, 15 min)

The "new imperialism" (roughly 1870–1914) differs from the older
colonialism of U02 in speed and scale. Earlier empires held coastal
forts and trading posts; the new empires claimed whole interiors, drew
borders on maps in Europe, and governed millions directly. Four motives
combined, and historians still argue about their weights — that
argument is the unit's Week 18 deliberation.

**1. Economic: raw materials and markets.** Industrial economies
needed cotton, rubber, palm oil, copper, tin, and later oil; their
factories needed buyers beyond saturated home markets. A colony could
supply the first and be forced to buy the second — sometimes by law,
as when colonial tariffs favored the ruling country's goods. But note
the check on this motive: most colonies were poor markets, and many
never paid for their own conquest. Economics explains *why empires
wanted in*; it does not by itself explain *why they rushed in all at
once*.

**2. Strategic: coaling stations, canals, and rivals.** Steamships
needed coal depots along their routes; the Suez Canal (opened 1869)
made the route to India and Asia shorter and the states along it more
valuable — Britain occupied Egypt in 1882, nine years before the canal
was even fully under its control. Once one power grabbed a port, rivals
feared being shut out, so they grabbed the next port. Fear multiplied
claims: the scramble fed itself.

**3. Ideological: nationalism, racism, and "civilizing missions."**
Newspapers and politicians sold empire as national greatness; racial
theories — now discredited, then respectable in European salons —
ranked peoples and claimed Europeans had a duty to rule "lesser"
peoples. Missionaries, some sincere, often paved the way for traders
and soldiers. Ideology did not create the economic incentives, but it
made voters and soldiers accept costs they might otherwise have
refused.

**4. The men on the spot.** Traders, explorers, missionaries, and
officers far from home made treaties, raised flags, and started wars
that their governments then felt obliged to back. King Leopold II of
Belgium — who never visited Africa — built a private empire in the
Congo through agents and treaties whose signers often could not read
them. Empire was not only ordered from capitals; it was improvised at
the edges and ratified later.

**Worked example 1 — weighing motives in one case:** Why did Britain
occupy Egypt in 1882? Economic: the Suez Canal carried trade to India.
Strategic: a rival (France, which had built the canal) must not control
it. Ideological: newspapers framed it as restoring order. A
single-motive answer ("for the canal") is incomplete — the canal was
both an economic asset and a strategic chokepoint, and the occupation
was sold at home as a rescue mission. Good historical answers stack
motives and say which one triggered the decision.

**Worked example 2 — the "10% to 90%" claim:** Around 1870, Europeans
controlled roughly 10% of Africa, mostly coastal enclaves. By 1914 they
controlled roughly 90%; only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.
That is a change of about 80 percentage points in about 45 years —
faster than any earlier colonial expansion. When you meet a dramatic
number, do two checks: *who counted what?* (here, "control" means
claimed and administered territory, not settled population) and *what
does it leave out?* (it leaves out the resistance that slowed, shaped,
and sometimes reversed the advance — Lesson 04).

## Part 2 — Berlin writes the rules (explicit teaching, 15 min)

**The conference.** From November 15, 1884 to February 26, 1885,
representatives of 14 nations — 13 European powers plus the United
States — met in Berlin at Chancellor Otto von Bismarck's invitation.
No African leader was invited. The meeting was called mainly to settle
rival claims in the Congo basin before they caused a European war.

**The General Act (February 26, 1885).** Its key provisions, in
paraphrase (the full text is public domain; see the resource pack):

- Trade and navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers were to be free
  to all nations.
- The Congo basin was recognized as the Congo Free State under
  Leopold II's personal sovereignty — a colony owned by one man, not
  by Belgium.
- For *future* coastal claims, a power had to notify the others and
  show **effective occupation** — real administration on the ground,
  not just a flag planted on a beach.

That last rule is the one that mattered most, and it backfired as a
peace measure: to claim territory, powers now had to occupy it
*quickly*, with soldiers and administrators, before a rival did. The
rule meant to prevent war accelerated the scramble instead — an
unintended consequence worth remembering whenever a rule promises to
tame competition.

**What partition did to African societies.** Borders drawn in Europe
cut across existing peoples and kingdoms — the same language group
might end up in three colonies, and rival groups might be forced into
one. Colonial rule then taxed people in cash, which forced farmers
into wage labor or cash crops; it seized land for settlers and mines;
and it replaced or subordinated existing authorities. The Congo Free
State became the extreme case: rubber quotas enforced by the Force
Publique, hostage-taking, and mutilation as punishment, exposed by the
British consul Roger Casement's 1904 report and by missionaries and
journalists. International outrage eventually forced Leopold to hand
the Congo to Belgium in 1908 — but Belgian rule kept forced labor and
the extraction economy. Resistance and reform changed the ruler; they
did not end the system.

**The pattern beyond Africa.** The same decades saw European and
American pressure on China (spheres of influence after the Opium Wars
of 1839–42 and 1856–60; the Boxer Rebellion of 1899–1901 against foreign
privilege), the partition of Southeast Asia, and Japan's answer: the
Meiji Restoration of 1868, which industrialized Japan on its own terms
and defeated China in 1894–95 and Russia in 1904–05. Japan is the
unit's control case: industrialization without colonization was
possible, but it required a state strong enough to direct it — and
Japan then built its own empire in Korea and Taiwan.

**Worked example 3 — reading the General Act like a historian:**
Article language about "free trade" and "the moral and material
well-being of the native populations" sounds generous. Ask: *who
enforces it?* The Act created no inspectorate, no court, and no
African voice. Language without enforcement is a promise without a
mechanism — compare the 1833 Factory Act's four inspectors (Lesson
02), which at least existed. When you read a treaty, always find the
enforcement clause; its absence is itself evidence.

**Worked example 4 — from demand to policy:** Trace one chain.
British textile mills need cheap cotton → cotton must come from
outside Britain → Egypt grows cotton under British influence → when
Egypt's ruler defaults on loans and nationalists threaten the canal,
Britain occupies Egypt (1882) → the occupation secures cotton and the
canal, and is justified at home as restoring order. Each arrow is a
claim you can test: was cotton really a motive? Did the occupation
actually secure it? A chain is only as strong as its weakest arrow —
find it before you trust the chain.

## Guided practice (10 min)

With the adult or a partner, answer aloud or in notes:

1. Name the four motives and give one concrete example of each.
2. Why did "effective occupation" speed up the scramble instead of
   slowing it?
3. Who was *not* at Berlin, and why does that absence matter for the
   Act's legitimacy?
4. Compare the Congo Free State (1885–1908) with British India: one
   was a personal possession, the other a state-run empire. Did that
   difference protect ordinary people in either case? (No — both used
   forced labor and extraction; the owner differed, the mechanism
   rhymed.)

unintentionally cause? (Answer: a claim required real administration;
it caused a race to occupy territory quickly — the scramble.)

  sphere of influence, chokepoint, quota* before the lesson; the General

  reference only.
- **Alternate response mode:** the map task may be done as a labeled
  list (region → ruling power) instead of a shaded map.
- **Extension:** research Menelik II of Ethiopia: how did Adwa (1896)
  happen, and why did Ethiopia's combination — imported rifles, a
  united army, and diplomacy — succeed where others failed?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- "The Berlin Conference divided Africa like a cake in one meeting."
  It set rules; the actual partition took three decades of treaties,
  wars, and resistance. The conference is a starting gun, not the race.
- "Colonies were profitable for the ruling country." Often they were
  not, once conquest and administration costs are counted; prestige,
  strategy, and private profits (for companies and concessionaires)
  frequently mattered more than the treasury's balance sheet.
- "Africans did not resist." They resisted constantly — Lesson 04 is
  built on that correction.
