# Lesson 04 — Resistance, Reform, and Competing Visions

**Unit 04 · Week 18, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can compare anti-colonial resistance, labor
organization, women's suffrage, and nationalist movements, and explain
how liberal, socialist, and nationalist thinkers disagreed about what
industrial empire was and what should replace it.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal.

**Readiness check (5 min):** Lesson 03 ended with a system — empire —
that claimed to be inevitable. Write one sentence answering: *if a
system claims to be inevitable, what is the most powerful thing its
opponents can do?* (Answer: show that it was chosen, and can be
unchosen — by resisting, by organizing, or by imagining an alternative.
That is this lesson.)

## Part 1 — Resistance was constant (explicit teaching, 15 min)

**Armed resistance.** African, Asian, and Indigenous peoples fought
colonial conquest from the first landing to the last — and kept
fighting under colonial rule. Samori Touré built a West African empire
that resisted France for nearly two decades before his capture in 1898.
Ethiopia under Menelik II defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896, the rare
outright battlefield victory of an African state over a European army,
won with imported rifles, a large united force, and careful diplomacy
that kept Italy isolated. In Sudan, the Mahdist state held off Britain
and Egypt from 1881 until 1898. In India, the 1857 Rebellion — called
by the British a "mutiny," by many Indians the First War of
Independence — nearly ended British rule and led instead to direct
Crown rule. In China, the Boxers (1899–1901) attacked foreign privilege
and missionaries; the foreign powers crushed them and imposed a huge
indemnity. None of these movements "failed" in the simple sense:
each forced the empire to spend more, concede something, or change
its methods.

**Everyday resistance and cultural survival.** Not all resistance
carried rifles. Farmers evaded taxes by growing food instead of cash
crops; workers slowed down, deserted, or broke tools; religious and
language communities kept schools and worship alive under bans;
newspapers in colonized cities — in Lagos, Bombay, Cairo — argued for
rights in the empire's own legal language. The unit's rule: where you
find a colonial order, look for the refusal inside it; empires that
looked total from the capital looked negotiated, evaded, and contested
from the village.

**Reform movements at home.** In Britain and Europe, the same decades
produced labor organization (unions, strikes, and finally labor parties),
democratic reform (the vote extended to working men in stages; women
organized for suffrage — in Britain, the Pankhursts' Women's Social and
Political Union, founded 1903, used militant tactics after decades of
peaceful petitioning), and nationalist movements (in Ireland, Poland,
Italy, Germany, and the Balkans, peoples demanded states of their own).
These movements shared a discovery: the industrial order could be
changed, because it had been made by human decisions — and decisions
can be remade.

**Worked example 1 — why Adwa matters beyond Ethiopia:** Adwa did not
end the scramble. But it proved a European army could be beaten by an
African state that combined modern weapons, unity, and diplomacy. Its
lesson for this unit: outcomes were not decided by technology alone.
Organization and politics decided whether technology helped the
invader or the defender. Any explanation of conquest that mentions
only guns is incomplete.

**Worked example 2 — resistance vs. reform:** Samori Touré fought an
empire from outside its institutions; Chartists and unionists fought
from inside their own country's politics. Both are resistance, but
they use different tools and face different risks. When comparing
movements, name the tool (arms, strikes, petitions, newspapers, courts)
and the audience (the colonizer, the home public, the world) — that
pair tells you what the movement could and could not do.

## Part 2 — Three visions of what an economy is for (explicit teaching, 15 min)

**Liberals** (in the 19th-century sense) defended private property,
free markets, and free trade, and argued that empire was a mistake
when it distorted markets — British liberal J.A. Hobson, in
*Imperialism* (1902, summarized here, not quoted), argued that empire
served financiers and armaments firms while taxpayers paid for
conquest. For liberals, the fix was freer trade, democratic control of
foreign policy, and reform at home.

**Socialists** argued that the problem was not bad empire but
capitalism itself: workers produced the wealth and owners kept it, at
home and abroad. Marx and Engels wrote in the *Communist Manifesto*
(1848) — public domain — "The history of all hitherto existing society
is the history of class struggles." Their remedy was collective
ownership of the means of production and international solidarity of
workers: "Workers of the world, unite!" was a claim that a Manchester
weaver and a Bombay mill hand shared an interest against their
respective owners. Indian thinker Dadabhai Naoroji's "drain theory"
(summarized here) made a parallel economic argument: Britain drained
India's wealth through taxes, salaries to British officials, and
trade rules — an early version of the claim that empire was a system
of extraction, not development.

**Nationalists** argued that the problem was who ruled: a people
should govern itself, in its own state, in its own language and
institutions. Nationalism inspired both European unification
movements and anti-colonial movements — and it cut both ways: it
justified new empires (a "great nation" deserved colonies) and
liberation from them (a colonized nation deserved a state). The same
idea, pointed in opposite directions, is one of history's most
important patterns to notice.

**Two original model passages.** Written for this unit to model the
*kinds* of arguments made at the time. They are not real documents.

> **Model passage C — in the manner of a colonial administrator's
> report (original, written for this unit):** "The district is quiet
> since the road was cut and the tax collected in coin. The people
> grumble at the tax but bring their cotton to the depot, and the
> school takes thirty boys. There was trouble at the river village
> over a land survey; the chiefs were called in and the matter
> settled. Progress is slow but visible, and the railway will finish
> what the road began."

> **Model passage D — in the manner of a colonized intellectual's
> response (original, written for this unit):** "They call it quiet
> because we do not march on their depot. We pay their tax by growing
> their cotton instead of our food, and then buy back our own grain at
> their prices. Their school takes thirty boys and teaches them to
> despise their fathers' language. They measure progress by the miles
> of railway they lay through land that was never theirs to measure."

**Worked example 3 — comparing C and D:** Both describe the same
district. The administrator counts roads, taxes, and schoolboys and
calls it progress; the intellectual counts what was given up — food
crops, language, land — and calls it loss. The facts overlap (a road, a
tax, a school); the *frame* differs. Ask of each: what does this writer
count as a cost, and what as a benefit? That question is D2.Eco.2 in
practice: marginal benefits and costs depend on whose margin you stand
on.

**Worked example 4 — three visions, one factory:** A liberal looks at
a Bombay textile mill and says: "Let Indian cloth compete fairly;
remove the tariffs that favor Manchester." A socialist says: "The
mill's workers, Indian or British, should own the mill." A nationalist
says: "The mill should serve an independent India." Same mill, three
different problems — unfair competition, ownership, sovereignty — and
three different remedies. When you compare visions, always name the
problem each vision sees, or you will compare slogans instead of
arguments.

## Guided practice (10 min)

With the adult or a partner, answer aloud or in notes:

1. Name one armed resistance, one everyday resistance, and one home
   reform movement from this lesson, and state each one's tool and
   audience.
2. In model passages C and D, list two facts both writers agree on and
   two judgments on which they differ.
3. Complete the sentence three ways: "For a liberal, the problem with
   empire was ___; for a socialist, ___; for a nationalist, ___."
4. Why did nationalism both build empires and break them? (Answer: it
   claimed every "nation" deserved a state — which justified great
   powers' expansion and colonized peoples' independence alike.)

## Applied task

Choose one movement from this lesson (Adwa, the 1857 Rebellion, the
Boxers, Chartism, or the suffrage campaign) and write a one-page brief:
who acted, what they wanted, what tools they used, what they achieved,
and what limited them. End with one sentence on what this case teaches
about whether industrial empire was inevitable. (Scoring guidance is
in `answer-key.md`.)

## Exit check (3 min)

Without looking: state the liberal, socialist, and nationalist
critiques of industrial empire in one clause each. (Liberal: empire
distorts markets and serves special interests. Socialist: capitalism
exploits workers at home and abroad. Nationalist: peoples should rule
themselves.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult pre-teaches *concessionaire,
  indemnity, solidarity, means of production* before the lesson;
  passages C and D are labeled as modeled writing, not real documents.
- **Alternate response mode:** the applied task may be presented as a
  timeline with annotations instead of prose.
- **Extension:** read a summary of Hobson's *Imperialism* (1902) and
  test his claim: did financiers really profit most from empire, or
  did prestige and strategy matter more? Give one case for each side.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- "Resistance failed because empires lasted." Empires lasted *despite*
  constant resistance, and resistance raised their costs and forced
  reforms; duration is not consent.
- "Socialism and nationalism are the same because both opposed
  empire." They opposed it for different reasons and often opposed
  each other — socialists wanted international worker solidarity,
  nationalists wanted a state of their own.
- "Marx invented the idea that workers were exploited." He gave it a
  theory and a program; workers' own unions, petitions, and strikes
  were already saying it in practice.
