# Unit 03 — Enlightenment, Revolutions, Rights, and Consequences

**Grade 9 · Social Studies · Modern world history · Weeks 11–14 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-27 for issue #45; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Between 1689 and 1825, a set of ideas about rights, government, and human
equality traveled from books and pamphlets into revolutions on three
continents — and then ran into the limits of who counted as a rights-holder.
This unit follows that journey: the Enlightenment thinkers who argued that
governments exist by the consent of the governed; the American and French
revolutions that turned those arguments into declarations and constitutions;
the Haitian Revolution, in which enslaved people seized freedom for
themselves and paid a terrible price for international recognition; and the
Latin American independence movements that broke an empire but kept many of
its hierarchies. It ends where honest history must end: with the question
of whose rights each revolution secured, whose it denied, and what later
generations did with the promises left unkept.

The unit's inquiry question runs through all four weeks: **when a revolution
declares rights "universal," who actually gets them — and what happens to the
people left out?**

A note on sources: learner readings are original passages written for this
unit or short public-domain excerpts from documents such as the U.S.
Declaration of Independence (1776) and the French Declaration of the Rights
of Man (1789). Secondary claims are summarized and linked, never copied.
Where scholars debate a figure or a date, the unit says so and teaches the
range, not a single settled number.

**Sensitive content (adult preview required):** this unit covers enslavement,
revolutionary violence (including the Reign of Terror), and the economic
punishment of Haiti after independence — documentary and textual sources
only, no graphic imagery. The adult previews every Week 13 source, gives
advance notice before the Haitian Revolution sessions, frames discussion
around evidence and human dignity, and offers an observation/reflection
alternative for any activity the learner finds overwhelming. See
[teacher-guide.md](teacher-guide.md) §8 and the track
[scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md) §6–§7.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain Enlightenment ideas and their circulation** — describe
   natural rights, the social contract, separation of powers, and
   Wollstonecraft's rights-of-women claim; explain how print, letters,
   salons, and reading aloud carried ideas, and who could and could not
   access them. (D2.His.2.9-12; D2.Civ.14.9-12)
2. **Compare the American and French revolutions as rights experiments**
   — give long-term and triggering causes for each, compare the 1776 and
   1789 declarations claim by claim, and explain what each revolution's
   constitution delivered and what it reversed. (D2.His.14.9-12;
   D2.Civ.12.9-12)
3. **Analyze the Haitian Revolution and Latin American independence** —
   explain Haiti's causes, course, and significance as the only successful
   revolution by enslaved people; explain the 1825 indemnity and its
   consequences with verified figures; and compare independence outcomes
   across Spanish America using Bolívar's Jamaica Letter (1815).
   (D2.His.3.9-12; D2.His.14.9-12)
4. **Evaluate whose rights each revolution secured** — complete an
   inclusion/exclusion analysis for women, enslaved people, Indigenous
   nations, and free people of color across four revolutions, and explain
   at least two reasons each exclusion persisted. (D2.His.4.9-12;
   D2.His.5.9-12)
5. **Build a source-based argument about universal rights** — trace one
   rights idea across three revolutions, weigh later writers' perspectives
   on the same events (D2.His.6.9-12), and argue a position on whether
   rights can be called universal, with evidence, a counterclaim, and a
   note on what the sources cannot show. (D2.His.16.9-12; D3.1–D3.4.9-12;
   D4.1–D4.4.9-12)

**Source notes (checked at authoring, 2026-10-27):**

- Thinker dates used: Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* (1689);
  Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748); Rousseau, *The Social
  Contract* (1762); Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*
  (1792). Dates are first-publication years for the works as commonly
  cited; the unit teaches ideas, not a claim that any revolutionary read a
  specific edition.
- U.S. Declaration of Independence: adopted July 4, 1776. Short quotations
  in Lesson 02 follow the National Archives transcription of the Stone
  Engraving (public domain). French Declaration of the Rights of Man and
  of the Citizen: adopted August 26, 1789 (public domain; the unit uses a
  short original paraphrase plus two short quoted articles).
- Haitian Revolution: enslaved uprising begins August 1791 in
  Saint-Domingue; Louverture rises to leadership; French forces under
  Leclerc fail to restore slavery (1802–03); independence declared January
  1, 1804, under Dessalines. Indemnity: France's 1825 ordinance demanded
  150 million gold francs, reduced to 90 million gold francs in 1838;
  Haiti borrowed from French banks to pay and was still paying in 1947.
  The unit teaches the 150→90 figures and the borrowing mechanism, not a
  single modern-dollar equivalent (estimates vary widely).
- Bolívar's Jamaica Letter: dictated September 6, 1815, in Kingston,
  Jamaica, addressed to Henry Cullen; UNESCO Memory of the World Register
  (Latin America and the Caribbean, 2016). Pétion's Haiti aided Bolívar in
  1816–17 with ships, soldiers, and a printing press, asking in return that
  Bolívar abolish slavery in liberated lands — a promise Bolívar made but
  implemented only partly and late.
- French Revolution dates used: Bastille July 14, 1789; Declaration of
  the Rights of Man August 1789; Reign of Terror 1793–94; Napoleon's coup
  November 1799. U.S. dates used: Constitution written 1787, in effect
  1789; Bill of Rights ratified 1791. Spanish American independence wars
  run roughly 1810–1825; the unit names outcomes, not a single
  independence day.
