# Answer Key — Unit 03: Enlightenment, Revolutions, Rights, and Consequences

*For the guiding adult only. Every lesson practice item, Practice Set
item, quiz question, assessment task, and project criterion is solved
or scored here. Open responses accept any answer meeting the stated
reasoning standard.*

---

## Lesson 01 — Guided practice

1. **Four ideas, in own words (acceptable):** Natural rights — rights
   people hold by nature, before government. Social contract —
   government is legitimate only with the people's consent. Separation
   of powers — split lawmaking, enforcement, and judging so no one body
   holds all power. Women's rights claim — if men hold rights by nature,
   women must too; exclusion needs a reason that survives scrutiny.
2. **Ban backfiring (any two):** banned books gain notoriety and
   demand; smuggling networks spread them further; the ban signals the
   government fears the ideas; summaries and rumors spread the argument
   even where the text is absent.
3. **Error analysis:** Montesquieu did not invent democracy — democratic
   elements existed in ancient Athens and elsewhere long before 1748,
   and separation of powers is a design for *limiting* concentrated power
   within a government, not the same as rule by the people. The sentence
   confuses an institutional design with a form of rule.

## Lesson 01 — Independent practice

1. **Match:** (a) natural rights → (iii); (b) social contract → (i);
   (c) separation of powers → (ii); (d) women's rights claim → (iv).
2. **Paragraph (acceptable):** must name two channels (reading aloud,
   sermons, songs, tavern talk, theater) and explain how each carried
   the idea to a non-reader. Full marks require the idea itself to be
   named, not just "the news."
3. **Explain your reasoning (acceptable argument):** Locke's premises
   (natural rights, government by consent) imply an enslaved person,
   held without consent and denied liberty, has a right to resist —
   because slavery violates the very rights government exists to
   protect. **Objection (acceptable):** a defender of slavery might
   argue Locke himself invested in slaveholding enterprises and
   treated enslaved Africans as property, or that his "property" right
   protected owners. The key point: Locke's *theory* and Locke's
   *practice* pull apart — learners who notice this earn full marks.

## Lesson 01 — Applied task + exit check

- **Pamphlet opening (acceptable):** uses one idea explicitly (for
  example, consent of the governed), argues for an assembly chosen by
  residents, and includes one sentence for hearers (for example, "If
  you hear this read in the square, ask your neighbor what consent
  should mean for our town").
- **Exit check:** Locke — natural rights/social contract; Montesquieu
  — separation of powers; Rousseau — social contract/general will;
  Wollstonecraft — women's rights. Most-threatening idea: accept any
  reasoned choice (social contract/natural rights most commonly, since
  they deny divine right directly).

## Lesson 01 — Image observation (Practice Set A3 answers)

(a) Jobs visible: printer working the press lever; typesetter arranging
metal type; young worker stacking sheets; (also acceptable: workers
bundling tied pamphlets). (b) Tied bundles imply many identical copies
produced for distribution, not single handwritten copies. (c) Missing

## Lesson 02 — Guided practice

1. **American causes table (acceptable):** Long-term — British taxation
   and trade controls after the Seven Years' War; colonial
   self-government traditions and objection to taxation without
   representation; Enlightenment consent arguments. Triggering —
   Boston Tea Party (1773) and Coercive Acts (1774); Lexington and
   Concord (April 1775).
2. **Error analysis:** the Declaration of the Rights of Man (August
   1789) opened the revolution's rights phase; the revolution continued
   through the republic (1792), the Terror (1793–94), and Napoleon's
   coup (1799). Dating the end to 1789 confuses a declaration with an
   outcome.
3. **Social-contract lines:** U.S. — "Governments are instituted among
   Men, deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed."
   French — sovereignty resides in the nation (Article 3). Both make
   legitimacy depend on the people/nation rather than the king.

## Lesson 02 — Independent practice

1. **Sequencing:** (c) Declaration of Independence, 1776 → (a)
   Bastille, 1789 → (e) French Declaration, August 1789 → (b) Bill of
   Rights, 1791 → (d) Napoleon's coup, 1799. Note: Bastille (July 14)
   precedes the French Declaration (August 26) in the same year.
2. **Against "America succeeded, France failed":** America kept its
   republic and constitution, but also kept slavery and exclusion;
   France lost its republic to Napoleon but kept the abolition of
   feudal privilege and the rights language that later movements reused.
   Full marks require evidence on both sides of the contrast.
3. **Two imagined sentences (acceptable):** French peasant, 1790 —
   hope that feudal dues are gone, worry about bread and taxes. Enslaved
   Virginian, 1776 — hearing "all men are created equal" while
   remaining enslaved exposes the declaration's exclusion. Both must be
   labeled imagined, not quotations.

## Lesson 02 — Applied task + exit check

- **Editor's note (acceptable):** names a choice with a reason tied to
  the town's situation, states what the chosen declaration promises,
  and names at least one exclusion honestly.
- **Exit check:** American long-term (taxation/trade controls;
  self-government) + triggering (Tea Party/Coercive Acts; Lexington);
  French long-term (privilege/tax inequality; state debt) + triggering
  (Estates-General/National Assembly; Bastille; bread crisis). Rights
  named: life, liberty, pursuit of happiness (U.S.); liberty, property,
  security, resistance to oppression (France).

## Lesson 03 — Guided practice

1. **Timeline order:** (a) 1791 uprising → (b) 1804 independence →
   (c) 1815 Jamaica Letter → (d) 1825 indemnity → (e) 1862 U.S.
   recognition.
2. **Error analysis:** 1838 reduced the demand from 150 million to 90
   million gold francs; it did not cancel the debt. Haiti kept paying
   until 1947. Reduction ≠ forgiveness.
3. **One sentence each (acceptable):** Haiti proved enslaved people
   could win freedom and found a state. Spanish American independence
   mostly preserved creole elite power, slavery (for decades), and
   Indigenous land loss.

## Lesson 03 — Independent practice

1. **Computation:** 150 − 90 = 60 million more; 60 ÷ 150 = 40% of the
   original demand.
2. **Bolívar's "not yet prepared" (acceptable):** sincere reason —
   wars had destroyed institutions and left deep divisions; republics
   need literate citizens, courts, and habits of self-rule that
   colonial rule had not built. Hearing as excuse — for an Indigenous
   or enslaved person, "not yet" indefinitely postponed the freedom
   others already enjoyed, and protected creole power.
3. **U.S. recognition delay (acceptable, any two):** fear that Haiti's
   example would encourage enslaved people in the U.S. South;
   diplomatic deference to France (a trading partner and former ally);
   domestic slavery politics (southern planters opposed recognizing a
   Black republic).

## Lesson 03 — Applied task + exit check

- **Minister's letter (acceptable):** states Haiti's war costs, objects
  to paying former owners for freed people, and argues a consequence
  (revenue diverted from schools, roads, hospitals; debt dependence).
  Must be labeled imagined.
- **Exit check:** (a) 1804, Dessalines. (b) 150 million gold francs
  (1825), reduced to 90 million (1838). (c) Any one valid difference:
  Haiti led by enslaved people and abolished slavery at founding;
  Spanish America led by creoles and kept slavery for decades.

## Lesson 04 — Guided practice

1. **Table correction (acceptable):** any cell revised with a reason
   (for example, qualifying the French 1794 abolition with the 1802
   restoration, or noting free Black voting rights in some early U.S.
   states). The adult checks the reason, not the cell chosen.
2. **Error analysis:** women (Wollstonecraft 1792, de Gouges 1791) and
   enslaved people (Louverture, Haitian revolutionaries, petitioners)
   raised rights claims in the revolutions' own years. The claims were
   heard and rejected or postponed — which is historically different
   from never being made.
3. **1802 restoration (acceptable):** economic interest (sugar profits
   from Saint-Domingue) and/or political fear (Napoleon's need for
   revenue and planter support; fear of a powerful Black state). Either
   defended with a reason earns full marks.

## Lesson 04 — Independent practice

1. **Own-country row:** full marks either for a sourced row or for the
   refusal sentence explaining why memory alone is insufficient.
   Guessing from memory earns no marks for this item.
2. **"Universal or not rights" paragraph (acceptable):** any position
   (agree/disagree/qualify) with evidence from two revolutions. The
   key checks evidence use, not the position taken.
3. **Sourcing sentences (acceptable):** 1880 French textbook — likely
   minimizes the indemnity or frames Haiti as a cautionary failure.
   2004 Haitian historian — centers the indemnity as punishment and
   Haiti's founding achievement. Difference explained by author's
   society, sources, audience, and purpose. Both labeled imagined.

## Practice Set B

- **B1:** (c) 1776 → (b) July 1789 → (e) August 1789 → (a) 1791 →
  (d) 1799.
- **B2:** U.S. — sovereignty: the people (consent of the governed);
  right: life, liberty, pursuit of happiness; excluded: enslaved people,
  women, Indigenous nations. France — sovereignty: the nation; right:
  liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression; excluded:
  women, enslaved people in colonies (until 1794, then again after
  1802).
- **B3 (acceptable):** long-term — privilege/tax inequality; state debt
  and court spending. Triggering — Estates-General/National Assembly
  crisis; Bastille; bread-price crisis.
- **B4:** bread prices alone ignore the tax/privilege system, state
  debt, and the political crisis that converted hardship into
  revolution.
- **B5:** supporting — America kept a stable republic and constitution;
  France's republic fell to Napoleon by 1799. Complicating — France kept
  the abolition of feudal privilege and rights language; America kept
  slavery, so "kept its republic" does not mean "kept its promises."

## Practice Set C

- **C1:** 1791 → 1804 → 1815 → 1825 → 1862.
- **C2:** (a) 60 million; 40%. (b) 1947 − 1825 = 122 years. (c) 90
  million remained enormous for a small war-damaged country, and the
  mechanism (paying former owners, funded by high-interest loans) is
  what made it crushing.
- **C3:** Account A — maker: French planter, 1805; purpose: defend the
  old colonial order / seek sympathy and compensation; claim: rebellion
  ruined a productive colony. Hides: slavery's violence, and that the
  "order" rested on enslaved labor. Account B — maker: Haitian officer,
  1805; purpose: defend independence's legitimacy; claim: freedom was
  bought with lives. Hides: postwar hardship and internal divisions
  (acceptable). Account B better explains the indemnity's logic from
  France's side only in reverse — accept either account with a reasoned
  justification: A shows the planter grievance France acted on; B shows
  why Haiti experienced the demand as punishment. The adult checks
  that maker/purpose/claim/hiding are all addressed.
- **C4:** (a) Spanish rule denied Americans government, trade, and
  dignity. (b) Independence is inevitable; Spanish America should form
  a confederation, though its peoples need preparation for republican
  government. (c) Liberty vs. order; independence vs. preparedness.
- **C5:** Pétion aided Bolívar to spread emancipation and to gain an
  ally against European powers; the condition shows Haiti understood
  itself as a revolutionary state whose legitimacy rested on abolition,
  not merely on national independence.

## Practice Set D

- **D1:** each revolution needs one evidence piece (declaration line,
  law, or event) showing the idea at work or violated, plus a
  one-sentence conclusion. The adult checks that evidence is specific
  (named document/event), not generic ("they believed in rights").
- **D2:** 8 cells; each "gained/denied/mixed" plus evidence phrase.
  Score 1 pt per cell with both elements; half marks (adult judgment)
  for a correct verdict without evidence.
- **D3:** economic interest — planters/slaveholders and the 1802
  restoration; political fear — elites fearing power transfer to the
  poor or formerly enslaved; inherited ideas — racial/gender prejudice
  among revolutionaries; war/instability — postponement of emancipation
  or reform during invasion or civil war.
- **D4:** differences explained by author's society (slaveholding vs.
  post-emancipation scholarship), sources available (Haitian archives
  vs. colonial accounts), and purpose/audience (national celebration
  vs. critical history). Any two factors with explanation earn full
  marks.
- **D5:** Wollstonecraft (1792) and de Gouges (1791) published women's
  rights claims during the revolutions; the claims were rejected, not
  unthought.

2. **Thinkers:** (a) Locke → (iii) 1689; (b) Montesquieu → (iv) 1748;

   state debt/court spending; French triggering — Estates-General
   crisis, Bastille, or bread-price crisis.
4. **Declarations:** (a) social contract. (b) Any one valid group per
   declaration: U.S. — enslaved people, women, Indigenous nations;
   France — women, enslaved people in colonies (at 1789).
5. **Sequencing:** (d) 1776 → (b) July 1789 → (a) August 1789 →

6. **Short explanation (acceptable):** the Declaration's abolition of
   feudal privilege and its rights language survived Napoleon and were
   reused by later movements; a text's influence is not measured only
   by the regime that immediately followed it.

## Unit assessment

- **A1:** (a) Locke → (iii); (b) Montesquieu → (i);
  (c) Rousseau → (iv); (d) Wollstonecraft → (ii).
- **A2:** correct order: (d) U.S. Declaration, 1776 → (e)
  *Vindication*, 1792 → (f) Napoleon's coup, 1799 → (a) Haitian
  independence, 1804 → (b) Jamaica Letter, 1815 → (c) indemnity
  ordinance, 1825.
- **A3:** 150 million gold francs (1825 ordinance); 90 million gold
  francs (1838 reduction); payments ended 1947.
- **B1:** Passage 1 — Philadelphia printer, 1776; position: supporter
  of independence; claim: consent of the governed justifies breaking
  with the king. Passage 2 — Parisian market woman, 1793; position:
  ordinary person disappointed by unmet promises; claim: rights mean
  little without bread and safety from arrest.
- **B2:** Passage 2 better illustrates the promise/daily-life gap,
  because it measures the revolution by food prices and arrests rather
  than by its declarations. Accept Passage 1 with a reasoned defense
  only if it explicitly addresses the gap (it does not — it states
  the promise, not its failure).
- **B3:** Agree (one reason): the Terror's executions and Napoleon's
  coup betrayed the Declaration's promises. Disagree (one reason):
  feudal privilege stayed abolished and rights language survived for
  later movements; or the Terror was one phase (1793–94) of a
  decade-long revolution.
- **C1 rubric (20 pts):** claim stated clearly 4; evidence from three
  revolutions, including one exclusion and one later use by an excluded
  group 8; counterclaim fairly stated with reply 4; source-limit note
  2; clarity and organization 2.
- **D:** 8 cells scored as in Practice Set D2; measure sentence (2
  pts) must name the measure (for example, "by abolition of slavery at
  founding, Haiti extended rights further"). A verdict without a named
  measure earns 0 for the sentence.
