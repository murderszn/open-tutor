# Lesson 03 — Haiti and Latin America: Revolutions on Different Terms

**Unit 03 · Week 13, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain why the Haitian Revolution was
unique, why it was treated differently from the American and French
revolutions, and how Latin American independence movements both used and
limited revolutionary ideas.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal, timeline (SVG in
`assets/`).

**Readiness check (5 min):** From U02, recall Saint-Domingue: France's
richest sugar colony, worked by enslaved Africans, with a small white
planter elite, a growing free population of color, and an enslaved
majority. Ask: *if the French Revolution declares "men are born and
remain free and equal in rights," what must happen in Saint-Domingue —
and who will fight to stop it?* Hold that tension; it drives this lesson.

**Sensitive-content note:** this lesson discusses enslavement, war, and
punishment after independence. Sources are documentary and textual
only. The adult previews this lesson with the learner, and any learner
may choose the observation/reflection alternative for any activity.

---

## Part 1 — Haiti: the revolution enslaved people made themselves (explicit teaching, 15 min)

In August 1791, enslaved people in Saint-Domingue rose in a coordinated
uprising. Within years, under the leadership of Toussaint Louverture — a
formerly enslaved man who became a brilliant general and statesman —
they defeated the colony's planters, then Spanish and British invaders,
and then, when Napoleon sent an army in 1802 to restore slavery,
defeated the French expedition as well. On January 1, 1804, Jean-Jacques
Dessalines declared independence: Haiti became the second independent
nation in the Americas and the first created by formerly enslaved
people — the only successful revolution by enslaved people in history.

Three things make Haiti world-historically significant, and each needs
its own sentence in your notes:

1. **It proved the universal claim true in practice.** Enslaved people
   did what the declarations said all people could do: claim rights and
   found a state. Haiti exposed every exclusion of 1776 and 1789 at once.
2. **It terrified slaveholding powers.** The United States, Britain,
   Spain, and France all had slavery or slave colonies. Their governments
   feared Haiti's example would spread — and in the U.S. South, Haiti
   became a name planters used to frighten each other. Recognition was
   withheld for decades: the United States did not recognize Haiti until
   1862, during its own Civil War.
3. **It was punished economically.** In 1825, France sent warships and
   demanded an "indemnity" — payment to former slaveholders for their
   "lost property," meaning the very people who had freed themselves.
   The original demand was **150 million gold francs**, later reduced
   to **90 million gold francs in 1838**. Haiti borrowed from French
   banks at high interest to pay, and was still paying in **1947** —
   122 years after the demand. Money that could have built schools,
   roads, and hospitals went to Paris instead. Historians call this the
   "double debt": Haiti paid for its freedom, then paid interest on the
   loans it took to pay.

**Worked example 1 — the indemnity arithmetic:** 150 million minus 90
million is 60 million; 60 ÷ 150 = 0.40, so the reduction was 40%. The
remaining 90 million was still, by every contemporary account, an
enormous sum for a small, war-damaged country — "reduced" did not mean
"affordable." A common error is to treat the reduction as generosity;
the mechanism (paying former owners for freed people, funded by
high-interest loans) is the point, not the discount.

**Worked example 2 — testing a claim:** A textbook says, "Haiti failed
because its leaders were unfit to govern." Test it with Part 1: Haiti
faced diplomatic isolation, a crushing indemnity, and the constant
threat of re-invasion — conditions no new nation could easily survive.
A fair judgment weighs *both* internal decisions *and* external
punishment. Blaming only the victims' leaders repeats the very
dismissal this lesson asks you to notice.

## Part 2 — Latin America: independence without the same revolution (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Spanish America's wars of independence (roughly 1810–1825) were led
mostly by **creoles** — American-born people of Spanish descent who were
denied high office by **peninsulares** (officials born in Spain) and
burdened by trade monopolies. When Napoleon invaded Spain in 1808 and
imprisoned the Spanish king, creole leaders argued that sovereignty had
"returned" to the American peoples — and many moved from loyalty to
independence.

**Simón Bolívar's Jamaica Letter (September 6, 1815, Kingston,
Jamaica)** is the classic statement of the creole case. Written in exile
after patriot defeats, addressed to the English merchant Henry Cullen,
it argues that Spanish rule denied Americans government, trade, and
dignity; that independence is inevitable; and that Spanish America
should form a great confederation — while also warning, frankly, that
its peoples were not yet prepared for full republican government.
Bolívar's honesty about that tension is the document's value: he wanted
liberty and order at once, and the two pulled against each other.

The outcomes differed from Haiti's in a crucial way: Spanish American
independence mostly *transferred* power from peninsulares to creoles
while leaving large structures intact — Indigenous communities still
lost land, enslaved people were freed slowly and unevenly (Bolívar
himself promised abolition under pressure from Haiti's president
Alexandre Pétion, who gave him ships, soldiers, and a printing press in
1816–17 on that condition, but emancipation came late and piecemeal),
and women gained no vote. Mexico's independence (1821) and Brazil's
(1822, under a Portuguese prince) show the range: "independence" did not
always mean "republic," and it rarely meant "equality."

**Worked example 3 — comparing two independence stories:** Haiti and
Venezuela both won independence from a European empire. Difference
that matters most: *who led and who benefited.* Haiti's revolution was
led by enslaved people and abolished slavery at its founding; Venezuela's
was led by creole planters and kept slavery for decades. Same word —
independence — very different distributions of freedom. When you compare
revolutions, always ask: independence *for whom*?

## Guided practice (10 min)

1. Timeline check: place these in order — (a) Haitian uprising begins,
   (b) Haitian independence, (c) Jamaica Letter, (d) French indemnity
   ordinance, (e) U.S. recognition of Haiti. (Answers: 1791, 1804,
   1815, 1825, 1862.)
2. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "France forgave Haiti's debt in
   1838." Explain the error in two sentences (the 1838 change reduced
   the demand from 150 to 90 million; Haiti kept paying until 1947 —
   reduction is not forgiveness).
3. In one sentence each, state what Haiti proved and what Spanish
   American independence mostly preserved.

## Independent practice

1. Compute and explain: if Haiti's indemnity had stayed at 150 million
   instead of 90 million, how many million more would it have owed?
   What fraction of the original demand is that extra amount? (60
   million; 40%.)
2. Explain-your-reasoning: Bolívar wrote that Spanish Americans were
   "not yet prepared" for full republican government. Give one reason
   a creole leader might sincerely believe that in 1815, and one reason
   an Indigenous or enslaved person might hear it as an excuse. Both
   answers can be reasonable — support each with a sentence.
3. Short paragraph: why did the United States wait until 1862 to
   recognize Haiti? Use at least two causes from Part 1 (fear of the
   example spreading to enslaved people in the U.S.; diplomatic
   deference to France; domestic slavery politics).

## Applied task

Write a 5–7 sentence letter from a Haitian government minister in 1826
to the French government, responding to the indemnity ordinance. Your
letter must: state what Haiti already paid for its freedom (in lives and
war), name the mechanism you object to (paying former owners), and make
one argument about consequences (what the payments will cost Haiti's
future). Label it as an imagined letter, not a real document.

## Exit check

Answer from memory: (a) What year did Haiti declare independence, and
who led that declaration? (b) What were the two indemnity figures, and
when was the second set? (c) Name one way Latin American independence
differed from Haiti's revolution.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** all dates and figures are repeated in the
  timeline; the adult may read Part 1 aloud. Learners may answer
  timeline items by ordering cards.
- **Alternate response mode:** the minister's letter may be dictated,
  or delivered as a speech to the adult.
- **Extension:** investigate one more case — Mexico, Brazil, or Gran
  Colombia after Bolívar — and add a row to your comparison table:
  who led, who benefited, what changed, what stayed. Note which facts
  you could verify and which remain debated.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "Haiti is poor because Haitians failed" —
the indemnity, isolation, and invasion threats are structural causes
that must be weighed before any judgment about leadership. (2)
"Latin America copied the U.S." — creole leaders read many models,
including French and British ones, and adapted them to societies with
slavery, large Indigenous populations, and powerful churches; copying
is too simple. (3) Treating the indemnity as a footnote — for this
unit, it is a central consequence: a revolution's aftermath can punish
its winners for generations. See [teacher-guide](../teacher-guide.md)
§4 and the [answer key](../answer-key.md).
