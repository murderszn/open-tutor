# Lesson 04 — Whose Rights? Inclusion, Exclusion, and the Writers of History

**Unit 03 · Week 14, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can complete an inclusion/exclusion analysis
for four revolutions, explain why exclusions persisted, and judge how
later writers' perspectives shaped each revolution's story.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal, your comparison
tables from Lessons 02–03 and Project Parts 1–3.

**Readiness check (5 min):** Quick recall — name one group each of these
revolutions *excluded* from the rights it proclaimed: (a) the American
Revolution, (b) the French Revolution, (c) the Haitian Revolution, (d)
Spanish American independence. (Possible answers: (a) enslaved people,
women, Indigenous nations; (b) women, enslaved people in colonies until
1794 — and again after 1802; (c) Haiti excluded least at its founding,
but women still lacked political equality; (d) enslaved people,
Indigenous communities, women.) If your answers differ, good — that
disagreement is today's work.

---

## Part 1 — The inclusion table (explicit teaching, 15 min)

A revolution's promises can be tested the same way every time: take
the groups living under the old order and ask, for each, *did this
revolution give them rights, and which ones?* The table below is a
starting frame — your project work may refine it, and historians
legitimately disagree on some cells.

| Revolution | Women | Enslaved people | Indigenous nations | Free people of color / propertyless men |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| American (1776–83) | No vote; property and legal limits continued | Slavery continued; some northern states began gradual emancipation | Land-taking continued; no recognized sovereignty in practice | Property requirements for voting persisted in most states; free Black men could vote in a few states, then lost ground |
| French (1789–99) | No vote; Olympe de Gouges's 1791 Declaration of the Rights of Woman answered the 1789 text and was ignored | Slavery abolished 1794, restored by Napoleon 1802 | Not a central category in metropolitan France; colonial subjects excluded | Free men of color gained citizenship in 1792 after struggle; then reversal under Napoleon |
| Haitian (1791–1804) | Legal equality proclaimed, but political power stayed male-dominated | Slavery abolished at the founding — the revolution's defining achievement | Not applicable as a separate category in Saint-Domingue | Free people of color were central leaders of the revolution itself |
| Spanish American (1810–25) | No vote; legal subordination continued | Emancipation gradual and late; slavery persisted decades in Brazil (to 1888) and Cuba (to 1886) | Land loss continued; republics claimed Indigenous territory | Creole elites gained power; Indigenous, Black, and mixed-race majorities gained little political power |

Read the table in two directions. Across a row: no revolution delivered
its promises evenly. Down a column: some groups (notably enslaved
people in Haiti) won through their own revolution what other
revolutions denied them — proof that exclusion was a choice, not a law
of nature.

**Why did exclusions persist?** At least four interacting reasons recur:

1. **Economic interest.** Planters, slaveholders, and landowners stood
   to lose wealth and labor from emancipation or land reform.
2. **Political fear.** Elites feared that extending rights would
   transfer power — to the poor, to the formerly enslaved, to women —
   and destabilize the new order.
3. **Inherited ideas.** Even revolutionaries who believed in natural
   rights often held racial and gender prejudices that let them exempt
   whole groups without feeling inconsistent.
4. **War and instability.** Revolutions that faced invasion or civil
   war postponed hard choices in the name of unity — and postponement
   became permanence.

**Worked example 1 — using the table to test a claim:** Claim: "The
French Revolution freed everyone." Test: women — no vote; enslaved
people in colonies — freed 1794, re-enslaved 1802. Verdict: false as a
general claim; true only for a window (1794–1802) and only for some.
Strong historical writing replaces "freed everyone" with "freed some,
then reversed it" — precision is the argument.

**Worked example 2 — explaining persistence without excusing it:**
Why did the American republic keep slavery after declaring that "all
men are created equal"? A complete answer needs at least two of the
four reasons above (for example, economic interest of southern planters
*and* political fear that pressing abolition would break the new union).
Naming causes is not the same as approving them — explanation and
judgment are different moves, and this unit asks for both, separately.

## Part 2 — How later writers shaped the story (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Revolutions are also fought over in history books. Three patterns recur:

- **Heroic national stories** (19th-century textbooks in the U.S.,
  France, and Latin America) celebrated founders and minimized
  exclusions, violence, and the roles of women, enslaved people, and
  Indigenous nations.
- **Dismissive stories about Haiti** treated its revolution as a
  catastrophe or a curiosity, rather than as a founding act — a
  perspective shaped by the slaveholding societies writing the books.
- **Recovery stories** (20th- and 21st-century scholarship) restored
  the actors earlier stories erased: Haitian generals and soldiers,
  women pamphleteers like de Gouges and Wollstonecraft, Indigenous
  negotiators, and the free people of color who pressed for citizenship.

The lesson for your own work: when two accounts of the same revolution
disagree, ask who wrote each, when, for whom, and what sources they
could use. A history written in 1850 in a slaveholding country and a
history written in 2000 from Haitian archives will not — and should
not — sound the same. That difference is evidence about the writers,
not just about the events.

**Worked example 3 — sourcing two accounts:** Account A (1850, U.S.
textbook): "Haiti fell into ruin after independence." Account B (modern
scholarship): "Haiti was diplomatically isolated and forced to pay an
indemnity that consumed its revenues." Both mention hardship; only B
names the external mechanisms. Sourcing question to ask: what could
Account A's author *not* say comfortably in 1850 — and what does that
silence tell you? (In a slaveholding republic, crediting a Black
republic's achievements and naming white powers' punishment was
politically costly.)

## Guided practice (10 min)

1. Pick one cell of the Part 1 table you disagree with or want to
   qualify, and write a two-sentence correction with a reason.
2. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Exclusions happened because
   people back then just didn't think about women or enslaved people."
   Explain in two sentences why this is wrong (Wollstonecraft, de
   Gouges, Louverture, and enslaved petitioners *did* raise these
   claims at the time — they were heard and rejected, which is
   different from never being raised).
3. Which of the four persistence reasons best explains the French
   restoration of slavery in 1802? Defend your choice in two sentences
   (economic interest — sugar profits — plus political fear both work;
   the key rewards a reasoned choice, not one "right" reason).

## Independent practice

1. Extend the table: add a row for *your own country's or community's*
   founding story only if you can fill it from sources you have
   actually read — otherwise, write one sentence explaining why you
   will not fill it from memory. (Guessing at history is the error
   this task trains you to avoid.)
2. Explain-your-reasoning: "Rights are either universal or they are not
   rights at all." Write a paragraph agreeing, disagreeing, or
   qualifying this sentence, using evidence from at least two
   revolutions. This paragraph is a draft for your verdict brief
   (Project Part 4) — keep it.
3. Sourcing task: imagine a French textbook from 1880 and a Haitian
   historian writing in 2004 (the bicentennial of independence) each
   describing the indemnity. Write one sentence each would plausibly
   write, and one sentence explaining why they differ. Label both as
   imagined reconstructions, not quotations.

## Applied task

Deliberation prep (for Week 14, S4): prepare two positions — (A) rights
declared in 1776/1789 *were* universal, because later generations used
them to win inclusion; (B) rights declared then were *not* universal,
because a promise that excludes most people at birth is not universal
in any meaningful sense. For each position, write one claim, two pieces
of evidence from this unit, and one reply to the other side's best
argument.

## Exit check

From memory: name the four reasons exclusions persisted, and give one
example of each from this unit. Then answer: which revolution's story
changed most when later writers recovered erased actors, and why?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the table may be completed orally or with the
  adult scribing; learners may fill two rows deeply rather than all
  four thinly.
- **Alternate response mode:** deliberation prep may be recorded as
  audio or drawn as a two-sided argument map.
- **Extension:** read the actual text of Olympe de Gouges's
  Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791, public domain, in
  translation) and compare its articles with the 1789 Declaration:
  which articles does she mirror, and which does she add? Write a
  half-page comparison.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "Exclusion means the ideas were worthless"
— no: excluded groups *used* the same ideas to demand inclusion, which
is why the ideas mattered even when their authors failed them.
(2) "Later historians just make things up" — recovery scholarship
rests on archives (Haitian records, petitions, pamphlets) that earlier
writers ignored; new evidence, not new opinions, drives revision.
(3) Confusing explanation with excuse — keep the two moves labeled
separately in discussion. See [teacher-guide](../teacher-guide.md) §5
and the [answer key](../answer-key.md).
