# Lesson 01 — Enlightenment Ideas: Rights, Contracts, and Who Got to Read Them

**Unit 03 · Week 11, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain four Enlightenment ideas in my own
words — natural rights, the social contract, separation of powers, and
women's rights — and explain how those ideas traveled and who could reach
them.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook/inquiry journal, the unit's
print-shop reconstruction image (or its text alternative below).

**Readiness check (5 min):** In U02 you saw empires justify rule by
conquest, birth, and God's favor. Write one sentence answering: *if a king
rules because God chose him, what could a subject never legitimately do?*
(Answer: question or replace the king — the whole point of "divine right"
is that it cannot be challenged from below.) Today's thinkers attack
exactly that premise.

---

## Part 1 — Four ideas that unmade divine right (explicit teaching, 15 min)

In the 1600s and 1700s, European writers began arguing that government is
not a gift from God to a king but a human arrangement that must be judged
by whether it serves people. Four ideas matter most for this unit.

**1. Natural rights (John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government*, 1689).**
Locke argued that by nature every person holds rights to life, liberty,
and property — rights that exist before any government and that no
government may simply take away. Government's job is to protect them.
Locke wrote in England just after Parliament had replaced one king with
another, and his argument gave that replacement a principle: a ruler who
violates the people's rights breaks the bargain.

**2. The social contract (Locke, then Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social
Contract*, 1762).** If government is a bargain, then it rests on the
*consent of the governed*. Rousseau pushed the idea further: legitimate
authority comes from the "general will" — the shared interest of the
people as a whole — not from a king, a church, or a wealthy few. Notice
the explosive consequence: if consent is withdrawn, the government loses
its legitimacy. Revolution stops being treason and becomes, in this
theory, a right.

**3. Separation of powers (Montesquieu, *The Spirit of the Laws*,
1748).** Montesquieu warned that when one person or body makes the laws,
enforces them, and judges disputes, liberty dies — "there can be no
liberty" where powers are united. His remedy: split power among
legislative, executive, and judicial branches so each checks the others.
This is an engineering answer to a political problem: don't just hope
rulers are good; design institutions that make abuse hard.

**4. Women's rights (Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of
Woman*, 1792).** Wollstonecraft turned the new rights language back on
its authors: if all men have natural rights, on what ground are women
denied education and legal standing? Her answer — none that survives
reason — made her the first major writer to demand that "the rights of
man" include women. She also exposed the unit's central tension early:
rights language can be universal in words and narrow in practice.

**Worked example 1 — matching ideas to claims:** A pamphlet says, "The
king taxed us without our assembly's consent, so his government has
broken its bargain with us." Which idea is doing the work? *The social
contract*: the claim is not "the tax is high" but "consent was missing,
so legitimacy is gone." A common error is to label every complaint about
a king "natural rights." Ask instead: is the complaint about a right
violated (natural rights), consent missing (social contract), or power
concentrated (separation of powers)?

**Worked example 2 — testing Wollstonecraft's move:** A writer in 1792
says, "All men are created equal." Wollstonecraft's reply, in effect:
"Then show me the reason women are excluded — or admit the sentence is
incomplete." This is modeled reasoning you will reuse all unit: take a
universal claim, find the group it silently excludes, and ask what
justification is offered. If none survives, the claim needs rewriting.

## Part 2 — How ideas traveled, and who could reach them (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Ideas do not travel by themselves; they travel through a *public sphere*
of print shops, newspapers, pamphlets, letters, salons (hosted discussion
gatherings, often run by women), and coffeehouses. A pamphlet could be
printed in days, carried by ship in weeks, and read aloud in a tavern to
people who could not read at all — so "reach" was wider than literacy
rates suggest. But reach was not equal, and the limits matter as much as
the spread:

- **Literacy:** most ordinary people in the 1700s could not read fluently.
  Reading aloud, sermons, and songs carried ideas further than books did.
- **Enslavement and colonial rule:** enslaved people in Saint-Domingue
  were legally forbidden to read in many contexts, yet news of
  revolutionary France still reached the colony through sailors, free
  people of color, and domestic workers — ideas traveled along the very
  trade routes built for empire (U02).
- **Censorship:** French royal censors banned dangerous books, so
  forbidden works were printed abroad and smuggled in. Banning a book
  often advertised it.
- **Cost and language:** books were expensive; pamphlets were cheap.
  Ideas written in French or English reached colonial elites first, and
  only later — translated, summarized, retold — reached others.

## Guided practice (10 min)

1. In your own words, define each of the four ideas in one sentence
   each — without copying the lesson's wording.
2. A colonial governor bans a pamphlet. Using Part 2, give two reasons
   the ban might *increase* the pamphlet's influence rather than kill it.
3. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Montesquieu invented democracy."
   Explain what is wrong with that sentence in two sentences (hint:
   separation of powers is a design *within* government, not the same
   thing as rule by the people; and earlier societies had democratic
   elements long before 1748).

## Independent practice

1. Match: (a) natural rights, (b) social contract, (c) separation of
   powers, (d) women's rights claim — to: (i) "government needs the
   people's consent," (ii) "split lawmaking, enforcement, and judging,"
   (iii) "rights exist before government," (iv) "if men have rights,
   why not women?"
2. Write a paragraph: choose one idea and explain how a person who could
   not read might still have encountered it in 1780. Name at least two
   channels (reading aloud, sermons, songs, tavern talk, theater).
3. Explain-your-reasoning: Locke defended property rights strongly.
   Could an enslaved person in Saint-Domingue use Locke's own theory to
   argue for freedom? Give a two-sentence argument *and* a one-sentence
   objection a defender of slavery might raise. (There is no single
   right answer; the key rewards reasoning that uses Locke's premises.)

## Applied task

Draft the opening three sentences of a pamphlet *you* would print in
1790, arguing that your town's assembly should be chosen by the people
who live there. Use at least one of the four ideas explicitly, and end
with one sentence aimed at readers who will hear it read aloud rather
than read it themselves.

## Exit check

Name the four ideas and one thinker for each, from memory. Then answer:
which idea do you think a king would find most threatening, and why?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** key terms are defined in the margins of this
  lesson; the adult may read Part 2 aloud. Learners may answer the image
  questions orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** the applied pamphlet may be dictated,
  drawn as a four-panel sequence (idea → press → reader → listener), or
  recorded.
- **Extension:** research one real salon host or pamphleteer (for
  example, the Paris salons or Thomas Paine's *Common Sense*, 1776) and
  write a half-page on how their audience differed from Locke's
  original readers. Note what you could and could not verify.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "The Enlightenment caused the revolutions"
— it supplied language and arguments, but grievances, taxes, war debts,
and colonial conditions supplied the fuel; ideas alone do not topple
governments. (2) "Everyone read Locke" — almost no revolutionary foot
soldier read the *Second Treatise*; most met its ideas secondhand, in
slogans and sermons. (3) Wollstonecraft as a footnote — treat her as a
theorist in her own right, not as "also, women." See
[teacher-guide](../teacher-guide.md) §2 and the
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
