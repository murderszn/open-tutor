# Project — The Rights Declarations Dossier

**Unit 03 investigation · worked across the four S3 sessions (Weeks
11–14) · ~4 sessions plus assembly time**

## Question

When a revolution declares rights "universal," who actually gets them —
and what happens to the people left out?

## Deliverable

A dossier of four parts, assembled in a folder or binder, plus a final
verdict brief (Part 4). The dossier is the unit's main evidence that
Objectives 1–5 are met; the unit assessment (Week 14, S5) draws on it
but is completed without it.

## Materials

- Lessons 01–04, Practice Sets A–D, the SVG revolution timeline
  (`assets/revolutions-timeline.svg`), notebook/inquiry journal.
- No special purchases. All sources are the unit's lessons, the
  resource pack's free no-account references, and public-domain
  declaration texts.

## Part 1 — Ideas-to-declaration trace (Week 11, S3)

Choose one Enlightenment idea (natural rights, social contract,
separation of powers, or women's rights). Trace it through two links:

1. The thinker: who wrote it, in what work and year, in one sentence
   each.
2. The declaration: one declaration (1776 or 1789) that uses the idea
   — quote or paraphrase the line, and explain in 3–4 sentences how
   the line uses the idea (not merely repeats its words).

**Deliverable:** one page: idea, thinker, declaration line, explanation.

## Part 2 — Two-revolution comparison (Week 12, S3)

Compare the American and French revolutions on a consistent frame:

| Frame | American Revolution | French Revolution |
|---|---|---|
| Two long-term causes | | |
| One triggering cause | | |
| Declaration and its key claim | | |
| What the constitution delivered | | |
| One reversal or exclusion | | |

**Deliverable:** completed table plus a 4–5 sentence verdict: which
revolution's outcome better matched its declaration, and by what
measure? (Name your measure explicitly.)

## Part 3 — Haiti case study (Week 13, S3)

Build a one-case study with four elements:

1. **Timeline:** 1791 (uprising) → 1804 (independence) → 1825
   (indemnity ordinance) → 1838 (reduction to 90 million) → 1947
   (payments end). Each entry: one sentence.
2. **Two sources:** one account from the perspective of a French
   planter or official, one from a Haitian perspective. Both must be
   labeled as *original passages written for this unit* (or as
   paraphrases of Lesson 03), never as quotations from real people.
   For each: maker, purpose, one claim, one thing it hides.
3. **Consequence chain:** at least three linked consequences of the
   indemnity (for example: indemnity → bank loans → revenue diverted
   → schools and roads unbuilt → long-term poverty). Each link must be
   stated as a claim with a reason, not as a certainty where scholars
   debate the size of effects.
4. **Judgment paragraph:** did Haiti's revolution "succeed"? Answer
   with a measure (independence kept? slavery abolished? prosperity
   achieved?) and say how the answer changes with the measure.

**Deliverable:** two pages: timeline, sources, chain, judgment.

## Part 4 — Verdict brief (Week 14, S3)

Answer the project question in a brief of 10–14 sentences:

- **Claim:** are rights declared in 1776/1789 universal? (Yes, no, or
  qualified — all three can earn full marks if argued.)
- **Evidence:** at least three pieces from at least two revolutions,
  including at least one exclusion and one later use of rights language
  by an excluded group.
- **Counterclaim:** the strongest argument against your claim, fairly
  stated, with a reply.
- **Source-limit note:** one sentence on what your sources cannot show
  (for example, the voices missing from the written record, or the
  uncertainty in indemnity effects).

**Deliverable:** the brief itself, plus a one-line list of the sources
you used (lesson numbers and resource-pack references).

## Rubric (40 pts total)

| Criterion | 9–10 pts (strong) | 5–8 pts (partial) | 0–4 pts (weak/missing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Evidence accuracy (10)** | Dates, figures, and claims match the lessons; indemnity figures correct; no invented quotations | Mostly accurate; one or two errors that do not change the argument | Repeated errors, invented quotations, or wrong indemnity figures |
| **Comparison frame (10)** | Same frame applied consistently; verdict names its measure | Frame mostly consistent; measure implied but unstated | Different frames per revolution; no verdict |
| **Source handling (10)** | Every account sourced by maker/purpose/limits; reconstructions labeled as such | Most accounts sourced; one label missing | Accounts presented as fact or as real quotations when they are not |
| **Argument quality (10)** | Claim, evidence, fair counterclaim, source-limit note all present; reasoning explicit | Most elements present; counterclaim weak or source note missing | Claim only, or argument by assertion without evidence |

**Scoring bands:** 34–40 secure (proceed to U04); 24–33 partial
(revise the weakest part with the teacher guide's prompts before the
unit assessment); below 24 (revisit Lessons 02–03 with the adult and
resubmit one part).
