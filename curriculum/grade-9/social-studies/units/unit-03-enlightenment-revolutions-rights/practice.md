## Practice Set A — Enlightenment ideas and circulation (Week 11, S2)

**A1. Vocabulary in context (4 pts).** Use each term correctly in a
sentence of your own: natural rights, social contract, separation of
powers, public sphere.

**A2. Idea sorting (4 pts).** Label each claim with the idea doing the
work — natural rights (NR), social contract (SC), separation of powers
(SP), or women's rights claim (WR):
(a) "No one may be imprisoned without a trial before independent
judges." (b) "If women share men's reason, they deserve men's rights."
(c) "A ruler who breaks faith with the people may be replaced."
(d) "One assembly must not write, enforce, and judge the same law."

**A3. Print-shop observation (5 pts).** Using Lesson 01's image or its
text alternative, answer: (a) three jobs visible; (b) what tied bundles
imply about copy numbers; (c) two groups who spread or heard ideas but
do not appear in the image; (d) why the image's status as an AI
reconstruction matters for what it can prove.

**A4. Circulation channels (4 pts).** A pamphlet printed in Paris in
1788 reaches a village where most people cannot read. Name and explain
two channels by which its ideas could still reach villagers.

**A5. Error analysis (3 pts).** "Locke caused the American Revolution."
Explain in three sentences why this sentence overstates one man's
influence (ideas need grievances, organization, and triggering events;
most revolutionaries met Locke secondhand; other causes — taxes, war
debt, colonial self-government — were necessary too).

**Writing Workshop — Week 11, S4.** Write one paragraph connecting one
Enlightenment idea to one later revolution. Requirements: a claim
sentence, the idea named and defined, the revolution named, and one
piece of evidence (a declaration line, a pamphlet, or an event) linking
them. Exchange paragraphs with the adult: does the evidence actually
show the idea at work, or only that the words were used? Revise once.

## Practice Set B — Two declarations compared (Week 12, S2)

**B1. Sequencing (5 pts).** Order earliest → latest: (a) U.S. Bill of
Rights ratified, (b) Bastille stormed, (c) Declaration of Independence
adopted, (d) Napoleon's coup, (e) French Declaration of the Rights of
Man.

**B2. Declaration close read (6 pts).** For each text, state in your
own words: (a) who the text says holds sovereignty; (b) one right it
names; (c) one group it does not free or enfranchise. (Texts: U.S.
Declaration of Independence, 1776; French Declaration of the Rights of
Man, 1789.)

**B3. Causes table (4 pts).** Complete for the French Revolution: two
long-term causes and two triggering causes, each in one sentence.

**B4. Single-cause test (3 pts).** "The French Revolution happened
because of bread prices." Give two reasons this explanation is
incomplete (the tax/privilege system and state debt were long-term
conditions; political crisis — the Estates-General and the National
Assembly — turned hardship into revolution).

**B5. Explain your reasoning (4 pts).** A historian writes, "The
American Revolution kept its republic; the French Revolution lost
its." Give one piece of evidence supporting this contrast and one
piece complicating it (France kept the abolition of feudal privilege
and rights language; America kept slavery and exclusion — "kept" needs
specifying in both cases).

**Writing Workshop — Week 12, S4.** Argument: which revolution
delivered more of what it promised? Write 8–10 sentences: claim, two
evidence pieces, one counterclaim with a reply, and a sentence stating
your measure of "delivered" (for example, stable institutions, rights
extended, or promises kept for the most people). The adult checks that
the counterclaim is fairly stated, not a straw man.

## Practice Set C — Haiti, indemnity, and contrasting accounts (Week 13, S2)

**C1. Timeline (5 pts).** Place in order with years: Haitian uprising
begins; Haitian independence; Jamaica Letter; French indemnity
ordinance; U.S. recognition of Haiti.

**C2. Indemnity arithmetic (4 pts).** (a) The demand fell from 150
million to 90 million gold francs. How many million was the reduction,
and what percentage of the original demand was that? (b) Haiti was
still paying in 1947. How many years after the 1825 ordinance is that?
(c) In one sentence, why does "reduced" not mean "affordable"?

**C3. Contrasting accounts (6 pts).** Read these two short original
passages (written for this unit, labeled as reconstructions, not
quotations):

> *Account A (a French planter writing in 1805):* "Saint-Domingue was
> ruined by rebellion. The colony that fed France now feeds no one, and
> its people have traded order for anarchy."
>
> *Account B (a Haitian officer writing in 1805):* "We bought our
> freedom with our lives and our children's lives. France calls it ruin
> because France no longer owns us."

For each account, state: maker, likely purpose, and one claim it makes.
Then state one thing each account hides, and which account better
explains why France demanded an indemnity in 1825 — with a reason.

**C4. Bolívar's argument (4 pts).** From Lesson 03, state in your own
words: (a) what the Jamaica Letter argues about Spanish rule; (b) what
it argues about Spanish America's future; (c) one tension inside
Bolívar's own position (liberty vs. order; independence vs.
preparedness for republican government).

**C5. Perspective check (3 pts).** Why did Pétion's aid to Bolívar
come with a condition about slavery, and what does that condition tell
you about Haiti's self-understanding in 1816? (Two sentences.)

**Writing Workshop — Week 13, S4.** Perspective writing: why was
Haiti's revolution treated differently in its own century? Write two
paragraphs: the first names at least two causes (fear of the example
spreading; diplomatic deference to France; racism in slaveholding
societies); the second explains which cause you find most important and
why. Cite at least one figure (indemnity, recognition year, or
independence year) as evidence.

## Practice Set D — Tracing one idea across three revolutions (Week 14, S2)

**D1. Trace (6 pts).** Choose one idea — natural rights, social
contract, or separation of powers — and trace it across three
revolutions (American, French, Haitian, or Spanish American). For each
revolution, give one piece of evidence (a declaration line, a law, or
an event) showing the idea at work *or* being violated. Three
revolutions × one evidence piece each, plus a one-sentence conclusion
about whether the idea grew stronger or weaker across your three cases.

**D2. Inclusion table (8 pts).** Complete the Lesson 04 table from
memory for two revolutions of your choice (rows) and all four groups
(columns): women, enslaved people, Indigenous nations, free people of
color / propertyless men. Each cell: "gained," "denied," or "mixed,"
plus a phrase of evidence. (8 cells × 1 pt.)

**D3. Persistence reasons (4 pts).** Name the four reasons exclusions
persisted (economic interest, political fear, inherited ideas, war and
instability) and match each to one example from this unit.

**D4. Writers' perspectives (4 pts).** An 1850 U.S. textbook calls
Haiti's independence "ruin"; a modern history calls it "a founding
act punished by indemnity." Explain in three sentences why the two
accounts differ, naming at least two factors (author's society and
slavery politics; sources available; purpose/audience).

**D5. Error analysis (3 pts).** "People back then never thought about
women's rights." Refute in two sentences using Wollstonecraft and de
Gouges (both raised the claim in print in 1791–92; the claims were
heard and rejected, not unthought).

**Writing Workshop — Week 14, S4.** Deliberation prep (see Lesson 04
applied task): two positions, each with a claim, two evidence pieces,
and a reply to the other side's best argument. The adult checks that
both sides are argued fairly — a deliberation won by caricaturing the
other side is marked down, not up.
