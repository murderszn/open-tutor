# Answer Key — Unit 04: Cell Cycle, Mitosis, and Meiosis

**Teacher use only.** Kept separate from student materials. Every lesson
item, practice task, quiz question, assessment task, and project row is
solved here; the adult checks reasoning, not just final numbers. Model
organism: 2n = 4 (n = 2) unless stated otherwise. Human: 2n = 46, n = 23.
Counting rule used throughout: **one centromere = one chromosome**,
however many chromatids it holds.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice.** 1. Order: G1 → S → G2 → mitosis → cytokinesis.
Checkpoints sit between stages (end of G1, G2/M transition, metaphase);
G0 is an exit from G1, not a step in the cycle. 2. Yes — S phase comes
before G2, so by G2 the DNA is already copied. 3. Pause at the G2/M
checkpoint: dividing with half-copied DNA would give daughters with
incomplete instruction sets.

**Independent practice.** 1. Chain: G1 — growth + building blocks; S —
DNA copied (sister chromatids formed); G2 — final preparations and
energy stores; mitotic phase — chromosomes sorted (mitosis) and
cytoplasm split (cytokinesis). 2. Before S: 6 chromosomes, 6 chromatids.
After S: 6 chromosomes, 12 chromatids. Each daughter: 6 chromosomes, 6
chromatids. 3. Interphase holds growth, normal cell function, and the
whole DNA-copying job; the mitotic phase only sorts and splits what
interphase prepared, so it is brief by comparison. 4. Acceptable
correction: G0 cells are alive and working — they have simply left the
division cycle. Mature nerve cells sit in G0 permanently while
transmitting signals for a lifetime; "not dividing" is not "dead."

**Application.** Circle with arcs G1, S, G2, mitotic phase; three stars
(G1 checkpoint: "Is the DNA undamaged and the cell ready?"; G2/M
checkpoint: "Is all DNA copied correctly?"; metaphase checkpoint: "Are
all chromosomes attached to the spindle?"); G0 exit arrow from G1.

**Exit check.** 1. S phase. 2. S phase made a complete copy of every
chromosome first, and mitosis dealt one copy of each to each daughter.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice.** 1. Prophase, prometaphase, metaphase, anaphase,
telophase. 2. 2n = 6: 12 chromatids line up at metaphase (6 chromosomes);
each daughter receives 6 chromosomes. 3. The metaphase checkpoint holds
anaphase until every chromosome is attached; starting early would drag
an unattached chromosome to the wrong side and unbalance the daughters.

**Independent practice.** 1. Sketch must show: prophase (condensing
chromosomes), prometaphase (fibers attached), metaphase (4 chromosomes,
8 chromatids on the plate), anaphase (sisters parting), telophase +
cytokinesis (two cells, 4 chromosomes each). 2. 2n = 8: after S, 8
chromosomes and 16 chromatids; each daughter, 8 chromosomes. 3. A cut
heals by mitosis, which copies the genome exactly — the replacement
cells must run the same program as the lost skin cells, not a shuffled
one. 4. False. After mitosis each daughter has the *same* chromosome
number as the parent.

**Application.** Metaphase model: 4 duplicated chromosomes on the line;
anaphase model: sisters moving apart; final sketch: two sets of 4
single-chromatid chromosomes, compositions identical.

**Exit check.** 1. Mitosis turns one nucleus into two genetically
identical nuclei (cytokinesis then makes two cells). 2. 10 chromosomes.

**Extension.** 30 trillion ≈ 2^45 (2^45 = 35,155,827,968,000 ≈ 35
trillion), so about 45 doublings is the minimum. The real number of
rounds is larger because cells die, many lineages stop dividing, and
not every division adds a surviving cell.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice.** 1. Final model: four cells, each with 2
chromosomes (one dark-or-light member per pair, with the marked
crossing-over swap visible in at least one gamete set). 2. n = 3 →
2^3 = 8 combinations. Check by listing: with pairs A, B, C and two
parental origins each, the sequences AAA, AAB, ABA, ABB, BAA, BAB, BBA,
BBB cover all 8. 3. Sister chromatids are identical copies, so swapping
segments between them changes nothing; only nonsister chromatids of
homologs carry different versions to exchange.

**Independent practice.** 1. 2n = 6: after meiosis I, 3 chromosomes per
cell (each still 2 chromatids); after meiosis II, 3 chromosomes per
gamete (1 chromatid each). Combinations: 2^3 = 8. 2. Two S phases would
copy the DNA twice, so gametes would remain diploid; fertilization
would then double the number every generation (46 → 92 → 184 …). One
S phase plus two divisions is what halves the number. 3. Errors: (a)
meiosis produces four *varied* cells, not identical ones; (b) it is not
"mitosis twice" — mitosis twice would give 4 diploid cells, while
meiosis gives 4 haploid cells because the second division has no
preceding S phase. 4. Zygote: 23 + 23 = 46. The number is stable because
each generation halves (meiosis) and then restores (fertilization).

**Application.** 12 coin-flip draws for 2 pairs: expected share is 3 per
combination (12 ÷ 4). Any tally is acceptable if the learner notes
whether all 4 appeared and why a small sample can miss a real
possibility (random draws need not cover every outcome).

**Exit check.** 1. Two divisions, one replication. 2. Crossing over
(prophase I) and independent assortment (metaphase I).

**Extension.** Meiosis-I nondisjunction for 2n = 4: gametes carry 3, 3,
1, 1 chromosomes (the stuck pair gives one side an extra chromosome
and the other side one fewer; meiosis II then splits sisters without
changing those totals).

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice.** 1. Self-check against the lesson table; the row
most often missed is "daughter cells" (2 vs. 4) or "chromosome number"
(2n vs. n). 2. Mitosis: budding rebuilds body tissue from the parent's
own genome — identical cells, no gametes involved. 3. 22 + 23 = 45.

**Independent practice.** 1. Acceptable: mitosis copies the full genome
into two identical cells; it never halves the number, so a mitotic
"offspring" cell would carry 46 chromosomes, and fertilization would
then double the count — gametes must be haploid, and only meiosis
halves. 2. Gametes: 3, 3, 4, 2. (Normal meiosis-II cell: two gametes with
3 each. Affected cell: one gamete with 4 chromatids-as-chromosomes,
one with 2.) 3. A mitosis error sits in one body-cell line; a meiosis
error sits in a gamete, so every cell of the resulting organism carries
it. 4. False as stated: the *mechanics* of separating sisters resemble
mitosis, but meiosis II starts from haploid cells with duplicated
chromosomes and ends with haploid products, while mitosis starts
diploid and ends diploid.

**Application.** Caption must name: the mis-separated pair, the four
gamete counts (3, 3, 1, 1 for 2n = 4), and the fertilization consequence
(a 3-gamete plus a normal 2-gamete gives 5; a 1-gamete plus a normal
2-gamete gives 3).

**Exit check.** 1. Mitosis: a cutting is body growth, needing identical
full-genome cells, not gametes. 2. Nondisjunction is the failure of
homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice.** 1. Without assortment, only the parent's 2 original
combinations would appear, about 50 each. The table shows all 4
combinations near 25 each, which rules that out. 2. No: experiment C
shows mutations, not inheritance. You would also need evidence the
mutations occurred in cells leading to gametes. 3. Acceptable sample:
"Heritable variation comes from meiotic combinations (experiment A),
replication errors (experiment B), and environment-caused mutations
(experiment C)."

**Independent practice.** 1. 15 ÷ 2 = 7.5×. A small rate matters because
it applies at every generation across a whole population: rare events
accumulate. 2. It would weaken the meiotic-combination source. Check
next: whether the gamete sample was random and large enough, and
whether crossing over still produced recombinant chromatids even if
whole-chromosome combinations looked unshuffled. 3. Acceptable CER
paragraph: claim (more than one source) + evidence (A: all 4
combinations, 27/24/26/23; B: 2 → 15 per 10,000 with repair weakened; C:
2 → 47 per 10,000 with UV) + reasoning (each experiment isolates one
variable, so each supports a different source; together they show
variation has multiple causes). 4. Skin cells are not in the gamete
line: their mutations die with the body and are never transmitted,
however the mutation arose.

**Application.** Any honest objection earns credit (e.g., "100 gametes
is a small sample" — answered: the counts cluster tightly around the
predicted 25; "the strains might differ in another way" — answered: the
brief states the single difference). Judge the rebuttal, not the side.

**Exit check.** 1. Meiotic combinations; replication errors;
environment-caused mutations. 2. Experiment C: 2 → 47 per 10,000.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice.** 1. "The cells express different genes" is true.
Predicted observable difference: different proteins made, hence
different jobs (e.g., contraction vs. barrier/protection). 2. Correct
order: (a) checkpoints fail → (b) damaged cells divide → (c) errors
accumulate → (d) a tumor grows. 3. Mitosis alone gives *more* identical
cells; differentiation must be added to make them *different* —
tissues and organs, not just a bigger ball of cells.

**Independent practice.** 1. Acceptable paragraph: the genome is a
library every cell owns; each cell type reads a different selection of
books; the books do not change, the reading list does; that is why
identical DNA yields different cells. 2. "Just decide" is wrong because
expression patterns are stabilized during development by regulatory
states; a mature cell cannot casually re-read its whole library, though
some cells retain limited flexibility. 3. Fewer cell generations mean
fewer accumulated errors and less spread: early detection catches the
process before the error load and the mass grow. 4. False: cancer
typically requires several control failures accumulated over time; a
single mutation is usually repaired, neutral, or insufficient.

**Application.** Manual must name: mitosis as the copier, differentiation
(expression choices) as the department assignment, checkpoints as
inspectors, and the consequence when inspectors fail (uncontrolled
division, accumulating errors).

**Exit check.** 1. Differentiation is a cell becoming specialized for a
particular job. 2. Mitosis provides more cells; differentiation provides
different cell types.

## Practice sessions

**A.** 1. G1, S, G2, mitosis, cytokinesis. 2. G1: growth + building
blocks; S: DNA copied (sister chromatids); G2: final preparations;
mitosis: chromosomes sorted into two nuclei; cytokinesis: cytoplasm
split, two cells. 3. (a) Pause, G1 checkpoint. (b) Proceed, G2/M
checkpoint. (c) Pause, metaphase checkpoint. (d) Stays in G0 — the
signal does not override permanent G0 in a mature nerve cell. 4. Table
as printed (10/10; 10/20; 10/10). 5. Acceptable rewrite: "Interphase is
the active preparation period: the cell grows and copies its DNA."

**B.** 1. (a) Metaphase. (b) Anaphase. (c) Prophase. (d) Telophase into
cytokinesis. (e) Prometaphase. 2. Metaphase: 8 chromosomes, 16
chromatids. Daughters: 8 chromosomes each. 3. Chromosome count is a
centromere count: S phase doubles chromatids, not centromeres, so the
count stays 8 while the DNA amount doubles. 4. Not mitosis: separating
homologs is meiosis I. If a mitotic cell did it, daughters would carry
one homolog of each pair (haploid number, still duplicated) instead of
identical diploid sets. 5. Correction: after S phase the cell has 46
chromosomes and 92 chromatids; after division each daughter has 46
chromosomes and 46 chromatids.

**C.** 1. Expected 25 per combination; observed 27/24/26/23 — a close
fit (all within 3 of 25). 2. 15 ÷ 2 = 7.5×; isolated variable: the
repair system (no UV in either group). 3. 47 ÷ 2 = 23.5×; isolated
variable: UV exposure (same standard strain). 4. The headline claims
inheritance, but experiment C shows only mutation counts; it is silent
on whether the mutations were in gamete-forming cells. 5. Acceptable
sample: "Meiotic combinations are real — experiment A shows all 4
combinations near 25 each — but they are not the only source:
experiment B shows repair-weakened cells mutating 7.5× more often, and
experiment C shows UV raising mutations 23.5×."

**D.** 1. Acceptable differences: e.g., muscle cells make contractile
proteins, skin cells make barrier proteins — because those genes are
expressed in one cell type and not (or less) in the other, while the DNA
sequence is the same. 2. (a) Skipped S: daughters with half the
instructions — nonviable or severely impaired. (b) Ignored metaphase
checkpoint: one daughter with an extra chromosome, one missing one.
(c) Erased expression pattern: loss of specialization; the cell stops
doing its job reliably. (d) Repeated checkpoint failure: accumulating
errors, tumor growth in the conceptual model. 3. Acceptable: "Mitosis
gives more cells; differentiation makes them different — an organism
needs both more and different." 4. No contradiction: the red blood cell
had the genome during development and ejected its nucleus as part of
its specialization; it is a named exception to the general rule, not
evidence of a second genome.

## Formative quiz (week 18)

1. S phase replicates DNA; without a complete copy, daughters would
   receive partial instruction sets. 2. G1, S, G2. 3. (a) 6 chromosomes,
   12 chromatids. (b) 6 chromosomes, 6 chromatids each. 4. Prophase
   (chromosomes condense, envelope breaks down), prometaphase (fibers
   attach, envelope gone), metaphase (chromosomes on the plate),
   anaphase (sisters pulled apart), telophase (envelopes re-form,
   cytoplasm about to split). 5. identical. 6. Four cells, 2 chromosomes
   each: meiosis I separates homologs (4 → 2 per cell), meiosis II
   separates sisters (→ 4 cells with 2 each). 7. Crossing over: segment
   swap between nonsister chromatids of homologs, prophase I.
   Independent assortment: random orientation of each pair at the
   plate, metaphase I. 8. 2^4 = 16. 9. Replication errors (experiment B:
   2 → 15 per 10,000) or environment-caused mutations (experiment C:
   2 → 47 per 10,000). 10. gene expression. 11. 47 chromosomes;
   nondisjunction. 12. True.

## Unit assessment

**Part A.** 1. After S: 4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids; the count did not
double because sister chromatids share centromeres. 2. Metaphase: 4
chromosomes, 8 chromatids on the plate; daughters: 4 chromosomes each.
3. After meiosis I: 2 cells, 2 chromosomes each (4 chromatids each).
After meiosis II: 4 cells, 2 chromosomes each (2 chromatids each, each
counted as a chromosome). 4. Mitosis daughters: 46 each. Gametes: 23
each. Combinations: 2^23 = 8,388,608.

**Part B.** 5. Table as in Lesson 4; two sentences must state that
mitosis preserves number for body growth/repair while meiosis halves
and shuffles for gametes, so neither can substitute for the other.
6. Gametes: 3, 3, 1, 1. Zygote: 5 (from a 3-gamete + normal 2) or 3 (from
a 1-gamete + normal 2); full marks for either with arithmetic shown.
7. Paragraph must: state one genome, explain expression differences,
use "gene expression" correctly, and avoid claiming different DNA.

**Part C.** 8. Full marks: claim (more than one source) + at least one
number from each experiment (A: 27/24/26/23 across all 4 combinations;
B: 2 → 15 per 10,000; C: 2 → 47 per 10,000) + all three sources named +
one stated limit (fictional practice data; experiment C alone does not
prove gamete-line inheritance; small samples). A paragraph citing only
two experiments, or naming sources without numbers, earns at most 5/8.
