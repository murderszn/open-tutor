# Lesson 06 — Differentiation, and When Control Fails

**Student goal:** I can explain how one genome produces many cell types,
and describe at a conceptual level what happens when cell-division
control fails.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 12 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "A skin cell and a nerve cell in your body carry the same DNA. So
why don't they look or act alike?" Accept guesses. The answer is not
different DNA — it is different *use* of the same DNA, and today is
about that switchboard.

## Teaching

**Differentiation** is the process by which a cell becomes specialized
for a particular job. Every cell in a multicellular organism starts from
one fertilized egg, and (with rare exceptions) every cell carries the
same genome. What differs is **gene expression**: which genes are turned
on, in which cells, at which times, and how strongly.

Think of the genome as a library every cell owns, and gene expression as
which books each cell has open. A muscle cell has the books for
contractile proteins open; a red blood cell, during its development, has
the hemoglobin books open (and later loses its nucleus entirely — an
edge case worth naming, not a contradiction: losing the nucleus is part
of that cell's specialization). A nerve cell has signaling books open.
The books themselves are identical; the reading list is not.

Mitosis plus differentiation is how one cell becomes a complex organism
(HS-LS1-4's second half): mitosis makes *more* cells; differentiation
makes them *different*. Growth alone would give a bigger ball of
identical cells. Differentiation gives tissues, organs, and systems.

**When control fails.** The cell cycle's checkpoints (Lesson 1) exist
because division must be regulated: divide when the body needs new
cells, stop when it does not. If checkpoint control breaks down, cells
can divide when they should not, ignore stop signals, and accumulate
further errors as they keep dividing. A growing mass of such cells is a
**tumor**; when cells from it invade other tissues or spread, the
condition is called **cancer**. This unit treats that mechanism
conceptually only: it does not diagnose, predict, or discuss any
person's health, and it draws no conclusions about any individual's
risk. The teaching point is the control logic — a system that normally
pauses for damage, and what uncontrolled division means for the
organism — not medicine.

Two clarifications that prevent later confusion: (1) cancer is not a
single disease and not caused by one single event; it typically involves
several control failures accumulated over time. (2) Not every tumor
spreads; the word "tumor" names the mass, "cancer" names the spreading,
invasive condition.

**Key vocabulary:** *differentiation* (becoming specialized),
*gene expression* (which genes are active in a cell, when, and how
strongly), *tumor* (a mass of cells dividing without normal control),
*cancer* (conceptual: cells that divide uncontrollably and invade or
spread).

## Worked example 1 — same genome, different jobs

**Question:** A cheek cell and a liver cell from one person are tested.
Their DNA sequences match. Yet one makes large amounts of digestive
enzymes and the other does not. Explain the difference without
contradicting the matching DNA.

**Reasoning:** The DNA *sequence* is the same, but gene expression
differs: in the liver cell, the genes for those enzymes are turned on
strongly; in the cheek cell, they are off or low. Differentiation is
not a change in the instruction book — it is a change in which
instructions are being read. That is why "same genome" and "different
cells" are compatible facts, not a paradox.

## Worked example 2 — tracing the control failure

**Question:** A cell's G1 checkpoint stops working, so it divides even
when its DNA is damaged. Its daughters inherit the damage, and one of
their checkpoints later fails too. Explain, step by step, why the
problem gets worse over generations of cells rather than staying flat.

**Reasoning:** Each failed checkpoint removes one safety net. The first
failure lets damaged DNA into daughter cells; those daughters copy the
damage (S phase copies whatever is there, errors included); a second
failure lets further errors accumulate in the next generation. Because
each generation inherits the previous errors *and* can add new ones,
the total error load grows rather than resetting. Uncontrolled division
plus accumulating errors is the conceptual core of tumor growth: more
cells, worse instructions, fewer working brakes.

## Guided practice

1. Two cells have identical genomes. Cell A expresses genes P and Q;
   cell B expresses genes Q and R. Which claim is true: "the cells have
   different DNA" or "the cells express different genes"? What observable
   difference would you predict between them?
2. Order this causal chain: (a) checkpoints fail, (b) damaged cells
   divide, (c) errors accumulate across generations, (d) a tumor grows.
   (b → a is wrong; correct order: a → b → c → d — checkpoint failure
   first, then division of damaged cells, then accumulation, then the
   mass.)
3. Why is "mitosis makes more cells" an incomplete explanation for how a
   fertilized egg becomes a human? What must be added?

## Independent practice

1. Explain differentiation to a younger student using the library
   analogy, in four sentences. Your explanation must include why the
   genome stays the same.
2. A student says, "If all cells have the same DNA, a muscle cell could
   just decide to become a nerve cell." What is wrong with "just
   decide," and what actually locks a cell's identity in place?
   (Expression patterns are stabilized by regulatory states built
   during development; a mature cell cannot casually re-read its whole
   library. Some cells retain flexibility, but "deciding" is not the
   mechanism.)
3. A tumor is found early, while small and non-spreading. Using the
   control-failure logic, explain why early detection matters in terms
   of cell generations, not just size.
4. True or false, with a correction if false: "Cancer is caused by a
   single mutation in a single cell." (False: it typically requires
   several control failures accumulated over time; a single mutation
   alone is usually repaired, harmless, or insufficient.)

## Application

Write a one-paragraph "system manual" for the human body as if it were
an organization: who copies the instructions (mitosis), who decides
which instructions each department reads (differentiation), and who
inspects the work (checkpoints). End with one sentence on what happens
when the inspectors stop showing up.

## Exit check

1. In one sentence, what is differentiation?
2. Name the two halves of HS-LS1-4's model: what does mitosis provide,
   and what does differentiation provide? (More cells; different cell
   types.)

## Supports

- If "gene expression" feels abstract: use the library analogy, then
  drop it — ask the learner to restate the idea without the analogy
  ("which genes are on").
- If cancer discussion raises personal worries: acknowledge, redirect
  to the conceptual mechanism, and invite the learner to talk with a
  trusted adult or clinician about any personal concern. No health
  information is collected in this unit.
- Oral, drawn, or dictated answers are accepted for every item.

## Extension

Stem cells are cells that have not yet fully differentiated and can
become several cell types. Propose one reason medicine is interested in
them, and one reason their use raises questions that science alone
cannot settle. (A paragraph; no position is graded as "correct.")
