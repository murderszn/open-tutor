# Lesson 04 — Mitosis vs. Meiosis: Same Parts, Different Jobs

**Student goal:** I can compare mitosis and meiosis by purpose, divisions,
and products, and explain what happens when chromosome separation fails.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 7 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Printed copy of [`assets/cell-division-stages.svg`](../assets/cell-division-stages.svg)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "Your body needs two things: replace a scraped knee, and produce a
child who is not your clone. Which division does each job, and why can't
one division do both?" Collect answers; today's table settles it.

## Teaching

Mitosis and meiosis share parts — DNA replication, spindle fibers,
chromosome sorting — but they answer different questions:

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Growth, repair, replacement; asexual reproduction in some organisms | Gamete production for sexual reproduction |
| DNA replications | 1 (S phase) | 1 (S phase) |
| Divisions | 1 | 2 (no S phase between them) |
| Daughter cells | 2 | 4 |
| Chromosome number in daughters | Same as parent (2n → 2n) | Half the parent (2n → n) |
| Genetic identity of daughters | Identical to parent (barring rare errors) | Varied: crossing over + independent assortment |
| Where it happens (humans) | Body (somatic) cells throughout life | Reproductive cells in ovaries/testes |

The table is the lesson's memory device: **mitosis copies, meiosis
halves and shuffles.**

**Nondisjunction** is the failure mode: homologous chromosomes (meiosis
I) or sister chromatids (meiosis II, or mitosis) fail to separate, so
one daughter cell gets an extra chromosome and another gets one too few.
Example for 2n = 4 with a meiosis-I failure of one pair: the four
gametes end up with 3, 3, 1, 1 chromosomes instead of 2, 2, 2, 2. If a
gamete with an extra chromosome is fertilized by a normal gamete, the
zygote has one extra chromosome overall; if a gamete missing one is
fertilized, the zygote is one short. Most such zygotes do not develop;
the concept matters here because it shows why exact separation is not a
detail — it is the whole point of the sorting machinery and its
checkpoints.

Mitosis can mis-separate too, but the consequence is different: one
body cell line carries the error, not every cell of a new organism.
That asymmetry is worth stating plainly: errors in meiosis affect the
next generation; errors in mitosis affect part of one body.

**Key vocabulary:** *somatic cell* (body cell, not a gamete),
*nondisjunction* (failure of chromosomes/chromatids to separate),
*asexual reproduction* (one parent, offspring genetically identical
barring mutation).

## Worked example 1 — which division?

**Question:** For each job, name the division and defend it in one
sentence: (a) a lizard regrows its tail; (b) a flowering plant makes
pollen; (c) your bone marrow replaces blood cells daily.

**Reasoning:** (a) **Mitosis** — the tail must be rebuilt from the
lizard's own genome, identical cells, no halving. (b) **Meiosis** —
pollen grains are gametes; they must be haploid so fertilization
restores the full number. (c) **Mitosis** — blood cells are body cells
being replaced, and each replacement must carry the full instruction
set.

## Worked example 2 — nondisjunction counts

**Question:** A human cell (2n = 46) undergoes meiosis. One homologous
pair fails to separate in meiosis I. What chromosome numbers do the
four gametes carry? If the gamete with the extra chromosome is
fertilized by a normal gamete (n = 23), what is the zygote's count?

**Reasoning:** Normal meiosis I would give two cells with 23
chromosomes each. With one pair stuck together, one cell gets 24
chromosomes and the other gets 22. Meiosis II splits sisters without
changing those totals, so the four gametes carry **24, 24, 22, 22**.
Fertilizing the 24 gamete with a normal 23 gamete gives a zygote with
**47 chromosomes** — one extra. (This is the counting logic only; which
chromosome is extra, and what it means medically, is beyond this unit.)

## Guided practice

1. Fill in the comparison table from memory, then check it against the
   lesson table. Which row did you miss, and why is that row the one
   people confuse?
2. A hydra buds a new hydra from its body wall. Mitosis or meiosis?
   Defend your answer with the table's "purpose" row.
3. In the nondisjunction example above, what would the zygote's count
   be if the *22* gamete were fertilized by a normal 23 gamete? (45.)

## Independent practice

1. Write a three-sentence explanation a younger student could follow:
   why can't mitosis make gametes?
2. A cell with 2n = 6 undergoes meiosis II nondisjunction (sister
   chromatids of one chromosome fail to separate in one of the two
   meiosis-II cells). Work out the four gamete chromosome counts.
   (Normal MII cell: two gametes with 3 each. Affected MII cell: one
   gamete with 4, one with 2. Full set: 3, 3, 4, 2.)
3. Explain why a mitosis error in one skin cell is less consequential
   for offspring than a meiosis error in a gamete.
4. True or false, with a correction if false: "Meiosis II is exactly
   like mitosis." (False in one respect: the cells entering meiosis II
   are haploid with duplicated chromosomes, so its products are haploid;
   the *mechanics* of separating sisters resemble mitosis.)

## Application

Take the Lesson 3 pipe-cleaner model and deliberately mis-separate one
pair in meiosis I. Lay out the four resulting gamete sets, label each
count, and write a caption: "What went wrong, and what it would mean
after fertilization." Keep the caption to three sentences.

## Exit check

1. Mitosis: 2 cells, 2n each. Meiosis: 4 cells, n each. Which division
   would you use to grow a plant cutting into a new plant, and why?
2. Define nondisjunction in one sentence.

## Supports

- If the table blurs: cover the "Meiosis" column and reconstruct it
  from the rule "one replication, two divisions."
- If nondisjunction counts slip: track one pair at a time; the other
  pairs separate normally and just add their counts.
- Oral, drawn, or modeled answers are accepted for every item.

## Extension

Some organisms reproduce asexually by mitosis alone for many generations
and then switch to sexual reproduction under stress. Propose, in a
paragraph, one advantage of each mode that would explain the switch —
variation when conditions change, versus speed and certainty when
conditions are good.

## Teacher note

Students often answer "meiosis" for any reproduction question, including
regrowth and cloning. Insist on the purpose row: gametes vs. body cells.
Also correct the "meiosis II = mitosis" shortcut: same mechanics,
different starting ploidy, different products. Answers: see
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
