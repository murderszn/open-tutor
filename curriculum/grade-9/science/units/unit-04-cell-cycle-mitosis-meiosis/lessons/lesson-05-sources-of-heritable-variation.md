# Lesson 05 — Where Heritable Variation Comes From

**Student goal:** I can make and defend a claim, using data, that
heritable variation comes from meiotic combinations, replication errors,
and/or environment-caused mutations.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 10 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- The fictional practice dataset below (also used in Practice C)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "Two parents have three children. None of the three is genetically
identical to a parent or to each other. Name as many causes as you can
before we open the data." Most learners name meiosis; few name
replication errors or environmental mutations unprompted. Today's data
adds the missing causes.

## Teaching

HS-LS3-2 asks for a claim defended with evidence, not a list memorized.
The three sources it names are:

1. **New genetic combinations through meiosis.** Crossing over builds
   recombinant chromatids; independent assortment deals parental
   chromosomes into new combinations. Every gamete is a fresh hand.
   Evidence for this source looks like: gametes (or offspring) showing
   combinations that neither parent chromosome carried intact.
2. **Viable errors occurring during replication.** DNA copying is
   remarkably accurate, but errors occur. Most are repaired; a few
   survive ("viable") and become permanent changes — **mutations** — in
   the daughter cells. If the error happens in a cell line that leads to
   gametes, it is heritable.
3. **Mutations caused by environmental factors.** Some agents damage DNA
   — for example, ultraviolet (UV) light and certain chemicals. A
   mutation caused this way is heritable only if it occurs in a cell
   that will become (or produce) gametes; a mutation in a skin cell is
   not passed to children, however it arose.

A strong claim names which sources the evidence supports and which it
does not, and says what would change the answer. "Variation exists,
therefore all three sources acted" is not a defense — it is a guess
wearing a lab coat.

### Fictional practice dataset — NOT real observations

A teacher invented these numbers to practice claim defense. No real
organism was measured.

| Experiment | Setup | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A. Gamete combinations | 100 gametes sampled from one parent with 2n = 4 (n = 2); each gamete's two chromosomes classified by parental origin | All 4 possible combinations appeared: 27, 24, 26, 23 |
| B. Replication-error strain | Two yeast-like lab strains grown identically without UV: standard strain vs. a strain with a weakened copying-repair system; mutant colonies counted per 10,000 colonies | Standard: 2 per 10,000. Weakened-repair: 15 per 10,000 |
| C. UV exposure | Standard strain grown with and without UV light; mutant colonies counted per 10,000 colonies | No UV: 2 per 10,000. With UV: 47 per 10,000 |

Read experiment A as evidence about meiosis: with only 4 combinations
possible, all 4 appeared in roughly equal shares (about 25 each) —
exactly what independent assortment predicts. Read B as evidence about
replication: the only difference between the strains is repair, and the
mutant count rose from 2 to 15. Read C as evidence about environment:
the only difference is UV, and the count rose from 2 to 47.

**Key vocabulary:** *mutation* (a lasting change in DNA sequence),
*viable error* (a copying error that survives repair and cell division),
*recombinant chromatid* (a chromatid carrying a new mix of parental
segments after crossing over), *claim–evidence–reasoning* (the argument
frame from U01).

## Worked example 1 — defending "meiosis causes variation"

**Question:** Use experiment A to defend the claim that meiosis creates
new combinations. What is the evidence, and what is the reasoning?

**Reasoning:** *Claim:* meiosis produces new chromosome combinations.
*Evidence:* in 100 gametes from a 2n = 4 parent, all 4 combinations
predicted by independent assortment appeared, in near-equal counts
(27, 24, 26, 23). *Reasoning:* if gametes simply copied the parent, only
the parent's two original combinations would appear; the presence of
all four, in the proportions random assortment predicts, shows the
parental chromosomes were dealt into new combinations. (Crossing over
would add recombinant chromatids on top of this whole-chromosome
shuffling.)

## Worked example 2 — separating two mutation causes

**Question:** A student claims experiments B and C prove the same thing.
Do they? Use the numbers.

**Reasoning:** No. Both show higher mutant counts, but they isolate
different causes. In B, the only difference is the repair system (no UV
in either group): 2 → 15 per 10,000 points to errors made *during
replication* that survived because repair was weak. In C, the only
difference is UV (same standard strain): 2 → 47 per 10,000 points to
damage *from the environment*. Same outcome measure, different isolated
variable, different cause. A claim that names only one of them is
incomplete; a claim that merges them is imprecise.

## Guided practice

1. In experiment A, what counts would you expect if independent
   assortment were *false* and gametes only copied parental
   chromosomes? (Only 2 combinations, about 50 each.) How does the real
   table rule that out?
2. Experiment C's UV group shows 47 per 10,000. Does that prove UV
   causes *heritable* variation? What extra fact would you need?
   (You would need to know the mutations occurred in cells leading to
   gametes; the table alone shows mutations, not inheritance.)
3. Draft a one-sentence claim covering all three sources, citing one
   experiment per source.

## Independent practice

1. A critic says experiment B's difference (2 vs. 15) is too small to
   matter. Answer with arithmetic: how many times higher is 15 than 2?
   (7.5×.) Why does a small absolute rate still matter over many
   generations?
2. Suppose experiment A had found only 2 combinations (50 and 50).
   Which source would that result have *weakened*, and what would you
   check next? (Meiotic recombination/assortment; check whether the
   sample was truly random, and whether crossing over still produced
   recombinant chromatids.)
3. Write a full CER paragraph defending this claim: "Heritable variation
   comes from more than one source." Cite all three experiments.
4. A skin cell acquires a UV-caused mutation. Explain why that mutation
   is not heritable, even though UV is a genuine mutation source.

## Application

You are the skeptic. Choose one of the three sources and write the
strongest objection you can to its evidence (sample size, alternative
explanation, missing control). Then answer your own objection. Trade
with a partner or the adult and judge whose rebuttal is stronger.

## Exit check

1. Name the three sources of heritable variation in HS-LS3-2.
2. One line: which experiment supports the environmental-mutation
   source, and what is its key number pair? (Experiment C: 2 → 47 per
   10,000.)

## Supports

- If CER structure slips: use the frame "Claim (one sentence) →
  Evidence (numbers, not adjectives) → Reasoning (why the numbers
  point to this cause and not another)."
- If "heritable" blurs: heritable = present in a gamete or its cell
  line. A mutation anywhere else dies with the body.
- Oral or dictated answers are accepted for every item.

## Extension

Real-world connection (conceptual only, no personal data): some
families carry a weakened DNA-repair system and develop certain cancers
more often. Explain, using experiment B's logic, why a repair weakness
raises mutation rates in *body* cells too — and why that is a health
matter for doctors, not a conclusion this unit can draw about any
individual.

## Teacher note

Watch for two errors: (1) "mutations are always harmful" — most are
neutral, some harmful, a few useful in context; the unit's claim is
about *variation*, not benefit; (2) treating the fictional dataset as
real — remind learners at least twice that these numbers were invented
for practice, and that a real claim would need real, replicated data.
