# Lesson 03 — Meiosis: Two Divisions, Four Varied Cells

**Student goal:** I can model meiosis as one DNA replication followed by
two divisions, explain crossing over and independent assortment, and
predict chromosome numbers and combination counts in the gametes.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Printed copy of [`assets/cell-division-stages.svg`](../assets/cell-division-stages.svg)
- Two colors of pipe cleaners (dark = one parent's chromosomes, light =
  the other parent's), beads for centromeres

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "If a human egg and sperm each carried the full 46 chromosomes,
how many would the child have? And the grandchild?" (92, then 184 — the
number doubles every generation.) Something must halve the number before
fertilization. That something is today's lesson.

## Teaching

**Meiosis** is the nuclear division that produces **gametes** — sex cells
(egg and sperm in animals) — with half the chromosome number. It starts
like mitosis: one round of DNA replication in S phase. Then, unlike
mitosis, it runs **two** divisions in a row, with no second S phase in
between:

- **Meiosis I — the separating division.** Homologous chromosomes —
  the two members of each pair, one inherited from each parent — pair up
  and then separate. Two cells result, each with half the chromosomes
  (but each chromosome still holds two sister chromatids).
- **Meiosis II — the splitting division.** Sister chromatids separate,
  much like mitosis. Four cells result, each with one chromatid per
  chromosome: haploid (n) cells.

Two events in meiosis I create variation:

1. **Crossing over (prophase I).** Homologous chromosomes pair tightly
   (a **tetrad**: four chromatids) and swap segments between nonsister
   chromatids. The chromatids that result carry new combinations of
   genes that existed in neither parent chromosome alone.
2. **Independent assortment (metaphase I).** Each homologous pair lines
   up at the middle independently of the other pairs. Which member faces
   which pole is random per pair, so the combination of parental
   chromosomes each gamete receives is a lottery draw.

The counting rule: for a parent cell with n chromosome pairs, the number
of possible chromosome combinations in its gametes from independent
assortment alone is **2^n**. Crossing over adds far more variety on top,
because it creates new chromatids rather than just reshuffling whole
chromosomes.

For our model cell (2n = 4, n = 2): meiosis produces **4 cells, each
with n = 2 chromosomes**, and independent assortment alone gives
**2^2 = 4** possible combinations. For humans (n = 23): **2^23 =
8,388,608** combinations — over eight million — before crossing over is
even counted. That is why siblings (except identical twins, who come
from one fertilized egg) are never genetically identical.

Note the boundary: this unit teaches the *logic* of meiosis — halve,
shuffle, split — not the biochemical mechanism of each step, and it does
not require memorizing every sub-stage name. The stage names in the SVG
are a map, not a spelling test.

**Key vocabulary:** *meiosis* (two-division reduction division producing
gametes), *gamete* (haploid sex cell), *homologous chromosomes* (the two
members of a chromosome pair, same genes in the same order, different
versions possible), *tetrad* (four chromatids of a paired homologous
set), *crossing over* (segment swap between nonsister chromatids in
prophase I), *independent assortment* (random orientation of each pair
at metaphase I), *haploid (n)* (one set of chromosomes), *diploid (2n)*
(two sets).

## Worked example 1 — counting through meiosis, 2n = 4

**Question:** A parent cell has 2n = 4. Track it: after S phase, after
meiosis I, after meiosis II. Give chromosomes and chromatids per cell
at each stop.

**Reasoning:** After S phase: **4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids** (4 pairs
of sisters). Meiosis I separates homologs: **2 cells, each with 2
chromosomes, 4 chromatids** (each chromosome still a sister pair).
Meiosis II separates sisters: **4 cells, each with 2 chromosomes, 2
chromatids** — and each chromatid is now a chromosome, so each gamete
is **n = 2**. One replication, two divisions: 4 → 2 → 4 cells, with the
chromosome number halved exactly once, in meiosis I.

## Worked example 2 — combinations

**Question:** A fruit fly has 2n = 8 (n = 4). How many chromosome
combinations can independent assortment alone produce in its gametes?
How does that compare with a human's?

**Reasoning:** Fruit fly: **2^4 = 16** combinations. Human: **2^23 =
8,388,608** combinations. The formula is exponential: each extra pair
doubles the possibilities. And both numbers *understate* real variation,
because crossing over creates new chromatids that the 2^n count treats
as whole parental chromosomes.

## Guided practice

1. With pipe cleaners, build a 2n = 4 parent cell after S phase (4
   duplicated chromosomes: 2 dark pairs, 2 light pairs). Run meiosis I
   (separate homologs) and meiosis II (separate sisters). Count
   chromosomes in each of the four final cells.
2. A cell has n = 3 pairs. How many combinations does independent
   assortment alone allow? (2^3 = 8.) List a method for checking: write
   all 8 as sequences of parental origin (e.g., for pairs A, B, C: AAA,
   AAB, … — one letter per pair, two choices each).
3. Explain why crossing over must happen between *nonsister* chromatids
   of homologous chromosomes to create something new — and why swapping
   between sister chromatids would change nothing.

## Independent practice

1. A parent cell has 2n = 6. State chromosomes per cell after meiosis I
   and after meiosis II, and the number of combinations from independent
   assortment alone (n = 3 → 8).
2. Explain, in a short paragraph, why meiosis has two divisions but only
   one S phase. What would go wrong with two S phases? (Each gamete
   would stay diploid; fertilization would double the number each
   generation.)
3. A student says, "Meiosis makes four identical cells, like mitosis
   twice." Identify the two errors in that sentence.
4. Human gametes: a sperm cell has 23 chromosomes. After fertilization
   with a normal egg, how many chromosomes does the zygote have, and
   why is that number stable across generations?

## Application

Design a combination lottery: for a 2n = 4 parent, flip a coin for each
of the 2 pairs (heads = dark member goes to "your" gamete, tails =
light). Repeat 12 times and tally how many of the 4 possible
combinations appear. Write one sentence about whether 12 draws were
enough to see all 4 — and why a small sample can miss a real
possibility.

## Exit check

1. How many divisions does meiosis have, and how many DNA replications?
2. Name the two meiosis-I events that create variation.

## Supports

- If homologs vs. sisters tangle: homologs are the *pair* (one from each
  parent, same genes); sisters are the *copies* (identical, made in S
  phase). Meiosis I separates the pair; meiosis II separates the copies.
- If 2^n confuses: build the tree for n = 2 (4 leaves), then n = 3 (8
  leaves) before computing n = 23.
- Oral, drawn, or modeled answers are accepted for every item.

## Extension

Nondisjunction preview (Lesson 4 develops it): if one homologous pair
fails to separate in meiosis I, what chromosome numbers result in the
four gametes? Sketch it for 2n = 4 and check your counts against the
answer key before Lesson 4.

## Teacher note

The deepest misconception here is "meiosis = mitosis twice." Attack it
with counts, not slogans: mitosis twice would give 2, then 4 cells each
with 2n chromosomes; meiosis gives 4 cells with n. Also watch for
students treating crossing over as "chromosomes swapping whole
chromosomes" — it swaps *segments* between chromatids. Answers: see
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
