# Lesson 01 — The Cell Cycle: Grow, Copy, Divide

**Student goal:** I can describe the cell cycle in order, say what happens
in G1, S, and G2, and explain why DNA must be copied before a cell
divides.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Index cards: G1, S, G2, mitosis, cytokinesis, checkpoint, G0

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "A skin cell divides to replace a scraped patch. Before it splits,
what problem must it solve?" Accept guesses. The key problem: after the
split there must be *two* complete instruction sets, but the cell starts
with *one*. Today's lesson is the cell's solution: grow, copy, then
divide — in that order, with checkpoints along the way.

## Teaching

A **cell cycle** is the ordered series of events in a cell's life, from
one division to the next. It has two big phases:

1. **Interphase** — the long preparation period. The cell lives its
   normal life, grows, and copies its DNA. Most of a cell's life is spent
   here.
2. **Mitotic phase** — the short division period: **mitosis** (the nucleus
   divides) followed by **cytokinesis** (the cytoplasm divides), producing
   two daughter cells.

Interphase has three stages, and their order matters:

- **G1 phase (first gap):** the cell grows, does its normal jobs, and
  builds the materials it will need — including the building blocks of
  DNA. "Gap" is a misleading name: the cell is busy, not idle.
- **S phase (synthesis):** the cell **replicates its DNA**. Every
  chromosome is copied, so each chromosome now consists of two identical
  copies called **sister chromatids**, joined at a **centromere**. After
  S phase, the cell holds twice the DNA it started with — exactly what
  two daughter cells will need.
- **G2 phase (second gap):** the cell finishes preparations for division:
  more growth, energy stores topped up, and the proteins that will move
  chromosomes built and checked.

Then the mitotic phase runs: mitosis sorts the copied chromosomes into
two equal sets, and cytokinesis splits the cytoplasm. Result: two
daughter cells, each with a complete instruction set.

**Checkpoints** are control points where the cell checks conditions
before proceeding. The three major ones are: near the end of G1 (is the
DNA undamaged and the cell ready?), at the G2/M transition (is all DNA
copied correctly?), and during metaphase of mitosis (are all chromosomes
properly attached to the spindle before they are pulled apart?). If a
check fails, the cycle pauses for repair — or the cell may leave the
cycle.

**G0 phase** is the exit ramp: a cell that is not preparing to divide.
Some cells rest in G0 temporarily and re-enter later; some, like mature
nerve cells and heart muscle cells, stay in G0 permanently.

The one-sentence version: **grow (G1), copy (S), prepare (G2), divide
(mitosis + cytokinesis) — and check at every gate.**

**Key vocabulary:** *cell cycle* (ordered events from one division to
the next), *interphase* (growth + DNA-copying preparation period),
*G1* (growth and building-block accumulation), *S phase* (DNA
replication; sister chromatids formed), *G2* (final division
preparations), *mitotic phase* (mitosis + cytokinesis), *checkpoint* (a
control point that can pause the cycle), *G0* (non-dividing state),
*sister chromatids* (identical copies of one chromosome, joined at the
centromere).

## Worked example 1 — why copy first?

**Question:** A cell skips S phase and divides anyway. Each daughter
cell gets half of the parent's chromosomes. Why is that a failure, not a
shortcut?

**Reasoning:** The parent's instruction set is the whole genome. If the
cell divides without copying, each daughter receives only part of the
instructions — like photocopying half a recipe book for each of two
cooks. Neither daughter can run the full cell program. Copying in S
phase is what makes division safe: one set becomes two identical sets,
so each daughter gets the *complete* instructions. That is why the
cycle's order is non-negotiable: copy before you split.

## Worked example 2 — counting chromatids

**Question:** A lab organism's cell has 4 chromosomes before S phase.
How many chromosomes and how many chromatids does it have after S phase?
After mitosis and cytokinesis, how many chromosomes does each daughter
cell have?

**Reasoning:** Before S: 4 chromosomes, each a single chromatid → 4
chromatids. S phase copies each chromosome: still **4 chromosomes**,
because a chromosome counts as one unit even when it holds two copies —
but now **8 chromatids** (4 pairs of sister chromatids). Mitosis
separates the sister chromatids, one copy to each side; cytokinesis
splits the cell. Each daughter cell receives **4 chromosomes**, each a
single chromatid again. Count chromosomes by centromeres: one centromere
per chromosome, before and after copying.

## Guided practice

1. Put the seven cards in cycle order and say one sentence about each
   stage. (Cards: G1, S, G2, mitosis, cytokinesis — plus checkpoint and
   G0 placed where they belong: checkpoints between stages, G0 as an
   exit from G1.)
2. A cell is in G2. Has its DNA been copied yet? How do you know?
3. A checkpoint at the G2/M transition finds DNA that is only half
   copied. What should the cell do, and why is pausing better than
   dividing?

## Independent practice

1. Write the cell cycle as a four-step chain (G1 → S → G2 → mitotic
   phase) and, under each step, write what the cell gains or finishes.
2. A cell has 6 chromosomes before S phase. State its chromosome count
   and chromatid count (a) before S, (b) after S, (c) in each daughter
   cell after division.
3. Explain in two sentences why interphase is most of a cell's life even
   though "division" is the famous part.
4. A student says, "G0 means the cell is dead." Correct the error in one
   paragraph, using nerve cells as your example.

## Application

Draw the cycle as a circle with four labeled arcs (G1, S, G2, mitotic
phase). Mark the three checkpoints with a star and write, beside each
star, the question that checkpoint asks. Add a G0 exit arrow leaving
from G1.

## Exit check

1. Name the phase in which DNA is copied.
2. A daughter cell after mitosis has the same chromosome number as its
   parent. What made that possible?

## Supports

- If "chromosome vs. chromatid" tangles: count centromeres. One
  centromere = one chromosome, however many chromatids it holds.
- If the order slips: the mnemonic is "Grow, Copy, Prepare, Divide."
- Oral or drawn answers are accepted for every item; the adult scribes
  when writing stamina lags.

## Extension

Research question (no answer required today): some cells divide every
day (skin, gut lining) and some almost never (nerve). Propose one reason
a body would want *both* speeds, and one risk of a cell that divides too
fast. Bring your proposal to Lesson 6.

## Teacher note

The most common misconception is that chromosome number doubles in S
phase ("4 becomes 8 chromosomes"). It does not: chromatid number doubles
while chromosome number stays 4, because sister chromatids share one
centromere. Drill the centromere-counting rule in every counting task
this unit. Answers: see [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
