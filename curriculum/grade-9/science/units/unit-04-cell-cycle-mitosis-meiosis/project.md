# Project — Chromosome Modeling Investigation

**Question:** Can a physical model prove that mitosis copies while
meiosis halves and shuffles — and can you count the difference?

**Sessions:** 8–9 (modeling), 14–15 (report and revision), 19
(presentations). Adult-supervised; no living material, no microscope,
no hazardous substance. The observation alternative is the labeled stage
SVG plus the combination table below — a learner who cannot use pipe
cleaners completes every deliverable with drawings and tables.

## Materials

- Pipe cleaners in two colors: dark = chromosomes from one parent, light
  = chromosomes from the other parent (one consistent color per
  homologous pair)
- Beads, stickers, or twist ties for centromeres
- Coins or tokens for the combination lottery
- Notebook, the stage SVG, and this brief

## Modeling part 1: Mitosis

*Session 8.*

1. Build a parent cell with 2n = 4: two homologous pairs. Pair 1: one dark
   and one light pipe cleaner. Pair 2: one dark and one light pipe
   cleaner. (Four single pipe cleaners = four chromosomes before S.)
2. Run S phase: add an identical copy beside each pipe cleaner, joined
   at a bead centromere. You now hold 4 chromosomes, 8 chromatids.
3. Run mitosis: arrange the duplicated chromosomes at a metaphase line,
   then separate sister chromatids to opposite sides. Split into two
   daughter sets.
4. Record: chromosomes and chromatids at each stop (before S, after S,
   at metaphase, per daughter). Photograph or sketch the final two sets.

## Modeling part 2: Meiosis and combinations

*Session 9.*

1. Rebuild the same parent cell after S phase (4 chromosomes, 8
   chromatids).
2. Run meiosis I: pair the homologs (dark with light, per pair), swap a
   short segment between two nonsister chromatids of pair 1 to model
   crossing over (mark the swapped piece with a twist tie), then
   separate the homologs into two cells.
3. Run meiosis II: separate the sister chromatids in each cell. You
   should hold four gamete sets, each with 2 chromosomes.
4. Combination lottery: for each of the two pairs, flip a coin to decide
   which member enters "your" gamete (heads = dark, tails = light).
   Repeat 20 times. Tally the four possible combinations (dark/dark,
   dark/light, light/dark, light/light) and compare with the expected
   share of 5 out of 20 each.
5. Record: the four gamete chromosome counts, the lottery tally, and one
   sentence on whether 20 draws were enough to see all four combinations
   (usually yes, but a small sample can miss one — and a missed
   combination in a small sample is not proof it cannot happen).

## Deliverables

1. **Model record:** sketches or photos of the mitosis daughter sets and
   the meiosis gamete sets, each labeled with chromosome and chromatid
   counts.
2. **Count table:** one row per stop (before S, after S, metaphase of
   mitosis, per mitotic daughter, after meiosis I per cell, per gamete)
   with chromosomes and chromatids.
3. **Lottery table:** the 20-draw tally with expected vs. observed
   counts per combination.
4. **Written report (300–500 words):** the question, the method in your
   own words, the count table's story, the lottery result, and a
   claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph answering: "Why does mitosis
   preserve the chromosome number while meiosis halves it?" Cite at
   least one count from each modeling part.
5. **Presentation (Session 19, 3–5 minutes):** walk the adult through
   one mitosis set and one gamete set, pointing to what is identical
   and what is varied, and state your claim in one sentence.

## Report critique and revision

*Sessions 14–15.* Exchange reports with a partner or the adult. The
critic checks: (a) does every count in the table match the model? (b)
does the claim cite counts, not just stage names? (c) is the lottery
interpretation honest about small samples? Revise, rebuild any model
set whose counts disagree with the table, and resubmit. The revised
report is the graded artifact; the first draft is not graded.

## Capstone presentations

*Session 19.* Present the model sets and the one-sentence claim. The
adult asks one follow-up from the rubric's "reasoning" row (for example:
"Why didn't the chromosome number double in S phase?"). A strong answer
uses the centromere rule.

## Rubric (40 points)

| Criterion | Full marks | Partial | Missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Model accuracy — mitosis (10) | Both daughter sets correct: 4 chromosomes each, identical composition; S-phase doubling shown | One set correct, or chromatid counts off by one step | Sets missing or counts wrong throughout |
| Model accuracy — meiosis (10) | Four gamete sets, 2 chromosomes each; homolog separation then sister separation visible; crossing-over swap marked | Gamete counts right but division order unclear, or swap unmarked | Gamete counts wrong or only one division modeled |
| Count table (6) | Every row correct (chromosomes and chromatids) | One row wrong | Two or more rows wrong or missing |
| Lottery and interpretation (6) | 20 draws tallied; expected 5 each stated; honest note on small samples | Tally present but no expected comparison, or overclaim from a small sample | No tally or invented results |
| Report claim (CER) (8) | Claim answers the question; cites at least one mitosis count and one meiosis count; reasoning connects counts to copy-vs.-halve | Claim present but cites only one part, or reasoning is a restatement | No claim, or claim contradicts the counts |

**Scoring note:** a model that disagrees with its own table loses marks
in both rows — consistency between model and table is the skill being
assessed, not craft quality. Pipe-cleaner neatness is never scored.
