# Practice Sessions — Unit 04: Cell Cycle, Mitosis, and Meiosis

Four named sessions, referenced in the [unit pacing](README.md#20-session-pacing).
Every item is solved in the [answer key](answer-key.md). Model organism
for counting tasks: a lab cell with **2n = 4** (n = 2), unless another
number is stated. Human reference: **2n = 46, n = 23**.

## Session A: Ordering the cycle, checkpoint calls

*Session 2, after Lesson 1.*

1. Arrange these events in order: cytokinesis, G2, S, mitosis, G1.
   (Answer: G1, S, G2, mitosis, cytokinesis.)
2. For each of the five events, write what the cell has or does that it
   did not have or do before that event.
3. Checkpoint scenarios — for each, say whether the cell should proceed
   or pause, and which checkpoint is involved:
   - (a) G1 checkpoint finds a broken DNA strand.
   - (b) G2/M checkpoint finds every chromosome copied and undamaged.
   - (c) Metaphase checkpoint finds one chromosome unattached to the
     spindle.
   - (d) A mature nerve cell receives a growth signal. (It stays in G0;
     the signal does not override permanent G0 in this cell type.)
4. A cell has 10 chromosomes before S phase. Fill in the table:

   | Stage | Chromosomes | Chromatids |
   |---|---|---|
   | Before S | 10 | 10 |
   | After S | 10 | 20 |
   | Each daughter after division | 10 | 10 |
5. Error analysis: "Interphase is the resting phase, so nothing important
   happens there." Rewrite the sentence correctly, naming the two most
   important interphase events (growth and DNA replication).

## Session B: Stage identification and chromosome counts

*Session 4, after Lesson 2.*

1. Identify each stage from its description:
   - (a) Chromosomes line up at the cell's middle. (Metaphase.)
   - (b) Sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles. (Anaphase.)
   - (c) Chromosomes condense; the nuclear envelope starts to break
     down. (Prophase.)
   - (d) Nuclear envelopes re-form; the cytoplasm is about to split.
     (Telophase, moving into cytokinesis.)
   - (e) Spindle fibers attach to centromeres; the envelope is gone.
     (Prometaphase.)
2. A cell with 2n = 8 completes S phase. State chromosomes and chromatids
   at metaphase, and chromosomes per daughter cell after cytokinesis.
   (Metaphase: 8 chromosomes, 16 chromatids. Daughters: 8 chromosomes
   each.)
3. Explain why the answer to "how many chromosomes at metaphase?" is the
   same number as before S phase, even though the DNA amount doubled.
4. A student draws anaphase with homologous chromosomes separating
   instead of sister chromatids. Is that mitosis? Explain what division
   the drawing actually shows, and what would go wrong if a mitotic cell
   really separated homologs that way. (That is meiosis I, not mitosis;
   in mitosis it would give daughters with the wrong chromosome sets —
   one homolog of each pair per daughter, i.e., haploid daughters from a
   diploid parent — instead of identical diploid copies.)
5. Human application: a skin cell (2n = 46) divides. A learner claims each
   daughter gets 92 chromatids "because 46 doubled." Correct the claim
   with the centromere rule: after S phase there are 46 chromosomes and
   92 chromatids; after division each daughter has 46 chromosomes and
   46 chromatids.

## Session C: Variation-claim data tasks

*Session 11, after Lesson 5. Dataset: fictional practice data from
[Lesson 5](lessons/lesson-05-sources-of-heritable-variation.md).*

1. Experiment A (100 gametes, 2n = 4): the four combinations appeared as
   27, 24, 26, 23. Compute the expected count per combination under
   random assortment (100 ÷ 4 = 25) and state whether the data fit that
   expectation closely. (Yes: all four counts are within 3 of 25.)
2. Experiment B: standard strain 2 per 10,000; weakened-repair strain 15
   per 10,000. Compute the fold increase (15 ÷ 2 = 7.5×) and state which
   variable was isolated. (Repair system; no UV in either group.)
3. Experiment C: no UV 2 per 10,000; UV 47 per 10,000. Compute the fold
   increase (47 ÷ 2 = 23.5×) and state which variable was isolated. (UV
   exposure; same standard strain.)
4. A headline reads, "UV light rewrites your children's genes." Using
   experiment C alone, explain why the headline overclaims: what fact
   about *where* the mutation occurred is missing? (Whether the
   mutations were in cells leading to gametes; a skin-cell mutation is
   not inherited.)
5. Write a three-sentence CER paragraph: "Meiotic combinations are a
   real source of variation, but they are not the only one." Cite one
   number from each experiment.

## Session D: One genome, many jobs; what-breaks scenarios

*Session 13, after Lesson 6.*

1. A muscle cell and a skin cell from one person have identical DNA
   sequences. List two observable differences between them, and explain
   each with gene expression (not different DNA).
2. What-breaks scenarios — name the failed part and the consequence:
   - (a) A cell skips S phase and divides. (Daughters with half the
     instructions; nonviable or severely impaired.)
   - (b) The metaphase checkpoint is ignored and anaphase starts with
     one chromosome unattached. (Unequal distribution: one daughter
     with an extra chromosome, one missing one.)
   - (c) A differentiated cell's expression pattern is erased so it
     reads the whole library at once. (Loss of specialization; the cell
     stops doing its job reliably.)
   - (d) Checkpoints fail repeatedly over many generations.
     (Accumulating errors; tumor growth in the conceptual model.)
3. Explain why a fertilized egg needs *both* mitosis and
   differentiation, using the words "more" and "different" in your
   answer.
4. A red blood cell has no nucleus. Does that contradict "every cell
   carries the same genome"? Explain the edge case in two sentences.
   (No: the cell *had* the genome during development and ejected its
   nucleus as part of specialization; the rule describes the organism's
   cells generally, and this is a named exception, not a second genome.)
