# Lesson 01 — Designing Fair Investigations

**Student goal:** I can name the variables in an investigation, explain what
a control does, and say why repeated trials matter.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Index cards for the vocabulary (one term per card)
- Two paper cups, two dried beans, a measuring spoon, water (for the
  readiness demo)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

The adult places a bean in each of two cups, waters one cup ("a spoon of
water"), and leaves the other dry. Ask: "If only the watered bean sprouts,
what made the difference?" Confirm the learner points to the *water*, not
the cup or the bean. If the learner names everything ("the cup, the water,
the bean"), keep the lesson at this one demo and re-check after the first
worked example.

## Teaching

A **question** becomes an **investigation** when we can test it by changing
one thing, measuring what happens, and keeping everything else the same. The
part we change on purpose is the **independent variable** — it stands alone,
we decide it. The part we measure is the **dependent variable** — the result
*depends* on what we changed. Everything else we hold steady is a
**controlled variable**. And we always need a **control**: a comparison group
that does *not* get the change, so we can see whether the change actually
caused the difference.

Why **repeated trials**? Living things vary. In a classic seed-sprouting
experiment, nine of ten watered seeds might sprout while one does nothing —
maybe that seed was dead, unhealthy, or slow. With only one seed per group,
a single dud seed would flip the conclusion. More trials make it less likely
that random variation tricks us.

**Key vocabulary:** *variable* (anything that can change or differ),
*independent variable* (what the investigator changes), *dependent variable*
(what is measured), *controlled variable* (what is held the same), *control*
(the unchanged comparison group), *repeated trials* (running the test more
than once, or on more than one subject).

## Worked example 1 — "Does water affect bean sprouting?"

**Question:** Does the amount of water change how many bean seeds sprout?

The adult sets up two groups of 10 dried beans each, in labeled cups with
damp paper towels. Group A gets one spoon of water per day; Group B gets no
water. After a week, 9 of 10 sprout in Group A, 0 of 10 in Group B.

Naming the parts aloud:

- **Independent variable:** water (given vs. not given) — *we* chose this.
- **Dependent variable:** number of seeds that sprouted — this is the
  measured result.
- **Controlled variables:** same bean type, same cup type, same light, same
  room, same starting dampness.
- **Control:** Group B (no water) — the baseline that shows what happens
  without the treatment.
- **Repeated trials:** 10 seeds per group, not one — because one seed might
  just be a dud.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the control group shows that seeds left alone
do not sprout on their own, so the sprouting in Group A is tied to the
water, not to something else about the cups. The single seed that failed in
Group A does not overturn the result *because* the pattern repeats across
the other nine.

## Worked example 2 — "Does cooking oil make bean plants grow taller?"

A learner proposes: water one plant with plain water, and a second plant
with water mixed with cooking oil. The oiled plant grows taller.

The adult walks through what went wrong:

- There is **no control** for the oil: both plants differ in more than oil?
  No — actually the deeper flaw is there is only **one plant per group**,
  so any difference could be that one plant was simply healthier to begin
  with.
- A fairer version: 6 plants per group; Group A gets plain water, Group B
  gets the same water *plus* the same amount of oil; same light, same soil,
  same pot size; measure height after two weeks; compare the *group*
  averages, not one plant against one plant.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a single plant per group is a single trial. If
that plant was already stronger, we would credit the oil for something the
oil never did. Repetition protects us from our own wishful thinking.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Work through these together, 4–6 minutes each.

1. For the bean-sprout experiment: which is the independent variable — the
   number of seeds that sprouted, or the water? *(Hint: which one did the
   experimenter choose?)*
2. A learner tests whether light affects sprouting: Group A in a sunny
   window, Group B in a dark closet. Name one controlled variable that must
   be held the same. *(Hint: what else differs between a window and a
   closet besides light?)*
3. Another learner tests three foods for starch (Sessions 7–9 preview): she
   dips a cracker, a banana slice, and a cheese cube in iodine, but uses
   twice as much iodine on the cheese. What is wrong with the comparison?
4. Error analysis: "I only need one trial if I am very careful." Explain
   why carefulness does not replace repetition, using the dud-seed example.
5. Rewrite this untestable question so it can be investigated: "Why is
   bread the best food?" *(Hint: "best" is an opinion — pick one measurable
   thing, like starch content.)*

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. A learner investigates whether temperature affects how fast sugar
   dissolves: one cup of cold water, one cup of warm water, same sugar
   amount, same stirring. Identify the independent variable, the dependent
   variable, and two controlled variables.
2. Name the control group in the sugar-dissolving test, or explain why this
   test is a *comparison* rather than having a separate control. (Accept:
   there is no no-treatment control; the two temperatures are compared to
   each other, which is fine as long as we say so.)
3. Design: "Does the amount of starch differ between white bread and whole
   wheat bread?" List your independent variable, dependent variable, at
   least three controlled variables, your control/comparison, and how many
   trials you would run.
4. Error analysis: A learner runs the starch test once per food and
   concludes "potatoes have more starch than rice." Name two things wrong
   with this conclusion.
5. Explain in 2–3 sentences why the control group in the bean experiment
   (no water) was necessary — what alternative explanation does it rule
   out?
6. A friend says, "My experiment proves oil helps plants grow because the
   oiled plant was taller." Write a 3-sentence reply explaining the design
   flaw and what would make the claim trustworthy.

## Application

Draft the investigation plan you will run in Sessions 8–9: "Which of my
four chosen foods contains starch, and which contains fat?" Write the
question, the independent and dependent variables, the controlled variables,
the control or comparison, and the number of trials. The adult checks the
plan against Lesson 1's criteria before the lab sessions.

## Exit check

Without looking back: define *independent variable*, *dependent variable*,
*control*, and *repeated trials* in your own words, and give one example of
each from today's bean experiment.

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** keep the three vocabulary cards visible
  (independent / dependent / control); point to the cups while naming each.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the design while the
  adult scribes, or draw the two groups and label what changes.
- **Extension:** design a second version of the bean experiment that tests
  *amount* of water (three levels) instead of water vs. no water. What
  becomes the independent variable now? What would the control be?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often label "the thing I care about" as the independent variable
(they care about sprouting, so they call sprouting independent). Re-anchor:
*independent = what you decide and change; dependent = what you watch and
measure.* Another common slip: treating the control as "the boring group
that doesn't matter." Stress that the control is the only thing that lets
the difference mean anything. Answer-key references: [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
