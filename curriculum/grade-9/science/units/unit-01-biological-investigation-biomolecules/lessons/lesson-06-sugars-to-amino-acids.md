# Lesson 06 — From Sugars to Amino Acids: Revising Explanations

**Student goal:** I can model how a sugar's atoms recombine with other
elements to form an amino acid, and I can revise an explanation with
evidence.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 12 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Atom tokens from Lesson 5, plus a fourth color/letter for nitrogen (N)
- The learner's Sessions 8–9 lab data

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "Glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆. An amino acid contains nitrogen too. Can glucose
alone become an amino acid?" Confirm the learner answers *no* — a new
element must join — before the teaching block. If the learner says yes,
revisit Lesson 3's atom pattern (C,H,O for all; N added for protein).

## Teaching

Here is the unit's central claim (HS-LS1-6): **the carbon, hydrogen, and
oxygen in sugars combine with other elements to form amino acids and other
large carbon-based molecules.** Amino acids are the monomers of proteins,
and every amino acid contains nitrogen — an element sugars do not have.

How does the nitrogen get there? In nature, plants absorb nitrogen
compounds from the soil (with the help of soil microbes) and combine that
nitrogen with the C, H, O from sugars they built; animals get nitrogen by
eating plants or other animals. The chemistry is multi-step and this unit
does **not** teach the full pathway — that belongs to later chemistry. What
this unit teaches is the **modeling move**: take the atoms you have (C, H, O
from sugar), add the atoms you need (N from another source), rearrange them
into a new molecule, and **account for every atom**.

A **model** is a simplified representation that shows the important parts
of something too small, too large, or too complex to handle directly. A
model is *useful*, not *true*: our token model shows atom accounting
correctly, but it does not show real bond angles, real reaction steps, or
real energy changes. Naming what a model leaves out is part of using it
honestly.

Finally, **revise with evidence**: the explanation you wrote in Lesson 2's
application ("what evidence I will collect") now meets real data. Where the
data disagree with the prediction, the *explanation* changes — not the
data.

**Key vocabulary:** *amino acid* (monomer of proteins; contains C, H, O, N),
*model* (a simplified, useful representation — not the real thing),
*revise* (change an explanation because the evidence requires it).

## Worked example 1 — the atom-accounting model

The learner takes one glucose cluster (C₆H₁₂O₆, 24 atoms) and a "nitrogen
source" token set (the adult provides 1 N token plus 3 H tokens, labeled
as a simplified stand-in — **not** a real molecule, and the adult says so).

Build: rearrange the 24 + 4 = 28 tokens into two groups — a small
"leftover" group and a larger "new molecule" group that includes the N.

- Before: 6 C, 15 H, 6 O, 1 N = 28 atoms.
- After (one acceptable arrangement): new molecule with 6 C, 13 H, 5 O,
  1 N = 25 atoms; leftover group (a water-like H₂O) with 2 H, 1 O = 3
  atoms.
- Check: 25 + 3 = 28 ✓. Every atom accounted for; nitrogen is now part of
  the large molecule.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** this is a *model of the idea* (atoms
recombine; new elements join; nothing vanishes) — not a claim about the
real biochemical pathway, which has many steps and helper molecules. The
honest sentence is: "This model shows that a sugar's atoms *can* recombine
with nitrogen into a larger molecule while conserving atoms." The model
earns its keep by making the conservation checkable.

## Worked example 2 — revising an explanation with evidence

Lesson 3 prediction: "Peas are mostly carbohydrate — the iodine test will
turn blue-black."

Session 9 data: two of three pea trials turned blue-black; one stayed
brownish-orange; the nutrition label shows 9g carbohydrate and 5g protein
per serving.

- **Old explanation:** "Peas are carbohydrate (C, H, O)."
- **Revised explanation:** *Claim:* Peas contain both starch and protein.
  *Evidence:* 2 of 3 iodine trials turned blue-black (starch present, with
  one inconsistent trial noted); the label lists 5g protein per serving.
  *Reasoning:* the positive iodine trials license the starch inference;
  the single negative trial is reported honestly as variation, not hidden;
  label grams are standardized evidence for protein.
- **What changed:** the claim went from "mostly carbohydrate" to "both
  starch and protein," and the inconsistent trial is *included*, not
  erased.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** revising is not admitting failure — it is the
job. The peer critique in Session 15 will specifically reward visible
revisions.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. With tokens, build the Worked example 1 model yourself. Run the
   conservation check aloud: count before, count after.
2. Name two things the token model shows accurately and two things it
   leaves out. *(Hint: accurate — atom counts, recombination; leaves out —
   real shapes, real steps, energy.)*
3. Take one of your Session 9 results that *disagreed* with your Lesson 3
   prediction. Write the old claim and the revised claim side by side.
4. Error analysis: a learner hides the one negative pea trial "because it
   was probably a mistake." Explain why honest reporting beats a clean
   story, using the dud-seed idea from Lesson 1.
5. A friend says "models are just toys — the real molecule is what
   matters." In 2–3 sentences, defend the token model: what can it show
   that a formula (C₆H₁₂O₆) cannot?

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. Build a second recombination: start with a 3-carbon "sugar piece"
   (3 C, 6 H, 3 O — label it a fictional practice molecule) plus 1 N and
   2 H from a nitrogen source. Rearrange into a new molecule containing
   the N plus a leftover water-like group. Show the before/after counts.
2. Write the honest-model sentence for your build: "This model shows ___.
   It does not show ___."
3. Choose your most surprising Session 9 result. Write a full revised CER:
   old claim, new claim, the exact evidence, the reasoning, and what you
   still do not know.
4. Explain in 3–4 sentences how nitrogen gets from soil into the protein
   of an egg, naming each transfer. (Plants absorb nitrogen compounds →
   build amino acids → chicken eats plants → builds egg protein.)
5. A learner claims "the iodine test proves peas are *only* starch." Write
   a 3-sentence reply using the label evidence and the limits of the test.
6. Reflect: which changed more during this unit — your ideas about food,
   or your ideas about how science works? Give one specific example of
   each.

## Exit check

Without notes: (a) state the HS-LS1-6 claim in your own words; (b) explain
why the token model is useful *and* what it leaves out; (c) describe one
revision you made to an explanation because of evidence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** the sentence frame "My model shows ___, but it does
  not show ___" posted for the whole lesson.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may photograph each model stage
  and dictate the counts while the adult scribes the inventory table.
- **Extension:** look up the general structure of an amino acid (central
  carbon, amino group, carboxyl group, side chain — labeled search link in
  the [resource pack](../resource-pack.md)) and compare it with your token
  model: what does the real structure add that tokens cannot show?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

"Sugars turn directly into amino acids in one step" — keep repeating that
the real pathway has many steps; the model shows the *atom logic*, not the
*pathway*. "The model is wrong because real molecules aren't colored
balls" — that is exactly the point of naming what a model leaves out; a
model is judged by what it *shows*, not by being the thing. Third: learners
may treat the nitrogen-source tokens as a real molecule — the adult must
label them a simplified stand-in every time. Answer-key references:
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06).
