# Lesson 03 — The Four Biomolecule Classes

**Student goal:** I can name the four biomolecule classes, give a food
example of each, and say which atoms each class is built from.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Printed copy of
  [`assets/biomolecule-food-sorting.png`](../assets/biomolecule-food-sorting.png)
- Index cards: one biomolecule class per card

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Show the four index cards (carbohydrate, lipid, protein, nucleic acid) and
ask what each word brings to mind — accept any association, right or wrong.
Then ask: "What are all four made of?" If the learner says "food" or
"chemicals," confirm the direction and introduce the atom answer in the
teaching block. (Nucleic acid is usually the unknown one — that is
expected.)

## Teaching

All living things are built mostly from four families of large,
carbon-containing molecules — **biomolecules**:

1. **Carbohydrates** — the body's quick fuel and, in plants, structure.
   Built from **carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen** (C, H, O). Examples: starch in
   bread and potatoes, sugar in fruit.
2. **Lipids** — long-term energy storage, insulation, cell membranes. Built
   from **carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen** too — but with far less oxygen per
   carbon than carbohydrates, which is why fats pack more energy per gram.
   Examples: cooking oil, butter. (Lipids are not true polymers — they have
   no single repeating monomer — but they are still large carbon-based
   molecules.)
3. **Proteins** — builders and workers: muscle, enzymes, immune defenses.
   Built from **carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and nitrogen** (C, H, O, N).
   Examples: egg white, beans, meat.
4. **Nucleic acids** — information storage: DNA and RNA. Built from
   **carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and phosphorus** (C, H, O, N, P).
   Every living cell contains them — so every fresh food made of cells
   contains nucleic acids, whether or not we test for them.

The pattern to remember: every biomolecule is built from carbon, hydrogen,
and oxygen; proteins add nitrogen; nucleic acids add nitrogen *and*
phosphorus. That pattern is the whole engine of Lesson 5 and Lesson 6: life
takes the C, H, O from sugars and combines them with other elements to
build everything else.

**Key vocabulary:** *carbohydrate* (sugars and starches; C, H, O),
*lipid* (fats and oils; C, H, O with little O), *protein* (chains of amino
acids; C, H, O, N), *nucleic acid* (DNA/RNA; C, H, O, N, P), *atom*
(the smallest unit of an element), *molecule* (two or more atoms bonded
together), *biomolecule* (a large carbon-based molecule in living things).

## The generated illustration — food-sorting activity

![Four grocery foods in a row: a slice of white bread, a small bottle of golden olive oil with one oil drop, a peeled hard-boiled egg cut in half showing the yolk, a bowl of green peas. AI-generated illustration used as a discussion prompt, not as lab evidence.](../assets/biomolecule-food-sorting.png)

*Caption (also in [assets/README.md](../assets/README.md)): An
AI-generated picture of four grocery foods. Which biomolecule class does
each food's main nutrient belong to — and how would you test your guess?*

**Activity:** Before any testing, the learner writes a prediction for each
food: which biomolecule class its main nutrient belongs to, and which atoms
it should therefore contain.

- Bread → carbohydrate → C, H, O
- Olive oil → lipid → C, H, O
- Egg → protein → C, H, O, N
- Peas → carbohydrate (starch) → C, H, O — *and* a discussion point: peas
  are living cells, so they also contain nucleic acids (C, H, O, N, P).

**Text-only alternative** (carries every detail the activity needs): "The
picture shows, left to right: one slice of white sandwich bread; a small
clear bottle of golden olive oil with one oil drop beside it; one peeled
hard-boiled egg cut in half showing the yellow yolk; a small white bowl of
green peas."

**Honest framing to state aloud:** this picture is a *prompt*, not
*evidence*. You cannot tell starch from protein by looking. The predictions
get tested in Sessions 7–9 — and a wrong prediction is a good prediction,
because it gives the evidence something to overturn.

## Worked example 1 — classifying a nutrition label

A granola bar label reads: total carbohydrate 29g, total fat 6g, protein 4g.

- **Carbohydrate claim:** the bar's main biomolecule class is carbohydrate.
  *Evidence:* 29g carbohydrate vs. 6g fat and 4g protein. *Reasoning:* the
  largest gram amount identifies the dominant class (a label-based
  inference, not a chemical test).
- **Atom inventory:** mostly C, H, O (carbohydrate), plus smaller amounts
  of C, H, O, N from the protein.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the label is *evidence*, not proof — labels
round numbers and list only what regulations require. It is still useful
evidence because it comes from standardized testing, not from looking at
the bar.

## Worked example 2 — where the atoms come from

A learner asks: "If bread is carbohydrate (C, H, O) and egg is protein
(C, H, O, N), where does the extra nitrogen come from?"

The adult draws the chain: plants take in carbon dioxide (C, O) and water
(H, O) and build sugars (C, H, O); with nitrogen absorbed from the soil
they build amino acids (C, H, O, N); amino acids link into proteins. Animals
get their nitrogen by eating plants or other animals. Nothing appears from
nowhere — **every atom in a biomolecule came from somewhere**, and Sessions
10–12 will track them.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** this is the unit's central idea in miniature
(HS-LS1-6): sugars supply C, H, O; other elements join them; large
molecules result. The model in the project will make every atom visible.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. Name the four biomolecule classes and the atom set of each, from memory
   with the cards face-down. *(Hint: start with "all four have C, H, O"
   and add what is extra.)*
2. Butter: mostly lipid. Which atoms? A friend says "lipids have no
   oxygen." Correct the friend using today's teaching.
3. A sports drink lists 34g carbohydrate, 0g fat, 0g protein. Which class
   dominates? What can you *not* conclude from this label?
4. Error analysis: "Nucleic acids are only in the genetics unit, so peas
   don't have any." Explain the error.
5. Predict-then-test setup: write your four predictions for the
   illustration foods in a table with columns *Food | Predicted class |
   Predicted atoms | How I will test it*.

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. Classify each as carbohydrate, lipid, protein, or nucleic acid, and list
   its atoms: (a) the starch in a potato, (b) the oil in a peanut,
   (c) the albumin in egg white, (d) the DNA in a strawberry cell.
2. A label reads: fat 14g, carbohydrate 8g, protein 7g per serving. Which
   biomolecule class dominates? Show the comparison.
3. Explain in 2–3 sentences why lipids and carbohydrates can both be "C, H,
   O" yet behave so differently (energy density, water mixing). *(Hint:
   the proportion of oxygen differs.)*
4. Draw an atom-key: one circle labeled C, one H, one O, one N, one P.
   Under each, write which biomolecule classes contain it.
5. A learner claims "vitamins are a fifth biomolecule class." Using the
   definition of biomolecule (large, carbon-based, built from many atoms),
   evaluate the claim.
6. Write a 4-sentence paragraph: "Where the nitrogen in an egg's protein
   came from," tracing it back through a food chain to soil or air.

## Application

Finish the prediction table from guided practice for all four foods in the
illustration. These predictions become the "prediction" column of your
Sessions 8–9 lab data table, and the Session 15 peer critique will compare
predictions against test results.

## Exit check

From memory: the four classes, each class's atoms, and one food example
per class. Then one sentence: why is the illustration a prompt and not
evidence?

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** the atom pattern chant — "C-H-O for all, N for
  protein, P for the code" — with the index cards as visual anchors.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort printed food pictures
  into four labeled boxes instead of writing the table.
- **Extension:** look up the element symbols and atomic numbers for
  C, H, O, N, P in
  [`resources/periodic_table_elements.csv`](../../../../../../resources/periodic_table_elements.csv)
  (columns: `atomic_number`, `symbol`, `name`, `category`,
  `state_at_room_temp`; real reference data, not measurements). Record the
  five rows in your notebook — you will need the atomic numbers in
  Session 11.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

"Nucleic acids = genetics unit only" is the classic: stress that DNA is in
every cell, including food cells, from day one. Second: learners hear "C,
H, O" for both carbs and lipids and conclude they are the same — push the
*oxygen proportion* difference and its consequence (energy per gram; the
nutrition guide's 4 vs. 9 cal/g is a good anchor, verified in
[resources/cooking_and_nutrition.md](../../../../../../resources/cooking_and_nutrition.md)).
Third: "protein has nitrogen, so nitrogen must come from the air we
breathe" — plants absorb nitrogen compounds from soil (via microbes);
animals get it by eating. Answer-key references:
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
