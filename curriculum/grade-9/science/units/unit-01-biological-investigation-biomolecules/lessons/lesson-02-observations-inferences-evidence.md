# Lesson 02 — Observations, Inferences, and Evidence

**Student goal:** I can tell an observation from an inference, and I can
build a claim–evidence–reasoning explanation.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 3 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- An ice cube in a clear cup (for the readiness demo)
- A brown paper square and a drop of cooking oil (for Worked example 2)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

The adult holds up the ice cube: "Tell me one thing you *observe*." (Cold,
clear, wet, melting — acceptable.) Then: "Tell me one thing you can guess
but did not directly see." (It was in a freezer; it is made of water —
acceptable.) Confirm the learner hears the difference: one is *sensed*, the
other is *concluded*. If the learner insists every statement is an
observation, slow down here before the teaching block.

## Teaching

An **observation** is information gathered with the senses (or with tools
that extend the senses, like a thermometer): what you can see, hear, touch,
smell, or measure. An **inference** is a conclusion drawn *from*
observations — a best guess that goes one step beyond what was sensed.

Science runs on a three-part structure called
**claim–evidence–reasoning (CER)**:

- **Claim:** the answer to the question — what you think is true.
- **Evidence:** the observations and measurements that support the claim.
- **Reasoning:** the "because" — the scientific idea that connects the
  evidence to the claim and explains *why* the evidence counts.

A claim without evidence is an opinion. Evidence without reasoning is a
pile of facts. The reasoning is what makes someone else able to follow your
thinking and check it.

**Key vocabulary:** *observation* (sensed or measured information),
*inference* (conclusion drawn from observations), *claim* (the answer you
defend), *evidence* (observations and data supporting the claim),
*reasoning* (the scientific idea linking evidence to claim).

## Worked example 1 — the melting ice cube

The adult places the ice cube in the cup and the learner watches for two
minutes.

- **Observation:** "The cube's edges are rounding and a clear liquid is
  collecting at the bottom of the cup."
- **Inference:** "The room is warmer than the ice, so heat is moving into
  it." (Not directly sensed — inferred from the melting.)
- **CER:** *Claim:* The ice is melting because the room is warmer than the
  ice. *Evidence:* Edges rounded; liquid collected; the cup feels colder
  than the table. *Reasoning:* Heat flows from warmer to cooler objects, so
  the ice gains heat and changes from solid to liquid.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** notice that "the ice is melting" is itself an
inference from observations (rounded edges, liquid). Scientists keep the two
separate so that someone else can check the raw observations against a
different inference.

## Worked example 2 — the brown-paper test (preview of Lesson 4)

The adult places one drop of cooking oil on brown paper and one drop of
water on a second square. After two minutes the oil spot looks translucent
when held to the light; the water spot has dried.

- **Observation:** "The oil square has a translucent spot that stays when
  held up to the light; the water square looks unchanged."
- **Inference:** "The oil contains fat, and fat soaks into paper
  differently than water."
- **CER:** *Claim:* The liquid on the first square is a fat. *Evidence:*
  It left a lasting translucent spot; the water control left none.
  *Reasoning:* Fats are lipids, and lipids leave translucent marks on brown
  paper while water evaporates without a mark — and the water square is the
  control that rules out "wetness alone" as the cause.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the water square is a control *for the test
itself* — it proves the paper reacts to fat, not just to any liquid. Without
it, "wet paper looks different" would be an undefeated alternative
explanation.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. "The banana turned brown in iodine." Observation or inference?
   *(Hint: did anyone sense brownness, or conclude it?)*
2. "The banana contains starch." Observation or inference? What would the
   evidence and reasoning be? *(Hint: what did the iodine do, and why does
   that count?)*
3. Rewrite as a CER: "Bread has starch because the iodine drop turned
   blue-black on it." Identify which part is the claim, which the evidence,
   and supply the missing reasoning.
4. Error analysis: a learner writes "The cracker has starch. I saw it."
   What is missing from this explanation?
5. Which is stronger evidence for "this food contains fat" — a translucent
   spot on brown paper, or "it feels greasy"? Defend your choice with the
   control from Worked example 2.

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. List three observations and two inferences about the classroom (or
   kitchen) around you right now.
2. "The potato slice turned dark blue-black where the iodine touched it."
   Label: observation or inference?
3. "Therefore the potato contains starch." Label: observation or inference?
   Write the evidence and the reasoning that support it.
4. A learner claims "Butter contains fat because it is yellow." Identify
   the flaw: is yellowness evidence of fat? What test would actually
   provide evidence?
5. Write a full CER for this question: "Does cooked rice contain starch?"
   (Evidence will come from the Session 8 lab; for now, state what
   evidence you *will* collect and what reasoning will connect it.)
6. Error analysis: two learners disagree. One says "observations are facts
   and inferences are guesses, so inferences don't belong in science." In
   3–4 sentences, explain why science needs both — and how CER keeps
   inferences honest.
7. Rewrite this weak explanation into a full CER: "Eggs have protein
   because the label says 6g."

## Application

Take your Session 1 investigation plan and add a CER template for each
food you will test: the claim you will defend ("Food X contains / does not
contain starch"), the exact evidence you will record (color observed, spot
appearance, label grams), and the reasoning you expect to use. Leave the
evidence column blank until the lab.

## Exit check

Define *observation*, *inference*, *claim*, *evidence*, and *reasoning* in
your own words, and explain in one sentence why the water square in Worked
example 2 was necessary.

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** a two-column "Sensed it / Concluded it" chart on
  the board for the whole lesson; the learner sorts each statement into a
  column before writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may record observations as
  labeled drawings (the iodine color, the paper spot) and dictate the
  inference.
- **Extension:** read the "Variability and repetition" idea from the Khan
  Academy controlled-experiments article (see
  [resource pack](../resource-pack.md)) and explain how repeated trials
  change what counts as *strong* evidence.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The big one: "I observed that it has starch." Learners collapse test result
into observation. Keep pressing: the *observation* is the color change; the
*starch* is the inference, licensed by the reasoning "iodine turns
blue-black with starch." Second: learners treat reasoning as a repeat of
the evidence ("the evidence is it turned blue-black, and the reasoning is
that it turned blue-black"). Require the reasoning to name the scientific
idea, not restate the data. Answer-key references:
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
