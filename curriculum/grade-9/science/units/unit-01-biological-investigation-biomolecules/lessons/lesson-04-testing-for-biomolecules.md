# Lesson 04 — Testing for Biomolecules

**Student goal:** I can run a starch test and a lipid test with controls,
and I can use a nutrition label as evidence.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 7 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Brown paper squares, cooking oil, water dropper (demo materials)
- Printed nutrition labels from 3–4 packaged foods (adult collects)
- For the adult only: iodine solution in a dropper bottle, small cups

## Safety (read aloud before any testing)

The **adult** handles the iodine solution — never the learner. Nothing is
tasted at any point. Hands are washed after testing. If iodine is not
available, the investigation runs label-only (see
[project.md](../project.md#label-only-alternative)) — the unit still works.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Hold up the brown paper demo from Lesson 2: oil spot vs. water spot. Ask:
"Which square is the control, and what does it rule out?" Confirm: the
water square is the control; it rules out "wetness alone causes the spot."
If the learner cannot name the control's job, revisit Lesson 2's Worked
example 2 before continuing.

## Teaching

Two classic food tests, each with a built-in control:

**Iodine test for starch.** The adult places one drop of iodine solution on
a small food sample. Iodine solution is normally brownish-orange. If starch
is present, the spot turns **blue-black**. No color change means no starch
detected. The **control**: a drop of iodine on plain water or on a food
known to have no starch (e.g., a piece of plain cooked egg white) — it must
stay brownish-orange, proving the iodine itself is not causing the color.

**Brown-paper test for lipids.** Rub or press the food on brown paper, or
place a drop of liquid on it. Let it sit. A **translucent spot** that stays
when held to the light means fat is present. The **control**: a drop of
water on a second square — it dries with no lasting spot, proving wetness
alone does not make the mark.

**Nutrition labels as evidence.** Packaged-food labels list grams of total
carbohydrate, total fat, and protein per serving, from standardized testing.
A label is evidence — useful, but an inference step removed from a chemical
test, and it tells you nothing about *which* carbohydrate (sugar vs.
starch) is present. In this unit, labels supply the **protein** evidence
(because the protein test reagents are not safe for a home lab) and
cross-check the carb/fat tests.

**Recording precisely:** every test gets one row — food, test used,
reagent/control, observation (exact color or spot description), inference.
"Turned dark" is not precise; "turned blue-black within 30 seconds" is.

**Key vocabulary:** *iodine test* (blue-black = starch), *brown-paper test*
(translucent spot = lipid), *nutrition label* (standardized gram evidence),
*false positive* (a test says "present" when it is not — the control guards
against this).

## Worked example 1 — testing a cracker

The adult places a small piece of plain cracker in a cup and adds one drop
of iodine. Within 30 seconds the spot turns blue-black.

- **Observation:** "The iodine drop on the cracker turned blue-black in
  about 30 seconds."
- **Control:** iodine on a water drop stayed brownish-orange.
- **Inference:** the cracker contains starch.
- **CER:** *Claim:* The cracker contains starch. *Evidence:* blue-black
  color appeared on the cracker but not on the water control. *Reasoning:*
  iodine turns blue-black in the presence of starch; the control shows the
  iodine alone does not make that color.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** one test, one food, is one trial. In the lab
you will test each food **three times** (three separate samples) because a
single drop can mislead — a crumb of something else, an uneven drop.

## Worked example 2 — the label as protein evidence

An egg's nutrition label (per large egg): protein 6g, fat 5g,
carbohydrate 0.6g.

- **Observation:** the label lists 6g protein — the largest of the three
  numbers after rounding.
- **Inference:** egg is protein-rich; its dominant biomolecule class is
  protein (C, H, O, N).
- **What the label cannot tell us:** whether the protein is albumin or
  another protein, or anything about nucleic acids (labels do not list
  them).

**Reasoning to name aloud:** notice the label says *nothing* about DNA —
but Lesson 3 taught that every cell contains nucleic acids. Absence of
label evidence is not evidence of absence. That is a reasoning move the
assessment will ask for.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. The adult demos iodine on a banana slice: blue-black appears. Name the
   observation, the inference, and the control you would want to see.
2. A learner rubs cheese on brown paper: translucent spot. A second learner
   says "that's just because the cheese was wet." How does the water
   control answer the second learner?
3. Error analysis: a learner tests one potato slice once, sees blue-black,
   and writes "potatoes have more starch than rice." Name two design flaws
   (hint: Lesson 1).
4. A label reads: carbohydrate 0g, fat 12g, protein 0g (one tablespoon of
   oil). Which biomolecule class dominates, and which atoms?
5. Your Session 8 plan: list your four foods, and for each, which test(s)
   you will use and what your control will be.

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. For each test below, state what a positive result looks like and what
   the control must show: (a) iodine test for starch, (b) brown-paper test
   for lipids.
2. A learner's data table has one column: "Food — starch? (yes/no)." List
   three columns that are missing for a precise record.
3. Design the controls for testing four foods for starch: what exactly is
   the control, and when is it run? (Accept: iodine on water, run alongside
   each batch, or iodine on a known starch-free food.)
4. A label reads: carbohydrate 29g, fat 6g, protein 4g. (a) Which class
   dominates? (b) What can you infer about protein's atoms here?
   (c) What can you *not* learn about nucleic acids from this label?
5. Error analysis: "The brown paper tore, so I held the food over a lamp
   and saw it was shiny. That's a positive lipid test." Explain why this
   breaks the test's logic.
6. Write the precise-observation rule in your own words, then rewrite these
   sloppy records precisely: (a) "it changed color," (b) "spot appeared,"
   (c) "label says some protein."

## Application

Finalize the lab plan: four foods, the tests for each, controls, three
trials per food, and the data table with columns *Food | Trial | Test |
Observation (precise) | Inference*. The adult approves the plan and
confirms allergy and safety checks before Session 8.

## Exit check

Without notes: describe both tests (what you do, what positive looks
like), name each test's control and what it rules out, and explain in one
sentence why protein evidence comes from labels in this unit.

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** a test-card for each test — *Do / Positive looks
  like / Control shows* — kept on the bench during the lab.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may photograph (or the adult may
  photograph) each test result and label the photo with the observation,
  instead of drawing it.
- **Extension:** research how the Benedict's test detects reducing sugars
  (labeled search link in the [resource pack](../resource-pack.md)) and
  explain why this unit uses iodine instead. *(Hint: heating and stronger
  reagents need a real lab.)*

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

"Blue-black = starch, so no color change = no biomolecules at all" — push
back: the test detects *starch*, not all carbohydrates (sugar water gives
no blue-black) and not lipids or protein. Second: learners record the
*inference* ("has starch") in the observation column — keep redirecting to
sensed language first. Third: the idea that three trials means "test three
different foods" — trials are repeats of the *same* test on the *same*
food. Answer-key references: [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
