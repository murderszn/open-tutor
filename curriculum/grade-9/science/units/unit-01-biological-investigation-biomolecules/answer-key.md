# Answer Key — Unit 01: Biological Investigation and Biomolecules

Worked solutions for every lesson item, practice task, quiz question,
assessment task, and project rubric row. Kept separate from student
materials. Accept any response that demonstrates the same reasoning, even
with different wording.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. The water is the independent variable — the experimenter chose to give
   or withhold it. The number sprouted is the dependent variable — the
   measured result.
2. Accept any one: same seed type/age, same water amount for the watered
   tray across trials, same tray size, same room temperature. (Light is the
   independent variable and must *not* be controlled away; a common slip
   is naming light itself as the controlled variable.)
3. The iodine amount is an uncontrolled variable confounded with food type:
   more reagent on the cheese could cause a stronger color regardless of
   starch content. The comparison is unfair — same iodine amount on every
   food.
4. Carefulness reduces mistakes, not random variation: even a perfectly
   careful single trial can land on a dud seed, an uneven iodine drop, or
   a contaminated sample. Repetition averages out variation that care
   cannot see.
5. Accept any measurable rewrite, e.g.: "Which bread — white or whole
   wheat — shows a stronger starch reaction with iodine?" (measurable =
   color change across three trials per bread).

**Independent practice**

1. Independent: water temperature. Dependent: time for the sugar to
   dissolve (or amount dissolved after a fixed time). Controlled: sugar
   amount, water volume, cup type, stirring rate.
2. Accept the comparison framing: the two temperatures are compared to each
   other; there is no separate no-treatment control, and that is fine as
   long as it is stated. (Also accept: the cold-water cup can serve as the
   baseline control.)
3. Independent: bread type. Dependent: starch-test result (color intensity
   / speed of blue-black appearance). Controlled: same iodine amount, same
   sample size, same timing, same observer. Control/comparison: the two
   breads compared to each other, plus iodine-on-water control. Trials:
   three per bread.
4. (a) Only one trial per food — no repetition, so a single bad drop
   decides. (b) No controlled variables stated (iodine amount, sample
   size) — the comparison may be unfair.
5. Without the no-water group, sprouting in the watered group could be
   explained by "seeds sprout on their own in cups" — the control rules
   out every cause except the water.
6. Accept a 3-sentence reply covering: one plant per group means no
   repetition — the oiled plant might simply have been healthier; a fair
   test needs several plants per group, identical conditions, and a
   comparison of group results.

**Exit check:** independent = what the investigator changes; dependent =
what is measured; control = the unchanged comparison group; repeated trials
= running the test more than once/on more subjects. Examples from the bean
experiment as in the lesson.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice**

1. Observation — brownness was sensed (seen).
2. Inference — starch was concluded from the color, not sensed directly.
   Precise evidence: the iodine drop on the banana turned blue-black
   (the question's "turned brown" is sloppy recording — see Practice B).
   Reasoning: iodine turns blue-black in the presence of starch.
3. Claim: "Bread has starch." Evidence: "the iodine drop turned blue-black
   on it." Missing reasoning: "iodine turns blue-black in the presence of
   starch, so the color change indicates starch."
4. Missing: evidence (what was observed, precisely) and reasoning (why the
   observation counts). "I saw it" is not evidence.
5. The translucent spot is stronger: the water control square proves
   wetness alone leaves no lasting spot, so the spot is tied to fat, not
   to liquid. "Feels greasy" has no control and no shared standard.

**Independent practice**

1. Accept any three sensed statements (e.g., "the wall is white," "I hear
   a fan," "the table feels cool") and two conclusions (e.g., "the fan was
   left on," "this room was recently painted").
2. Observation.
3. Inference. Evidence: the dark blue-black color where iodine touched the
   potato. Reasoning: iodine turns blue-black in the presence of starch.
4. Flaw: yellowness is not evidence of fat — color is not the test. The
   brown-paper test (translucent spot vs. water control) provides actual
   evidence.
5. Accept a CER skeleton: claim ("cooked rice contains/does not contain
   starch"), evidence-to-collect (iodine color at 30 s across 3 trials +
   water control), reasoning (iodine–starch color rule).
6. Accept: inferences are necessary — no one *sees* starch, atoms, or the
   past; science tests inferences against observations. CER keeps them
   honest by forcing the evidence and the connecting idea into the open
   where others can check them.
7. Accept: Claim — eggs contain protein. Evidence — the label lists 6g
   protein per large egg (standardized testing). Reasoning — labels report
   grams from laboratory analysis, so 6g is measured evidence, not a guess.

**Exit check:** observation = sensed/measured; inference = concluded;
claim = the defended answer; evidence = the observations/data; reasoning =
the scientific idea connecting them. The water square was necessary to rule
out "wetness alone makes the spot."

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. Carbohydrates — C, H, O. Lipids — C, H, O (little O). Proteins — C, H,
   O, N. Nucleic acids — C, H, O, N, P.
2. Correction: lipids *do* contain oxygen — just proportionally less than
   carbohydrates. The friend is wrong about "no oxygen."
3. Carbohydrate dominates (34g vs. 0/0). Cannot conclude: which
   carbohydrate (sugar vs. starch), or anything about micronutrients or
   nucleic acids.
4. Error: nucleic acids are in every living cell, including pea cells —
   "genetics unit" is where we study them in detail, not the only place
   they exist.
5. Accept a completed four-row table with predictions, atoms, and a test
   plan per food (iodine for starch, brown paper for lipids, labels for
   protein).

**Independent practice**

1. (a) carbohydrate — C, H, O. (b) lipid — C, H, O. (c) protein — C, H, O,
   N. (d) nucleic acid — C, H, O, N, P.
2. Fat dominates: 14g > 8g > 7g.
3. Accept: both are C/H/O, but lipids have far less oxygen per carbon;
   that proportion difference changes how they pack energy and whether
   they mix with water.
4. Accept a correct key: C in all four; H in all four; O in all four; N in
   proteins and nucleic acids; P in nucleic acids only.
5. Vitamins are small molecules, not large carbon-based polymers — they do
   not fit the biomolecule definition (large, carbon-based, built from
   many atoms). Not a fifth class.
6. Accept a chain: nitrogen compounds in soil → absorbed by plants (with
   microbial help) → built into plant amino acids/proteins → chicken eats
   plants → chicken builds egg protein from those amino acids.

**Exit check:** four classes, atoms, and one food each, as in the lesson;
the illustration is a prompt, not evidence, because appearance cannot
reveal composition — only tests can.

## Lesson 4

**Guided practice**

1. Observation: blue-black color on the banana slice. Inference: the
   banana contains starch. Wanted control: iodine on water (or on a
   known starch-free food), which must stay brownish-orange.
2. The water control square shows wetness alone leaves no lasting
   translucent spot — so the cheese's spot is tied to fat, not to being
   wet.
3. (a) One trial per food — no repetition. (b) The comparison across foods
   is unfair unless iodine amount, sample size, and timing were controlled
   (not stated).
4. Lipid dominates (12g fat vs. 0/0). Atoms: C, H, O.
5. Accept a finalized four-food plan with tests, controls, three trials,
   and the required data-table columns.

**Independent practice**

1. (a) Iodine: positive = blue-black; control (iodine on water) must stay
   brownish-orange. (b) Brown paper: positive = lasting translucent spot;
   control (water square) must dry with no spot.
2. Missing columns (any three): trial number, control run?, precise
   observation wording, inference kept separate, time.
3. Control: iodine on a drop of water (or on a known starch-free food),
   run alongside each batch of food tests — it must stay brownish-orange
   to prove the reagent is not causing the color.
4. (a) Carbohydrate dominates (29g). (b) Protein's atoms: C, H, O, N.
   (c) Nothing — labels do not list nucleic acids; absence from the label
   is not evidence of absence.
5. The test's logic requires the brown-paper + light comparison against
   the water control; "shiny under a lamp" is a different, uncalibrated
   observation with no control — it cannot be scored as the test.
6. Accept precise rewrites, e.g.: (a) "Iodine on the bread turned
   blue-black at the drop site within 30 seconds." (b) "A translucent spot
   remained on the brown paper after 2 minutes, visible against light."
   (c) "The label lists 7g protein per serving."

**Exit check:** iodine test (drop, blue-black = starch; control = iodine
on water, rules out reagent-caused color); brown-paper test (rub/drop,
lasting translucent spot = lipid; control = water square, rules out
wetness-caused spot). Protein evidence comes from labels because protein
test reagents are not safe for a home lab.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. Accept a correct token build: 45 atom tokens remain (12 C, 22 H, 11 O).
2. The water tokens were not added — they came from the monomers (one H
   from one glucose, one OH from the other).
3. The learner forgot to subtract the released water: maltose has 45
   atoms, not 48. Check: count before (48), subtract water (3), count
   after (45).
4. Error: the water is not created from nothing — its atoms (2 H, 1 O)
   came from the monomers. Atoms are conserved.
5. 4 glucoses: 3 waters released; final atoms = 96 − 9 = 87.

**Independent practice**

1. C: 12, H: 22, O: 11, total 45. Steps as in Worked example 1.
2. 5 × 24 = 120 starting; 4 waters released; 120 − 12 = 108 final.
3. Accept: the linkage between units differs (how each glucose connects to
   the next), which changes the polymer's shape and properties — same
   bricks, different architecture.
4. Maltose (45 atoms) + 1 water (3 atoms) = 48 atoms total in the two
   products (two glucoses, 24 each). The "extra" 3 atoms come from the
   water molecule added across the bond.
5. Accept any correct before/after map with the water shown leaving and a
   conservation check; fictional molecules must be labeled as such.
6. Accept: because you can count — if the after-count differs from the
   before-count (minus released water), the model is wrong in a way anyone
   can verify.

**Exit check:** monomer = small building block; polymer = large molecule
of linked monomers; dehydration synthesis = joining while releasing water;
hydrolysis = splitting by adding water. Conservation: atoms are neither
created nor destroyed in the rearrangement — demonstrated with 48 → 45.

**Task 4**

a. Start: 3 × 24 = 72. Waters released: 2. Final: C 18, H 34 (36−2), O 17

   18+34 = 52, +16 = 68 ≠ 66). **Recheck:** 3 glucoses = C₁₈H₃₆O₁₈ = 72

   O 18−2 = 16. Total: 18+32+16 = 66 ✓ (72 − 6 = 66 ✓). **Final answer:
   C 18, H 32, O 16, total 66.**
b. Accept any labeled model/description with: starting materials (sugar
   atoms + nitrogen source), the rearrangement, a correct conservation
   check, and one honest leaves-out sentence.

## Project rubric — scoring notes

- **Fair design (4):** plan names variables with the Lesson 1 meanings;
  controls run in the same batch; 3 trials per food per test; the table
  has all six columns and precise language.
- **Observation vs. inference (4):** spot-check five rows — all use sensed
  language in the observation column; inferences appear only in the
  inference column, labeled.
- **Evidence use (4):** every CER cites a color/time/gram; reasoning names
  the test's rule (iodine–starch, paper–lipid, label standardization);
  controls referenced at least once.
- **Atom model (4):** before/after counts shown and equal (minus released
  water); N present in the product; a "leaves out" sentence present.
- **Revision (4):** the revision log names a specific overturned prediction
  with the evidence that overturned it; inconsistent trials appear in the
  data, not hidden.
- **Communication (4):** the 5-minute defense states one claim, shows the
  evidence, walks the model, and answers the challenge question with
  reasoning rather than appeal to authority.
