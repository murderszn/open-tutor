# Practice Sessions — Unit 01

Named practice, game-free (grade-9), and review sessions referenced in the
[unit pacing](README.md#20-session-pacing-4-weeks--5-sessions-50-minutes-each).
Each session is 50 minutes with a movement break. Solutions for every task
are in the [answer key](answer-key.md#practice-sessions).

## Session A — Protocol critique: spot the flaw

**Goal:** apply Lesson 1's fair-investigation criteria to flawed designs.
**Session 2 of 20.**

For each scenario, name every design flaw (variables, control, trials) and
rewrite the design fairly.

1. **The fertilizer test.** One tomato plant gets fertilizer; a second gets
   none. The fertilized plant grows taller. The learner concludes fertilizer
   works. *(Flaws: one plant per group; no repeated trials; possibly
   uncontrolled light/soil.)*
2. **The sugar-water test.** Three cups: cold, warm, hot water; a spoon of
   sugar in each; the learner stirs the hot cup vigorously but barely stirs
   the cold one. *(Flaw: stirring is an uncontrolled variable confounded
   with temperature.)*
3. **The "best bread" test.** A learner wants to know which bread has the
   most starch. She tests white bread three times, whole wheat once, and
   rye not at all, then ranks all three. *(Flaws: unequal trials; a food
   never tested cannot be ranked.)*
4. **The missing control.** A learner dips four foods in iodine, sees
   blue-black on two, and concludes those two contain starch — but never
   tested iodine on water. *(Flaw: no control; cannot rule out that the
   iodine always looks that way on food.)*
5. **Rewrite challenge.** Pick scenario 1 or 4 and write a complete fair
   protocol: question, variables, control, trials, and what "precise
   record" means for that test.

## Session B — Data-table design and error analysis

**Goal:** design precise data tables and catch recording errors.
**Session 4 of 20.**

1. **Build the table.** Design the data table you will actually use in
   Sessions 8–9. Required columns: *Food | Trial (1–3) | Test | Control
   run? | Observation (precise) | Inference*. Add any column you think is
   missing and defend it.
2. **Fix the sloppy records.** Rewrite each precisely:
   - "Bread — turned dark" →
   - "Oil — spot" →
   - "Egg — label says protein" →
   - "Peas — trial 2 weird" →
3. **Error analysis.** A learner's table shows: cracker trial 1
   blue-black, trial 2 blue-black, trial 3 brownish-orange. She deletes
   trial 3 "because it must have been a bad drop." Write a 3-sentence
   response explaining what to do instead (keep it, note it, consider a
   fourth trial).
4. **Precision drill.** The adult performs (or describes) three mock
   observations; the learner records each in under 20 words with color,
   time, and location words. Example target: "Iodine on cracker turned
   blue-black at the drop site within 30 seconds."

## Session C — Atom inventory and polymer math

**Goal:** fluent atom accounting for dehydration synthesis.
**Session 11 of 20.**

All molecules here are real (glucose, water, maltose) unless labeled
fictional practice molecules.

1. Two glucose molecules join. Show the C, H, O inventory before and
   after, the water released, and the conservation check (see
   [Lesson 5](lessons/lesson-05-building-large-molecules.md#worked-example-1--counting-the-maltose)).
2. A chain of 6 glucose monomers: starting atoms, waters released, final
   total. (Answer: 144 − 15 = 129.)
3. A fictional practice disaccharide is built from monomer X (C₅H₁₀O₅) and
   monomer Y (C₆H₁₂O₆). Compute the product's atom inventory after one
   dehydration synthesis. (Answer: C₁₁H₂₀O₁₀, 41 atoms.)
4. Hydrolysis check: a 4-glucose chain (how many atoms?) is fully split by
   hydrolysis. How many water molecules are consumed, and what is the total
   atom count of the products? (Chain: 96 − 9 = 87 atoms; 3 waters consumed;
   products total 87 + 9 = 96 = four free glucoses ✓.)
5. **Error analysis.** A learner computes a 10-glucose starch chain as
   240 atoms "because water doesn't count — it evaporates." Explain the
   error and redo the inventory correctly. (Answer: 213; the water's atoms
   must be subtracted because they leave the polymer.)

## Session D — CER revision drills

**Goal:** turn weak explanations into strong CERs.
**Session 13 of 20.**

1. **Upgrade it.** "Butter has fat. I saw the spot." → full CER with
   control and reasoning.
2. **Find the missing piece.** "Claim: rice has starch. Evidence: the
   iodine turned blue-black." What is missing? Write it.
3. **The honest revision.** A learner predicted no starch in apple; two
   trials stayed brownish-orange, one turned faintly blue-black at the
   edge. Write the revised CER, including how to report the odd trial.
4. **Reasoning vs. restating.** "Evidence: the spot stayed translucent.
   Reasoning: because it stayed translucent." Rewrite the reasoning to name
   the scientific idea.
5. **Peer-swap.** Exchange your Lesson 6 application draft with the adult
   (or a partner). The reviewer marks: claim stated? evidence precise?
   reasoning names the idea? revision visible? Return with one specific
   improvement each.

## Reference session guide — OpenStax Biology 2e §3.1

**Goal:** read a real college-level reference with adult support and pull
out the unit's core ideas. **Session 6 of 20.** Source:
[OpenStax Biology 2e §3.1 — Synthesis of Biological Macromolecules](https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/3-1-synthesis-of-biological-macromolecules)
(verified 2026-10-13; CC BY licensed; free, no account).

**Adult runs this session** — the reading level is college, so the adult
reads aloud and the learner tracks the ideas, not the vocabulary.

1. **Before reading (5 min):** the learner predicts what "dehydration
   synthesis" means from the word parts (*de-* / *hydra* / *synthesis*).
2. **Read the "Dehydration Synthesis" section aloud (15 min).** Pause at
   Figure 3.2 (two glucoses → maltose + water). The learner sketches the
   before/after in the notebook with atom counts.
3. **Pull the ideas (15 min):** the learner writes, in own words: (a) what
   a monomer and polymer are; (b) what is released when monomers join;
   (c) the glucose → starch/glycogen/cellulose example — same monomer,
   different polymers.
4. **Connect (10 min):** "How does this reading change or confirm what
   Lesson 5 taught?" The learner writes two sentences. Disagreements with
   the lesson are discussed, not hidden — the reading is the authority
   here, and the adult notes any lesson correction needed in the
   [teacher guide](teacher-guide.md).

**Accessibility:** the adult may substitute the labeled text alternative —
the Lesson 5 SVG text equivalent plus the lesson's own explanation — if
the reading session is not productive; the ideas, not the source, are the
requirement.
