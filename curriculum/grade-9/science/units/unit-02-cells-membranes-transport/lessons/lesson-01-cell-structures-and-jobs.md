# Lesson 01 — Cell Structures and Their Jobs

**Student goal:** I can name the major cell structures, describe each one's
job, and explain how the jobs work together to keep the cell alive.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Index cards: one structure per card (nucleus, ribosome, endoplasmic
  reticulum, Golgi apparatus, mitochondrion, chloroplast, cell wall,
  vacuole, cell membrane)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Hold up the cards one at a time and ask: "Have you met this word before?
What do you think it does?" Accept guesses — most learners know *nucleus*
and *cell membrane* from middle school and little else. That is exactly
where this lesson starts: today every structure gets a job title.

## Teaching

A cell is the smallest unit of life, and inside it is a working crew. Each
**organelle** — a specialized structure inside the cell — does one job, and
the jobs depend on each other the way a kitchen crew depends on the cook,
the dishwasher, and the person who orders supplies. If you can say what
each structure *does for the cell*, you understand the cell.

Meet the crew (jobs first, names second):

1. **Nucleus — the instruction vault.** It stores the cell's DNA, the
   instructions for building and running the cell. Nearly everything the
   cell does starts as an instruction copied out of the nucleus. (DNA's
   structure is Unit 05's work; today, "instructions live here" is
   enough.)
2. **Ribosomes — the builders.** They read the copied instructions and link
   amino acids into proteins. Proteins do most of the cell's actual work,
   so ribosomes are the construction crew.
3. **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) — the workshop and hallways.** A maze of
   folded membranes. The *rough* ER is studded with ribosomes and folds new
   proteins; the *smooth* ER builds lipids and handles other jobs. Either
   way, the ER is where products get made and moved along.
4. **Golgi apparatus — packaging and shipping.** It receives products from
   the ER, finishes them, wraps them in little membrane sacs, and addresses
   them to their destinations — inside or outside the cell.
5. **Mitochondria — the power stations.** They break down food molecules and
   release usable energy (in the molecule ATP) that powers everything else.
   A muscle cell has thousands; the energy bill is high where the work is
   hard.
6. **Chloroplasts — the kitchens (plants and algae only).** They capture
   light energy and build sugars — photosynthesis, which is Unit 03's whole
   story.
7. **Cell wall — the fence (plants, fungi, some others; never animals).** A
   stiff outer layer, mostly cellulose in plants, that holds the cell's
   shape and protects it.
8. **Vacuole — the storage tank.** Holds water, nutrients, and wastes. In
   plant cells one giant central vacuole can take up most of the cell and
   keeps the plant firm — a wilted plant is a plant whose vacuoles lost
   water.
9. **Cell membrane — the border and gate.** Every cell has one. It holds the
   cell together and, crucially, *chooses* what enters and leaves. Lessons
   3–6 are all about how this choosing works.

The pattern: the nucleus issues instructions → ribosomes and the ER build
→ the Golgi ships → mitochondria power the whole operation → the membrane
controls trade with the outside. No structure works alone.

**Key vocabulary:** *organelle* (a specialized structure inside a cell,
with its own job), *nucleus* (stores DNA instructions), *ribosome*
(links amino acids into proteins), *endoplasmic reticulum* (folded-membrane
workshop; rough = with ribosomes, smooth = without), *Golgi apparatus*
(finishes, packages, and ships cell products), *mitochondrion* (releases
energy from food), *chloroplast* (captures light to build sugars; plants
and algae), *cell wall* (stiff protective layer; plants, not animals),
*vacuole* (storage sac for water, nutrients, wastes), *cell membrane*
(border that controls what enters and leaves).

## Worked example 1 — "what breaks if this stops?"

**Question:** A cell's mitochondria suddenly stop working. Which jobs fail
first, and why?

**Reasoning:** Mitochondria supply the energy (ATP) that powers the other
jobs. Without energy: ribosomes cannot link amino acids (building costs
energy), the ER and Golgi cannot process and ship (moving and packaging
cost energy), and the membrane cannot run its energy-powered transport
(Lesson 6 preview). The nucleus still *holds* the instructions, but no job
that *uses* them can run. So the answer is not "one thing breaks" — it is
"the whole crew stalls for lack of power." This is the lesson's core move:
structures are understood by their *dependencies*.

## Worked example 2 — tracing a protein's journey

**Question:** A cell needs to release a digestive enzyme (a protein) to the
outside. Trace its path.

**Reasoning:** The instruction is copied from the **nucleus** → a
**ribosome** on the **rough ER** links amino acids into the enzyme →
the **rough ER** folds it and passes it along → the **Golgi apparatus**
finishes and packages it in a membrane sac → the sac fuses with the
**cell membrane** and releases the enzyme outside. Miss any stop and the
delivery fails: no ribosome, no protein; no Golgi, no packaging; no
membrane fusion, no exit. Notice the answer is a *path*, not a list —
structure *and* sequence.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. From memory with the cards face-down: name the nine structures and give
   each a one-phrase job title. *(Hint: start with "the vault, the
   builders, the workshop…")*
2. Which structures are found in plants but not in animal cells? What
   does each difference make possible for the plant?
3. "What breaks if this stops?" — the Golgi apparatus shuts down. List at
   least two downstream failures and explain each.
4. Error analysis: "The nucleus is the brain of the cell, so it tells the
   ribosomes what to do step by step." Explain what is right and what is
   misleading about the brain comparison. *(Hint: think about where the
   instructions actually go.)*
5. A cell is using lots of energy to move substances across its membrane
   (Lesson 6 preview). Which organelle is working hardest? How would you
   test that claim by comparing two cell types?

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. Draw the protein's journey from Worked example 2 as a five-stop flowchart
   with one-sentence job descriptions at each stop. (A written list with
   arrows is fine — drawing skill is not graded.)
2. "What breaks if this stops?" — the cell membrane becomes fully
   permeable (lets everything through). Predict two consequences for the
   cell and explain each.
3. A root cell (underground, no light) and a leaf cell are both plant
   cells. Which organelle does the leaf cell have that the root cell does
   not need? Explain.
4. Muscle cells contain far more mitochondria than skin cells. Explain why,
   using the "power station" job.
5. Error analysis: "Ribosomes are only found floating in the cytoplasm."
   Correct the statement and explain why the correction matters for the
   protein's journey.
6. Write a 4-sentence paragraph: "Why the cell is like a city, and where
   the comparison breaks down." (Every comparison has a limit — name one.)
7. Predict: a cell that secretes large amounts of protein (for example, a
   gland cell) would have an especially large amount of which two
   structures? Justify with their jobs.

## Application

Build the cell's "org chart": arrange the nine index cards to show who
depends on whom (arrows from supplier to user), and write one sentence per
arrow explaining the dependency. This chart becomes the reference you
check your Session 2 practice against — and the adult keeps it, because
Lesson 2 adds the plant/animal split to it.

## Exit check

From memory: the nine structures, each job in under ten words, and one
dependency (which job needs which other job to run).

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** the job-title chant — "vault, builders, workshop,
  shipping, power, kitchen, fence, tank, gate" — with the cards as visual
  anchors; home-language job titles welcomed beside the English terms.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort the cards into a
  dependency web on the table and explain it aloud instead of writing the
  flowchart.
- **Extension:** research (adult-approved source) one structure left out
  of today's list — for example, lysosomes or the cytoskeleton — and add
  it to the org chart with its job title and one dependency. Record the
  source.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The "brain" analogy for the nucleus over-attributes: the nucleus stores and
issues instructions but does not supervise each step — push the
instruction-vault framing. Second, learners often memorize structures as a
list and miss dependencies; the "what breaks if this stops?" move is the
antidote — use it every lesson this week. Third, "chloroplasts = plants,
mitochondria = animals" is wrong: plant cells have *both* (they still need
to release energy from sugars). Answer-key references:
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
