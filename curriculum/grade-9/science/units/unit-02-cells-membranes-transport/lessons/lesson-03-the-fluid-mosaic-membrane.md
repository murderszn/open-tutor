# Lesson 03 — The Fluid Mosaic Membrane

**Student goal:** I can describe the membrane's structure (phospholipid
bilayer, proteins, cholesterol, carbohydrate tags) and explain how that
structure makes it selectively permeable.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 20**

  (open on screen or printed)
- Craft materials for a bilayer model (two colors of clay/beads for heads

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

"Every cell has a border. If the border let *everything* through, what
would happen to the inside of the cell? If it let *nothing* through?"
Establish the need before the mechanism: the membrane must be *choosy* —
open enough for trade, closed enough to stay different inside.

## Teaching

The cell membrane is built from **phospholipids** — molecules with a split
personality. Each phospholipid has a **hydrophilic ("water-loving") head**
that mixes happily with water, and two **hydrophobic ("water-fearing")
tails** that avoid water. Because the fluid inside and outside the cell is
watery, the phospholipids arrange themselves into a **bilayer**: heads
facing the water on both sides, tails tucked together in the middle, hiding
from the water. Two layers, heads out, tails in — that is the whole
architecture, and it assembles itself wherever phospholipids meet water.

But a bare bilayer would be a plain wall. The real membrane is the
**fluid mosaic model**: a *fluid* double layer studded with a *mosaic* of
other parts, all drifting slowly like tiles floating on a pond:

- **Integral proteins** — embedded in the bilayer. Some are *channels*:
  tunnels that let specific substances through (the green tunnel in the
  diagram). Some are *pumps*: machines that push substances across at an
  energy cost (the orange PUMP in the diagram — Lesson 6).
- **Cholesterol** — small molecules wedged between the tails that keep the
  membrane flexible: stiff enough to hold together, fluid enough to let
  parts drift.
- **Carbohydrate chains** — sugar tags on the outer surface (attached to
  proteins or lipids) that act as ID badges, letting cells recognize each
  other — "self" vs. "non-self."

This structure *explains* **selective permeability** — the membrane's
choosiness. The rule falls straight out of the chemistry:

- **Cross freely through the bilayer:** small, uncharged molecules — oxygen
  (O₂) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) slip between the phospholipids (the red
  dashed arrow in the diagram). Small and hydrophobic enough to pass the
  tails.
- **Need protein help:** ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) and large or polar molecules
  like glucose. Ions are charged — the hydrophobic core repels them; large
  polar molecules cannot squeeze between the tails. So they go *through*
  channel proteins (facilitated diffusion, no energy — Lesson 4) or get
  *pumped* (active transport, costs energy — Lesson 6).

Read the diagram once more with the rule in mind: the red arrow (O₂)
passes *between* phospholipids; the green arrow (glucose) passes *through*
a protein; the orange pump moves Na⁺ *against* its gradient and is labeled
with its energy cost. Structure → function, three times over.

**Key vocabulary:** *phospholipid* (membrane molecule: hydrophilic head +
two hydrophobic tails), *bilayer* (two phospholipid layers, heads out,
tails in), *hydrophilic* (water-loving; mixes with water), *hydrophobic*
(water-fearing; avoids water), *fluid mosaic model* (the membrane as a
fluid phospholipid bilayer with a drifting mosaic of proteins, cholesterol,
and carbohydrate tags), *selectively permeable* (lets some substances
through and blocks others), *integral protein* (protein embedded in the
membrane; channels and pumps), *cholesterol* (membrane lipid that tunes
fluidity), *carbohydrate chain* (surface sugar tag for cell recognition).

## Worked example 1 — who needs a protein?

**Question:** Oxygen, glucose, and sodium ions (Na⁺) all need to cross a
cell membrane. Which cross freely, and which need protein help? Explain
each with the bilayer's chemistry.

**Reasoning:** Oxygen is small and uncharged → slips between the
phospholipid tails → *crosses freely* (simple diffusion). Glucose is large
(and polar) → cannot squeeze between the tails → needs a *channel protein*
(facilitated diffusion). Na⁺ is charged → the hydrophobic core repels it →
needs a *channel or pump* depending on direction. The adult names the
general move: *match the substance's properties (size, charge) to the
barrier's chemistry (hydrophobic core)* — that matching *is* selective
permeability.

## Worked example 2 — why the membrane is "fluid"

**Question:** The diagram is a still picture, but the model is called
*fluid*. What does "fluid" mean here, and why does it matter?

**Reasoning:** "Fluid" means the phospholipids and proteins are not locked
in place — they drift sideways within their layer, like people shifting
position in a crowd without leaving the crowd. It matters because a rigid
wall would crack when the cell changes shape (think of a white blood cell
engulfing food, or a red blood cell squeezing through a capillary), and
because proteins need to move to do their jobs. Cholesterol's role is to
keep this fluidity in the working range — not frozen, not falling apart.
A common error: "fluid means the membrane flows like water and leaks." No
— fluid *within* the layer, sealed *across* it.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. Build the bilayer: lay out two rows of heads-and-tails (clay, beads, or
   paper) with heads facing out on both sides. Point to each part and name
   it. *(Hint: "heads out" is the one rule that builds the whole thing.)*
2. For each substance — CO₂, a sodium ion, a small hydrophobic vitamin,
   glucose — say "through the bilayer" or "needs a protein," and justify
   with size/charge.
3. Error analysis: "The membrane is selectively permeable, so it lets
   nutrients in and keeps wastes out." What is right about this, and what
   important part is missing? *(Hint: think about direction — Lesson 6.)*
4. Why do the carbohydrate ID tags sit only on the *outer* surface? What
   job would they fail at on the inside?

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. From memory, sketch the bilayer cross-section (two rows of headed
   phospholipids, heads out) and label: hydrophilic head, hydrophobic
   tails, outside, cytoplasm. Check against the SVG; mark what you missed.
2. A new drug molecule is large and charged. Using the selective
   permeability rule, explain why simply swallowing it may not get it
   *into* cells — and what kind of helper it would need.
3. Cholesterol keeps the membrane fluid. Predict what happens to membrane
   function in extreme cold *without* enough unsaturated tails or
   cholesterol — explain using the "frozen vs. falling apart" range.
4. Write a 3-sentence paragraph: "How the fluid mosaic model explains
   selective permeability." (Structure → function; name at least two
   mosaic parts.)
5. The carbohydrate chains are called ID tags. Explain how a white blood
   cell could use them to tell a body cell from an invading bacterium.
6. Error analysis: "Proteins float in the membrane, so they eventually drift
   away into the water." Explain the error using the hydrophobic effect.
7. Look at the pump in the diagram: it moves Na⁺ from "few inside" to
   "many outside." In one sentence, say why this must cost energy.
   (Lesson 6 will build the full account.)

## Supports and extensions

  through, big needs a door" — with the bilayer model as the visual
  anchor.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build the full mosaic
  (bilayer + channel + pump + tag) from craft materials and narrate the
  three arrows aloud instead of sketching.
- **Extension:** read the Session 6 reference (OpenStax Biology 2e §5.1,
  "Membrane Fluidity") and add one sentence to your notes on how
  saturated vs. unsaturated tails change fluidity. Record the source.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

"The membrane is a solid wall with holes" — push *fluid* and *selective
chemistry*, not holes; channels are specific, not open gaps. "Hydrophobic
means hates all contact" — it means *avoids water*, and that avoidance is
exactly what builds the bilayer. Third: learners memorize "small = free,
large = protein" and forget *charge* — ions are small and still need help;
drill Na⁺ explicitly. Answer-key references:
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
