# Lesson 06 — Active Transport and Energy

**Student goal:** I can explain when cells must use active transport,
how ATP pays for it, and how transport connects to homeostasis.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 12 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- The membrane diagram
  ([`assets/membrane-cross-section.svg`](../assets/membrane-cross-section.svg))
  — focus on the orange PUMP
- The two-question index card from Lesson 4

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

"So far everything moved *down* its gradient for free. But the pump in
our diagram pushes Na⁺ from 'few inside' to 'many outside' — *up* the
gradient. What could possibly pay for that?" Collect ideas; the lesson
names the currency.

## Teaching

Diffusion — simple or facilitated — is downhill: high → low, no energy
bill. But cells constantly need to move substances **uphill**, against
their gradients: pumping sodium *out* of the cell even though sodium is
already scarcer inside, loading sugar *into* a cell that is already full
of it, keeping the inside composition stubbornly *different* from the
outside. That is **active transport**: movement against the concentration
gradient, and it always costs energy.

The energy currency is **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate), made by the
mitochondria (Lesson 1's power stations — the dependency web closes).
Think of ATP as a rechargeable battery: the cell charges it by breaking
down food, then spends it to do work — including running membrane pumps.
The classic example is the **sodium-potassium pump**: it pushes Na⁺ *out*
of the cell and pulls K⁺ *in*, both against their gradients, burning ATP
with every cycle. Look at the orange PUMP in the diagram: the arrow runs
from "few Na+ inside (low)" to "many Na+ outside (high)" — uphill — and
the label says the price out loud: *costs ATP energy*.

Two more transport modes complete the picture, both active (both cost
energy) and both for cargo too big for channels:

- **Endocytosis** — the membrane wraps around a large particle or droplet
  and pinches off a sac *into* the cell (a white blood cell engulfing a
  bacterium; a cell drinking in fluid).
- **Exocytosis** — the reverse: a sac fuses with the membrane and dumps its
  cargo *outside* (the Golgi's shipping from Lesson 1 *is* exocytosis —
  the protein's journey ends this way).

Why pay? Because of **homeostasis** — keeping internal conditions stable
while the outside world changes. Your cells hold their sodium low and
potassium high *on purpose*, against the gradients' constant pull toward
evening out. Stop the pumps and the gradients collapse: nerve cells cannot
fire, muscles cannot contract, the whole system drifts toward equilibrium
— which, for a living cell, is another word for dead. Diffusion evens
things out; life spends energy to *stay uneven*. That sentence is the
unit's thesis — write it down.

**Key vocabulary:** *active transport* (movement of a substance against
its concentration gradient; requires energy), *ATP* (adenosine
triphosphate — the cell's energy currency; made by mitochondria, spent to
do work), *sodium-potassium pump* (membrane protein that pumps Na⁺ out and
K⁺ in against their gradients, using ATP), *endocytosis* (membrane
wraps and brings large cargo into the cell), *exocytosis* (vesicle fuses
with the membrane and releases cargo outside), *homeostasis* (keeping
internal conditions stable despite outside change).

## Worked example 1 — spotting the energy bill

**Question:** A cell moves glucose inward through a channel protein. Later
the same cell pumps sodium outward with a pump protein. Which movement
costs ATP, and how do you know without being told?

**Reasoning:** Run the two-question card on each, then add the third
question: *"Is it with or against the gradient?"* Glucose: high outside →
low inside, through a channel → *with* the gradient → facilitated
diffusion → **no ATP**. Sodium: few inside → many outside → *against* the
gradient → **costs ATP**. The adult names the three-question machine the
learner now owns: **(1) Can it cross? (2) Which way does the gradient
point? (3) With or against?** — and only "against" ever sends an energy
bill.

## Worked example 2 — what happens when the pumps stop

**Question:** A poison blocks ATP production in mitochondria. Predict the
effect on a nerve cell's sodium gradient over the next hour, and explain.

**Reasoning:** Step 1 — the dependency chain: no ATP → the sodium-potassium
pump stops (Lesson 1: power station down, everything stalls). Step 2 — with
the pump stopped, Na⁺ still leaks *in* down its gradient through channels
(diffusion never sleeps). Step 3 — net result: inside Na⁺ rises toward
outside levels; the gradient *collapses* toward equilibrium. Step 4 — the
consequence: the nerve cell's signaling depends on that gradient (it is
the loaded spring behind every signal), so signaling fails. The one-line
version: *kill the energy, kill the unevenness, kill the function.*
Reasoning to name aloud: this is Lessons 1 + 3 + 4 + 6 chained — the unit
is one connected argument now, not six separate lessons.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. For each — O₂ into a muscle cell, glucose through a channel into a
   hungry cell, Na⁺ pumped out, a bacterium engulfed — run the
   three-question machine and name the transport type. *(Hint: question 3
   is the only one that can cost ATP.)*
2. The diagram's pump moves Na⁺ "against the gradient." In your own words:
   what does "against" mean here, and what pays for it?
3. Error analysis: "Active transport is faster than diffusion because it
   uses energy." Explain the error — what does the energy actually buy?
   *(Hint: speed vs. direction.)*
4. Connect to Lesson 1: which organelle must a cell rich in pumps also be
   rich in? Explain the dependency.

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. Sort these into passive vs. active with justification: (a) O₂ crossing
   the bilayer into a cell, (b) K⁺ pumped into a cell against its
   gradient, (c) water entering by osmosis, (d) a white blood cell
   engulfing a bacterium, (e) glucose leaving a cell through a channel
   down its gradient, (f) a vesicle releasing hormones outside the cell.
2. A cell needs to concentrate a nutrient inside itself to 10× the outside
   level. Explain why diffusion alone can never achieve this, and what the
   cell must do instead.
3. Write a 4-sentence paragraph: "Diffusion evens things out; life spends
   energy to stay uneven." Use the sodium-potassium pump and homeostasis
   in your explanation.
4. Endocytosis and exocytosis move *large* cargo. Explain in two sentences
   why a channel protein cannot do this job instead.
5. Error analysis: "Exocytosis is a type of diffusion because substances
   move out of the cell." Correct it — what defines diffusion, and why
   does exocytosis fail the definition?
6. Predict: a cell is placed in a solution with no usable energy source and
   its ATP runs out. Which transport processes continue, and which stop?
   Explain each.
7. Homeostasis preview (Unit 08): body temperature, blood sugar, and blood
   salt levels are all held steady. In one sentence each, say what
   "staying uneven" means for blood salt — and what osmosis (Lesson 5)
   would do if the body stopped regulating.

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** the chant — "with the flow, free to go; against
  the flow, ATP dough" — with the three arrows on the diagram as anchors.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort scenario cards
  (passive vs. active) on the table and defend each placement aloud.
- **Extension:** research (adult-approved source) one real consequence of
  pump failure — for example, what cyanide does to ATP production, or why
  the heart drug digitalis targets the sodium-potassium pump — and write a
  5-sentence explanation using the dependency chain from Worked example 2.
  Record the source. (Kept conceptual; no medical advice.)

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

"Energy makes it faster" — energy buys *direction against the gradient*,
not speed. "Active transport is only pumps" — endo/exocytosis count, and
learners forget them on sorts. Third: "homeostasis means everything stays
the same" — it means *regulated* stability around a set point, maintained
by constant work; the pump never gets to rest. Answer-key references:
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06).
