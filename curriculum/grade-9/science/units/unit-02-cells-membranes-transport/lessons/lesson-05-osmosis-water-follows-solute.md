# Lesson 05 — Osmosis: Water Follows Solute

**Student goal:** I can predict which way water moves by osmosis using
hypertonic, hypotonic, and isotonic, and explain my Sessions 8–9
investigation data with the gradient model.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 10 of 20**

- Your Sessions 8–9 osmosis data table (from
  [project.md](../project.md))
- The two-question index card from Lesson 4

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

"Your eggs (or potato slices) sat in three different solutions. Some
gained mass, some lost it. Before we name anything: which substance
actually moved through the membrane — the salt/sugar, or the water?"
Let the data argue; the teaching block gives the language.

## Teaching

**Osmosis** is diffusion of *water* across a selectively permeable
membrane. The twist that confuses everyone: we track the *solute* (the
dissolved stuff, like salt) to predict where the *water* goes — because
water moves toward the *higher solute concentration*, as if chasing
balance. **Water follows solute.**

The three words that run every osmosis prediction describe the solution
*relative to the cell*:

- **Hypertonic** — the solution has *more* solute than the cell. Water
  leaves the cell (following the solute out) → the cell shrinks.
- **Hypotonic** — the solution has *less* solute than the cell. Water
  enters the cell → the cell swells.
- **Isotonic** — solute concentrations match. Water crosses both ways
  equally → no net change.

Memory hook the unit will reuse: **"HYPER = high solute, water exits;
HYPO = low solute, water enters; ISO = equal, nothing net."** And the
two-question card still rules: (1) *Can it cross?* — water crosses through
the bilayer (slowly) and through water-channel proteins (fast); the
dissolved salt/sugar mostly cannot. (2) *Which way does the gradient
point?* — for water, toward the higher solute side.

Now read your Sessions 8–9 data with this language. If your egg in strong
salt water lost mass, say it precisely: "The salt solution was hypertonic
to the egg; water moved out of the egg by osmosis, down its gradient
toward the higher solute concentration; the egg lost mass." Every clause
is checkable — that is what makes it science rather than story.

Why this matters beyond the lab: your cells live bathed in fluids the body
keeps near-isotonic (blood is about 0.9% salt — *physiological saline* —
for exactly this reason). A red blood cell dropped in pure water swells
and can burst; in very salty water it shrivels. The body spends enormous
effort keeping the surroundings isotonic, because osmosis never sleeps.

**Key vocabulary:** *osmosis* (diffusion of water across a selectively
permeable membrane), *solute* (a substance dissolved in a fluid),
*hypertonic* (solution with higher solute concentration than the cell —
water leaves the cell), *hypotonic* (lower solute than the cell — water
enters), *isotonic* (equal solute — no net water movement),
*physiological saline* (about 0.9% salt solution matching body fluids).

## Worked example 1 — reading the egg data

**Question:** An egg (shell removed) starts at 62.4 g. After 24 hours in a
strong salt solution it masses 55.1 g. Was the solution hypertonic,
hypotonic, or isotonic to the egg? Explain with the gradient model.

**Reasoning:** Mass fell by 7.3 g — the egg *lost* water (percent change =
−11.7%). Water leaves when the outside solute is higher, so the solution
was **hypertonic**. Gradient account: solute high outside → water's
gradient points outward → net water movement out → mass drops. Check the
alternative: if it had been hypotonic, water would have entered and mass
would have risen — the data rejects that. Reasoning to name aloud: the
*conclusion* (hypertonic) is read *off the mass change*, and the
*explanation* runs through the gradient — conclusion from data,
mechanism from the model.

## Worked example 2 — predicting before measuring

**Question:** A potato slice goes into pure distilled water. Predict the
mass change and explain.

**Reasoning:** Two-question card. (1) *Can it cross?* Water crosses the
cell membranes; the potato's internal solutes mostly stay put. (2) *Which
way?* Pure water has ~zero solute — it is **hypotonic** to the potato
cells (their insides hold dissolved sugars and salts). Water's gradient
points inward, toward the higher solute concentration → water enters → the
slice **gains mass** (and firms up — turgid, like the Lesson 2 vacuole
discussion). Note the prediction is made *before* the scale is read — that
ordering (predict → measure → compare) is the investigation habit from
U01, reused on purpose.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. Take one row of your Sessions 8–9 data: state the mass change, name the
   solution hyper/hypo/isotonic, and write the two-sentence gradient
   explanation. *(Hint: "Water moved ___, toward the ___ solute side.")*
2. A red blood cell is placed in pure water. Predict what happens and
   explain — then say why hospitals use saline drips instead of pure
   water. *(Hint: what does "physiological" mean in the vocabulary?)*
3. Error analysis: "Osmosis is the diffusion of salt across the membrane."
   Correct it precisely — what moves, and what is tracked?
4. Your control cup (isotonic solution) shows a tiny mass change (+0.8%).
   Is your claim in trouble? Explain what a small control change means
   versus a large one. *(Hint: U01 — controls bound the noise.)*

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. Three cups: A = distilled water, B = 10% salt solution, C = 0.9% salt
   solution. Identical potato slices go in each. Rank the slices by final
   mass (highest to lowest) and justify each ranking with hyper/hypo/iso
   language.
2. A wilted lettuce leaf firms up in cold water. Explain using osmosis and
   connect it to the central vacuole (Lesson 2).
3. Write the memory hook in your own words and use it to classify: seawater
   (~3.5% salt) relative to a human red blood cell. Predict the cell's
   fate and explain.
4. Fictional practice dataset (labeled: invented for practice, not your
   measurements): four egg masses before/after 24 h in unknown solutions
   W, X, Y, Z: W: 60.0→60.2 g; X: 61.5→55.0 g; Y: 59.8→66.1 g; Z:
   60.5→60.4 g. Compute percent change for each, classify each solution,
   and identify which solution was most hypertonic. Show all work.
5. Error analysis: "The salt moved into the egg, which is why it got
   heavier." A learner's egg *lost* mass in salt water. Explain the two
   errors (direction of water, what the mass measures).
6. Explain in 3–4 sentences why the body keeps blood near-isotonic. Name
   one thing that would go wrong in pure water and one in very salty
   water.
7. Design check: your investigation used repeated trials. Explain in two
   sentences what repeated trials protect against in *this* lab
   specifically (not in general).

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** the chant — "HYPER high, water says bye; HYPO low,
  water in flow; ISO equal, net zero" — with your own data table as the
  anchor.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may explain each data row aloud
  using the two-question card, with the adult scribing the final paragraph.
- **Extension:** research (adult-approved source) how plant roots use
  osmosis to absorb water from soil, and write a 4-sentence explanation
  connecting root hairs, soil water, and the hypotonic/hypertonic
  language. Record the source.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

"Water moves to dilute the strong side" is *almost* right but backwards in
causal language — insist on "water moves down *its own* gradient toward
higher solute." "Salt moves instead of water" — the mass change is water;
the membrane blocks most solute. Third: learners classify the solution
without saying *relative to what* — hypertonic is meaningless without "to
the cell"; demand the full phrase. Answer-key references:
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05).
