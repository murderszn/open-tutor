# Lesson 02 — Plant vs. Animal Cells

**Student goal:** I can compare plant and animal cells structure by
structure and explain what each difference makes possible.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 3 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- The nine index cards from Lesson 1
- Two blank half-pages: one labeled "animal cell," one labeled "plant
  cell"

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

"Yesterday's crew had nine members. Which ones would a plant cell be
missing if plants were just like animals — and which extra ones does it
actually have?" Collect guesses without correcting; the teaching block
settles it.

## Teaching

Plant and animal cells run the *same core crew* — nucleus, ribosomes, ER,
Golgi, mitochondria, cell membrane — because both are eukaryotic cells
doing the same basic living. The differences are three, and each one buys
the plant something an animal gets another way:

| Structure | Animal cell | Plant cell | What the difference makes possible |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Absent — only the flexible membrane | Present — stiff cellulose layer outside the membrane | The plant holds its shape and stands upright without a skeleton; the animal cell can change shape, crawl, and engulf food |
| Chloroplasts | Absent | Present — capture light, build sugars | The plant makes its own food from sunlight (photosynthesis, Unit 03); the animal must eat |
| Vacuole | Small, several, temporary | One giant central vacuole | The plant stores large water reserves — a full vacuole presses outward and keeps the plant firm; a wilted plant has lost vacuole water |

Two corrections that matter:

- **Plant cells still have mitochondria.** Making sugar (chloroplast) is
  not the same as *releasing energy* from sugar (mitochondrion). The plant
  does both — it photosynthesizes by day and respires around the clock,
  exactly as Unit 03 will show.
- **"Bigger vacuole" is a strategy, not a decoration.** Because plants
  cannot walk to water, they bank it. The central vacuole is the plant's
  answer to being rooted in place — and it is why watering a wilted plant
  visibly revives it within hours.

How to read any cell diagram from here on: first find the membrane (the
border), then the nucleus (the vault), then scan for the three
difference-makers — wall, chloroplasts, giant vacuole. If none of the three
appear, you are looking at an animal cell.

**Key vocabulary:** *eukaryotic cell* (a cell with a nucleus and
membrane-bound organelles — both plant and animal cells qualify),
*cellulose* (the stiff carbohydrate of plant cell walls), *central
vacuole* (the large water-storage sac that firms plant cells). (All other
terms were taught in Lesson 1 and are reused, not redefined.)

## Worked example 1 — identifying the cell

**Question:** A diagram shows a cell with a nucleus, mitochondria, a cell
membrane — and no wall, no chloroplasts, and several small vacuoles. Plant
or animal? What is the evidence?

**Reasoning:** Check the three difference-makers: no cell wall, no
chloroplasts, no giant central vacuole. The shared crew (nucleus,
mitochondria, membrane) appears in both, so it proves nothing either way.
*Evidence:* the absence of all three plant-only structures. *Conclusion:*
animal cell. Reasoning to name aloud: the shared structures are
*non-diagnostic* — like finding "has a kitchen" in two houses — while the
difference-makers decide.

## Worked example 2 — what the difference buys

**Question:** "Plant cells have a wall, so they are better protected than
animal cells." Evaluate.

**Reasoning:** Partly true, mostly incomplete. The wall does protect and
support — that is real. But "better" ignores the *trade*: the wall makes
the plant cell rigid, so it cannot do what animal cells do with a flexible
membrane — change shape, crawl, or engulf food particles (endocytosis,
Lesson 6). Each design buys something and costs something. The adult names
the move: in biology, *structure fits lifestyle*, and every structure has a
trade-off. A one-sided "better" is always a warning sign in an
explanation.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. On your two half-pages, place the nine cards on each: which cards go on
   both pages, and which three go only on the plant page? *(Hint: the
   shared crew is bigger than the difference list.)*
2. A classmate says, "Plant cells don't need mitochondria because they
   have chloroplasts." Correct the classmate using the two different jobs.
3. Draw and label (or describe in writing) where the central vacuole sits
   in a plant cell, and explain in two sentences why a wilted plant
   recovers after watering.
4. Error analysis: "Animal cells have no vacuoles." Explain the error —
   what is true about animal-cell vacuoles?

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. Annotate your two half-page diagrams: label every structure you placed,
   and add one job-phrase per label (from Lesson 1). The plant page must
   show all three difference-makers.
2. Build the comparison table from the teaching block from memory, then
   check it against the lesson. Mark any row you missed — that row is your
   re-study target.
3. A newly discovered single-celled pond organism has chloroplasts but no
   cell wall. Which two plant features does it share, and which is it
   missing? What would you predict about how it gets water and holds its
   shape? (There is no single right lifestyle answer — defend your
   prediction.)
4. Explain why a plant cell can afford to be box-shaped and rigid while a
   white blood cell must stay flexible. Name the structures involved.
5. Write a 3-sentence paragraph: "Why finding a nucleus in a diagram tells
   you almost nothing about plant vs. animal — and what to look for
   instead."
6. Extension preview: the cell membrane appears on *both* pages. In one
   sentence, predict what job it does that both cell types need. (Lessons
   3–6 will confirm or revise your prediction.)

## Application

Finish both annotated diagrams and file them in your notebook — they are
the diagrams you will reconstruct from memory in the Week 10 review
(Session 17), and the membrane detail you add in Lesson 3 goes on the same
pages.

## Exit check

From memory: the three plant/animal differences and, for each, what the
difference makes possible (the "buys" column, not just the name).

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** the difference chant — "wall, kitchen, tank" —
  with the three plant-only cards pulled aside as visual anchors.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build both cells from clay
  or beads (one consistent shape per structure) and explain the differences
  aloud instead of drawing.
- **Extension:** look up one more plant-only or animal-only structure
  (for example, plasmodesmata in plants or lysosomes prominent in animal
  cells) from an adult-approved source, add it to the correct diagram with
  a job phrase, and record the source.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

"Chloroplasts replace mitochondria" is the big one — stress *making* sugar
vs. *releasing energy* from sugar as two different jobs, both needed. "No
vacuoles in animal cells" is the second — small and temporary is still a
vacuole. Third: learners treat the wall as pure armor and miss the
rigidity trade-off; the white-blood-cell contrast fixes it. Answer-key
references: [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
