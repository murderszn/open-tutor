## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. Nucleus — instruction vault (stores DNA); ribosomes — builders (link
   amino acids into proteins); endoplasmic reticulum — workshop/hallways
   (rough folds proteins, smooth builds lipids); Golgi apparatus —
   packaging and shipping; mitochondria — power stations (release energy
   from food as ATP); chloroplasts — kitchens (capture light, build sugars;
   plants/algae); cell wall — fence (stiff support; plants, not animals);
   vacuole — storage tank (water, nutrients, wastes); cell membrane —
   border and gate (controls entry/exit).
2. Cell wall, chloroplasts, and the giant central vacuole are plant-only
   (three, not two). The wall holds the plant's shape upright without a
   skeleton; chloroplasts let the plant make its own food from sunlight;
   the central vacuole banks water and keeps the plant firm.
3. Golgi shutdown: (a) proteins from the ER are never finished, packaged,
   or addressed — secreted proteins (enzymes, hormones) never reach their
   destinations; (b) membrane proteins are never delivered to the membrane
   — the membrane cannot renew its channels and pumps, so transport
   degrades. Both are downstream of the shipping step.
4. Right: the nucleus holds the instructions that direct cell activity.
   Misleading: "brain… tells step by step" implies active supervision of
   each step. In fact copies of the instructions leave the nucleus and
   ribosomes read them independently — the nucleus issues, it does not
   micromanage.
5. Mitochondria. Test: compare a high-transport cell type (e.g., a root
   hair cell absorbing minerals, or a kidney tubule cell) with a
   low-transport one (e.g., a fat-storage cell) and predict — then check
   in an adult-approved micrograph source — that the high-transport cell
   carries more mitochondria.

**Independent practice**

1. Flowchart (arrows = sequence): nucleus (copies instruction) →
   ribosome on rough ER (links amino acids) → rough ER (folds the protein)
   → Golgi apparatus (finishes, packages in a sac) → cell membrane (sac
   fuses, enzyme released outside). Accept any equivalent five-stop path
   with one job sentence per stop.
2. Fully permeable membrane: (a) every gradient collapses — needed
   substances leak out, outside substances flood in; the cell cannot keep
   its inside different from the outside, so homeostasis fails; (b) the
   cell loses osmotic control — it will swell or shrivel with its
   surroundings and may burst. Either consequence must be tied to the loss
   of *selectivity*, not just "the wall is gone."
3. The leaf cell has chloroplasts; the root cell does not need them —
   there is no light underground, so photosynthesis is impossible there.
   Both still need mitochondria (respiration runs in both).
4. Muscle contraction is mechanical work and costs ATP every cycle;
   mitochondria supply it, so muscle cells carry thousands. Skin's main
   job is barrier protection — low energy demand, few mitochondria.
5. Correction: many ribosomes are attached to the rough ER (the "rough"
   in its name). It matters because proteins built there feed directly
   into the ER for folding and shipping (the secretory path from Worked
   example 2); free-floating ribosomes make proteins for use *inside* the
   cell.
6. Accept any paragraph with a real similarity (specialized parts with
   jobs, transport networks, border control, power supply, waste handling)
   AND a real limit, e.g.: cities are planned top-down, cells are not; a
   city can buy anything, a cell is limited by membrane selectivity; city
   districts do not reproduce. The limit is what is graded.
7. Rough ER and Golgi apparatus — the builder/folder and the
   packager/shipper of the secretory path; a secreting cell runs that path
   at full volume.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice**

1. Both pages: nucleus, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus,
   mitochondria, cell membrane, small vacuoles. Plant page only: cell
   wall, chloroplasts, giant central vacuole.
2. Correction: chloroplasts *make* sugar using light (photosynthesis);
   mitochondria *release energy* from sugar (respiration). The plant does
   both — it photosynthesizes by day and respires around the clock.
3. The central vacuole is the large water sac filling most of a plant
   cell. Watering: soil water is hypotonic to the cell → water enters by
   osmosis → the vacuole refills and presses outward → the plant firms up.
4. Error: animal cells *do* have vacuoles — small, several, temporary
   (storage and transport). What they lack is the single giant *central*
   vacuole of plants.

**Independent practice**

1. Required labels — animal page: nucleus, ribosomes, ER, Golgi,
   mitochondria, cell membrane, small vacuoles, each with a job phrase.
   Plant page: all of the above plus cell wall, chloroplasts, central
   vacuole, each with a job phrase. Job phrases may be the learner's own
   words if the meaning matches Lesson 1.
2. Three rows: (wall | absent | present, cellulose | holds shape/erect
   without skeleton vs. flexible); (chloroplasts | absent | present |
   makes own food vs. must eat); (vacuole | small, several | one giant
   central | banks water, firms the plant).
3. Shares chloroplasts (photosynthesis) with plants; missing the wall.
   Defensible predictions: absorbs water by osmosis across its membrane;
   without a wall it cannot resist osmotic pressure, so it likely lives in
   near-isotonic water or actively regulates; shape held by the membrane
   and internal supports — flexible rather than rigid. Any prediction is
   acceptable if tied to the missing wall.
4. The wall gives the plant rigidity for support (it cannot move, so
   stiffness is an asset); the white blood cell must squeeze through
   capillaries and wrap around pathogens (endocytosis) — both need a
   flexible membrane with no wall.
5. Accept any 3-sentence paragraph making the point: the nucleus is shared
   (non-diagnostic), so identification must use the three difference-makers
   (wall, chloroplasts, central vacuole).
6. Both cell types must trade with their surroundings while keeping their
   insides different — the membrane's controlled exchange (nutrients in,
   wastes out, insides protected) is needed by every cell.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. Physical bilayer: two rows; every head (blue circle) faces outward
   toward the water side; tails point inward and meet in the middle. The
   adult checks: no head buried in the tail region, no tails pointing at
   water.
2. CO₂ — through the bilayer (small, uncharged). Sodium ion — needs a
   protein (charged; the hydrophobic core repels it). Small hydrophobic
   vitamin — through the bilayer (hydrophobic, small enough). Glucose —
   needs a protein (large and polar; cannot squeeze between tails).
3. Right: the membrane is choosy. Missing: direction is not one-way —
   wastes must also *exit*, needed substances are kept *in*, and some
   substances are pumped *against* their gradients. "Nutrients in, wastes
   out" as a slogan hides the export half and the uphill half.
4. The tags' job is recognition *by other cells* (self vs. non-self for
   the immune system, tissue formation). Inside the cell there is nothing
   to recognize them — the job only exists at the outer surface.

**Independent practice**

1. Sketch: two head-rows facing out, tails meeting midline; labels
   hydrophilic head, hydrophobic tails, outside, cytoplasm. Check against
   the SVG; the orientation (heads out) is the graded element.
2. Large and charged → blocked by the hydrophobic core and too big to slip
   between tails. It needs a dedicated transporter/channel — or a
   delivery trick (e.g., packaging in a lipid vesicle). Any answer naming
   the size/charge barrier and a protein-mediated route is acceptable.
3. Without enough kinks/cholesterol, cold packs the straight tails tight —
   the membrane "freezes": proteins cannot drift, transport and shape
   change fail, and the brittle layer can crack and leak.
4. Accept any 3-sentence paragraph with structure → function and at least
   two mosaic parts (e.g., bilayer chemistry explains who passes;
   proteins add specific doors; cholesterol tunes fluidity; tags handle
   recognition).
5. Body cells carry "self" carbohydrate tags the immune system learns to
   tolerate; the bacterium lacks them (or carries foreign patterns), so
   the white blood cell reads it as non-self and engulfs it.
6. Error: membrane proteins have hydrophobic regions sitting in the
   hydrophobic core — drifting out into water would expose those regions
   to water, which the hydrophobic effect forbids. They drift *sideways*
   within the layer, never out of it.
7. "Against" means from low concentration to high — the direction diffusion
   would never take on its own. Something must pay for that ordering, and
   the payment is ATP energy.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**

1. Prediction (acceptable variation in wording): the dye molecules move
   outward/downward from the concentrated cloud into the clear water
   (high → low concentration); at equilibrium the glass is a uniform pale
   blue; because the concentration gradient drives net diffusion until
   concentrations even out. All three vocabulary words must appear.
2. O₂ moves *in*, net (high outside → low inside). The gradient persists
   because the cell keeps *burning* O₂ in respiration — consumption holds
   the inside low.
3. Error: particles do not "run out of energy" — their motion is random
   thermal jiggling that never stops. At equilibrium they still cross both
   ways equally; only the *net* movement is zero.
4. O₂ through the bilayer — simple diffusion, no energy. Glucose through a
   channel — facilitated diffusion, no energy. CO₂ through the bilayer —
   simple diffusion, no energy.

**Independent practice**

1. Boxes: 12 dots left, 3 right; arrow left → right labeled "net
   movement"; sides labeled high/low; one sentence ("more wanderers start
   crowded, so net flow goes high → low"). Equilibrium: dots conserved
   (15 total) — accept 7/8 or 8/7.
2. CO₂ moves *out* (high inside → low outside). It helps the cell by
   removing the waste product of respiration — the mitochondria's work
   would otherwise poison the cell with its own exhaust.
3. Glass B is darker at equilibrium: same water volume, five times the dye
   → higher final concentration → darker uniform color. Concentration, not
   total dye alone, is the point.
4. Accept any paragraph using all three words correctly: smell molecules
   jiggle randomly and spread down their gradient until the room reaches
   equilibrium; the book's molecules are locked in a solid — no free
   random motion, no spreading.
5. Correction: the protein provides a *path*, not energy. Facilitated
   diffusion is still down-the-gradient movement; energy is only required
   *against* the gradient.
6. Glucose entry effectively stops. The cell keeps using its internal
   glucose, so the inside concentration falls further — the gradient
   steepens, but with the door blocked nothing crosses; the cell faces a
   glucose shortage despite plenty outside.
7. Similarity: both show net high → low movement toward equilibrium.
   Difference: the glass has no membrane — the dye moves freely through
   water; across a membrane only *permeable* substances cross, and the
   membrane's selectivity (Lesson 3) decides *what* may move at all.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. Format (applied to the learner's own row): "Mass went from ___ g to
   ___ g (___%). The solution was ___tonic to the egg. Water moved
   ___ (in/out) by osmosis, down its gradient toward the higher solute
   concentration on the ___ side." Example: "62.4 g → 55.1 g (−11.7%).
   The salt solution was hypertonic to the egg. Water moved out by
   osmosis, toward the higher solute concentration outside."
2. Pure water is hypotonic to the red blood cell → water rushes in → the
   cell swells and can burst (lyse). Hospitals use ~0.9% saline because it
   is isotonic to body fluids — no net water movement, no swelling or
   shriveling.
3. Correction: osmosis is the diffusion of *water* (not salt) across a
   selectively permeable membrane. We *track* the solute to predict which
   way the *water* goes.
4. No — a +0.8% control change is noise (drying, handling, scale
   precision). The control's job is to *bound* that noise: the claim
   stands if the experimental changes (e.g., ±10%) dwarf it.

**Independent practice**

1. Ranking by final mass: A (distilled, hypotonic → gains) > C (0.9%
   saline, isotonic → ~unchanged) > B (10% salt, hypertonic → loses most).
   Each ranking must name hyper/hypo/iso *relative to the potato cells*.
2. The leaf's cells are water-depleted; cold water is hypotonic to them →
   water enters by osmosis → central vacuoles refill and press outward →
   the leaf firms (turgor restored).
3. Seawater (~3.5% salt) is hypertonic to the red blood cell (~0.9%) →
   water leaves the cell → it shrivels (crenation).
4. W: +0.2/60.0 = **+0.3%** → isotonic. X: −6.5/61.5 = **−10.6%** →
   hypertonic. Y: +6.3/59.8 = **+10.5%** → hypotonic. Z: −0.1/60.5 =
   **−0.2%** → isotonic. Most hypertonic: **X** (largest water loss).
   Work must show the subtractions and divisions.
5. Two errors: (a) the egg *lost* mass, so nothing net entered — water
   left; (b) the membrane blocks most salt anyway — the mass change
   measures *water* movement, not salt movement.
6. Accept any 3–4 sentences covering: every cell is bathed in blood, so
   blood tonicity *is* each cell's environment; pure water around cells →
   swelling/bursting; very salty → shriveling; either breaks cell function.
7. Repeated trials protect against egg-to-egg differences (size, membrane
   thickness), inconsistent drying/handling, and scale error — so a single
   odd egg cannot drive the conclusion.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice**

1. O₂ into muscle — simple diffusion, no ATP (with the gradient, through
   the bilayer). Glucose through a channel, high → low — facilitated
   diffusion, no ATP. Na⁺ pumped out, low → high — active transport, costs
   ATP. Bacterium engulfed — endocytosis, active, costs ATP (membrane
   wrapping is work).
2. "Against" = from lower concentration to higher — the direction diffusion
   would never take spontaneously. The payment is ATP energy, spent by the
   pump protein each cycle.
3. Error: energy does not buy speed — it buys *direction*. Diffusion down
   a steep gradient can be fast and free; active transport is defined by
   moving *against* the gradient, which is impossible without an energy
   input.
4. Mitochondria — the pumps burn ATP, and mitochondria make ATP by
   releasing energy from food. A pump-rich cell without matching power
   stations could not pay its transport bills (Lesson 1's dependency web).

**Independent practice**

1. (a) simple diffusion — passive; (b) active transport — passive? No:
   **active**, against the gradient, ATP; (c) osmosis — passive; (d)
   endocytosis — active; (e) facilitated diffusion — passive; (f)
   exocytosis — active. Each needs the type *and* the justification.
2. Diffusion only moves substances high → low; it can never *build* a
   10× uphill difference — net flow would erase it. The cell must pump
   the nutrient against its gradient using ATP (active transport).
3. Accept any paragraph with: the pump as the "stay uneven" example, an
   osmosis case showing what happens without regulation, and homeostasis
   named as the maintained stability.
4. Channel proteins are narrow, specific pores for small molecules and
   ions — a bacterium or a vesicle of hormones cannot fit. Large cargo
   needs the membrane itself to wrap (in) or fuse (out): endo/exocytosis.
5. Correction: diffusion is *passive* net movement of molecules *down* a
   gradient. Exocytosis is *active* — a vesicle fusing with the membrane,
   costing energy — so it fails both parts of the definition.
6. Continue: simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis — none
   require ATP. Stop: pumps/active transport, endocytosis, exocytosis —
   all spend energy the cell no longer has.
7. "Staying uneven" for blood salt: the body holds ~0.9% despite water
   drunk and salt eaten. Without regulation, osmosis would drag every
   cell toward equilibrium with the last meal — swelling after water,
   shriveling after salt.

## Practice sessions

**Session A**

- Part 1: 1 → Golgi apparatus (unfinished proteins pile up; nothing is
  addressed for delivery). 2 → mitochondria (no ATP; every energy-costly
  job stalls). 3 → nucleus (no new instructions copied; construction
  halts). 4 → (central) vacuole (water storage lost; the plant cannot
  stay firm). 5 → ribosomes (amino acids sit unused; no proteins built).
  6 → chloroplasts (no photosynthesis; the plant must live off stored
  food while still respiring).
- Part 2: at least eight labeled dependency arrows, e.g.: nucleus →
  ribosomes ("instructions to build"); ribosomes → rough ER ("proteins
  to fold"); ER → Golgi ("products to package"); Golgi → membrane
  ("vesicles to release"); mitochondria → everything ("ATP to spend");
  membrane → everything ("controlled trade"). The "farthest cascade"
  pick is graded on its defense — mitochondria (energy for all) and the
  membrane (selectivity for all) are both defensible.
- Part 3: "Most important" is the wrong question because the structures
  are *interdependent* — importance depends on which job you need. True
  restatement: "The Golgi is essential to the secretory pathway; without
  it, ER products are never finished, packaged, or shipped."

**Session B**

- Part 1: the wall/chloroplast/giant-vacuole outline is the plant cell
  (evidence: the three difference-makers); the other is the animal cell
  (evidence: none of the three, small vacuoles only). Required labels as
  in Lesson 02 independent practice 1.
- Part 2: the three-row table (see Lesson 02 key); circled misses are the
  re-study list.
- Part 3: the rebuttal must grant real advantages (support, protection,
  no skeleton needed) and name the trade-off (rigidity rules out shape
  change, crawling, and engulfing food), ending with the general
  principle: structure fits lifestyle, and every structure costs
  something.

**Session C**

- Part 1: 1 → inward, simple diffusion, no ATP. 2 → outward, simple
  diffusion, no ATP. 3 → inward through the channel, facilitated
  diffusion, no ATP. 4 → blocked: little/no entry; the gradient persists
  (and steepens as the cell uses internal glucose). 5 → outward through
  the channel, down the gradient — facilitated diffusion, passive, no
  ATP. 6 → water in, osmosis (distilled water is hypotonic). 7 → water
  out, osmosis (salt solution is hypertonic). 8 → no net change —
  isotonic.
- Part 2: with no channels the green particles bounce around their own
  side — they cannot cross the membrane. Adding a leakage channel lets
  them diffuse down their gradient until concentrations even out. What the
  simulation cannot show: real molecules, real ATP costs, the 3D crowded
  cell interior, or any specific cell type — it is a simplified model.
- Part 3: the error is calling channel-mediated *down-gradient* movement
  "active." Moving through a channel down the gradient is facilitated
  diffusion — passive. "Active" requires *against* the gradient *plus* an
  energy cost.

**Session D**

- Part 1: 1 → simple diffusion. 2 → active transport. 3 → osmosis.
  4 → facilitated diffusion. 5 → endocytosis (active). 6 → exocytosis
  (active). 7 → active transport. 8 → simple diffusion. 9 → facilitated
  diffusion. 10 → simple diffusion. Two defended with the three-question
  machine, e.g. scenario 7: (1) Na⁺ cannot cross the bilayer (charged) —
  needs the pump; (2) gradient points inward (many outside); (3) movement
  is outward = against → active, ATP.
- Part 2: the tree must route: bilayer-crossable + small/uncharged →
  simple diffusion; protein + with gradient + small → facilitated;
  protein + with gradient + water → osmosis; against gradient or large
  cargo → active (pump vs. endo/exocytosis). It must sort scenarios 4
  (facilitated) and 7 (active) correctly.
- Part 3: true part — osmosis *is* a kind of diffusion (water down its
  gradient). Sloppy part — water-channel proteins (aquaporins) exist, but
  they do not change the energy story: the movement is still passive and
  still free. Precise restatement: "Osmosis is the passive diffusion of
  water across a selectively permeable membrane; channel proteins may
  speed it, but it never costs ATP."
