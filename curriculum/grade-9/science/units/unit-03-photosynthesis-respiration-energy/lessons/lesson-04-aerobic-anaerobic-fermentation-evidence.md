# Lesson 04 — Aerobic vs. Anaerobic: Fermentation Evidence

**Student goal:** I can compare aerobic and anaerobic conditions, name
fermentation products, and use a data table to defend a claim about what
yeast needs.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 7 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- The yeast data table below (fictional practice data — labeled as such;
  it stands in for a classroom demonstration the adult may run instead)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "Bread dough rises in a warm kitchen but barely moves in a cold
one, and it does not rise at all without yeast. What is the yeast doing?"
Accept guesses. Today you test those guesses against numbers — and learn
what happens when oxygen runs out.

## Teaching

Lesson 3's respiration used oxygen. That is **aerobic** respiration:
"aero" for air. With oxygen, cells break food down fully, mostly in the
mitochondria, and capture the most usable energy. But cells can also
break food down without oxygen. That is **anaerobic** respiration, and its
most familiar form is **fermentation**: a partial breakdown that happens
in the cytoplasm, captures less usable energy, and leaves byproducts
behind.

The byproducts depend on the organism. Yeast (a single-celled fungus)
ferments sugar into **carbon dioxide and ethanol** (alcohol) — the carbon
dioxide is what makes bread rise and beer foam, and the ethanol is what
makes wine alcoholic. Your muscle cells, when they work so hard that
oxygen delivery cannot keep up, ferment sugar into **lactate** (lactic
acid) — a short-term workaround, not the main energy route. In both
cases the breakdown is incomplete: the byproducts still hold chemical
energy the cell did not capture. That is why anaerobic breakdown releases
less usable energy than aerobic breakdown of the same sugar.

How do we know yeast needs sugar, and that warmth matters? Look at this
practice dataset. It is fictional — invented for this lesson, not
measured in a lab — but it is shaped exactly like real classroom yeast
data, and you will analyze it the same way you will analyze your own
leaf-disk data in the project. Each setup is a bottle with a balloon over
its mouth; the balloon's circumference (in centimeters) shows how much
carbon dioxide the yeast released.

| Setup | 0 min | 10 min | 20 min | 30 min |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: warm water + sugar + yeast | 0 cm | 12 cm | 25 cm | 38 cm |
| B: warm water + yeast, no sugar | 0 cm | 1 cm | 2 cm | 2 cm |
| C: cold water + sugar + yeast | 0 cm | 3 cm | 6 cm | 9 cm |

Read it like a scientist: A rises fast, B stays nearly flat, C rises
slowly. Sugar matters (A vs. B: same warmth, different food). Warmth
matters (A vs. C: same food, different temperature). Yeast without food
cannot ferment, and cold slows the process without stopping it.

**Key vocabulary:** *aerobic* (with oxygen; full breakdown, most usable
energy), *anaerobic* (without oxygen; partial breakdown, less usable
energy), *fermentation* (anaerobic breakdown in the cytoplasm),
*ethanol* (the alcohol made by yeast fermentation),
*lactate* (the product of muscle-cell fermentation).

## Worked example 1 — Rates from the table

**Question:** What is the average rate of balloon growth for setup A and
for setup C over 30 minutes, and how many times faster is A?

**Reasoning:** Rate = change ÷ time. A: 38 cm ÷ 30 min = 1.27 cm/min
(about 1.3 cm/min). C: 9 cm ÷ 30 min = 0.30 cm/min. Ratio: 1.27 ÷ 0.30 ≈
4.2 — setup A is about four times faster than setup C. Warmth does not
just help a little; in this dataset it roughly quadruples the rate.
Always show the division: a rate without its calculation is just an
opinion.

## Worked example 2 — Why setup B stays flat

**Question:** Setup B has warm water and live yeast, yet its balloon
barely grows. Why is warmth not enough?

**Reasoning:** Fermentation breaks down food. Warmth speeds the process,
but it cannot replace the food itself. With no sugar, the yeast has
nothing to break down, so almost no carbon dioxide is made — the 1–2 cm
is background noise (trapped air, tiny residues), not fermentation.
This is the same logic as a control in any experiment: B isolates sugar
as the variable by removing it while keeping everything else the same.

## Guided practice

1. **Predict.** If setup A kept its average rate for another 10 minutes,
   what circumference would you predict at 40 minutes? Then explain why
   a real balloon would probably not keep growing forever (the sugar
   runs out, the balloon stretches and resists, yeast activity changes).
2. **Identify the variable.** In the A-vs.-C comparison, what is the
   independent variable, what is the dependent variable, and what is
   held constant?
3. **Name the process.** For each case, say aerobic or anaerobic and
   name a product: (a) yeast in sealed dough; (b) a marathon runner's
   leg muscles at steady pace; (c) a sprinter's leg muscles in the last
   10 meters of a 100-meter dash.

## Independent practice

1. A new setup D (warm water + sugar + yeast, but with a hole in the
   balloon) shows 0 cm at every time. Does that mean the yeast did not
   ferment? Explain what the measurement actually measures and why a
   broken instrument cannot prove a negative.
2. Bread rises because of carbon dioxide from yeast fermentation, yet
   finished bread is not alcoholic. Explain where the ethanol goes in a
   sentence that a baker would accept (most of it evaporates during
   baking; the small remainder is part of bread's flavor, not enough to
   matter as alcohol).
3. **Explain your reasoning.** A learner says: "My legs burned the day
   after a hard workout because lactic acid built up and stayed there."
   Correct the claim: lactate is cleared quickly after exercise (within
   about an hour), and next-day soreness comes mainly from tiny muscle
   damage and repair, not from leftover lactate.
4. A sealed jar of pond mud bubbles slowly for weeks. Is the bubbling
   more likely aerobic or anaerobic, and what gas is most likely in the
   bubbles? (Anaerobic: oxygen in sealed mud is used up quickly, so
   decomposers ferment; bubbles are mostly carbon dioxide, with methane
   possible in waterlogged mud — Lesson 6 returns to this.)

## Application — pick the right process for the job

For each job, choose aerobic respiration, yeast fermentation, or muscle
fermentation, and defend the choice in one sentence: (a) rising bread
dough; (b) a marathon runner at mile 20; (c) a weightlifter's final
repetition; (d) a yogurt culture thickening milk (bacteria fermenting
milk sugar into lactic acid — the same fermentation family as muscle
lactate, and a good check that fermentation is not only a yeast trick).

## Exit check

Write two short product lists: aerobic respiration of glucose gives…;
yeast fermentation of glucose gives…; muscle fermentation of glucose
gives…. Then add one sentence: why does the anaerobic list release less
usable energy?

## Supports

- Color-code the table rows by hand (A fast, B flat, C slow) and describe
  the pattern in words before calculating any rate.
- Rate calculations accept a calculator; the reasoning, not the arithmetic,
  is the skill being checked.
- The fermentation examples accept spoken answers.

## Extension

Brewers control temperature precisely because warmth speeds fermentation
but too much heat kills yeast. Using this lesson's data logic, design a
fair test of "what temperature is too hot for yeast" — name the variable,
the control, and what you would measure (balloon growth or bubble rate
across a temperature range, with a no-yeast control; the adult judges
safety before anything is run).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: fermentation is "not respiration" (it is anaerobic
respiration, a partial form); lactate causes next-day soreness (it does
not); yeast "eats" sugar the way animals eat (it breaks sugar down
chemically — the same process, different scale). Solutions:
[answer key](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
