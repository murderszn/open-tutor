# Lesson 06 — Energy Flow vs. Matter Cycling: Choosing Models

**Student goal:** I can choose the right model for a question, evaluate
claims about energy and matter, and explain what happens when oxygen
runs out in an ecosystem.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 12 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- The Lesson 05 cycle diagram and your Lesson 05 model, if built

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "Three models sit on the table: a food chain, a food web, and a
carbon-cycle diagram. A learner asks, 'Where does the energy go?' Which
model do you hand them — and which model would mislead them?" Accept
guesses. Today you learn that choosing the model is half of answering the
question.

## Teaching

A model is a tool, and tools fit jobs. A **food chain** (grass → rabbit →
fox) shows one feeding path and makes energy loss easy to see: each
arrow passes only a fraction of the energy onward. A **food web** shows
the real tangle — rabbits eat many plants, foxes eat many animals — and
is better for questions about what happens when one species disappears.
A **cycle diagram** (like Lesson 05's) shows matter returning, and is
the right tool for carbon questions. Hand a learner the wrong model and
they will answer the wrong question confidently: a food chain cannot show
carbon returning to the air, and a cycle diagram hides how little energy
reaches a top predator. Good scientists name the model, say what it
shows, and say what it hides.

Now the anaerobic corner of ecosystems. Lesson 4 showed yeast fermenting
without oxygen. The same thing happens in nature wherever oxygen runs
out: waterlogged soils, lake bottoms, the guts of animals, sealed compost.
There, decomposers still break down dead material, but anaerobically —
partially, slowly, and with different products. Aerobic decomposition
returns mostly carbon dioxide; anaerobic decomposition can also release
methane (marsh gas), the bubbling gas of wetlands. The carbon still
cycles — matter is conserved in both cases — but the products and the
energy captured differ, exactly as in yeast versus muscle. "Aerobic vs.
anaerobic" is not a lab curiosity; it is a fork in the carbon cycle that
depends on one condition: is oxygen available?

Finally, two claims to retire. "Plants make energy" is false: plants
transform light energy into chemical energy; they create nothing.
"Energy is used up" is sloppy but points at something true: usable energy
decreases at each step as heat leaves, even though total energy is
conserved. When you hear an energy claim, ask two questions: "Usable for
what?" and "Where did the heat go?" Those two questions dismantle most
energy myths.

**Key vocabulary:** *food chain* (one feeding path),
*food web* (overlapping feeding paths), *model* (a simplified
representation chosen for a question — every model hides something),
*aerobic decomposition* (breakdown with oxygen, returning carbon
dioxide), *anaerobic decomposition* (breakdown without oxygen, partial,
sometimes releasing methane).

## Worked example 1 — Evaluating an energy claim

**Question:** A learner claims: "Energy is recycled like matter, because
animals give off heat and plants absorb heat." Evaluate the claim with
evidence.

**Reasoning:** The claim fails on the second half. Animals do give off
heat, but plants cannot run photosynthesis on heat — they need light of
the right wavelengths, captured by chlorophyll. Heat radiates away and
warms the surroundings; it does not re-enter the food chain as usable
energy. Verdict: false. Matter cycles because atoms are reused;
energy flows one way because each transfer loses usable energy as heat.
The claim confuses "energy exists" with "energy is usable" — and in
ecology, only usable energy counts.

## Worked example 2 — A real energy budget with real variation

**Question:** A meadow study (fictional practice data) finds producers
capture 5,000 energy units; herbivores gain 620 units; carnivores gain
38 units. What percentage reaches each level, and is the one-tenth rule
supported or refuted?

**Reasoning:** Herbivores: 620 ÷ 5,000 = 0.124 = 12.4%. Carnivores:
38 ÷ 620 = 0.061 = 6.1% (note: the percentage is of the herbivore level,
not of the producers — 38 ÷ 5,000 = 0.76% of producer energy).
Verdict: roughly supported, not exact. 12.4% and 6.1% bracket 10% — the
rule of thumb describes the pattern (a large loss at each step), not a
precise constant. A scientist reports the measured percentages and calls
10% an approximation, not a law. That honesty is the difference between
using a model and worshipping it.

## Guided practice

1. **Pick the model.** For each question, choose food chain, food web,
   or cycle diagram, and say what the model shows and hides: (a) "How
   much energy reaches a wolf?" (b) "What happens to rabbits if foxes
   disappear?" (c) "Where does the carbon in a fallen log go?"
2. **Aerobic or anaerobic?** For each setting, predict the decomposition
   products: (a) an open compost heap, turned weekly; (b) the mud at the
   bottom of a still pond; (c) a sealed jar of wet leaves.
3. **Fix the claim.** Rewrite each false claim so it is true: (a)
   "Plants make energy from sunlight." (b) "Top predators get the most
   energy because they eat the most."

## Independent practice

1. A lake's deep water loses its oxygen in summer. Predict two changes:
   one for decomposition products, one for fish that need oxygen.
   (Decomposition shifts anaerobic, possibly releasing methane; fish
   must move to oxygenated water or die — oxygen is a condition, not a
   guarantee.)
2. Draw or list a food web with at least four species and two
   cross-links (one species eating two foods, or one food eaten by two
   species). Explain in one sentence why a web answers "what if this
   species disappears?" better than a chain.
3. **Explain your reasoning.** A learner says: "If energy is conserved,
   then a food chain should pass 100% of its energy upward, and the
   one-tenth rule must be wrong." Resolve the apparent contradiction:
   conservation counts heat and waste as outputs, so total energy is
   conserved while usable energy for the next level shrinks.
4. A wetland is drained for farming. Using aerobic vs. anaerobic
   reasoning, predict whether methane release rises or falls, and why
   (it falls: drained soil gains oxygen, so decomposition shifts from
   anaerobic methane-producing to aerobic carbon-dioxide-producing —
   while noting the drained wetland loses its other ecosystem roles).

## Application — revise your cycle model

Take your Lesson 05 carbon-cycle model (or the unit SVG) and add an
anaerobic branch: a waterlogged-soil box where decomposition without
oxygen releases methane as well as carbon dioxide, with an arrow showing
methane eventually breaking down in the air to carbon dioxide. Write one
paragraph explaining what the new branch changes about the model and one
sentence about what it still hides (for example, it still does not show
energy quantities, timescales, or ocean chemistry).

## Exit check

In your own words, explain the difference between matter cycling and
energy flow, using one example of each — and name the model you would
use to show each one.

## Supports

- Model-choice questions accept a table (question | model | shows |
  hides).
- Percentages accept a calculator; show each division.
- The model revision accepts a labeled list instead of a redrawn diagram.

## Extension

Engineers propose capturing methane from landfills and burning it for
energy. Using this unit's logic, evaluate the proposal in three
sentences: burning converts methane to carbon dioxide and water and
releases usable energy (good: less methane, which is a stronger
heat-trapping gas per molecule than carbon dioxide); but it still releases
carbon dioxide, so it is a harm-reduction step, not a carbon-free one;
and the carbon still cycles — nothing is destroyed.

average); energy conservation means 100% transfer upward (heat and waste
are outputs too); anaerobic means "no life" (anaerobic decomposers and
fermenters thrive without oxygen). Solutions:
