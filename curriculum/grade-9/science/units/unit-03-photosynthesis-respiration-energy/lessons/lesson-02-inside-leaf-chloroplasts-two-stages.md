# Lesson 02 — Inside the Leaf: Chloroplasts and Two Stages

**Student goal:** I can explain where photosynthesis happens in a leaf,
what chlorophyll does, and how its two stages divide the work.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 3 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Index cards: mesophyll, stomata, guard cell, chloroplast, stroma,
  thylakoid, granum, chlorophyll

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "If a leaf is a factory, where are the machines, where do the raw
materials come in, and where does the finished sugar go?" Accept guesses.
Today you name the rooms of the factory and the two shifts that run it.

## Teaching

Photosynthesis does not happen everywhere in a plant. In land plants it
happens mostly in leaves, in a middle layer of cells called the
**mesophyll**. Gases enter and leave through tiny adjustable openings in
the leaf surface called **stomata** (singular: stoma), each flanked by two
**guard cells** that swell or shrink to open or close the pore. Carbon
dioxide comes in through the stomata; oxygen goes out the same way. Water
arrives from the roots. So the factory has doors — and doors can close.

Inside a mesophyll cell, the machines are **chloroplasts**: green
organelles with an outer and an inner membrane. Inside the chloroplast is
a fluid called the **stroma**, and floating in the stroma are stacks of
flat discs called **thylakoids** — one stack is a **granum**. Embedded in
the thylakoid membrane is **chlorophyll**, the green pigment that first
catches light. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light best and reflects
green light — that reflected green is why leaves look green to us. A leaf
is green because of the light it throws away, not the light it uses.

Photosynthesis runs in two sequential stages:

1. **Light-dependent reactions** — in the thylakoid membrane. Chlorophyll
   absorbs light; the energy splits water molecules, releasing oxygen,
   and loads two energy carriers, ATP and NADPH, with captured energy.
   Without light, this stage stops.
2. **The Calvin cycle** (light-independent reactions) — in the stroma.
   It does not use light directly. It spends the ATP and NADPH from stage
   1 to assemble sugar (first a three-carbon compound called G3P) from
   carbon dioxide. Because it depends on stage 1's products, it still
   stops soon after the light goes out.

The division matters: stage 1 captures energy; stage 2 spends it building.
If either stage fails, no sugar is made — which is why a plant in the
dark stops making food even though its chloroplasts are still there.

**Key vocabulary:** *mesophyll* (the middle leaf layer where most
photosynthesis happens), *stomata* (adjustable leaf openings for gas
exchange), *guard cell* (a cell that opens/closes a stoma),
*chloroplast* (the organelle where photosynthesis happens),
*stroma* (the fluid inside a chloroplast, site of the Calvin cycle),
*thylakoid* (a disc in the chloroplast; its membrane holds chlorophyll),
*granum* (a stack of thylakoids), *chlorophyll* (the green pigment that
absorbs light), *light-dependent reactions* (stage 1, in the thylakoid
membrane: water split, oxygen released, ATP and NADPH made),
*Calvin cycle* (stage 2, in the stroma: carbon dioxide assembled into
sugar using ATP and NADPH).

## Worked example 1 — Stomata close on a hot, dry day

**Question:** On a hot, dry day a plant closes its stomata to conserve
water. What happens to photosynthesis, and why?

**Reasoning:** Closed stomata save water, but they also shut the door for
carbon dioxide. With less CO₂ entering, the Calvin cycle has less raw
material to build sugar from, so sugar production slows — even in bright
light. The plant trades food production for water conservation. The
correct chain is: stomata close → less CO₂ in → Calvin cycle slows →
less sugar. Light alone cannot fix a missing ingredient.

## Worked example 2 — Where does the oxygen come from?

**Question:** Photosynthesis releases oxygen. Does that oxygen come from
the carbon dioxide or from the water? Explain using the two stages.

**Reasoning:** It comes from the water. In the light-dependent reactions,
water molecules are split, and the oxygen from water is released. The
oxygen atoms in carbon dioxide end up in the sugar and in the water made
later, not in the released O₂. A useful check: if a plant is given water
made with a traceable form of oxygen, the released oxygen carries that
same trace — the fingerprint points to water, not to CO₂.

## Guided practice

1. **Match the part to the job.** For each structure, write its job in
   one sentence: mesophyll, stomata, guard cell, chloroplast, thylakoid,
   stroma, chlorophyll.
2. **Sort the stages.** Put each item in stage 1 or stage 2: "water is
   split," "ATP and NADPH are made," "carbon dioxide is assembled into
   sugar," "happens in the thylakoid membrane," "happens in the stroma,"
   "needs light directly," "uses ATP and NADPH."
3. **Predict.** A plant is moved from white light to green-only light of
   the same brightness. Predict whether photosynthesis speeds up, slows
   down, or stays the same, and give the chlorophyll reason.

## Independent practice

1. Draw and label a chloroplast from the description in the teaching
   section (outer/inner membrane, stroma, thylakoid stack, granum label),
   or write the same information as a labeled list if drawing is hard.
   Every label must appear as text, not color alone.
2. A gardener sprays a plant's leaves with a waxy coating that seals the
   stomata shut. Explain two consequences: one for water, one for
   photosynthesis.
3. Why do leaves look green in a sentence that uses the words "absorb"
   and "reflect" correctly?
4. **Explain your reasoning.** A learner says: "The Calvin cycle is
   called light-independent, so it runs just as well at midnight as at
   noon." Is that right? Explain what actually happens at midnight and
   why.

## Application — two factories, one broken door

Two identical plants sit in the same bright room. Plant A has open
stomata; plant B's stomata were sealed shut (by the gardener's wax in
question 2). After a week, which plant has made more sugar, and which
measurement would prove it — leaf size, plant mass, or oxygen bubbles?
Defend your choice and explain why the other two measurements are weaker
evidence here. (Mass is the strongest: it records stored sugar and
growth; bubbles measure only one moment's oxygen release, and leaf size
can change with water alone.)

## Exit check

Name the two stages of photosynthesis, where each happens, and what each
one produces that the other stage needs or uses.

## Supports

- Build the chloroplast from craft materials (a flattened ball for the
  chloroplast, a stack of coins for a granum) before labeling a diagram.
- The stage-sorting task accepts a two-column table instead of sentences.
- "Stoma" vs. "stroma": stoma is the mouth-like opening; stroma is the
  bed-like fluid. Say both aloud and spell them side by side.

## Extension

Some plants (such as desert succulents) open their stomata at night and
store carbon dioxide for daytime use. Using this lesson's logic, explain
what problem that solves and what new problem it creates (night opening
saves water but means the plant must store CO₂ overnight and spend energy
managing it — a tradeoff, not a free lunch).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: leaves are green because they absorb green light
(the opposite); the Calvin cycle needs no light at all (it needs stage
1's products); oxygen comes from CO₂ (it comes from split water).
