# Lesson 03 — Cellular Respiration: Bonds and Net Energy

**Student goal:** I can explain cellular respiration as bonds breaking
and forming with a net release of energy, balance its equation, and
contrast it with photosynthesis.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Index cards: glucose, oxygen, carbon dioxide, water, ATP, bond,
  net energy

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "You eat lunch, then run. Where does the energy for running come
from — the food itself, your muscles, or somewhere else?" Accept guesses.
Today you trace the energy from a sugar molecule's bonds to a muscle
cell's work, and you learn why the process never runs in reverse for free.

## Teaching

Every living cell — plant, animal, fungus, bacterium — needs usable
energy, and it gets it the same way: by rearranging molecules. That
process is **cellular respiration**. In words:

**sugar (glucose) + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + usable energy (ATP)
+ heat**

And in symbols — look familiar?

**C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy**

It is the reverse of the photosynthesis equation, but it is not
photosynthesis running backwards. It is a different process, in different
organelles (mostly the mitochondria, starting in the cytoplasm), running
all the time — day and night, in leaves and roots, in your muscles while
you sleep. Plants respire too: a plant that only photosynthesized and
never respired could never spend the energy it stored.

Why does rearranging molecules release energy at all? Think in bonds.
Breaking a chemical bond costs energy (you must pull atoms apart).
Forming a new bond releases energy (atoms settle into a more stable
arrangement). In respiration, the bonds of glucose and oxygen break, and
new bonds form in carbon dioxide and water. The energy released by
forming the new bonds is greater than the energy spent breaking the old
ones. The difference is the **net energy transfer** — energy that ends up
as ATP, the cell's spendable energy currency, plus heat that warms the
body. That net release is the whole point: if forming released less than
breaking cost, the cell would lose energy by respiring, and the process
would be useless.

Two clarifications that prevent years of confusion. First, breathing is
not respiration. Breathing moves gases in and out of the body; cellular
respiration is the chemical process inside cells that uses the oxygen
breathing delivers. Fish do not "breathe water" and plants do not
"breathe" carbon dioxide — all of them respire in their cells. Second,
respiration does not destroy matter. Count the atoms: left, carbon 6,
hydrogen 12, oxygen 18 (6 from glucose + 12 from 6O₂); right, carbon 6,
hydrogen 12, oxygen 18 (12 from 6CO₂ + 6 from 6H₂O). Balanced, as always.

**Key vocabulary:** *cellular respiration* (the chemical process that
breaks bonds in food and oxygen and forms new compounds, releasing usable
energy), *ATP* (the molecule cells spend directly for work),
*net energy transfer* (energy released by bond formation minus energy
spent on bond breaking — positive here, so energy is available),
*mitochondrion* (the organelle where most aerobic respiration happens).

## Worked example 1 — Bond bookkeeping with teaching numbers

**Question:** A teaching model (fictional numbers, not measured values)
says breaking the bonds in one glucose and its oxygen costs 100 energy
units, while forming the bonds in carbon dioxide and water releases 130
energy units. What is the net transfer, and what does its sign mean?

**Reasoning:** Net = released − spent = 130 − 100 = +30 energy units.
Positive means energy is left over after the rearrangement — that is the
energy the cell captures as ATP plus the heat released. If the numbers
were reversed (spent 130, released 100), the net would be −30, and the
cell would have to pay energy to respire — the opposite of what it
needs. The sign is the story: positive net release is why respiration
powers life.

## Worked example 2 — Why you breathe faster when you run

**Question:** A runner's breathing rate triples during a sprint. Explain
the change using the respiration equation.

**Reasoning:** Sprinting muscles do more work, so they spend ATP faster.
To rebuild ATP, cells respire faster, which consumes oxygen faster and
produces carbon dioxide faster. Breathing faster delivers the extra oxygen
and removes the extra carbon dioxide. The chain is: more work → more ATP
spent → faster respiration → more oxygen needed, more carbon dioxide
made → faster breathing. Breathing is the delivery service; respiration
is the factory.

## Guided practice

1. **Balance check.** Count carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen on both sides
   of C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O and state the verdict.
2. **Reverse or different?** List three ways respiration differs from
   photosynthesis besides the direction of the equation (where it
   happens, when it happens, what it is for).
3. **Night shift.** A plant sits in a dark room all night. Does it
   photosynthesize? Does it respire? Explain each answer, and say
   whether the plant is a net producer or net releaser of carbon dioxide
   overnight.

## Independent practice

1. A learner writes the respiration equation as C₆H₁₂O₆ + O₂ → CO₂ +
   H₂O + energy. Balance it correctly and explain the learner's error in
   one sentence (they forgot the coefficients — the molecule counts
   must match, not just the molecule kinds).
2. Explain in two sentences why a person feels warm after exercise, using
   the words "bonds," "heat," and "net."
3. **Explain your reasoning.** A learner says: "Respiration is just
   breathing, and plants don't breathe, so plants don't respire." Correct
   both halves of the claim.
4. A sealed jar holds a mouse and a plant in bright light for a day, then
   in darkness overnight (a thought experiment — never do this with a
   real animal). During the day, what gases does each organism take in
   and release? Overnight, what changes? Answer with two short gas lists.

## Application — which process, which organelle, which time?

For each situation, name the process (photosynthesis, respiration, or
both), the organelle most involved, and whether it happens in light,
dark, or both: (a) a leaf cell at noon; (b) a root cell at midnight;
(c) a muscle cell during a sprint; (d) an algae cell in a dark bottle.
Defend each answer with one sentence.

## Exit check

Write the balanced respiration equation and one sentence explaining what
"net energy transfer" means in your own words.

## Supports

- Use the same three-column atom table (C, H, O) as Lesson 1; the numbers
  are identical, which is itself a discovery.
- The night-shift question accepts a labeled day/night table instead of
  sentences.
- "Respiration" vs. "breathing": write both words on cards and sort
  examples under the right card.

## Extension

If bond formation always releases energy and bond breaking always costs
it, why does a log not burst into flame on its own? (Breaking the first
bonds needs an initial energy input — activation energy — which a spark
supplies; once started, the released energy keeps breaking more bonds.
Respiration avoids this problem by breaking bonds in controlled steps
with enzymes, capturing energy as ATP instead of one burst of flame.)

## Teacher note

respiration happens only at night in plants; energy is "made" by
