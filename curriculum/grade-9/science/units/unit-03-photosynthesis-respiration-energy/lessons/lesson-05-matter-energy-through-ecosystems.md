# Lesson 05 — Matter and Energy Through Ecosystems

**Student goal:** I can trace a carbon atom through photosynthesis,
respiration, and the carbon cycle, and explain why matter cycles while
energy flows one way.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 10 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Printed copy of [`../assets/energy-matter-cycle.svg`](../assets/energy-matter-cycle.svg)
  (the deterministic cycle diagram) or its text alternative below

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "You eat a potato. Follow one carbon atom from that potato into
your body. Where can it go next?" Accept guesses. Today you follow it all
the way around — and learn why the atom's journey never ends but the
energy's journey does.

Now the carbon story, step by step. A carbon atom floats in the air as
part of a carbon dioxide molecule. A leaf takes it in through a stoma
and, in the Calvin cycle, builds it into sugar. That sugar may become
starch in a potato, wood in a tree, or food for a caterpillar. If you eat
the potato, the carbon atom becomes part of your body — maybe a muscle
protein, maybe fat — until your cells respire it back to carbon dioxide
and you breathe it out. The same atom can then enter another leaf. That
loop is the **carbon cycle**: carbon moving between four great
**reservoirs** — the **biosphere** (living things), the **atmosphere**
(air), the **hydrosphere** (oceans and waters, which dissolve carbon
dioxide), and the **geosphere** (rocks, soils, and fossil fuels, where
carbon can sit for millions of years). Burning fossil fuels moves carbon
from the geosphere to the atmosphere fast; photosynthesis moves it back
slowly. Deforestation does double damage: fewer leaves to take carbon
in, plus the stored carbon in cut wood released when it burns or rots.

Energy tells a different story. Sunlight enters, becomes sugar, passes
from plant to herbivore to carnivore — and at every step, most of it
leaves as heat from respiration or is lost in uneaten parts and wastes.
A rough rule of thumb (not a law — real values vary widely) is that only
about a tenth of the energy at one feeding level reaches the next. Start
with 10,000 energy units captured by producers: herbivores gain about
1,000, the carnivores that eat them about 100, and top predators about
10. The missing energy did not vanish — it left as heat and waste. That
is why food chains are short: there is not enough usable energy left for
many levels, and why no ecosystem can run on recycled energy. Matter
cycles; energy flows through and out.

**Key vocabulary:** *carbon cycle* (carbon moving among biosphere,
atmosphere, hydrosphere, and geosphere), *reservoir* (a place where
carbon is stored, such as the atmosphere or fossil fuels),
*producer* (an organism that makes its own food, such as a plant),
*consumer* (an organism that eats other organisms),
*decomposer* (an organism that breaks down dead material),
*food web* (overlapping food chains in an ecosystem),
*energy flow* (one-way movement of energy through feeding levels,
ending as heat), *matter cycling* (atoms reused through ecosystems).

## Worked example 1 — Trace one carbon atom

**Question:** Trace one carbon atom from the air to a potato, to a person,
and back to the air. Name the process at each step.

**Reasoning:** (1) The atom enters a potato plant's leaf as CO₂ through
a stoma; photosynthesis (Calvin cycle) builds it into sugar, then starch.
(2) A person eats the potato; digestion and transport move the carbon
into the person's cells. (3) The person's cells respire the sugar;
cellular respiration releases the atom as CO₂, which the person exhales.
(4) The atom is back in the atmosphere, ready for another leaf. Four
steps, two named processes, one atom reused. If any step claimed the
atom was created or destroyed, the trace would be wrong — matter is
conserved.

## Worked example 2 — The shrinking energy budget

**Question:** Producers capture 10,000 energy units (fictional practice
data). Using the rough one-tenth rule, how much reaches herbivores,
carnivores, and top predators? Where did the rest go?

**Reasoning:** Herbivores: 10,000 × 0.1 = 1,000 units. Carnivores:
1,000 × 0.1 = 100 units. Top predators: 100 × 0.1 = 10 units. The rest —
9,000 + 900 + 90 = 9,990 units across the steps — left as heat from
respiration, in uneaten parts, and in wastes. The budget balances if you
count heat and waste as outputs: energy is conserved, but usable energy
shrinks at every step. That is why a "tenth" rule is about usable energy
for the next level, not about energy disappearing.

## Guided practice

1. **Reservoir sort.** Put each item in its reservoir: ocean water,
   a tree trunk, coal, the air, a fish, limestone rock. Then name one
   process that moves carbon out of each reservoir.
2. **Where does a tree's mass come from?** A seedling grows into a tree
   weighing hundreds of kilograms, while the soil in its pot loses almost
   no mass. Explain where the mass came from, using the photosynthesis
   equation. (Hint: in the 1600s, Jan Baptist van Helmont grew a willow
   for five years and found the tree gained a great deal while the soil
   lost almost nothing — the mass came mostly from air and water, not
   from soil.)
3. **Energy or matter?** For each statement, say whether it describes
   energy flow or matter cycling: (a) "A nitrogen atom in a dead leaf
   becomes part of a fungus, then a mouse." (b) "A hawk gains less
   usable energy than the rabbits it eats gained from grass."

## Independent practice

1. A forest is cut and burned. Name two effects on atmospheric carbon
   dioxide — one from the burning itself, one from the missing trees —
   and say which reservoir each effect moves carbon from or to.
2. Draw or list a four-reservoir carbon-cycle model with at least five
   labeled arrows (photosynthesis, respiration, feeding, decomposition,
   combustion). Every arrow must name the process and the direction.
3. **Explain your reasoning.** A learner says: "Energy is recycled just
   like matter — the heat animals give off goes back into plants."
   Correct the claim: plants cannot use heat to run photosynthesis (they
   need light), so heat leaving as heat is energy lost to the ecosystem,
   not energy waiting to be reused.
4. Ocean water absorbs carbon dioxide from the air. Is that good news,
   bad news, or both? Defend a two-sided answer (it slows atmospheric
   buildup, but it changes ocean chemistry — more dissolved CO₂ makes
   seawater more acidic, which stresses shell-building organisms).

## Application — the missing carbon audit

A school garden claims to be "carbon neutral" because it composts all its
waste. Audit the claim: list three carbon flows the claim ignores
(examples: carbon dioxide released by the gardeners' own respiration and
by transport of supplies; carbon in food brought in from outside; carbon
released by decomposition of compost itself, which returns CO₂ even as
it recycles nutrients). Decide whether "carbon neutral" is proven,
plausible, or unproven on the evidence given, and say what measurement
would settle it (a full carbon budget: carbon in vs. carbon out, measured
over a year).

## Exit check

Write two sentences: one explaining why matter can cycle forever, one
explaining why energy cannot — each sentence must use the word "heat"
or "atom" correctly.

## Supports

- Trace the atom with a token (a bead or coin) moved across a drawn cycle
  before writing the trace in words.
- The reservoir sort accepts a four-column table.
- The energy budget accepts a calculator; show each multiplication.

## Extension

Fossil fuels are photosynthesis from hundreds of millions of years ago,
stored in the geosphere. Using the cycle, explain why burning them in a
few centuries breaks the cycle's balance even though the same reactions
are involved (rate matters: the return path — photosynthesis and burial —
works on geological timescales, while combustion releases the stored
carbon almost instantly).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: plants gain mass from soil (Van Helmont refutes
it); energy is recycled like matter (heat loss ends the chain); burning
"destroys" carbon (it moves carbon from geosphere to atmosphere).
