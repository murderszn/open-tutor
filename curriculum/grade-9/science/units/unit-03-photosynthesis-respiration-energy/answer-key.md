# Answer Key — Unit 03: Photosynthesis, Respiration, and Energy Transformations

**Adult only.** Keep this file separate from student materials. Every
lesson item, practice task, quiz question, assessment task, and project
analysis step is solved here; open responses give sample reasoning and
acceptable variation. All datasets used in lessons and practice are
labeled fictional practice data in the student materials and are treated
as such here — never present them as lab measurements.

## Lesson 01 — Photosynthesis as energy transformation

**Guided practice.**
1. Right side oxygen: glucose supplies 6, 5O₂ supplies 10, total 16;
   left side supplies 18 (12 from 6CO₂ + 6 from 6H₂O). The equation is
   short 2 oxygens — the coefficient must be 6O₂, not 5O₂.
2. Word: carbon dioxide + water + light energy → glucose + oxygen.
   Symbols: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂.
3. Inputs: light energy (from the sun/lamp), water (from roots/soil),
   carbon dioxide (from the air). Outputs: glucose (stored in the
   plant), oxygen (released to the air).

**Independent practice.**
1. Not balanced. Left: C 3, H 6, O 9 (6 + 3). Right: C 6, H 12, O 12
   (6 + 6). Every count fails — the learner halved the inputs without
   halving the glucose.
2. 18CO₂ + 18H₂O → 3C₆H₁₂O₆ + 18O₂. Check: C 18 = 18, H 36 = 36,
   O 54 (36 + 18) = 54 (18 + 36).
3. Oxygen is released when water is split in the light-dependent
   reactions; the plant's purpose is storing energy as sugar. A
   byproduct is what a process happens to release, not what it is for.
4. Right: both processes exchange the same two gases in opposite
   directions. Wrong: photosynthesis is not breathing — breathing moves
   gases, while photosynthesis is a chemical process that builds sugar
   from CO₂ and water. Plants also respire (Lesson 3).

**Application (sunlit plant).** (a) Visible inputs: light (sunlight
streaming onto leaves) and water (the glass; water in the pot).
(b) Invisible input: carbon dioxide, an invisible gas in the air
around the plant — known because the equation requires it and because
enclosed-plant experiments show CO₂ falling as plants photosynthesize.
(c) Outputs: sugar (stored inside the plant, not directly visible) and
oxygen (visible only as bubbles in water or by measurement, not to the
naked eye in air).

**Exit check.** 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. Light energy
goes in; chemical energy is stored in sugar's bonds.

## Lesson 02 — Inside the leaf

**Guided practice.**
1. Mesophyll: middle leaf layer where most photosynthesis happens.
   Stomata: adjustable openings for gas exchange. Guard cell: opens or
   closes a stoma by swelling/shrinking. Chloroplast: organelle where
   photosynthesis happens. Thylakoid: disc whose membrane holds
   chlorophyll. Stroma: fluid inside the chloroplast, site of the Calvin
   cycle. Chlorophyll: green pigment that absorbs light.
2. Water split: stage 1. ATP/NADPH made: stage 1. CO₂ assembled into
   sugar: stage 2. Thylakoid membrane: stage 1. Stroma: stage 2. Needs
   light directly: stage 1. Uses ATP/NADPH: stage 2.
3. Slows down. Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue best and reflects
   green, so green-only light is mostly reflected, not captured.

**Independent practice.**
1. Labels required: outer membrane, inner membrane, stroma, thylakoid
   (disc), granum (stack). Accept a labeled list with the same five
   parts.
2. Water: less water lost (sealed stomata stop evaporation). Food:
   photosynthesis stops or slows sharply — no CO₂ can enter for the
   Calvin cycle.
3. Sample: "Leaves absorb red and blue light and reflect green light,
   so the green light reaching our eyes is the light they did not use."
4. Wrong. The Calvin cycle does not use light directly, but it spends
   ATP and NADPH made by stage 1. At midnight stage 1 stops, the
   carriers run out, and the Calvin cycle stops soon after.

**Application.** Plant A (open stomata) makes more sugar. Mass is the
strongest measurement: it records stored sugar and growth over the
week. Oxygen bubbles measure only one moment's release, and leaf size
can change with water uptake alone.

**Exit check.** Stage 1, light-dependent reactions, thylakoid membrane:
splits water, releases oxygen, makes ATP and NADPH. Stage 2, Calvin
cycle, stroma: uses ATP and NADPH to assemble sugar from CO₂.

## Lesson 03 — Cellular respiration

**Guided practice.**
1. Balanced. Left: C 6, H 12, O 18 (6 + 12). Right: C 6, H 12, O 18
   (12 + 6).
2. Any three: location (mitochondria/cytoplasm vs. chloroplast); timing
   (all the time vs. light-dependent); purpose (release stored energy
   vs. store energy); inputs/outputs reversed (accept if stated).
3. Photosynthesis: no (no light). Respiration: yes (runs day and night).
   Overnight the plant is a net releaser of CO₂: respiration releases
   CO₂ with no photosynthesis to take it back up.

**Independent practice.**
1. Balanced: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O. Error: the learner omitted
   coefficients — matching molecule kinds is not balancing; molecule
   counts must match.
2. Sample: "Breaking bonds costs energy and forming new bonds releases
   it; in respiration forming releases more than breaking costs, so the
   net release appears as usable energy and as heat that warms the body."
3. Both halves wrong. Breathing is gas movement in and out of the body;
   cellular respiration is the chemical process inside cells that breaks
   bonds and releases energy. Plants respire in their mitochondria (and
   cytoplasm), day and night.
4. Day: mouse takes in O₂, releases CO₂; plant (net) takes in CO₂,
   releases O₂ — photosynthesis exceeds respiration in bright light.
   Overnight: both take in O₂ and release CO₂ (plant respiration only).

**Application.** (a) Leaf at noon: both processes; chloroplast (photo-
synthesis) and mitochondrion (respiration); photosynthesis in light,
respiration both times. (b) Root at midnight: respiration only;
mitochondrion; both times (it is dark now). (c) Muscle sprint:
respiration (aerobic plus anaerobic); mitochondrion and cytoplasm;
both times. (d) Algae in dark bottle: respiration only; mitochondrion;
dark now, respiration both times.

**Exit check.** C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Net energy
transfer = energy released by forming new bonds minus energy spent
breaking old bonds; here it is positive, so energy is available.

## Lesson 04 — Aerobic vs. anaerobic

**Guided practice.**
1. Predicted: 38 + (38 ÷ 30 × 10) = 38 + 12.7 ≈ 51 cm. Real balloons fall
   short because sugar runs out, the stretched balloon resists further
   expansion, and yeast activity changes over time.
2. Independent variable: temperature (warm vs. cold). Dependent
   variable: balloon circumference (carbon dioxide released). Held
   constant: sugar amount, yeast amount, water volume, time points.
3. (a) Anaerobic (yeast fermentation): CO₂ + ethanol. (b) Aerobic:
   CO₂ + water + energy. (c) Anaerobic (muscle fermentation): lactate.

**Independent practice.**
1. No. The balloon measures trapped gas; a hole lets gas escape, so a
   flat balloon measures a broken instrument, not absent fermentation.
   Accept: the setup must be repeated with an intact balloon.
2. Most ethanol evaporates during baking; the small remainder contributes
   flavor, not meaningful alcohol content.
3. Correction: lactate is cleared from muscle and blood within about an
   hour after exercise. Next-day soreness comes mainly from tiny muscle
   damage and the repair process, not from leftover lactate.
4. Anaerobic. Oxygen in sealed mud is used up quickly, so decomposers
   ferment; bubbles are mostly carbon dioxide, with methane possible in
   waterlogged mud.

**Application.** (a) Yeast fermentation — CO₂ raises dough.
(b) Aerobic respiration — steady pace is oxygen-supplied.
(c) Muscle fermentation (with aerobic recovery) — the final repetition
outruns oxygen delivery. (d) Bacterial fermentation — milk sugar
fermented to lactic acid, the same fermentation family as muscle
lactate.

**Exit check.** Aerobic: CO₂ + water + usable energy. Yeast
fermentation: CO₂ + ethanol. Muscle fermentation: lactate. Anaerobic
breakdown is partial, so its byproducts still hold chemical energy the
cell did not capture — less usable energy per sugar.

## Lesson 05 — Matter and energy

**Guided practice.**
1. Ocean water: hydrosphere (out by photosynthesis uptake in surface
   waters / dissolving; accept any correct process). Tree trunk:
   biosphere (out by respiration, feeding, or decomposition). Coal:
   geosphere (out by combustion). Air: atmosphere (out by
   photosynthesis). Fish: biosphere (out by respiration or feeding).
   Limestone rock: geosphere (out by weathering/dissolving).
2. The mass came mostly from the air (CO₂) and water. Van Helmont's
   willow gained a great deal over five years while its soil lost
   almost nothing — soil cannot supply mass it did not lose.
3. (a) Matter cycling (an atom reused). (b) Energy flow (usable energy
   shrinking across levels).

**Independent practice.**
1. Burning: stored carbon (biosphere/geosphere wood) → atmosphere as
   CO₂. Missing trees: less CO₂ taken from the atmosphere into the
   biosphere — the uptake arrow weakens.
2. Model must show four reservoirs and at least five labeled arrows
   (photosynthesis, respiration, feeding, decomposition, combustion)
   with correct directions. Accept a labeled list.
3. Heat cannot power photosynthesis: chlorophyll captures light, not
   heat. Heat leaving an animal radiates away and does not re-enter the
   food chain as usable energy.
4. Both. Dissolving CO₂ slows atmospheric buildup, but it makes seawater
   more acidic, stressing shell-building organisms — a tradeoff, not a
   pure benefit.

**Application (garden audit).** Unproven. Ignored flows include:
gardeners' respiration, transport of supplies, carbon in imported food
and materials, and CO₂ released by decomposition of the compost itself.
Settling measurement: a full-year carbon budget (total carbon in vs.
total carbon out, measured, not asserted).

**Exit check.** Sample: "Matter cycles because the same atoms are reused
in new molecules forever." / "Energy cannot cycle because at each step
usable energy leaves as heat, which plants cannot use to photosynthesize."

## Lesson 06 — Models and anaerobic ecosystems

**Guided practice.**
1. (a) Food chain — shows stepwise energy loss; hides web complexity.
   (b) Food web — shows alternative prey and knock-on effects; a chain
   hides them. (c) Cycle diagram — shows carbon returning; a chain
   hides returns.
2. (a) Open compost: aerobic — mostly CO₂. (b) Pond-bottom mud:
   anaerobic — CO₂ plus methane possible. (c) Sealed wet leaves:
   anaerobic — CO₂ plus methane possible.
3. (a) "Plants transform light energy into chemical energy stored in
   sugar." (b) "Top predators receive the least usable energy, because
   only roughly a tenth of each level's energy reaches the next."

**Independent practice.**
1. Decomposition shifts anaerobic (methane possible); oxygen-dependent
   fish must move to oxygenated water or die.
2. Web must have at least four species and two cross-links; accept any
   correct web. Reason: a web shows alternative foods, so it predicts
   what happens when one species disappears better than a single chain.
3. Resolution: conservation counts heat and waste as outputs, so total
   energy is conserved while usable energy for the next level shrinks.
   The one-tenth rule is about usable energy, not total energy.
4. Methane release falls: drained soil gains oxygen, so decomposition
   shifts from anaerobic (methane-producing) to aerobic
   (CO₂-producing). Note the tradeoff: the drained wetland loses its
   other ecosystem roles.

**Application.** Accept any correct anaerobic branch (waterlogged-soil
box, decomposition without oxygen, methane arrow, methane → CO₂ in air)
plus a paragraph on what changes (methane as an additional carbon path)
and one sentence on what the model still hides (quantities, timescales,
ocean chemistry, or energy amounts — any one).

**Exit check.** Sample: "Matter: a carbon atom cycles from air to leaf
to animal to air again — best shown by a cycle diagram. Energy: usable
energy flows from sun to plant to animal and leaves as heat — best shown
by a food chain with shrinking amounts."

## Project — analysis guidance

- **Interpolation method (use for any half-float estimate):** find the
  two counts bracketing 5 floating disks; time = earlier time +
  (5 − earlier count) ÷ (later count − earlier count) × time interval.
  Session C example: 5 + (5 − 3) ÷ (7 − 3) × 5 = 7.5 min.
- **Cup comparisons:** dark vs. test isolates light; plain-water vs.
  test isolates the carbon dioxide supply. A difference in only one
  comparison still supports only that variable's claim.
- **Honest limitations:** indirect measure (oxygen accumulation, not
  sugar); leaf age, disk size, lamp heat; whole-disk counting; single
  run without repeats. A report that names these and labels provided
  data as provided (never as measured) meets the rubric's honesty bar
  even when results are inconclusive.
- **Scoring:** use the rubric in the project file (18 exemplary;
  14–17 secure; 10–13 developing; below 10 re-run with adult support).
  Score the report, not the plant: a slow or null result honestly
  reported can score full marks on analysis, explanation, and
  limitations.
