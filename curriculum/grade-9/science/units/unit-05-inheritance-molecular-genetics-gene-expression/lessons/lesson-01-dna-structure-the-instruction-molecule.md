# Lesson 01 — DNA Structure: The Instruction Molecule

**Student goal:** I can build a DNA model, explain the base-pairing rules,
and say why DNA's structure lets it store instructions and be copied.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Index cards for the vocabulary (one term per card)
- Two colors of beads or paper strips (the two strands), four bead colors
  or letter stamps for A, T, C, G (one per base), string or pipe cleaners
  for the backbone
- Printed copy of the Lesson 1 base-sequence handout (in the practice
  session, not needed now)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

The adult holds up the U04 pipe-cleaner chromosomes and asks: "In Unit 04
we said chromosomes carry the instructions, and genes are segments of those
instructions. What is the instruction molecule itself actually made of?"
Accept any honest answer, including "I don't know" — the point is to
surface the gap. If the learner says "DNA," ask: "What is DNA made of?"
Most learners can name the letters A, T, C, G but not what they stand for.
That is where the lesson starts.

## Teaching

**DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that stores the
instructions for building and running a cell. Every cell in your body
carries a complete copy — about 3 billion base pairs in a human cell,
packed into chromosomes. A **gene** is a segment of DNA that codes for one
product, usually a protein. A chromosome is one very long DNA molecule,
wrapped around proteins and folded up tight.

DNA is built from repeating units called **nucleotides**. Each nucleotide
has three parts: a sugar (**deoxyribose**), a **phosphate** group, and one
of four **nitrogenous bases**: **adenine (A)**, **thymine (T)**,
**cytosine (C)**, or **guanine (G)**. The sugar and phosphate of many
nucleotides link together into a backbone — think of it as the two rails
of a ladder. The bases point inward, like the rungs.

The two strands twist around each other into a **double helix**. The
strands run in opposite directions — **antiparallel** — like two one-way
streets facing opposite ways. And the rungs follow strict rules, the
**base-pairing rules**: **A always pairs with T, and G always pairs with
C**. A–T pairs are held by two hydrogen bonds; G–C pairs by three. That
is why the ladder is always the same width: a two-ring base (A or G, the
**purines**) always pairs with a one-ring base (T or C, the
**pyrimidines**). Two purines would be too wide; two pyrimidines too
narrow.

Why does this structure matter? Two reasons. First, **storage**: the order
of the four bases along a strand *is* the instruction — the sequence
spells out genes the way letters spell out words. Second, **copying**:
because each base has exactly one partner, each strand is a template for
the other. Pull the strands apart, build the matching partner for each
base, and you have two identical copies. That is the trick that makes cell
division (Unit 04) possible: DNA is copied in S phase before any division
because the molecule is built to be copied.

Two numbers worth remembering, measured by the scientists who worked out
the structure (Watson and Crick, building on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray
data): each base pair adds 0.34 nanometers along the helix, and one full
turn of the helix holds 10 base pairs (3.4 nm). You do not need to
memorize these — but they show that the model is a real, measured
structure, not a cartoon.

**Key vocabulary:** *DNA* (deoxyribonucleic acid — the instruction
molecule), *nucleotide* (one sugar + one phosphate + one base),
*deoxyribose* (the 5-carbon sugar in DNA), *phosphate* (the backbone link),
*nitrogenous base* (A, T, C, G — the information letters), *double helix*
(the twisted two-strand shape), *base pairing* (A–T, G–C), *complementary
strand* (the strand whose bases match another strand by the pairing rules),
*antiparallel* (the two strands run in opposite directions), *gene* (a DNA
segment that codes for a product), *chromosome* (one long DNA molecule,
packaged).

## Worked example 1 — "Build the matching strand"

**Given strand (5' → 3'):** A T G C C A T G

Build the complementary strand. Work base by base, using A–T and G–C:

| Given | A | T | G | C | C | A | T | G |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Match | T | A | C | G | G | T | A | C |

The complementary strand reads **T A C G G T A C** — and because the
strands are antiparallel, it runs 3' → 5' while the given strand runs
5' → 3'. Count the bonds: the sequence has four A–T pairs (4 × 2 = 8
bonds) and four G–C pairs (4 × 3 = 12 bonds), 20 hydrogen bonds in total. **Reasoning to name
aloud:** the pairing rules leave no choice — once you know one strand, the
other is determined. That is what "complementary" means, and it is why
copying works.

## Worked example 2 — "Why the ladder is always the same width"

A learner proposes a DNA ladder with an A–G rung and a T–C rung. The adult
walks through why the molecule cannot be built that way:

- A and G are both purines — two-ring bases. Put them across from each
  other and the rung is too wide; the backbone would bulge.
- T and C are both pyrimidines — one-ring bases. Put them across from
  each other and the rung is too short; the backbone would pinch in.
- Only a purine across from a pyrimidine (A–T or G–C) keeps the width
  constant, rung after rung — and the measured diameter of the helix is a
  uniform 2 nanometers.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the pairing rules are not a convention the
textbook invented; they are forced by the shapes of the molecules. A
common error is to memorize "A–T, G–C" without knowing *why* — the why
is the size match.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Work through these together, 4–6 minutes each.

1. Write the complementary strand for 5'-C C A T G G-3'. *(Hint: pair
   each base, then read the match strand in the opposite direction.)*
2. A DNA segment is 12 base pairs long. How many nucleotides does it
   contain in total, across both strands? *(Hint: each base pair is two
   nucleotides, one per strand.)*
3. Error analysis: "A pairs with G because both are purines, so they
   match." What is wrong, and what is the correct pairing?
4. A strand reads 5'-G G G A A A-3'. Its complement has how many
   hydrogen bonds holding the two strands together? Show the count.
   *(Hint: count A–T pairs and G–C pairs separately.)*
5. Explain in your own words why DNA can be copied accurately, using the
   words *template* and *complementary*.

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. Write the complementary strand (with direction) for
   5'-A T C G T A C G-3'.
2. A double-stranded DNA molecule is 30% adenine. What percentage is
   thymine? What percentage is guanine? Explain the reasoning. *(Hint:
   use the pairing rules, then the fact that the four percentages sum to
   100%.)*
3. A scientist measures a DNA sample: 22% cytosine. Predict the
   percentages of G, A, and T. Show your working.
4. Sketch a 6-base-pair ladder: label the sugar–phosphate backbone on
   both sides, draw the rungs as A–T or G–C pairs, and mark which strand
   runs 5' → 3'. (A labeled sketch, not art — accuracy is the grade.)
5. Explain why the two strands of DNA must be antiparallel for the
   base-pairing rules to work the same way along the whole molecule.
   (Accept: the 5' and 3' ends must line up so each base meets its
   partner; accept any answer that ties direction to the pairing.)
6. A learner says, "Genes and chromosomes are the same thing." Write a
   3-sentence correction: what a gene is, what a chromosome is, and how
   they relate.

## Application

Build a physical DNA model with beads or paper strips: two backbone
strands, at least 10 base pairs, with the correct pairing rules and the
strands running in opposite directions (mark the 5' and 3' ends). Twist
it gently into a helix. Then point to one gene-sized segment (any 6 base
pairs) and explain to the adult what that segment *is* in the cell: a
stretch of DNA whose sequence codes for a product. Keep the model — it
returns in Lesson 2.

## Exit check

Without looking back: name the three parts of a nucleotide, state the two
base-pairing rules, and explain in one sentence why those rules let DNA be
copied.

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** keep the four base cards (A, T, C, G) visible with
  their full names; point to the model while naming each part.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build the model and dictate
  the explanations while the adult scribes, or label a printed ladder
  diagram instead of drawing one.
- **Extension:** a human chromosome 1 holds about 249 million base pairs.
  At 0.34 nm per base pair, how long would it be stretched out? (About
  8.5 cm — and it fits in a nucleus a few micrometers across, which is why
  packaging matters.) Show the multiplication.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat A–T and G–C as arbitrary pairings to memorize, or
pair A with G because "both are purines." Anchor the rule in size:
purine-with-pyrimidine, every rung the same width. Another common slip:
thinking the two strands run the same direction — stress *antiparallel*
by marking the 5' and 3' ends on the model every time. A third: confusing
gene and chromosome — a chromosome is the whole packaged molecule; genes
are segments along it. Answer-key references: [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
