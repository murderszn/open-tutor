# Lesson 05 — Gene Expression: Same Genome, Different Jobs

**Student goal:** I can explain how one genome makes many cell types, and
how the environment can change which genes are expressed.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 10 of 20**

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Index cards: one per cell type (skin, muscle, red blood cell, neuron,
  liver) for the sorting activity

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "Your skin cells and your muscle cells carry the same DNA — the same
genome. So why is one making skin proteins and the other making muscle
proteins?" Accept "different genes are turned on" in any wording. If the
learner says "they have different DNA," correct it now: with rare
exceptions (such as mature red blood cells, which lose their nucleus),
every cell of one body carries the same genome. The difference is not the
instructions — it is which instructions are *used*.

## Teaching

**Gene expression** is the process of turning a gene on to produce its
RNA and protein. Every cell in your body carries the same genome — the
full set of DNA — but each cell type expresses only a subset of its genes.
A muscle cell expresses the genes for muscle proteins; a skin cell
expresses the genes for skin proteins. That selective use is
**differentiation** (Unit 04, Lesson 6): one genome, many cell types,
because different genes are switched on in different cells.

Expression is regulated at several steps. A gene can be switched on or
off at the DNA level (whether the DNA is unpacked and available), at
transcription (whether RNA is made), after transcription (whether the RNA
is processed and exported), at translation (whether the protein is built),
or after the protein is made (whether it is activated or broken down).
The adult does not need the names of every step — the idea is that
"having the gene" and "using the gene" are different things, and the cell
controls the use.

The environment can change expression, too. The classic example is the
Siamese cat. Siamese cats carry a version of the gene for the pigment
enzyme tyrosinase that works only at cooler temperatures. The cat's warm
body core stays pale, while its cooler ears, paws, and tail — where the
enzyme works — grow dark fur. Same genome in every cell; different
temperatures, different expression, different color. (A Siamese kitten
raised in a warmer room grows up paler; the gene did not change — its
expression did.)

Humans show the same logic. Sunlight increases melanin production in skin
cells — the genes for melanin were always there, but UV exposure turns
their expression up, and skin darkens. And identical twins, who share a
genome, still differ in measurable ways — fingerprints, for example —
because development is not a printed copy: the same instructions, read in
slightly different conditions, produce slightly different results.

Two cautions. First, "gene expression" does not mean "genes decide
everything": most traits come from many genes *plus* environment, and this
unit's one-gene examples are simplifications that make the logic visible.
Second, an environmental effect on expression is not the same as changing
the DNA sequence — a tan does not rewrite your genes, and it is not
inherited. Mutations (Lesson 6) change the sequence; expression changes
the use.

**Key vocabulary:** *gene expression* (turning a gene on to make its RNA
and protein), *differentiation* (one genome producing many cell types
through selective expression), *genome* (the full set of an organism's
DNA), *regulation* (the cell's control over when and how much a gene is
expressed), *environment* (conditions outside the gene — temperature,
light, nutrition — that can affect expression).

## Worked example 1 — "The Siamese cat's dark points"

A Siamese cat's body is pale cream; its ears, muzzle, paws, and tail are
dark brown. Work through the explanation:

1. Every cell carries the same gene for the pigment enzyme tyrosinase.
2. This version of the enzyme is temperature-sensitive: it works well in
   cooler skin and poorly in warmer skin.
3. The ears, paws, and tail are cooler than the body core, so the enzyme
   works there and pigment is made; the warm core makes little pigment.
4. Conclusion: the *pattern* is an expression pattern, not a DNA pattern
   — the same gene, read differently in different places.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** if the difference were in the DNA, the
dark-paw cells would need different genes from the pale-body cells. They
do not — they have the same genes, used differently. Temperature is the
switch.

## Worked example 2 — "Two cells, one genome"

A skin cell and a liver cell from the same person:

| | Skin cell | Liver cell |
|---|---|---|
| Genome | Complete, identical | Complete, identical |
| Genes expressed | Skin proteins (e.g., keratin) at high levels | Liver enzymes at high levels |
| Genes silent | Liver enzymes (mostly off) | Keratin (mostly off) |
| Housekeeping genes | On in both (energy, repair, basic structure) | On in both |

**Reasoning to name aloud:** both cells *could* make keratin or liver
enzymes — they hold the genes — but each expresses its own set and keeps
the rest mostly silent. "Mostly" matters: expression is a dial, not a
simple on/off switch, and the same gene can be expressed at different
levels in different cells.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Work through these together, 4–6 minutes each.

1. A muscle cell and a neuron have the same genome. Name one thing that
   must be true about their genes, and one thing that must differ.
2. A Siamese cat's tail is shaved and regrows in a warm bandage. Will the
   new fur be dark or pale? Explain using temperature and the enzyme.
3. Error analysis: "Identical twins have identical fingerprints because
   they have identical DNA." What is wrong?
4. A plant grown in bright light makes more chlorophyll than the same
   plant's clone grown in dim light. Did the DNA change? What changed
   instead?
5. Explain in 2–3 sentences why "you have the gene for X" does not mean
   "you will show trait X."

## Independent practice (6–8 meaningful tasks)

1. List three cell types in your body and, for each, name one protein or
   job that cell expresses at high levels and one it keeps mostly silent.
2. A gene is expressed in liver cells but not in brain cells. A learner
   says, "Then the brain cells must be missing that gene." Correct them
   in 2–3 sentences.
3. Sunlight increases melanin production in skin. Is the resulting tan
   inherited by the person's children? Explain, using the difference
   between expression and DNA sequence.
4. Two identical twin seedlings are grown — one in a sunny window, one in
   a dark cupboard. The sunny one is green and sturdy; the dark one is
   pale and spindly. Same genome. Explain the difference in two sentences.
5. A researcher finds a protein present in kidney cells but absent in
   heart cells. Name two steps at which this difference could be
   controlled (e.g., transcription vs. translation), and explain each in
   one sentence.
6. In your own words, explain differentiation to a younger learner in 3–4
   sentences, using the phrase "same instructions, different pages open."
7. Error analysis: "Gene expression proves the environment matters more
   than genes." Write a 3-sentence reply that puts both in their place.

## Application

Choose one human trait that varies with environment — tanning, muscle
growth with exercise, or height with childhood nutrition. Write a short
paragraph that (a) names the gene(s) or proteins involved in general terms,
(b) explains how the environment changes their expression, and (c) states
clearly what is *not* inherited (the acquired change) and what *is*
inherited (the genes and their regulatory setup). The adult checks that the
paragraph never claims the environment rewrites the DNA.

## Exit check

Without looking back: define *gene expression* and *differentiation*;
explain the Siamese cat's color pattern in two sentences; and state one
environmental influence on expression and what it does *not* change.

## Supports and extensions

- **Language access:** use the book analogy throughout — same book, different
  pages open — and keep the five cell-type cards visible during the sorting
  activity.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort the cell-type cards and
  dictate the explanations while the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** read OpenStax Biology 2e §16.1 (Regulation of Gene
  Expression) and list the five levels at which eukaryotic expression is
  regulated, in your own words, with one example each.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The most common error is treating the genome as a destiny printout: "you
have the gene, so you get the trait." Keep returning to the dial: genes
are expressed at levels, in places, at times. Second: confusing expression
with mutation — a tan is not a mutation and is not inherited; Lessons 5
and 6 must stay distinct on this point. Third: thinking different cell
types have different DNA — re-anchor with the Siamese cat every time the
error appears: same genes, different use. Answer-key references:
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05).
