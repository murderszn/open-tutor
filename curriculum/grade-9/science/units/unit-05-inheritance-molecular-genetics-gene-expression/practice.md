# Practice Sessions — Unit 05: Inheritance, Molecular Genetics, and Gene Expression

Four named practice sessions, referenced in the [unit pacing](README.md#20-session-pacing).
Each session is one 50-minute period. All answers are in the [answer
key](answer-key.md). Every dataset below is labeled **fictional practice
data** unless it is Mendel's historical counts, which are labeled as such.

## Session A — Base pairing and the ladder model

*Session 2 · after Lesson 1 · supports Objective 1*

**Goal:** build complementary strands fluently and use the pairing rules
to reason about DNA, not just recite them.

1. Write the complementary strand (with 5'/3' directions) for each given
   strand:
   a. 5'-A T G C A T-3'
   b. 5'-G C G C A T A T-3'
   c. 5'-T T A A C C G G-3'
2. A DNA molecule is 18% thymine. What are the percentages of A, G, and
   C? Show the two steps (pairing, then subtraction from 100%).
3. A segment has 10 base pairs: 6 are G–C and 4 are A–T. How many
   hydrogen bonds hold the two strands together across the segment?
   Which kind of pair contributes more bonds, and why?
4. Error analysis: a learner writes the complement of 5'-A T G-3' as
   3'-T A C-5' and then reads it as "TAC" from left to right as the
   complementary *sequence*. What did they get right, what is sloppy
   about the reading, and how should the complement be written if both
   strands are read 5' → 3'?
5. A chromosome is 50 million base pairs long. How many nucleotides is
   that, across both strands? How many hydrogen bonds would it have if
   42% of its pairs were G–C? (Show the multiplication; round to two
   significant figures.)
6. Explain in 2–3 sentences why Chargaff's observation — that A ≈ T and
   G ≈ C in any species' DNA — was a clue to the double-helix structure
   before anyone had seen it.

## Session B — Codon reading and mutation effects

*Session 4 · after Lesson 2 · supports Objective 2*

Use the [simplified codon chart](lessons/lesson-02-from-dna-to-protein-the-central-dogma.md#simplified-codon-chart-the-only-codons-this-unit-uses)
in Lesson 2. All sequences are given 5' → 3' for mRNA and 3' → 5' for DNA
templates unless stated otherwise.

1. Transcribe and translate: DNA template 3'-TAC CCT GGA TTT-5'.
   (Check: your protein starts with Met and ends with Lys.)
2. Transcribe and translate: DNA template 3'-TAC GTA ACC CAT-5'.
3. mRNA 5'-AUG UUA GGG UGA-3'. Translate it. What does the final codon
   do?
4. A point mutation changes the DNA template so that an mRNA codon
   changes from CCU to CCC. What happens to the protein? Name the kind
   of change.
5. A point mutation changes an mRNA codon from UAC to UAA. What happens
   to the protein? Name the kind of change, and explain why it matters
   more than the change in item 4.
6. A two-base deletion removes "CU" from the second codon (CCU becomes
   just C) in an mRNA that reads 5'-AUG CCU ACG GGA UAA-3'. Write the new
   mRNA, regroup into codons from the start, translate, and compare with
   the original protein (Met–Pro–Thr–Gly–Stop). What changed, and why?
7. Error analysis: "The mRNA is a copy of the DNA, so it has the same
   sequence as the DNA." Correct this in 2–3 sentences, naming the two
   differences (strand and base) that matter.

## Session C — Expression scenarios and environment

*Session 11 · after Lesson 5 · supports Objective 5*

**Goal:** apply the expression logic to new cases; keep "same genome,
different use" separate from "changed DNA."

1. A skin cell and a liver cell are compared. Both carry the gene for
   the structural protein keratin.
   a. Which cell expresses keratin at high levels, and which keeps it
      mostly silent?
   b. If a test found keratin protein in the liver cell, what are two
      possible explanations — one about expression, one about the test?
2. Arctic foxes grow a white coat in winter and a brown coat in summer.
   The genome does not change with the seasons. Explain the color change
   in terms of gene expression and environment in 3–4 sentences.
3. Two identical twin plants (clones) are grown side by side. One is
   watered; one is not. The watered plant grows twice as tall. Did their
   DNA differ? What differed instead? Would the watered plant's seeds
   grow tall without water? Explain.
4. A drug blocks transcription of one gene in liver cells only. The
   gene's DNA is untouched.
   a. Will the liver cells still make that protein? Why?
   b. Will the person's children be affected? Why?
5. Error analysis: "If a trait is genetic, the environment cannot change
   it." Give two counterexamples from this unit and explain each in one
   sentence.
6. A researcher measures the same protein at high levels in muscle cells
   and low levels in skin cells.
   a. Name three steps at which this difference could be regulated.
   b. For one of them, explain how a cell could turn expression up or
      down at that step.

## Session D — Pedigree and pattern drills

*Session 13 · after Lesson 6 · supports Objective 5*

Use the [Alvarez pedigree](lessons/lesson-06-inheritance-patterns-mutations-and-pedigrees.md#the-fictional-alvarez-pedigree-labeled-fictional-practice-data)
and the patterns from Lesson 6. All families below are fictional.

1. In the Alvarez pedigree, II-3 (Ee) and II-5 (Ee) have a second child.
   a. What is the probability the child has attached earlobes?
   b. What is the probability the child is a carrier?
   c. What is the probability the child has free earlobes *and* is a
      carrier? Show the Punnett square.
2. I-1 (Ee) and I-2 (ee) have a fourth child. What is the probability it
   has free earlobes? What is the probability it is Ee? (Careful: the
   two answers are the same number here — explain why.)
3. A couple with type A blood (mother, I^A i) and type B blood (father,
   I^B i) have three children. What is the probability all three have
   type O blood? What is the probability none of them has type O blood?
4. Snapdragons: cross a red plant (RR) with a pink plant (Rr). Give the
   genotype and phenotype ratios of the offspring.
5. A color-blind man (X^b Y) has children with a woman who is not
   color-blind and not a carrier (X^B X^B).
   a. What are the genotypes and phenotypes of their sons? Their
      daughters?
   b. Will any of their grandchildren be color-blind? Explain which
      grandchildren could be, and through whom.
6. A new mutation appears in a gene. It changes one codon so that the
   protein gains an amino acid it did not have before, but the protein
   still works.
   a. What kind of mutation is this most likely to be — substitution,
      insertion, or deletion — and why?
   b. If the mutation happened in a skin cell, can the person's children
      inherit it? If it happened in a gamete-forming cell, can they?
7. Error analysis: a learner looks at a pedigree where two parents with
   free earlobes have an attached-earlobe child and concludes, "The
   trait must be dominant, because the parents show it and the child
   doesn't." Correct them: name the pattern, the parents' genotypes,
   and the reasoning.
