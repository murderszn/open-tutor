# Project — The Family-Trait Investigation

**Sessions 8–9, 14–15, 18 · supports Objectives 1, 3, 4, 5**

You are the geneticist for a fictional family and a fictional pea
population. Your job: trace a trait through a family tree, test a
prediction against population counts, and defend a claim about how the
trait is inherited — with evidence, not opinion. All families and datasets
in this project are fictional and labeled as such; no real family
information is used, and you may complete every task from the fictional
data alone.

## The question

How is the attached-earlobe trait inherited in the Alvarez family, and
does the class's pea-flower data support the 3:1 prediction for a
heterozygous cross?

## Part 1 — The pedigree (Sessions 8–9)

Use the Alvarez pedigree from [Lesson
6](lessons/lesson-06-inheritance-patterns-mutations-and-pedigrees.md#the-fictional-alvarez-pedigree-labeled-fictional-practice-data).
Copy it into your notebook and label every person with a genotype,
justifying each deduction in one sentence beside the symbol. Your
pedigree must show:

- I-1's genotype, with the evidence that forces it;
- II-5's genotype, with the evidence that forces it;
- III-1's genotype, with the evidence that forces it;
- the probability that II-1 and II-4's next child has attached earlobes,
  with the Punnett square that gives it.

## Part 2 — The population data (Sessions 8–9)

Use the fictional F2 dataset from [Lesson
4](lessons/lesson-04-probability-and-trait-distributions.md#the-fictional-f2-dataset-labeled-fictional-practice-data):
400 F2 plants from a Bb × Bb self-cross — 296 purple, 104 white.

1. Compute the expected counts at 3:1 and the deviations.
2. State, in numbers, how good the fit is (deviation in plants and in
   percentage points).
3. Explain why 400 plants give a stronger test than 40 plants with the
   same percentages (use the 29-purple/11-white-of-40 comparison from
   Lesson 4's application).
4. State one thing this dataset *cannot* prove, and what would strengthen
   the conclusion (more plants? a repeat cross? a different trait?).

## Part 3 — The argument (Sessions 14–15)

Write a claim–evidence–reasoning report (one to two pages) with three
claims:

1. **The attached-earlobe trait is recessive.** Evidence: the pedigree
   cases where the trait appears in children of free-earlobed parents.
   Reasoning: why that pattern fits recessive inheritance and rules out
   dominant inheritance.
2. **The class's pea data support the 3:1 model for a Bb × Bb cross.**
   Evidence: expected vs. observed counts from Part 2. Reasoning: why a
   4-plant deviation in 400 supports the model, and what would have
   counted against it.
3. **DNA structure explains how the trait is passed on.** Evidence: the
   base-pairing rules, the central dogma, and meiosis from Lessons 1–3.
   Reasoning: trace the path from a parent's allele to a child's trait in
   four steps (DNA → gamete → zygote → protein → trait).

Peer critique (Session 15): swap reports with the adult (or, if working in
a group the adult supervises, with a partner). Check each claim for: is
the evidence actually stated as numbers or pedigree facts? Does the
reasoning connect the evidence to the claim, or just repeat it? Mark one
strength and one fix per claim, then revise.

## Capstone presentations (Session 18)

Present your investigation in 5 minutes: one visual (your pedigree or the
Punnett-square SVG), the three claims from Part 3, and the strongest piece
of evidence for each claim. Then the adult asks one challenge question —
for example, "What would change your mind about claim 2?" — and you answer
from your data, not from memory. The presentation is scored with the rubric
below.

## Deliverables

1. The labeled Alvarez pedigree with justified genotypes (Part 1).
2. The population-data table with expected counts, deviations, and the
   fit judgment (Part 2).
3. The claim–evidence–reasoning report, revised after critique (Part 3).
4. A 5-minute capstone presentation (Session 18): one visual (your
   pedigree or the Punnett-square SVG), the three claims, and the
   strongest piece of evidence for each.

## Rubric

| Criterion | Strong (3) | Adequate (2) | Needs work (1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pedigree genotypes | Every genotype correct with a one-sentence justification that names the evidence | Most genotypes correct; some justifications missing or vague | Several genotypes wrong or unjustified |
| Probability work | Punnett squares correct; probabilities stated per offspring; sample-size reasoning explicit | Punnett squares mostly correct; some probabilities stated as certainties | Punnett squares wrong or missing; probabilities confused with guarantees |
| Data comparison | Expected vs. observed computed correctly; fit judged in plants *and* percentage points; 40-vs-400 contrast explained | Expected vs. observed computed; fit judged in one unit only | Counts copied without comparison, or deviation misread |
| CER argument | Three claims, each with specific evidence and reasoning that connects it; counter-evidence addressed | Three claims present; some reasoning repeats the evidence instead of connecting it | Claims without evidence, or evidence without reasoning |
| DNA mechanism | Four-step trace (DNA → gamete → zygote → protein → trait) accurate and in order | Trace present with one step missing or out of order | Mechanism missing, or confuses gene with trait |
| Presentation | Visual supports the claims; strongest evidence named for each claim; answers a challenge question | Visual present; evidence named for most claims | Reads the report aloud; no visual use |

**Scoring:** 15–18 = ready to move on; 11–14 = revise the weakest row and
re-present; below 11 = re-teach the lowest objective (see the [teacher
guide](teacher-guide.md)) before starting Unit 06.

## Materials and safety

Notebook, pencils, printed pedigree and dataset handouts, craft materials
for the DNA model (reused from Lessons 1–2), the Punnett-square SVG. No
living material, no hazardous substance, no personal or family information
is used at any point. If the learner wants to discuss their own family's
traits, the adult keeps that conversation private, off the record, and
entirely optional — the project is complete from the fictional data alone.
