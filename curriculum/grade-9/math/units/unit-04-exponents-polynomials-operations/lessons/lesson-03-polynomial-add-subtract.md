# Lesson 03 — Polynomials: Adding and Subtracting

**Goal:** I can add and subtract polynomials, write the result in
standard form, and name its degree, leading coefficient, and
constant term.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook; colored pencils
optional, for circling like terms.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Combine like terms: `3x² + 5x − 4 + 2x² − 3x + 7`. If you got
`5x² + 2x + 3`, you are ready: adding polynomials is combining like
terms, with the terms kept in order. Today's question is what counts
as "like," what order the answer goes in, and what happens when a
minus sign stands in front of a whole polynomial.

## Teaching: a polynomial is a sum of terms (15 min)

A **term** is a number, a variable, or a product of numbers and
variables with whole-number exponents: `5`, `3x`, `-2x²`, `7xy`.
The number part of a term is its **coefficient**. **Like terms**
have exactly the same variables raised to exactly the same exponents:
`3x²` and `-2x²` are like terms; `3x²` and `3x` are not, because the
exponent differs.

A **polynomial** is a sum of terms. Names count terms: one term is
a **monomial**, two terms a **binomial**, three a **trinomial**.
The **degree of a term** is the sum of the exponents of its
variables (`3x²` has degree 2; `7xy` has degree 2; a constant such
as `5` has degree 0). The **degree of a polynomial** is the largest
degree among its terms. **Standard form** writes terms from highest
degree to lowest. The first term in standard form is the **leading
term**; its coefficient is the **leading coefficient**; the term
with no variable is the **constant term**.

Example: `4x³ − 2x + 5` is in standard form. Degree `3`, leading
coefficient `4`, constant term `5`. Note the missing `x²` term:
standard form does not require every degree to appear.

**Adding:** add coefficients of like terms; unlike terms stay
separate. **Subtracting:** subtract the *whole* second polynomial —
distribute the minus sign to every term of it, then combine like
terms. The most common error in this lesson is subtracting only the
first term of the second polynomial.

**Closure:** adding, subtracting, or multiplying two polynomials
always gives another polynomial — never a fraction with a variable
in the denominator, never a root of a variable. Polynomials are
closed under these operations, the way integers are closed under
addition, subtraction, and multiplication: `3 + (-5) = -2` is still
an integer, and `(x² + 1) + (2x − 3) = x² + 2x − 2` is still a
polynomial. Division is the exception: a polynomial divided by a
polynomial need not be a polynomial, just as an integer divided by
an integer need not be an integer.

### Worked example 1 — add, then read off degree and leading coefficient

> Add `(3x² + 5x − 4) + (2x² − 3x + 7)`.

1. Drop the parentheses (adding changes no signs) and group like
   terms: `(3x² + 2x²) + (5x − 3x) + (−4 + 7)`.
2. Combine: `5x² + 2x + 3`. Already in standard form.
3. Read off: degree `2`, leading coefficient `5`, constant term
   `3`. Check by evaluating at `x = 1`: the original sum is
   `(3 + 5 − 4) + (2 − 3 + 7) = 4 + 6 = 10`, and `5 + 2 + 3 = 10` ✓.

### Worked example 2 — subtract the whole polynomial

> Subtract `(4x³ − 2x + 5) − (2x³ + 3x − 5)`.

1. Distribute the minus to *every* term of the second polynomial:
   `4x³ − 2x + 5 − 2x³ − 3x + 5`. (The `−5` became `+5`; the `+3x`
   became `−3x`. Both changes are the point of this example.)
2. Combine like terms: `(4x³ − 2x³) + (−2x − 3x) + (5 + 5) =
   2x³ − 5x + 10`.
3. Read off: degree `3`, leading coefficient `2`, constant term
   `10`. Check at `x = 1`: `(4 − 2 + 5) − (2 + 3 − 5) = 7 − 0 = 7`,
   and `2 − 5 + 10 = 7` ✓.

## Guided practice (10 min, with hints)

1. Add `(2x² + x + 6) + (x² − 4x + 1)`. *Hint: group x² terms, x terms, constants.*
2. Subtract `(5x² − 3x + 2) − (2x² + x − 4)`. *Hint: write the second polynomial with every sign flipped before combining.*
3. Write `7 − 3x + 2x³` in standard form and name its degree,
   leading coefficient, and constant term. *Hint: highest degree first.*
4. Is `2x² + 1/x` a polynomial? Explain in one sentence.
   *Hint: check the exponent on x in the second term.*

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Add `(4x³ + x − 2) + (−x³ + 5x + 9)`.
2. Subtract `(6x² + 2x − 1) − (x² − 5x + 3)`.
3. Subtract `(x³ − 4x² + 7) − (2x³ + x² − 2x + 1)`.
4. A rectangle's sides are `2x + 3` and `x − 1` (units: feet).
   Write its perimeter as a polynomial in standard form.
   (Perimeter `= 2 · length + 2 · width`.)
5. Explain closure in your own words: why is the sum of two
   polynomials always a polynomial, while the quotient of two
   polynomials need not be? Give one example of each.

## Application (5 min)

A landscaper's fictional price list charges a polynomial cost (in
dollars) for two beds: bed A costs `3x² + 40x + 25`, bed B costs
`2x² − 10x + 15`, where `x` is a size factor. Write the combined
cost as a polynomial, and find it when `x = 2`. State whether the
combined cost is still a polynomial, and why.

## Exit check (5 min)

Add `(3x³ − x + 4) + (2x³ + 5x − 4)` and subtract
`(3x³ − x + 4) − (2x³ + 5x − 4)`. Give both results in standard
form with their degrees.

## Supports, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** read `−(2x³ + 3x − 5)` aloud as
  "minus the whole thing"; circle the second polynomial before
  flipping its signs.
- **Alternate response mode:** a learner may sort terms into
  piles by degree (by color or by column) before writing the sum.
- **Extension:** add `(2x²y + 3xy − y²) + (x²y − 5xy + 4y²)`
  (answer: `3x²y − 2xy + 3y²`; the `x²y` terms have degree `3`
  (exponents `2 + 1`), the `xy` and `y²` terms have degree `2` —
  so the polynomial has degree `3`), and explain why `x²y` and
  `xy²` are *not* like terms.
- **Teacher note — misconception:** the dropped-minus error
  (subtracting only the first term) and combining unlike terms
  (`x² + x = x³`) are the two errors to hunt in Practice C.
