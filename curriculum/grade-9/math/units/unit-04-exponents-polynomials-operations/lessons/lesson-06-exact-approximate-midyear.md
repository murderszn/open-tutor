# Lesson 06 — Exact, Approximate, and the Midyear Check

**Goal:** I can classify sums and products of rational and
irrational numbers, choose a sensible level of accuracy for a
context, and retrieve Units 01–03 skills for the midyear review.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, calculator for
approximations only.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Classify `3`, `1/2`, `√2`, and `√9` as rational or irrational.
If you got rational, rational, irrational, rational (since
`√9 = 3`), you are ready: a root sign does not make a number
irrational — `√9` is a whole number in disguise. Today's question
is what happens when rational and irrational numbers are added or
multiplied, and when a rounded answer is good enough.

## Teaching: rational + irrational stays irrational — with one exception to the product rule (15 min)

A **rational** number can be written as a ratio of integers
(`3 = 3/1`, `0.25 = 1/4`, `0.333… = 1/3`). An **irrational**
number cannot; its decimal neither terminates nor repeats
(`√2 ≈ 1.41421356…`, `π ≈ 3.14159…`).

**N-RN.3 rules:**

- Rational + rational = rational. Rational × rational = rational.
- Rational + irrational = irrational. (If `r + s` were rational,
  then `s = (r + s) − r` would be a difference of rationals, hence
  rational — contradiction. So the sum cannot be rational.)
- Nonzero rational × irrational = irrational. (Same argument with
  division: if `r·s` were rational and `r ≠ 0`, then `s = (r·s)/r`
  would be rational.)
- **The exception to watch:** irrational × irrational *can* be
  rational — `√2 · √2 = 2` — and can be irrational — `√2 · √3 =
  √6`. So for two irrationals, the product's type is not decided
  by the rule; you must compute it. The zero case is similar:
  `0 · √2 = 0`, rational, which is why the product rule above
  says *nonzero* rational.

**Choosing accuracy (N-Q.3):** the context decides the rounding,
not the calculator's display. Money is reported to cents; a board
cut from a `√50`-foot length is measured to the nearest sixteenth
of an inch in a shop, or to the nearest tenth of a foot in a rough
plan; a further algebra step keeps the exact form, because rounding
early compounds the error. State the place you rounded to, and use
`≈`. An answer with ten decimal places is not more accurate than
the measurement it came from — it is less honest.

**Midyear retrieval (Units 01–03, for Week 18):** the rest of this
lesson is a checkpoint, not new content. Solve one linear equation
with a justification, read one linear function from a graph's slope
and intercept, and solve one system by a method of your choice,
checking in both equations. The formative quiz (Session 16) samples
this unit; the culminating assessment (Session 18) samples this
unit plus two cumulative items.

### Worked example 1 — classify, then prove the type

> Classify `2 + √3`, `4 · √5`, `√2 · √8`, and `√2 · √3`.

1. `2 + √3`: rational + irrational = irrational. (`√3` is
   irrational, and no whole number added to it can cancel the
   non-repeating decimal.)
2. `4 · √5`: nonzero rational × irrational = irrational.
3. `√2 · √8 = √16 = 4`: irrational × irrational, and this time
   rational. This is the case the rule does *not* decide in
   advance — the computation decides it.
4. `√2 · √3 = √6`: irrational × irrational, and this time
   irrational (`6` is not a perfect square, so `√6` cannot be a
   ratio of integers). The pair (3) and (4) together is the lesson:
   never answer "irrational × irrational = irrational" as a rule.

### Worked example 2 — round at the end, and say so

> A side length is `√50` feet. A builder needs a number to cut
> to; an algebra problem later in the same project needs the exact
> length. Give both, correctly labeled.

1. Exact form: `√50 = 5√2` feet. This is what the algebra keeps.
2. Approximation: `5√2 ≈ 5 · 1.4142 = 7.071` feet, so to the
   nearest tenth, `≈ 7.1` feet. That is what the builder cuts to
   (and the builder's tape, not this calculation, sets the real
   precision).
3. What not to write: `√50 = 7.1`. The exact length is not `7.1`;
   `7.1` is a rounded stand-in, and writing `=` hides the gap.
   In the algebra step, using `7.1` instead of `5√2` would carry a
   rounding error into every later result.

## Guided practice (10 min, with hints)

1. Classify `5 + √7` and `3 · √2`, with a one-sentence rule for
   each. *Hint: which N-RN.3 case is each?*
2. Classify `√3 · √12` and `√3 · √5`. *Hint: multiply under one radical first, then decide.*
3. A length is `√72` feet. Give the exact form and an
   approximation to the nearest hundredth. *Hint: simplify first, then approximate.*
4. Solve `3x − 7 = 11` with a justification for each step
   (Unit 01 retrieval). *Hint: undo in reverse order, naming the step.*

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Classify each as rational or irrational, with a rule or a
   computation: `1/3 + √2`; `0 · √5`; `√5 · √20`; `√5 · √7`;
   `2√3 − 2√3`.
2. A rectangle's area is `(x + 2)(x + 3)` square feet with `x = 2`.
   Compute the exact area two ways (expand, then substitute; and
   substitute, then multiply) and confirm they agree.
3. A cost estimate is `$12.345` per unit for `37` units. Give the
   exact product, then the amount a bill would actually charge
   (to cents), and explain the difference in one sentence.
4. Solve the system `x + y = 9` and `2x − y = 3` by a method of
   your choice, checking in both equations (Unit 03 retrieval).

## Application (5 min)

A trail map (fictional) says a path is `√200` miles long, and a
second path is `√50` miles long. Simplify both lengths, add them
exactly, and then give the total to the nearest tenth of a mile
for a hiker's sign. Explain why the sign's number, useful as it
is, must not replace the exact sum in a calculation of total
distance for three laps.

## Exit check (5 min)

Classify `√6 · √10` and `√6 · √24`, showing the multiplication
under one radical. Then give `√80` exactly and to the nearest
tenth, labeled with `≈` for the approximation.

## Supports, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** post the four N-RN.3 cases as a
  small table; the phrase to rehearse is "the computation decides
  it" for irrational × irrational.
- **Alternate response mode:** a learner may classify by writing
  the rule's name ("rational + irrational") beside each item
  before writing "irrational."
- **Extension:** classify `(√2 + 1)(√2 − 1)` (answer: `1`,
  rational — a difference of squares hiding in the
  classification rules), and explain why this does not contradict
  the rule that a nonzero rational times an irrational is
  irrational.
- **Teacher note — misconception:** learners overgeneralize to
  "irrational × irrational = irrational" and write `=` for
  approximations. Both errors are hunted in Practice F.
