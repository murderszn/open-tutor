# Lesson 05 — Special Products and Structure

**Goal:** I can recognize difference-of-squares and perfect-square
patterns, use them to multiply and compute without expanding term
by term, and explain why `(x + a)²` is not `x² + a²`.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook; Lessons 03–04
area-model habits (every term meets every term).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Expand `(x + 3)(x − 3)` and `(x + 3)²` the long way. If you got
`x² − 9` and `x² + 6x + 9`, you are ready: the first product lost
its middle terms, and the second kept a middle term that a careless
answer drops. Today's question is why those two patterns always
behave that way — and how to spot them before you start.

## Teaching: structure is a shortcut you can prove (15 min)

Every special product below is just the distributive property,
done once in general, so you never have to redo it case by case.

**Difference of squares.** For any expressions `a` and `b`:

`(a + b)(a − b) = a² − ab + ab − b² = a² − b²`.

The two middle terms are opposites, so they cancel. The product
has only two terms — a square minus a square. That is why it is
called a difference of *squares*, and why spotting the pattern
matters: `(x + 5)(x − 5)` is `x² − 25` at a glance, and
`(2x + 3)(2x − 3)` is `4x² − 9` at a glance (note: `a = 2x`, so
`a² = 4x²`, not `2x²`).

**Square of a sum.** `(a + b)² = (a + b)(a + b) = a² + ab + ab +
b² = a² + 2ab + b²`. The middle term is *twice the product* of the
two terms. **Square of a difference.**
`(a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b²` — same middle term, with a minus sign.

**The error this lesson exists to kill:** `(x + 3)² = x² + 9`.
That answer squares each term and forgets the two cross terms
(`3x + 3x = 6x`). Squaring a sum is not squaring term by term; it
is multiplying the sum by itself, and the cross terms are real
products. The area model from Lesson 04 shows them: a square of
side `(x + 3)` splits into regions `x²`, `3x`, `3x`, and `9`.

**Mental computation.** The same patterns compute numbers:
`103 · 97 = (100 + 3)(100 − 3) = 100² − 3² = 10,000 − 9 = 9,991`,
and `47² = (50 − 3)² = 2,500 − 300 + 9 = 2,209`. Structure turns a
two-digit multiplication into a square and a subtraction.

### Worked example 1 — difference of squares, with a coefficient inside

> Multiply `(3x + 4)(3x − 4)` and `(x² + 2)(x² − 2)`.

1. First product: this is `(a + b)(a − b)` with `a = 3x`,
   `b = 4`. So the product is `a² − b² = (3x)² − 4² = 9x² − 16`.
   Check by expanding: `9x² − 12x + 12x − 16 = 9x² − 16` ✓.
2. Second product: `a = x²`, `b = 2`. Product: `(x²)² − 2² =
   x⁴ − 4`. Check at `x = 2`: `(6)(2) = 12` and `16 − 4 = 12` ✓.
3. What would *not* fit the pattern: `(x + 4)(x − 3)` is not a
   difference of squares, because the two constants differ. It
   expands to `x² + x − 12`, with a middle term that survives.

### Worked example 2 — binomial squares, and the missing-middle error

> Expand `(2x − 5)²`, and correct the claim `(x + 7)² = x² + 49`.

1. `(2x − 5)² = (2x)² − 2(2x)(5) + 5² = 4x² − 20x + 25`.
   Check at `x = 3`: `(1)² = 1` and `36 − 60 + 25 = 1` ✓.
2. The claim `(x + 7)² = x² + 49` drops the middle term.
   Correct form: `x² + 2(x)(7) + 49 = x² + 14x + 49`.
   Check at `x = 1`: the claim gives `50`; the correct value is
   `(8)² = 64`, and `1 + 14 + 49 = 64` ✓. The number `14` is the
   missing evidence: a wrong answer can be caught by one numeric
   check, which is why Unit 03's checking habit returns here.

## Guided practice (10 min, with hints)

1. Multiply `(x + 9)(x − 9)` and `(5x + 1)(5x − 1)`. *Hint: name a and b first.*
2. Expand `(x + 6)²` and `(3x − 2)²`. *Hint: middle term = 2ab, with the sign of the difference pattern.*
3. Compute `99 · 101` using a difference of squares. *Hint: 99 = 100 − 1, 101 = 100 + 1.*
4. Find and fix the error: " `(2x + 3)² = 4x² + 9`." *Hint: which term is missing, and what is it?*

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Multiply `(x + 8)(x − 8)`, `(4x + 5)(4x − 5)`, and
   `(x³ + 3)(x³ − 3)`.
2. Expand `(x − 4)²`, `(2x + 7)²`, and `(5x − 1)²`.
3. Compute `52²` using `(50 + 2)²`, and `98 · 102` using a
   difference of squares. Show the pattern step, not just the
   answer.
4. Decide whether each product fits a special-product pattern, and
   expand it: (a) `(x + 2)(x + 5)`; (b) `(3x − 1)(3x + 1)`;
   (c) `(x − 6)²`. For the one that does not fit, say why.
5. In two or three sentences, explain why `(a + b)²` has three
   terms while `(a + b)(a − b)` has two, using the middle terms.

## Application (5 min)

A square garden plot has side length `(x + 4)` feet. A second,
rectangular plot has length `(x + 4)` feet and width `(x − 4)`
feet. Write both areas as polynomials. For what value of `x` is
the rectangular area zero, and what does that mean for the plot?
(These are fictional practice plots; no real garden is described.)

## Exit check (5 min)

Expand `(3x − 4)²` and multiply `(2x + 9)(2x − 9)`. Then compute
`203 · 197` with a difference-of-squares pattern.

## Supports, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** post the three patterns with
  `a` and `b` labeled in a worked example; read `a² − b²` as "a
  squared minus b squared."
- **Alternate response mode:** a learner may verify a pattern
  answer by expanding the long way for one item per set, then use
  the pattern for the rest.
- **Extension:** expand `(x + y + 2)²` by treating `(x + y)` as
  `a` and `2` as `b` (answer: `x² + 2xy + y² + 4x + 4y + 4`), and
  compute `1003 · 997` (answer: `999,991`) by a difference of
  squares.
- **Teacher note — misconception:** the two durable errors are
  `(a + b)² = a² + b²` and squaring only the variable in `(2x)²`
  to get `2x²`. Require the `a = …, b = …` line before the
  pattern is applied. **Answer key:** `answer-key.md`, Lesson 05.
