# Lesson 04 — Modeling with Systems: Break-Even and Mixtures

**Goal:** I can build a system of two equations from a context, solve
it, and interpret the answer in the context's own terms.
**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook.

## Readiness check (5 min)

A shop sells adult tickets for $12 and child tickets for $7. If it
sold 10 tickets in total, what are two different ways the 10 could
split between adults and children? If you listed pairs like
`(10, 0)`, `(5, 5)`, or `(0, 10)`, you are ready: one equation
(`a + c = 10`) has many solutions, and today's work is adding the
second fact that pins down exactly one split.

## Teaching: name the quantities first, then write two facts (15 min)

Modeling with a system follows the same steps every time:

1. **Define the variables with units.** "Let `a` be the number of
   adult tickets" — not just "let `a` be adults."
2. **Write one equation per fact.** A total count gives one equation;
   a total amount of money gives another. Two facts, two equations.
3. **Solve by the method that fits.** Substitution fits when one
   equation is already solved or easy to solve for a variable;
   elimination fits when coefficients line up. Either is correct.
4. **Interpret and check in context.** A bare pair like `(106, 94)`
   is not an answer until you say what 106 and 94 count, and check
   that the pair makes sense: whole numbers for tickets, nonnegative
   amounts for money, and both original facts satisfied.

The intersection of the two lines is the **break-even point** when the
context compares two costs: the input value where the two costs are
equal. On one side of it, one option is cheaper; on the other side,
the other is. A fractional break-even (like 66⅔ minutes) is fine as
mathematics — but when the context counts whole minutes, compare the
whole numbers on either side of it, as in Worked example 2.

### Worked example 1 — two totals pin down one split

> A theater sells adult tickets for $12 and child tickets for $7.
> One evening it sells 200 tickets for $1,930 in total. How many of
> each kind were sold? (Fictional practice data.)

1. Define: let `a` = number of adult tickets, `c` = number of child
   tickets. Equations: `a + c = 200` (total tickets) and
   `12a + 7c = 1930` (total dollars).
2. Solve by substitution: from the first equation, `c = 200 − a`.
   Substitute: `12a + 7(200 − a) = 1930`, so `12a + 1400 − 7a = 1930`,
   `5a = 530`, `a = 106`. Then `c = 200 − 106 = 94`.
3. Check both facts: `106 + 94 = 200` ✓;
   `12(106) + 7(94) = 1272 + 658 = 1930` ✓.
4. Interpret: **106 adult tickets and 94 child tickets.** Both numbers
   are whole and nonnegative, as ticket counts must be.

### Worked example 2 — break-even between two plans

> Plan A costs $30 per month plus $0.20 per minute. Plan B costs $10
> per month plus $0.50 per minute. At how many minutes do the plans
> cost the same, and which plan is cheaper on each side? (Fictional
> practice data.)

1. Define: let `m` = minutes used in the month; costs are
   `A = 30 + 0.20m` and `B = 10 + 0.50m` dollars.
2. Set equal: `30 + 0.20m = 10 + 0.50m`, so `20 = 0.30m` and
   `m = 66⅔` minutes. The common cost is
   `30 + 0.20(66⅔) = $43.33` (and `10 + 0.50(66⅔) = $43.33` ✓).
3. Interpret in whole minutes: at 66 minutes, A costs $43.20 and B
   costs $43.00 — B is cheaper. At 67 minutes, A costs $43.40 and B
   costs $43.50 — A is cheaper. So: **below about 67 minutes, choose
   B; above it, choose A.** The exact break-even is 66⅔ minutes, but
   a phone plan bills whole minutes, so the practical switch happens
   between 66 and 67.

## Guided practice (10 min, with hints)

1. A shop sells pens for $3 and notebooks for $8. It sells 40 items
   for $215. How many pens and notebooks? (Fictional practice data.)
   *Hint: let `p` = pens, `n` = notebooks; write the count equation
   first.*
2. Plan P costs $25 plus $0.10 per unit; plan Q costs $0.30 per unit
   with no base fee. At how many units do they cost the same, and
   what is that cost? *Hint: set the two cost expressions equal.*

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. A fair sells adult tickets for $10 and child tickets for $6. One
   day it admits 320 visitors and collects $2,640. How many adult and
   child tickets? (Fictional practice data.)
2. Gym A charges $40 per month with no signup fee. Gym B charges $25
   per month plus a $120 signup fee (counted in the first month's
   total). After how many months do the total costs first become
   equal, and what is that total? (Fictional practice data.)
3. How many liters of a 10% salt solution and a 30% salt solution
   must be mixed to make 20 liters of a 24% salt solution? Let `x`
   and `y` be the liters of each; write the volume equation and the
   salt equation, then solve. (Fictional practice data.)
4. In two or three sentences, explain why the intersection of two cost
   lines answers the question "when do the two options cost the
   same?" rather than "which option is cheapest?"

## Application (5 min)

A snack stand sells lemonade for $2 a cup and cookies for $3 a bag.
One afternoon it sells 90 items for $210. How many cups and bags?
(Fictional practice data.) State the answer as a sentence, not a bare
pair.

## Exit check (5 min)

A theater sells adult tickets for $8 and student tickets for $5. It
sells 50 tickets for $340. How many of each? (Fictional practice
data.) Show the system, the solution, and both checks.

## Supports, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** require the "let" sentence with units
  before any equation is written; a system without defined variables
  earns no credit yet, even if the numbers are right.
- **Alternate response mode:** a learner may build the system as a
  two-column table (fact → equation) instead of prose, then solve
  from the table.
- **Extension:** redo Worked example 1 with adult tickets at $13
  instead of $12 (same totals: 200 tickets, $1,930). Show that the
  system gives `a = 88⅓`, which is not a whole number of tickets,
  and explain why that result proves the changed data cannot describe
  a real evening of whole-ticket sales.
- **Teacher note — misconception:** learners often write only one
  equation (usually the money one) and then stall. When that happens,
  ask: "what is the second fact the problem gave you?" — the count
  fact is almost always the missing equation. **Answer key:**
  `answer-key.md`, Lesson 04.
