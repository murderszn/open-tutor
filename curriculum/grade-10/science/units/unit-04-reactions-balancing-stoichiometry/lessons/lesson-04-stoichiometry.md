# Lesson 04 — Mole Ratios and Stoichiometry

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can use the coefficients of a balanced equation as a
mole ratio to compute how much product forms from a given reactant mass, and vice versa.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, Practice Set D, the 4-step setup card below.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Balance: N₂ + H₂ → NH₃. (Answer: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.)
2. If a recipe makes 2 cakes from 6 eggs, how many cakes do 12 eggs make? What is the
   eggs-to-cakes ratio, and why does it stay the same no matter how many cakes you bake?

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read Lesson 02: coefficients are relative amounts. The
recipe ratio is the whole idea of this lesson.)

## Teaching

### Coefficients are mole ratios

In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the coefficients say: 2 moles of H₂ react with 1 mole of O₂ to make 2 moles
of H₂O. They do **not** say 2 grams react with 1 gram — grams are not in the equation at all.
The coefficients are a ratio of *amounts in moles*, and that ratio never changes: double all the
amounts, halve them, use any scale — the ratio 2 : 1 : 2 stays.

That is why stoichiometry always goes through moles. Mass cannot be plugged into a mole ratio
directly; it must be converted to moles first, and the answer converted back to mass at the end.

### The 4-step setup (use it for every problem)

1. **Balance** the equation (if it is not already balanced).
2. **Convert** the given quantity to moles.
3. **Apply the mole ratio** from the coefficients: multiply by (wanted coefficient ÷ given
   coefficient).
4. **Convert** the answer to the wanted unit (usually grams).

Write the units at every step. A unit that does not cancel is a setup error, not an arithmetic
error — fix the setup, not the number.

### Worked example 1 — Mass of reactant to mass of product

*What mass of water forms from 4.0 g of hydrogen gas burning in excess oxygen?*

Balanced equation: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. (Oxygen is in excess, so hydrogen decides the amount.)

Step 1: balanced — 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
Step 2: moles of H₂ = 4.0 g ÷ 2.0 g/mol = 2.0 mol. (Molar mass H₂ = 2 × 1.0 = 2.0 g/mol.)
Step 3: mole ratio H₂O : H₂ = 2 : 2 = 1 : 1. Moles of H₂O = 2.0 mol × (2 ÷ 2) = 2.0 mol.
Step 4: mass of H₂O = 2.0 mol × 18.0 g/mol = **36 g**.
Sanity check: hydrogen is very light, so 4.0 g is a lot of moles (2.0), and each mole of H₂
makes a mole of much heavier water — 36 g is plausible. If you had answered 4.0 g, you would
have skipped the mole step and treated grams as if they were moles: the classic error.

### Worked example 2 — Mass to mass with a different ratio

*What mass of ammonia forms from 14.0 g of nitrogen gas reacting with excess hydrogen?*

Balanced equation: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.

Step 1: balanced — N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.
Step 2: moles of N₂ = 14.0 g ÷ 28.0 g/mol = 0.500 mol. (N₂ = 2 × 14.0 = 28.0 g/mol.)
Step 3: mole ratio NH₃ : N₂ = 2 : 1. Moles of NH₃ = 0.500 mol × (2 ÷ 1) = 1.00 mol.
Step 4: mass of NH₃ = 1.00 mol × 17.0 g/mol = **17.0 g**.
Check by conservation: reactants were 14.0 g N₂ plus the hydrogen consumed (1.50 mol × 2.0
g/mol = 3.0 g), total 17.0 g in, 17.0 g out. Mass is conserved — HS-PS1-7 in action. Notice the
check uses the *consumed* hydrogen, not all hydrogen present: excess hydrogen is not a reactant
amount in the balanced equation's ratio.

### Worked example 3 — Explaining an outcome (HS-PS1-2)

*Sodium and chlorine react to form sodium chloride. Why does the product have properties unlike
either reactant, and what does the balanced equation 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl tell you about the
amounts?*

Sodium is a soft reactive metal; chlorine is a toxic green gas; sodium chloride is a stable
white solid you eat. The product's properties are new because the atoms are rearranged: sodium's
single valence electron transfers to chlorine (Unit 03's electron story), forming Na⁺ and Cl⁻
ions in a lattice. The equation says the ratio is 2 mol Na : 1 mol Cl₂ : 2 mol NaCl — so 2.00 mol
of sodium (46.0 g) reacting completely needs exactly 1.00 mol of chlorine gas (71.0 g) and makes
2.00 mol of salt (117 g). Mass check: 46.0 + 71.0 = 117.0. Conserved.

## Guided practice

1. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. What mass of MgO forms from 12.2 g of Mg? (Mg 24.3, MgO 40.3.)
2. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. What mass of CO₂ forms from 8.0 g of CH₄? (CH₄ 16.0, CO₂ 44.0.)

## Independent practice

Do Practice Set D. Every problem must show all four steps with units; a bare answer earns no
marks even if correct, because the setup is the skill being taught.

## Application

A car exhaust claim says "burning 1 kg of petrol produces about 3 kg of CO₂." Use the methane
equation (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O) to explain why the product can weigh *more* than the fuel:
the oxygen comes from the air and adds mass. Compute: 16.0 g of CH₄ makes 44.0 g of CO₂ — a ratio
of 2.75 : 1. The claim's 3 : 1 is the same idea for a heavier fuel. (This is a reasoning task,
not a trick: mass is conserved once you count the air.)

## Exit check

1. C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O. What mass of CO₂ forms from 8.8 g of propane, C₃H₈? (C₃H₈ 44.0.)
2. In one sentence: why must step 2 (convert to moles) come before step 3 (mole ratio)?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** the 4-step setup is printed at the top of Practice Set D. Cover the answer, do
  one step, then uncover only that step to check — stepwise checking beats redoing whole problems.
- **Extension:** a reaction makes 0.250 mol of product from 0.750 mol of reactant A. What is the
  mole ratio of A to product in the balanced equation? (3 : 1 — and the equation must have a
  coefficient 3 on A for every 1 on the product.) Then: if the reactant's molar mass is 40.0
  g/mol, what mass of A was consumed? (0.750 × 40.0 = 30.0 g.)
- **Alternate response mode:** you may narrate your four steps aloud while the adult records them.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: plugging grams straight into the mole ratio; using the coefficient as a
mass; forgetting that diatomic elements (H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂) have molar masses twice the atomic
mass; balancing the equation *after* starting the calculation. Insist on the order: balance,
convert, ratio, convert. Answer-key reference: `../answer-key.md`, Lesson 04.
