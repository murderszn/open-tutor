# Lesson 05 — Limiting Reactants and Percent Yield

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can identify which reactant limits a reaction, compute
the theoretical yield and the leftover excess, and calculate percent yield from an actual yield.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, Practice Set E, the project prediction sheet.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. A sandwich needs 2 slices of bread and 1 slice of cheese. You have 10 slices of bread and 3
   slices of cheese. How many sandwiches can you make, and what runs out first?
2. From Lesson 04: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. If you have 4.0 mol of H₂ and 4.0 mol of O₂, which reactant
   decides how much water forms? (Answer: H₂ — it needs only 2.0 mol of O₂, so oxygen is in
   excess and hydrogen limits.)

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read Lesson 04: the mole ratio is a recipe. The ingredient
that runs out first ends the reaction, no matter how much of the others remain.)

## Teaching

### The limiting reactant

Most real reactions do not start with perfectly matched amounts. The **limiting reactant** is
the one that is used up first; it decides how much product can form. The other reactants are in
**excess**: some is left over when the reaction stops.

How to find it (never guess from masses — compare moles through the ratio):

1. Convert every given reactant mass to moles.
2. For each reactant, divide its moles by its coefficient. The smallest result is the limiting
   reactant. (This works because dividing by the coefficient asks: "how many reaction's-worth
   of this reactant do I have?")
3. Use the limiting reactant's moles and the mole ratio to find the product amount. Then, if
   asked, find the leftover: excess moles started minus excess moles consumed.

### Theoretical yield, actual yield, percent yield

- **Theoretical yield:** the mass of product the stoichiometry predicts — the maximum possible.
- **Actual yield:** the mass actually obtained in the real run.
- **Percent yield** = (actual yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100%.

Percent yield is almost always below 100%: product sticks to glassware, reactions do not finish,
side reactions happen, measurements lose material. A percent yield above 100% means something is
wrong — usually wet or impure product, or a weighing error — not a better-than-perfect reaction.

### Worked example 1 — Finding the limiting reactant

*10.0 g of hydrogen gas reacts with 64.0 g of oxygen gas: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Which reactant
limits, what mass of water forms, and what mass of the excess reactant remains?*

Step 1 — moles: H₂ = 10.0 ÷ 2.0 = 5.00 mol. O₂ = 64.0 ÷ 32.0 = 2.00 mol.
Step 2 — divide by coefficients: H₂: 5.00 ÷ 2 = 2.50. O₂: 2.00 ÷ 1 = 2.00. Smaller is O₂, so
**oxygen limits**.
Step 3 — product from the limiter: mole ratio H₂O : O₂ = 2 : 1. Moles of H₂O = 2.00 × 2 = 4.00
mol. Mass = 4.00 × 18.0 = **72.0 g**.
Step 4 — leftover: the ratio H₂ : O₂ is 2 : 1, so 2.00 mol of O₂ consumes 2.00 × 2 = 4.00 mol
of H₂. Started with 5.00 mol, so leftover H₂ = 5.00 − 4.00 = 1.00 mol = 1.00 × 2.0 = **2.0 g**.
Check: mass in = 10.0 + 64.0 = 74.0 g; mass out = 72.0 g water + 2.0 g leftover hydrogen =
74.0 g. Conserved. If your check fails, your limiting reactant is probably wrong.

A common error is picking the reactant with the smaller *mass* (10.0 g of hydrogen "looks"
smaller than 64.0 g of oxygen). Masses do not decide — moles through the ratio decide. Here the
smaller mass is actually the excess reactant.

### Worked example 2 — Percent yield

*From Lesson 04's guided problem, 12.2 g of magnesium should theoretically make 20.2 g of
magnesium oxide. A student actually collects 17.4 g. What is the percent yield, and what are two
plausible reasons it is below 100%?*

Percent yield = (17.4 ÷ 20.2) × 100% = **86.1%**.
Plausible reasons: some MgO stuck to the container and was not weighed; the magnesium did not
react completely (unreacted metal mixed in would actually raise the mass — so a careful student
names *loss* reasons: product left on glassware, or magnesium oxide powder lost during transfer).
A yield above 100% would point to impure or wet product, not to success.

## Application

The project (next session) reacts 4.2 g of baking soda with 100 mL of vinegar (assumed 5%
acetic acid, density ~1.0 g/mL). Work out now, before the bench session: which reactant limits,
and what mass of CO₂ should escape? (Balanced equation: NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O +
CO₂. Molar masses: NaHCO₃ 84.0, CH₃COOH 60.0, CO₂ 44.0.) The adult checks this prediction
before any mixing happens.

## Exit check

1. In one sentence: why can the actual yield never legitimately exceed the theoretical yield?
2. 2Al + 3CuSO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3Cu. You have 2.0 mol Al and 2.0 mol CuSO₄. Which limits?
   (Divide by coefficients: Al 2.0 ÷ 2 = 1.0; CuSO₄ 2.0 ÷ 3 = 0.67 — CuSO₄ limits.)

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** the sandwich analogy again: bread 10 slices ÷ 2 per sandwich = 5 sandwiches'
  worth; cheese 3 ÷ 1 = 3 sandwiches' worth. Cheese limits. The chemistry method is identical.
- **Extension:** a student mixes 5.0 g of baking soda with 50 mL of vinegar (5%). Show that the
  vinegar now limits (acetic acid: 50 g × 5% = 2.5 g = 0.0417 mol; baking soda: 5.0 ÷ 84.0 =
  0.0595 mol — vinegar is fewer reaction's-worth) and compute the CO₂ mass: 0.0417 × 44.0 =
  1.83 g, reported as 1.8 g. Explain why the project's 100 mL was chosen instead (to make baking
  soda the limiter, so the measured mass tracks the weighed solid).
- **Alternate response mode:** you may present the divide-by-coefficient step as a two-column
  table instead of sentences.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: choosing the limiter by smallest mass; forgetting to divide by the
coefficient; reporting percent yield without the ×100%; treating a >100% yield as a good result.

prediction here becomes a wrong project conclusion. Answer-key reference: `../answer-key.md`,
