# Lesson 02 — Writing and Balancing Chemical Equations

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can write a skeleton equation from a word description
and balance it by inspection using atom inventories, without changing any subscript.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, periodic table, Practice Set B.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Write the formula for calcium chloride (Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻). Why is it CaCl₂ and not CaCl?
2. In 2H₂O, how many hydrogen atoms and how many oxygen atoms are represented in total? (Answer:
   2 × 2 = 4 hydrogen atoms, 2 × 1 = 2 oxygen atoms. The coefficient multiplies everything in
   the formula.)

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read Unit 03, Lesson 02: subscripts belong to the formula;
coefficients belong to the equation.)

## Teaching

### Why equations must balance

A chemical equation is a bookkeeping statement. Atoms are not created or destroyed in a chemical
reaction (HS-PS1-7), so the number of atoms of each element on the left must equal the number on
the right. A **skeleton equation** has the right formulas in the right order but the wrong
counts. **Balancing** means choosing coefficients — the numbers in front of formulas — so that
every element's count matches on both sides.

Two rules that are never broken:

1. **Never change a subscript.** Changing H₂O to H₃O changes the substance. Only coefficients
   may change.
2. **A coefficient of 1 is not written.** If a formula has no visible coefficient, it means 1.

### The atom-inventory method

1. Write the skeleton equation with correct formulas (use Unit 03 skills for ionic compounds).
2. Make an inventory table: each element, its count on the left, its count on the right.
3. Balance one element at a time. Save hydrogen and oxygen for last — they often appear in
   several formulas. If an element appears in only one formula on each side, start there.
4. After each change, update the whole inventory. Never "fix" one element by breaking another
   without re-checking.
5. When every count matches, check that the coefficients have no common factor (2, 2, 2 can
   usually be reduced to 1, 1, 1 — but only if the equation still balances, which it will).
6. If you get stuck, you may balance with a fraction (½O₂) and then multiply the whole equation
   to clear it. Fractions are a tool, never the final answer.

### Worked example 1 — Hydrogen and oxygen

Skeleton: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.

| Element | Left | Right |
|---|---|---|
| H | 2 | 2 |
| O | 2 | 1 |

Oxygen is short on the right. Put a 2 in front of H₂O: H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. New inventory: H 2 vs 4,
O 2 vs 2. Now hydrogen is short on the left. Put a 2 in front of H₂: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Final
inventory: H 4 vs 4, O 2 vs 2. **Balanced: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.** Note that the 2 in front of H₂O
changed both H and O — that is why you update the whole inventory after every step.

### Worked example 2 — Methane combustion

Skeleton: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.

| Element | Left | Right |
|---|---|---|
| C | 1 | 1 |
| H | 4 | 2 |
| O | 2 | 3 (2 in CO₂ + 1 in H₂O) |

Carbon matches. Hydrogen: put 2 in front of H₂O → CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Now H 4 vs 4, O 2 vs
4. Oxygen: put 2 in front of O₂ → CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Final: C 1 vs 1, H 4 vs 4, O 4 vs 4.
**Balanced: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.** This is the equation Lessons 04–05 use for fuel problems.

### Worked example 3 — A harder one, with a polyatomic group kept intact

Skeleton: Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃ (rust formation, simplified).

Inventory: Fe 1 vs 2, O 2 vs 3. Balance iron first (it appears once per side): 2Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃
gives Fe 2 vs 2, O 2 vs 3. Now oxygen: the smallest whole-number fix is 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃, but that
changes iron again. Work it through: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃. Final: Fe 4 vs 4, O 6 vs 6.
**Balanced: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃.** The trick: when an element's counts are 2 and 3, their least
common multiple is 6 — balance to 6 of that element on both sides, then fix the other element.

## Guided practice

Balance each with a full inventory table. Do not skip the table — it is the method, not decoration.

1. N₂ + H₂ → NH₃
2. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (check: is this already balanced?)
3. Zn + HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

## Independent practice

Do Practice Set B. For each equation, write the skeleton, the inventory before and after, and
the balanced equation. A balanced equation without an inventory earns half marks — the inventory
is where the thinking shows.

## Application

A student balances H₂ + O₂ → H₂O by writing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂ and says "now oxygen matches and
hydrogen matches." Explain in two sentences why this is wrong even though the counts match.
(Hint: what substance is H₂O₂, and is it the product of burning hydrogen?)

## Exit check

1. Balance: KClO₃ → KCl + O₂. Show the inventory.
2. True or false: "Balancing an equation changes how much of each substance reacts, but never
   which substances they are." Defend your answer in one sentence.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** use coins or beads as atoms. Lay out the reactant side, then physically regroup
  them into products — the number of coins never changes, which is exactly what balancing says.
- **Extension:** balance C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O, then Al + CuSO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + Cu, keeping the
  sulfate group SO₄ intact as a unit wherever it appears on both sides. (Answers in the key.)
- **Alternate response mode:** you may dictate your inventory tables aloud.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: changing subscripts to balance (correct every time — it changes the
substance); balancing only the first element and stopping; treating a coefficient of 1 as if it
must be written; "balancing" by adding a new reactant or product. Watch for students who balance
H₂ + O₂ → H₂O as H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂ — hydrogen peroxide is a different, real substance, and this
error is the single best diagnostic in the lesson. Answer-key reference: `../answer-key.md`,
Lesson 02.
