# Lesson 03 — The Mole: Counting by Weighing

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can explain what a mole is, convert among mass, moles,
and particle counts, and compute a molar mass from the unit's rounded table.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, the mole-roadmap diagram
(`../assets/mole-roadmap.svg`), Practice Set C.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. A dozen means 12 of anything. If a baker sells eggs by the dozen, how many eggs are in 3
   dozen? How many in half a dozen?
2. From Unit 02: carbon's atomic mass is about 12.0 and hydrogen's is about 1.0. Which weighs
   more: one carbon atom or twelve hydrogen atoms?

(If the readiness check is shaky, remember: atomic mass is a *relative* scale. Carbon-12 is
defined as 12, and everything else is measured against it.)

## Teaching

### Why chemists count in moles

Atoms are too small to count one by one. A single gram of water contains about
3.34 × 10²² molecules — a number no one can count. So chemists count the way bakers do: by a
fixed bundle. A baker's bundle is a dozen (12). A chemist's bundle is a **mole**:
6.02 × 10²³ particles. That number is **Avogadro's number**, and it is chosen so that the bundle
size and the mass scale line up exactly: one mole of carbon-12 atoms weighs exactly 12 grams.

So a mole is not a mass and not a number of grams — it is a *count*, like "dozen". One mole of
anything contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles of it: atoms, molecules, or formula units.

### Molar mass: the bridge from count to mass

The **molar mass** of a substance is the mass of one mole of it, in grams per mole (g/mol). It is
read straight off the periodic table: an element's atomic mass number *is* its molar mass in
g/mol. For a compound, add the molar masses of its atoms.

This unit uses rounded molar masses (g/mol): H 1.0, C 12.0, N 14.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Mg 24.3,
Al 27.0, S 32.1, Cl 35.5, K 39.1, Ca 40.1, Fe 55.8, Cu 63.5, Zn 65.4. So:

- H₂O: 2(1.0) + 16.0 = **18.0 g/mol**
- CO₂: 12.0 + 2(16.0) = **44.0 g/mol**
- NaCl: 23.0 + 35.5 = **58.5 g/mol**
- NaHCO₃: 23.0 + 1.0 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = **84.0 g/mol**

### The three conversions (use the roadmap diagram)

1. **Mass → moles:** moles = mass ÷ molar mass.
2. **Moles → mass:** mass = moles × molar mass.
3. **Moles ↔ particles:** particles = moles × 6.02 × 10²³; moles = particles ÷ 6.02 × 10²³.

Keep the unit at every step. If the unit does not cancel to what you want, the setup is wrong —
that check catches most errors before they reach the answer.

### Worked example 1 — Mass to particles

*How many atoms are in 24.0 g of carbon?*

Step 1: moles = 24.0 g ÷ 12.0 g/mol = 2.00 mol.
Step 2: atoms = 2.00 mol × 6.02 × 10²³ atoms/mol = 1.20 × 10²⁴ atoms.
Check: 24.0 g is twice 12.0 g, so the answer should be twice Avogadro's number — 1.20 × 10²⁴.
It is. The unit check: g ÷ (g/mol) = mol; mol × (atoms/mol) = atoms. Both cancel correctly.

### Worked example 2 — Moles to mass

*What is the mass of 2.50 mol of sodium chloride?*

Molar mass NaCl = 58.5 g/mol. Mass = 2.50 mol × 58.5 g/mol = 146.25 g, reported as **146 g**
(three significant figures, matching 2.50). Check by size: 2.50 mol is two and a half times one
mole (58.5 g), and 2.5 × 58.5 = 146.25. Reasonable.

### Worked example 3 — Particles to mass (two steps)

*What is the mass of 3.01 × 10²³ molecules of carbon dioxide?*

Step 1: moles = 3.01 × 10²³ ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 0.500 mol. (3.01 is half of 6.02, so half a mole —
the arithmetic agrees with the estimate.)
Step 2: mass = 0.500 mol × 44.0 g/mol = **22.0 g**.
A common error is to multiply particles by molar mass directly, skipping the mole step. The
units expose it: particles × g/mol does not cancel to grams.

## Guided practice

1. How many moles are in 36.0 g of water? (Molar mass 18.0 g/mol.)
2. How many molecules are in 0.250 mol of ammonia, NH₃?
3. What is the mass of 1.50 mol of magnesium oxide, MgO? (Mg 24.3, O 16.0.)

## Independent practice

Do Practice Set C. Use the mole-roadmap diagram for every item until the three conversions are
automatic; then try the last two items without it.

## Application

A jeweller says a ring contains "one mole of gold atoms." A friend says that means the ring
weighs 6.02 × 10²³ grams. Explain the friend's error in two sentences, and state roughly what
the ring actually weighs. (Hint: gold's molar mass is about 197 g/mol — one mole of gold weighs
197 g. The friend confused the *count* with the *mass*.)

## Exit check

1. How many moles are in 9.0 g of water? Give the particle count too.
2. Which contains more atoms: 12.0 g of carbon or 12.0 g of magnesium? Explain without a
   calculator (hint: which atom is heavier, and what does that mean for how many fit in 12 g?).

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** the dozen analogy is not a simplification to outgrow — use it every time:
  "moles are to chemists what dozens are to bakers." Write "1 mol = 6.02 × 10²³ particles" at
  the top of every practice page.
- **Extension:** how many *oxygen atoms* are in 0.500 mol of CO₂? (Two per molecule, so
  0.500 × 2 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 6.02 × 10²³ oxygen atoms.) Then: what mass of CO₂ contains exactly
  1.00 mol of oxygen atoms? (0.500 mol of CO₂, so 22.0 g.)
- **Alternate response mode:** you may explain the dozen analogy orally instead of writing it.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: "a mole is a mass" (it is a count); "molar mass changes with amount"
(it is per mole, always); multiplying particles by molar mass directly; using atomic mass of one
element for a compound instead of summing. The exit-check question 2 is the key diagnostic: the
lighter atom (carbon) gives more atoms per gram. Answer-key reference: `../answer-key.md`,
Lesson 03.
