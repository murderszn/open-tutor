# Teacher Guide — Unit 04 (Adult Only)

## How to use this unit

Teach the six lessons in order; Lessons 01–02 build the tools (patterns, balancing), Lessons
03–05 build the quantitative chain (mole, ratio, limiter), and Lesson 06 is the checkpoint that
must not be skipped: the unit assessment tests error analysis and conservation checks directly,
and learners who only memorised procedures fail those items. Give feedback on every written
product before the next session begins, as the pacing plan assumes. Check the project prediction
(Session 14) before any bench work happens — a wrong prediction becomes a wrong conclusion.

## Safety responsibilities

- You check the vinegar label (5% acetic acid) before the project. If it differs, redo the
  prediction with the learner and record the change; do not let the learner discover the
  discrepancy from a mismatched yield.
- Goggles on before pouring, always. The reaction stays in an open beaker; never seal it.
- Steel wool is observed at room temperature only in Session 2. No heating, no flame, no
  concentrated acids or bases anywhere in this unit.
- If bench work is not possible, use the simulation route in `project.md` and mark the "own
  measurement" rubric row "simulation route" — never failed, never faked.

## Per-lesson notes

### Lesson 01 — Evidence and reaction types

- Watch for: "bubbles = chemical change" (boiling also bubbles — Session 2's salt-water control
  exists for this reason); "dissolving = chemical change" (the salt is still salt); colour change
  from mixing treated as reaction evidence.
- If the image activity stalls, ask: "Is the beaker being heated?" before discussing the fizz.
  The text alternative carries the same information — never let the picture be the only route.
- Reteach: if classification is shaky, drill the five patterns as sentence frames ("two become
  one", "one becomes two", "one swaps in", "two swap partners", "burns in oxygen") before
  returning to equations.

### Lesson 02 — Balancing

- Watch for: changing subscripts (correct every time — it changes the substance); balancing
  only the first element and stopping; H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂ offered as "balanced" (hydrogen peroxide
  is a real, different substance — this is the unit's best diagnostic, and the assessment tests
  it again in item 13).
- Reteach move: cover the learner's coefficients and ask for the inventory first. If the
  inventory is right, the coefficients follow; if it is wrong, the error is in counting, not in
  balancing.
- Do not accept a balanced equation without an inventory in Sessions 3–4. The inventory is the
  method, and dropping it early is how Lesson 05 errors start.

### Lesson 03 — The mole

- Watch for: "a mole is a mass"; multiplying particles by molar mass directly; using one
  element's atomic mass for a compound; forgetting diatomic elements (H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂ have
  molar masses twice the atomic mass).
- The exit-check question 2 (12.0 g of carbon vs. 12.0 g of magnesium) is the key diagnostic:
  equal masses of different elements contain different numbers of atoms. If the learner answers
  "equal because the masses are equal," reteach with the dozen analogy before Lesson 04.
- Tolerance: learners using unrounded periodic-table masses (e.g., 12.011 for carbon) will get
  slightly different last digits. Accept masses within ±2% of the key; require exact mole ratios
  and exact balanced coefficients.

### Lesson 04 — Stoichiometry

- Watch for: plugging grams into the mole ratio; using a coefficient as a mass; balancing after
  starting the calculation; forgetting the oxygen from the air in combustion mass checks.
- Insist on the order: balance, convert, ratio, convert. The application item (petrol → CO₂
  heavier than the fuel) is worth discussing aloud even for strong learners — it is the first
  time conservation feels counter-intuitive.
- Reteach move: if step 3 fails, go back to the recipe analogy (eggs to cakes) with the
  equation's own coefficients before retrying numbers.

### Lesson 05 — Limiting reactants and percent yield

- Watch for: choosing the limiter by smallest mass (Lesson 05's worked example is designed so
  the smaller mass is the excess — use it); forgetting to divide by the coefficient; reporting
  percent yield without ×100%; treating >100% as success (it signals wet/impure product or a
  weighing error).
- Check the project prediction now, not in Session 15. The expected prediction: baking soda
  limits (0.0500 mol vs. 0.0833 mol acetic acid), predicted CO₂ 2.20 g.
- The near-tie in Practice Set E item 3 (Zn 0.199 mol vs. HCl 0.200 mol reaction's-worth) is
  deliberate: it rewards learners who actually do the divide step instead of guessing.

### Lesson 06 — Midyear checkpoint

- Flaw A: molar-mass swap (44.0 is CO₂'s mass, used for CH₄). Corrected: 22.0 g CO₂.
- Flaw B: limiter by mass plus "add everything" — note it deliberately reuses Lesson 05's
  worked-example numbers. Corrected: O₂ limits, 72.0 g H₂O, 2.0 g H₂ left.
- Flaw C: inverted percent-yield ratio. Corrected: 86.1%.
- If a learner cannot diagnose all three, Session 19 must include a second error-analysis round
  before the assessment. Do not let the assessment be the first time they meet a flawed solution.

## Readiness signals and next steps

- **Ready for Unit 05 (gases):** the learner balances reliably, converts mass ↔ moles ↔
  particles without the roadmap, and identifies limiters by the divide step. Unit 05 assumes
  all three.
- **Reteach before Unit 05:** any learner below 40/56 on the unit assessment in the two
  weakest parts (see the assessment scoring guide). Reteach the weakest part first; a second
  attempt at the project prediction is a valid reteach task.
- **Midyear review (Session 19):** cumulative U01–U04 sheet — measurement precision (U01),
  atomic mass and the mole (U02/U04), fixed subscripts (U03/U04), and conservation checks
  (U04). The learner's Session 19 reteach list (Lesson 06 exit check) drives this session.

## Project marking notes

- Accept measured mass loss within ±0.5 g of the prediction as "prediction held" for full
  marks on the conclusion, provided the error discussion is honest. Typical real results run
  1.9–2.2 g against the 2.20 g prediction: CO₂ stays partly dissolved, droplets escape with the
  fizz, and the balance reads to ±0.1 g.
- A percent yield above 100% on the project means the final weighing happened before fizzing
  stopped, or vinegar splashed out and was counted as gas loss — discuss, do not punish, and
  offer a second trial.
- Sample data for the simulation route: predicted 2.20 g, sample measured loss 2.0 g,
  percent yield 91%. These are worked-example numbers, not a real bench run, and the report
  must label them as such.
