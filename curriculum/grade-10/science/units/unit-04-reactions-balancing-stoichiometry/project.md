# Project — How Much Gas Escapes? A Baking-Soda Stoichiometry Investigation

**Type:** bench investigation with a simulation alternative. **Time:** Sessions 14 (planning),
15 (bench), 17 (analysis and report). Adult supervision is required throughout.

## Question

When baking soda reacts with vinegar, carbon dioxide gas escapes from an open beaker. Can you
predict the mass of gas that escapes, and how close does the real result come to the prediction?

## Background (read before planning)

The reaction is:

NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂

In words: sodium hydrogen carbonate (baking soda) + acetic acid (vinegar) → sodium acetate +
water + carbon dioxide. The equation is already balanced: 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. The carbon dioxide
leaves as gas, so the beaker's mass falls. The mass lost *is* (almost) the mass of CO₂ produced:
the other products stay in the beaker.

Vinegar is assumed to be 5% acetic acid by mass with density about 1.0 g/mL. The adult checks
the bottle label first; if it says a different percentage, redo the prediction with that
percentage and record the change.

## Materials

- Digital balance (precision ±0.1 g or better), a glass beaker or sturdy cup, a spoon
- Baking soda (sodium hydrogen carbonate), household vinegar (about 5% acetic acid)
- Safety goggles, paper towels, notebook
- The bench illustration in `assets/reaction-bench-illustration.png`: use it to check your
  setup before you start — every item in your setup should match something in the picture, and
  the goggles should be on before anything is poured.

## Procedure

1. **Predict (Session 14, checked by the adult before any mixing).** For 4.2 g of baking soda
   and 100 mL of vinegar: convert both reactants to moles, identify the limiting reactant with
   the divide-by-coefficient step, and compute the predicted mass of CO₂. Record the prediction
   and the full working. Do not start until the adult has checked it.
2. **Set up.** Put the empty beaker on the balance and zero (tare) it. Put on goggles.
3. **Weigh the vinegar.** Pour about 100 mL of vinegar into the beaker. Record the exact mass
   (vinegar + beaker, after taring the beaker out — i.e., the vinegar mass alone).
4. **Weigh the baking soda.** In a separate small container, weigh 4.2 g of baking soda.
   Record the exact mass you actually used, even if it is not exactly 4.2 g — and if it is not,
   redo the prediction for the mass you actually used.
5. **React.** Add the baking soda to the vinegar a little at a time (to avoid fizzing over).
   Stir gently. Wait until the fizzing stops completely (at least 5 minutes).
6. **Weigh again.** Weigh the beaker and its contents. The mass loss is:
   (mass of vinegar + mass of baking soda) − (final mass).
7. **Record.** Complete the data table below with units on every number.

## Deliverables

1. The completed data table (above).
2. The prediction working: balanced equation, mole conversions, limiting-reactant step,
   predicted CO₂ mass.
3. A percent-yield calculation with the formula shown.
4. An error discussion (at least two real error sources, each with whether it raises or lowers
   the measured mass loss, and why).
5. A conclusion in 3–5 sentences: did the prediction hold, what does the result say about
   conservation of mass, and what would you change in a second trial?

## Simulation alternative

If bench work is not possible: run the PhET "Reactants, Products and Leftovers" simulation
(see the resource pack) with the sandwich/molecule settings, and work the project using the
answer key's sample data, clearly labeled "sample data, not my measurement." The error
discussion is then written from the simulation's behaviour (for example: the simulation assumes
complete reaction, so it cannot show incomplete mixing). The "own measurement" rubric row is
marked "simulation route" rather than failed.

## Safety

Goggles on before pouring. Vinegar in eyes: rinse with running water for 15 minutes and tell the
adult. Never seal the reacting container. Do not taste anything. Rinse the beaker and pour the
mixture down the drain with running water; wash hands.

## Rubric

| Criterion | Full marks | Partial marks | No marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prediction (working + limiter) | Balanced equation, both mole conversions, divide-by-coefficient step, correct predicted mass (2.2 g for 4.2 g baking soda with excess vinegar) | One step missing or a small arithmetic slip, but the limiter is right | No working, or the limiter is wrong |
| Data table | Every row filled with units; masses to ±0.1 g | One row missing or a unit missing | Table incomplete or units absent throughout |
| Percent yield | Formula shown, correct calculation from the learner's own numbers | Formula shown but arithmetic slip, or computed from uncorrected prediction | No calculation |
| Error discussion | Two real sources, each with direction (raises/lowers) and reason | Two sources but no direction, or one source only | Generic ("human error") with no mechanism |
| Conclusion | Answers all three prompts (prediction held? conservation? second-trial change) | Two of three prompts answered | One or none answered |
| Safety and setup | Goggles worn, illustration setup check done, no sealed container | Goggles worn but setup check skipped | Unsafe practice observed |

The project is marked complete when every row reaches at least partial marks and the safety row
reaches full marks. The adult records the result and any reteach in the teacher guide notes.
