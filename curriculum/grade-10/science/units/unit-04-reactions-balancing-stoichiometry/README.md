# Unit 04 — Reactions, Balancing, and Stoichiometry

**Grade 10 Science (Chemistry pathway) · Weeks 15–18 · 20 sessions of ~50 minutes**

Unit 04 of [issue #47](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/47). Status: **validated
draft** — delivered for educator review, not merged. This unit is self-contained for one grade-10
learner; a guiding adult supervises all bench work. There is no open flame, no concentrated acid
or base, and no toxic material in this unit: the bench observations use household vinegar (about
5% acetic acid), baking soda, and steel wool with adult supervision, and the project measures a
mass change as carbon dioxide escapes an open beaker. The adult previews every linked simulation
and video before the learner opens it, and checks the vinegar label (5% acetic acid) before the
project runs.

## Essential question

If atoms are never created or destroyed in a chemical reaction, can you predict — before you mix
anything — exactly how much product you will get, and what does it mean when the beaker disagrees?

## What the learner will be able to do

1. Name evidence of chemical change and distinguish it from a physical change, and classify a
   reaction as synthesis, decomposition, single replacement, double replacement, or combustion
   from its equation pattern (HS-PS1-2).
2. Write skeleton equations from word descriptions and balance them by inspection using atom
   inventories, without ever changing a subscript (HS-PS1-7).
3. Explain the mole as a counting unit, convert fluently among mass, moles, and particle counts
   using Avogadro's number and molar mass, and state the unit's rounded molar-mass table (HS-PS1-7).
4. Use mole ratios from a balanced equation to compute reactant and product quantities in
   mass–mass and mass–particle problems (HS-PS1-7).
5. Identify the limiting reactant from given quantities, compute the theoretical yield and the
   leftover amount of the excess reactant, and calculate percent yield from an actual yield
   (HS-PS1-7).
6. Construct and revise an explanation for the outcome of a simple chemical reaction from
   outermost-electron patterns and reaction-type patterns, and use a mathematical representation
   to support the claim that atoms, and therefore mass, are conserved (HS-PS1-2, HS-PS1-7).

Objectives 1–6 above serve track objectives 2, 7, and 12 of the
[grade-10 science track](../../README.md) and the practices named in
[scope-and-sequence.md](../../scope-and-sequence.md) (using mathematics and computational
thinking, analyzing data, constructing explanations from evidence). Crosscutting concepts:
**cause and effect** (reaction patterns predict products) and **energy and matter**
(atoms and mass are conserved; the mole links the particle scale to the bench scale).

## Prerequisites

- Unit 03: formulas of ionic and covalent compounds, ion charges from table position, and the

- Unit 02: atomic structure, the periodic table, and average atomic mass; the mole builds on the

- Unit 01: SI units, significant figures, data tables with units, and the structured report
  format (question, procedure, data, calculation, error analysis, conclusion) reused in the
  project report.
- Grade-9 math: proportional reasoning, rearranging formulas, and multi-step problems with units.
- Grade-9 science: the particle model of matter and conservation of mass in physical and
  chemical changes (MS-PS1-5 background).

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS), high-school (9–12) band.
Codes and descriptions were re-verified 2026-11-03 against the NSTA NGSS topic-arrangement
mirror (see the resource pack for the checked page). Descriptions below are short paraphrases
for mapping purposes, not reproductions of the standards text. **This crosswalk is a planning
reference, not a claim of state adoption, accreditation, or certification of alignment.**

- **HS-PS1-2** — construct and revise an explanation for the outcome of a simple chemical
  reaction based on the outermost electron states of atoms, trends in the periodic table, and
  knowledge of the patterns of chemical properties. Clarification: examples of chemical
  reactions could include the reaction of sodium and chlorine, of carbon and oxygen, or of
  carbon and hydrogen. Assessment boundary (applied throughout this unit): assessment is
  limited to chemical reactions involving **main-group elements and combustion reactions**.
  (Lessons 01, 06; project discussion; unit assessment.)
- **HS-PS1-7** — use mathematical representations to support the claim that atoms, and therefore
  mass, are conserved during a chemical reaction. Clarification: emphasis is on using
  mathematical ideas to communicate the proportional relationships between masses of atoms in
  the reactants and the products, and the translation of these relationships to the macroscopic
  scale using the mole as the conversion from the atomic to the macroscopic scale. Emphasis is
  on assessing students' use of mathematical thinking and not on memorization and rote
  application of problem-solving techniques. Assessment boundary: assessment does not include
  complex chemical reactions. (Lessons 02–06; practice sets; quiz; project; unit assessment.)

Numerical values used in this unit, and their status:

- Avogadro's number: 6.02 × 10²³ particles per mole (rounded for school use; the exact SI
  definition is 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹).
- Rounded molar masses used in **every** calculation and key in this unit (g/mol): H 1.0,
  C 12.0, N 14.0, O 16.0, Na 23.0, Mg 24.3, Al 27.0, S 32.1, Cl 35.5, K 39.1, Ca 40.1,
  Fe 55.8, Cu 63.5, Zn 65.4. Compound molar masses are sums of these: H₂O 18.0, CO₂ 44.0,
  NaCl 58.5, NaHCO₃ 84.0, CH₃COOH 60.0, NH₃ 17.0, MgO 40.3, C₃H₈ 44.0. A learner who uses
  more precise periodic-table masses will get slightly different last digits; the teacher guide
  states the accepted tolerance (±2% on masses, exact on mole ratios and balanced coefficients).
- Vinegar is treated as 5% acetic acid by mass with density about 1.0 g/mL. The adult checks
  the bottle label before the project; if the label says a different percentage, the project
  calculation is redone with that percentage and the change is recorded in the report.
- The project's sample data in the answer key are **worked example numbers**, not measurements
  from a real bench run. The learner's own measured mass loss is the project's real data.

## Safety summary

- Goggles on for all bench work. Vinegar is a mild acid: if it splashes in eyes, rinse with
  running water for 15 minutes and tell the adult at once. Never taste anything in the lab.
- No heating, no flame, no concentrated acids or bases anywhere in this unit. Steel wool is
  observed at room temperature only; it is not burned.
- The project beaker stays open on the bench so escaping gas can leave freely; never seal a
  reacting container. Wash hands after handling; rinse the beaker and pour the mixture down the
  drain with running water.
- Simulation alternative: if bench work is not possible, the learner runs the PhET simulations,
  works the project with the answer key's sample masses clearly labeled as sample data, and
  writes the error discussion from the simulation's behaviour instead of a bench measurement.
  The simulation route still earns full project marks except the "own measurement" rubric row,
  which is marked "simulation route" rather than failed.

## Differentiation and extension

- **Support:** the mole-roadmap diagram (`assets/mole-roadmap.svg`) stays on the desk for every
  calculation; balancing may start with physical counters (coins or beads) before symbols; the
  4-step stoichiometry setup is printed on Practice Set D.
- **Language access:** every new term is defined in context at first use and repeated in the
  vocabulary list; word equations are given beside every symbol equation in Lessons 01–02.
- **Extension:** derive the general gas-law preview (why 44.0 g of CO₂ occupies about 22.4 L at
  STP — stated, not derived, as a teaser for Unit 05); balance a redox-style equation with
  polyatomic ions kept intact as units; design a second project trial with a different baking
  soda mass and predict its mass loss before running it.
- **Alternate response modes:** oral explanation of a balanced equation counts for the same
  marks as a written one; the project report may be dictated to the adult and then checked by
  the learner, with dictation noted on the report.
