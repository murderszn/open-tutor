# Answer Key — Unit 04 (Adult Only)

All calculations use the unit's rounded molar masses (g/mol): H 1.0, C 12.0, N 14.0, O 16.0,
Na 23.0, Mg 24.3, Al 27.0, S 32.1, Cl 35.5, K 39.1, Ca 40.1, Fe 55.8, Cu 63.5, Zn 65.4.
Avogadro's number: 6.02 × 10²³ particles/mol. Masses are accepted within ±2% of the key;
mole ratios and balanced coefficients must be exact.

## Lesson guided practice and exit checks

### Lesson 01

- Guided 1: synthesis; H₂ + O₂ → H₂O (skeleton).
- Guided 2: decomposition; heating is the condition, not the evidence — the evidence is new
  substances (CaO and CO₂) with new properties.
- Guided 3: double replacement; evidence: white precipitate of silver chloride, AgCl.
- Exit 1: any two of: gas produced, precipitate, colour change, temperature change without
  heating/cooling, light, new odour. Weak alone: bubbles (boiling), colour from mixing.
- Exit 2: synthesis (magnesium + oxygen combine into one compound, MgO).

### Lesson 02

- Guided 1: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃.
- Guided 2: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ is already balanced (Ca 1/1, C 1/1, O 3/3).
- Guided 3: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂.
- Application: H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance from water. Matching counts
  with wrong formulas does not describe the reaction that happened.
- Exit 1: 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂ (K 2/2, Cl 2/2, O 6/6).
- Exit 2: true — coefficients change amounts; subscripts (identities) never change.

### Lesson 03

- Guided 1: 36.0 ÷ 18.0 = 2.00 mol.
- Guided 2: 0.250 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.51 × 10²³ molecules.
- Guided 3: MgO 40.3 g/mol; 1.50 × 40.3 = 60.5 g (60.45 rounded).
- Application: the friend confused count with mass. One mole of gold atoms weighs about 197 g
  (gold's molar mass), not 6.02 × 10²³ g.
- Exit 1: 9.0 ÷ 18.0 = 0.500 mol; 0.500 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules.
- Exit 2: carbon — carbon atoms are lighter (12.0 vs. 24.3), so 12.0 g holds more of them:
  1.00 mol C vs. 0.494 mol Mg.

### Lesson 04

- Guided 1: 12.2 ÷ 24.3 = 0.502 mol Mg; ratio 1:1; 0.502 × 40.3 = 20.2 g MgO.
- Guided 2: 8.0 ÷ 16.0 = 0.500 mol CH₄; ratio 1:1; 0.500 × 44.0 = 22.0 g CO₂.
- Exit 1: 8.8 ÷ 44.0 = 0.200 mol C₃H₈; ratio 3:1; 0.600 mol × 44.0 = 26.4 g CO₂.
- Exit 2: the ratio is defined for moles (particle counts), not grams; grams must be converted
  first because different substances have different masses per mole.

### Lesson 05

- Guided 1: N₂ 7.0 ÷ 28.0 = 0.250 mol (÷1 = 0.250); H₂ 3.0 ÷ 2.0 = 1.50 mol (÷3 = 0.500).
  N₂ limits. NH₃ = 0.250 × 2 = 0.500 mol × 17.0 = 8.5 g.
- Guided 2: 42.5 ÷ 50.0 × 100% = 85.0%.
- Application (project prediction): NaHCO₃ 4.2 ÷ 84.0 = 0.0500 mol; vinegar 100 mL ≈ 100 g ×
  5% = 5.0 g acetic acid ÷ 60.0 = 0.0833 mol. Ratio 1:1 — baking soda limits. CO₂ = 0.0500 ×
  44.0 = 2.20 g.
- Exit 1: theoretical yield is the maximum the stoichiometry allows; actual yield cannot exceed
  it without extra mass from impurity, water, or weighing error.
- Exit 2: CuSO₄ limits (2.0 ÷ 3 = 0.67 < 2.0 ÷ 2 = 1.0).

### Lesson 06

- Flaw A: faulty step — convert (wrong molar mass: 44.0 is CO₂'s, not CH₄'s). Corrected:
  8.0 ÷ 16.0 = 0.500 mol CH₄ → 0.500 mol CO₂ × 44.0 = 22.0 g.
- Flaw B: faulty step — limiting logic (limiter chosen by mass) and the "add everything" total.
  Corrected: O₂ limits (see Lesson 05 worked example), 72.0 g H₂O, 2.0 g H₂ left.
- Flaw C: faulty step — percent-yield ratio inverted. Corrected: 17.4 ÷ 20.2 × 100% = 86.1%.
  A yield above 100% signals error, not success.
- Exit 1: mass cannot be created; the most likely error is a wrong molar mass or ratio inflating
  the product, or a second reactant's mass left uncounted.

## Practice Set E (Lesson 05)

1. H₂ 8.0 ÷ 2.0 = 4.00 mol (÷2 = 2.00); O₂ 32.0 ÷ 32.0 = 1.00 mol (÷1 = 1.00). O₂ limits.
   H₂O = 1.00 × 2 = 2.00 mol × 18.0 = 36.0 g. H₂ consumed 2.00 mol; leftover 2.00 mol × 2.0
   = 4.0 g.
2. N₂ 28.0 ÷ 28.0 = 1.00 mol (÷1 = 1.00); H₂ 12.0 ÷ 2.0 = 6.00 mol (÷3 = 2.00). N₂ limits.
   NH₃ = 1.00 × 2 = 2.00 mol × 17.0 = 34.0 g.
3. Zn 13.0 ÷ 65.4 = 0.199 mol (÷1 = 0.199); HCl 14.6 ÷ 36.5 = 0.400 mol (÷2 = 0.200). Zn limits
   (just barely — this is why the divide step matters). H₂ = 0.199 mol × 2.0 = 0.40 g.
4. 21.5 ÷ 25.0 × 100% = 86.0%.
5. Possible causes: product still wet or impure (extra mass weighed as product); balance not
   tared (weighing error); unreacted reactant mixed into the product. No — a reaction cannot
   be "better than perfect"; >100% always signals measurement or purity error.

## Practice Set F (Lesson 06)

1. Balanced: 2C₄H₁₀ + 13O₂ → 8CO₂ + 10H₂O. 11.6 ÷ 58.0 = 0.200 mol C₄H₁₀; CO₂ ratio 4:1;
   0.800 mol × 44.0 = 35.2 g.
2. Balanced: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Mg 4.8 ÷ 24.3 = 0.198 mol (÷1 = 0.198); HCl 7.3 ÷ 36.5 =
   0.200 mol (÷2 = 0.100). HCl limits. H₂ = 0.100 mol × 2.0 = 0.20 g.
3. 36.0 ÷ 40.3 × 100% = 89.3%. Loss-type reason: product left on glassware or lost in transfer.
   Measurement-type reason: balance read before fizzing stopped, or tare error.
4. C + O₂ → CO₂. 12.0 g C = 1.00 mol; O₂ consumed = 1.00 mol × 32.0 = 32.0 g; total in =
   44.0 g = CO₂ out (1.00 mol × 44.0). The extra mass came from the air's oxygen.
5. The reasoning is wrong: the number of *molecules* is not conserved (3 → 2 here); the number
   of *atoms* of each element is (4 H and 2 O on both sides), and therefore total mass is.

## Unit assessment

1. Single replacement: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu. Evidence: blue colour of Cu²⁺ fades and a new
   reddish-brown solid (copper metal) coats the strip — new substances, not mixing.
2. C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O (C 2/2, H 4/4, O 6/6).
3. Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂ (Fe 1/1, H 2/2, Cl 2/2).
4. Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ (Na 2/2, C 1/1, O 3/3, H 2/2, Cl 2/2).
5. 22.0 ÷ 44.0 = 0.500 mol; 0.500 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules.
6. 3.00 × 17.0 = 51.0 g.
7. Equal: Mg 24.3 ÷ 24.3 = 1.00 mol; C 12.0 ÷ 12.0 = 1.00 mol. Both contain 6.02 × 10²³ atoms.
8. 5.4 ÷ 27.0 = 0.200 mol Al; H₂ ratio 3:2; 0.300 mol × 2.0 = 0.60 g.
9. 16.0 ÷ 16.0 = 1.00 mol CH₄; H₂O ratio 2:1; 2.00 mol × 18.0 = 36.0 g. Check: O₂ consumed
   2.00 mol × 32.0 = 64.0 g; in 16.0 + 64.0 = 80.0 g; out CO₂ 44.0 + H₂O 36.0 = 80.0 g.
10. H₂ 6.0 ÷ 2.0 = 3.00 mol (÷2 = 1.50); O₂ 32.0 ÷ 32.0 = 1.00 mol (÷1 = 1.00). O₂ limits.
    H₂O = 2.00 mol × 18.0 = 36.0 g. H₂ consumed 2.00 mol; leftover 1.00 mol × 2.0 = 2.0 g.
11. 38.5 ÷ 44.0 × 100% = 87.5%. Plausible reason: product lost in transfer or left on glassware
    (any genuine loss mechanism accepted).
12. Sodium's single valence electron transfers to chlorine's nearly full outer shell, forming
    Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a lattice — a new substance with new properties. Mole ratio
    2 mol Na : 1 mol Cl₂ : 2 mol NaCl.
13. H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance from water. An equation must name the
    correct substances *and* balance their atoms; counts alone do not make a true equation.
14. Mg 12.2 ÷ 24.3 = 0.502 mol; MgO = 0.502 × 40.3 = 20.2 g. O₂ consumed = 0.251 mol × 32.0 =
    8.0 g. Mass in 12.2 + 8.0 = 20.2 g = mass out. Consistent with conservation.
15. 8.4 ÷ 84.0 = 0.100 mol NaHCO₃; ratio 1:1; 0.100 × 44.0 = 4.40 g CO₂.
16. Different elements have different masses per atom, so equal masses hold different numbers
    of atoms. The mole fixes the count: one mole of any element is 6.02 × 10²³ atoms, and its
    mass is that element's molar mass — so weighing 12.0 g of carbon and 24.3 g of magnesium
    gives equal numbers of atoms (1.00 mol each).

## Project (sample data for the simulation route; the learner's own data governs the bench route)

- Prediction: NaHCO₃ 4.2 ÷ 84.0 = 0.0500 mol; acetic acid 100 g × 5% = 5.0 g ÷ 60.0 = 0.0833
  mol. Baking soda limits. Predicted CO₂ = 0.0500 × 44.0 = 2.20 g.
- Sample measured mass loss: 2.0 g. Sample percent yield: 2.0 ÷ 2.20 × 100% = 91%.
- Error sources (each with direction): CO₂ remaining dissolved in the liquid *lowers* the
  measured loss (gas counted as escaped has not left); droplets carried out by vigorous fizzing
  *raise* the measured loss (liquid mass lost is not CO₂); weighing before fizzing fully stops
  *lowers* the measured loss (gas still to escape); balance precision ±0.1 g moves the result
  either way by up to about 5% of the predicted value.
- Conclusion (sample): the prediction held within about 10%; the small shortfall is consistent
  with dissolved CO₂ and measurement precision, not with mass being created or destroyed; a
  second trial could stir longer and wait a full 10 minutes before the final weighing.
