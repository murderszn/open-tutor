# Answer Key — Unit 03 (Adult Only)

Every item below was solved independently of the student pages and reconciled with them.
Accept equivalent wording where the reasoning is the same; reject a correct label with no
required reasoning where the item states that reasoning is required.

## Lesson exit checks (short answers)

- **Lesson 01:** guides: ≥ 1.7 ionic; 0.4–1.7 polar covalent; < 0.4 nonpolar covalent.
  ΔEN = 0.15 → nonpolar covalent; it cannot be ionic because ionic requires a large
  difference (≥ 1.7 guide) plus, typically, a metal with a nonmetal. Metallic bond: metal
  ions held in a shared sea of delocalised valence electrons.
- **Lesson 02:** CO₂ has 16 valence electrons; carbon forms 4 bonds (two double bonds).
  Ion structures must show brackets around the whole structure and the charge outside.
  Hydrogen needs 2 electrons (a duet); oxygen needs 8 (an octet).
- **Lesson 03:** a double bond counts as ONE domain — that is why CO₂ has 2 domains and is
  linear. Four domains with 2 lone pairs → bent, ~104.5°. CO₂ is nonpolar because its two
  equal, opposite bond dipoles cancel by symmetry.
- **Lesson 04:** solid NaCl does not conduct because its ions are locked in the lattice;
  salt water conducts because the ions are free to move. Melting ice breaks forces between
  water molecules, not O–H bonds. One single test separating metal from ionic solid:
  conductivity of the solid (metal conducts, ionic solid does not) — because the metal's
  electron sea moves while the ionic lattice locks its ions.
- **Lesson 05:** three steps: (1) name particles and arrangement, (2) name forces,
  (3) connect force to function. A long-chain polymer stretches because chains uncoil and
  slide (weak between-chain forces), but does not fall apart because the chains themselves
  are held by strong covalent bonds. Pure silicon is a poor conductor because all its
  valence electrons are locked in bonds; doping adds a few mobile electrons, so it conducts
  in a controlled way.
- **Lesson 06:** HF is covalent despite ΔEN = 1.78 because (a) the 1.7 cut-off is a guide on
  a continuum, not a law, and (b) hydrogen's single electron and lack of inner shells mean
  it does not form a clean H⁺ lattice ion; bulk evidence (boiling point ~20 °C, molecular
  behaviour) confirms separate molecules. "A molecule of salt" is corrected to: NaCl forms a
  lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ formula units, not molecules. Rule: state uncertainty explicitly
  when evidence conflicts, and name the further test.

## Lesson guided practice (short answers)

- **Lesson 01:** (1) MgO: ΔEN = 3.44 − 1.31 = 2.13 → ionic; magnesium transfers electrons to
  oxygen. (2) C–H: ΔEN = 0.35 → nonpolar covalent; near-equal sharing. (3) Metallic bond:
  the electron sea moves (conductivity) and lets layers slide (bending).
- **Lesson 02:** (1) CH₄: carbon central, four single bonds, no lone pairs on carbon; total
  8 valence electrons. (2) NH₃: nitrogen central, three bonds, one lone pair on nitrogen;
  total 8. (3) OH⁻: O 6 + H 1 + 1 (charge) = 8; single bond, three lone pairs on oxygen,
  brackets with ⁻.
- **Lesson 03:** (1) NH₃: 4 domains (3 bonds + 1 lone pair) → trigonal pyramidal, ~107°,
  polar (dipoles do not cancel). (2) CH₄: 4 domains, 0 lone pairs → tetrahedral, 109.5°,
  nonpolar (four identical bonds cancel by symmetry). (3) BF₃: 3 domains, 0 lone pairs →
  trigonal planar, 120°, nonpolar (three identical bonds cancel by symmetry).
- **Lesson 04:** (1) Metallic: moving electron sea explains conductivity; sliding layers
  explain bending. (2) Covalent molecular: it does not conduct when dissolved (no free ions)
  and melts low (only weak between-molecule forces broken). (3) Graphite's layers have
  mobile electrons within each sheet; diamond's electrons are locked in a 3-D network of
  bonds, so no charge moves.
- **Lesson 05:** (1) A thin metal mesh conducts because its electron sea moves, and bends
  because ion layers slide in the sea. (2) A raincoat's long nonpolar chains repel water
  (no strong attraction to polar water) and fold because chains slide past each other.
  (3) A hammer needs toughness (resistance to fracture on impact); diamond is hard but
  brittle, so it would fracture rather than absorb the blow.
- **Lesson 06:** (1) CO₂ is nonpolar because its two polar bonds are equal and opposite and
  cancel; bond polarity alone does not decide molecular polarity. (2) Metallic: the
  observation that rules out an ionic lattice is bending without breaking — a shifted ionic
  lattice puts like charges together and shatters. (3) Check the elements (two nonmetals
  usually share) and bulk evidence (melting point, conductivity, solubility) before
  concluding; near 1.7, character is mixed.

## Practice Set C

1. NH₃: 4 domains (3 bonds + 1 lone pair) → trigonal pyramidal, ~107°, polar (dipoles do
   not cancel; the lone pair reinforces the nitrogen side).
2. CH₄: 4 domains, 0 lone pairs → tetrahedral, 109.5°, nonpolar (four identical bonds
   cancel by symmetry).
3. BF₃: 3 domains, 0 lone pairs → trigonal planar, 120°, nonpolar (three identical bonds
   cancel by symmetry).
4. H₂O: 4 domains (2 bonds + 2 lone pairs) → bent, ~104.5°, polar (dipoles do not cancel).
5. CO₂: 2 domains (two double bonds) → linear, 180°, nonpolar (equal, opposite dipoles
   cancel).
6. SO₂: 3 domains (one double bond + one single bond + one lone pair) → bent, polar
   (dipoles do not cancel). Accept an angle stated as "about 120°, reduced by the lone pair"
   (actual ~119°); do not require an exact value.

## Practice Set D

1. Lowest to highest: CO₂ (−78.5 °C sublimation) < H₂O (0 °C) < NaCl (801 °C) < Cu
   (1085 °C) < diamond (~3550 °C sublimation). Reason at each step: only weak forces
   between CO₂ molecules; weak forces between H₂O molecules; ionic lattice attractions for
   NaCl; metallic sea for Cu; a continuous network of strong covalent bonds for diamond.
2. Solid MgO: no (ions locked). Molten MgO: yes (ions free). Sugar solution: no (no free
   ions; sugar molecules are neutral). Copper wire: yes (electron sea moves).
3. NaCl: yes — water pulls its ions apart. Sucrose: yes — its polar molecules are attracted
   to water. Copper: no — the metallic sea is not broken apart by water. Diamond: no — its
   network bonds are far too strong for water to overcome.
4. Graphite has layers with mobile electrons within each sheet, so charge moves along a
   layer. Diamond locks every electron in bonds in a 3-D network, so no charge moves.
5. Ionic solid. Strongest evidence: it shatters (brittle lattice) and its solution conducts
   (free ions) while the solid does not (locked ions).
6. CaF₂: high melting point (ionic lattice); solid nonconductor; solution (where it
   dissolves) conductor. Accept "dissolves slowly/slightly" with the lattice-strength reason.
   A good checking test: conductivity of the solid versus the solution, or brittleness on
   striking. (Note: CaF₂ is only slightly soluble in water; the key point is the pattern —
   solid nonconductor, solution conductor — not a claim of high solubility.)

## Practice Set E

1. The conductor must be metal (electron sea moves and layers slide, so it conducts and
   bends); the insulator must be polymer (long chains with no free charge; it flexes
   because chains slide, and it insulates because no charge moves).
2. A network solid's bonds run in all directions, so no weak direction lets the edge deform
   or wear; a metal would deform and a polymer would cut or melt.
3. The raincoat's chains are nonpolar hydrocarbons, so polar water is not attracted to them;
   it folds because chains slide past each other on weak between-chain forces.
4. Pure silicon locks all valence electrons in bonds, so no charge moves. Replacing a few
   silicon atoms with phosphorus (5 valence electrons) leaves one extra electron per
   replacement atom free to move, so the material conducts in proportion to the doping.
   It is designed because its function was set by deliberately changing its structure.

## Practice Set F

2. H₂S: 1 + 1 + 6 = 8; sulfur central, two single bonds, two lone pairs on sulfur. Shape:

3. (a) Corrected: NaCl has no molecules; 1465 °C is its boiling point as a lattice

## Project unknowns (Part B)

- U1 → ionic: dissolves, solution conducts, solid does not, hard and brittle, high melting
  point. Ruled out: covalent molecular (a molecular solid would not give a conducting
  solution or a high melting point). Further test: brittleness on striking, or crystal shape.
- U2 → metallic: conducts as a solid, bends, does not dissolve. Ruled out: ionic (an ionic
  solid does not conduct as a solid and does not bend). Further test: conductivity after
  melting, or malleability under a hammer (adult-supervised).
- U3 → covalent molecular: dissolves, solution does not conduct, soft, low decomposition
  temperature. Ruled out: ionic (an ionic solution would conduct). Further test: melting
  behaviour or solubility in a nonpolar solvent (adult-approved only).
- U4 → network covalent: no dissolving, no conductivity, very hard, very high melting
  point. Ruled out: metallic (a metal would conduct) and ionic (an ionic solid would
  usually dissolve at least partly and conduct in solution). Further test: hardness
  comparison or conductivity along one direction (graphite check).

### Part C

1. Lowest to highest: CO₂ (−78.5 °C) < H₂O (0 °C) < NaCl (801 °C) < diamond (~3550 °C).
   Reasons: weak between-molecule forces for CO₂ and H₂O; ionic lattice for NaCl;
   continuous network of strong covalent bonds for diamond (4 pts: 1 order, 3 reasons;
   accept H₂O above CO₂ justified by stronger between-molecule forces in water).
2. Graphite's layers contain mobile electrons that move within a sheet; diamond locks every
   electron in bonds in a 3-D network, so no charge can move (4 pts: 2 + 2).
3. (a) Ionic (2 pts). (b) Strongest evidence: solution conducts (free ions) and solid does
   not (locked ions), plus high melting point (whole lattice must be overcome) — any two
   stated clearly (2 pts). (c) Ruled out, for example, covalent molecular (a molecular
   solution would not conduct) or metallic (a metal would conduct as a solid); further
   test: brittleness, crystal examination, or conductivity of the melt (2 pts: 1 + 1).

### Part D (10 pts, per the assessment rubric)

Full marks require: the provided structure described accurately (3); forces named —
electron sea for copper, strong along-chain bonds plus weak between-chain forces for the
polymer (3); function connected to those forces — current carried by moving electrons and
bending by sliding layers, or folding/stretching/waterproofing by chain sliding and
nonpolar chains (3); no unsupported property claimed, for example do not accept "plastic
conducts when wet" or "copper is waterproof because it is shiny" (1). A paragraph that
only names the material and its job, without forces, earns at most 3 points.
