# Unit 03 — Chemical Bonding, Structure, and Properties

**Grade 10 Science (Chemistry pathway) · Weeks 11–14 · 20 sessions of ~50 minutes**

Unit 03 of [issue #47](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/47). Status: **validated
draft** — delivered for educator review, not merged. This unit is self-contained for one grade-10
learner; a guiding adult supervises all work. There is no open flame, no concentrated acid or
base, and no toxic material in this unit: the investigation tests solubility and low-voltage
conductivity of household solids and uses published melting-point data for the melting
comparison, so no sample is heated to melting. The adult previews every linked simulation and
video before the learner opens it, and approves the conductivity tester before it is switched on.

## Essential question

Why does the same kind of particle — an atom — build materials as different as table salt, sugar,
copper wire, and diamond, and can you predict a material's behaviour from how its particles hold
together?

## What the learner will be able to do

1. Classify a bond as ionic, polar covalent, or nonpolar covalent from the elements involved and
   from the electronegativity difference (ΔEN), and explain why the ΔEN cut-offs are guides on a
   continuum, not laws — including one case (hydrogen fluoride) where the guide alone misleads.
2. Draw Lewis structures for simple molecules and polyatomic ions (single, double, and triple
   bonds; lone pairs; brackets and charges for ions), and use them to count bonding and lone-pair
   electron domains.
3. Predict molecular shape with VSEPR for up to four electron domains (linear, trigonal planar,
   tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal, bent) and decide whether a molecule is polar or nonpolar
   from bond polarity plus symmetry.
4. Explain, from particle structure, why ionic solids, covalent molecular substances, metals, and
   network solids differ in melting behaviour, electrical conductivity, solubility, hardness,
   and malleability — and use bulk observations to infer relative strength of the electrical
   forces between particles (HS-PS1-3).
5. Use position on the periodic table and outermost-electron patterns to predict bond type and
   likely properties for main-group elements (HS-PS1-1).
6. Communicate, for a provided designed material, why its molecular-level structure makes it
   work for its job — for example why a metal conducts, why a long-chain polymer is flexible but
   durable, or why a network solid is hard (HS-PS2-6).

Objectives 1–6 above serve track objectives 5, 6, and 12 of the
[grade-10 science track](../../README.md) and the practices named in
[scope-and-sequence.md](../../scope-and-sequence.md) (developing and using models, planning and
carrying out investigations, analyzing data, constructing explanations from evidence).
Crosscutting concepts: **patterns** (bond type follows electron patterns) and **structure and
function** (bulk properties follow particle structure).

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS), high-school (9–12) band.
Codes and descriptions were re-verified 2026-10-27 against the NSTA NGSS topic-arrangement
mirror (see the resource pack for the checked page). Descriptions below are short paraphrases
for mapping purposes, not reproductions of the standards text. **This crosswalk is a planning
reference, not a claim of state adoption, accreditation, or certification of alignment.**

- **HS-PS1-1** — use the periodic table as a model to predict the relative properties of
  elements based on the patterns of electrons in the outermost energy level of atoms.
  Clarification: properties that can be predicted include reactivity of metals, types of bonds
  formed, numbers of bonds formed, and reactions with oxygen. Assessment boundary (applied
  throughout this unit): assessment is limited to **main-group elements**, and does not include
  a quantitative understanding of ionization energy beyond relative trends. (Lessons 01, 03,
  06; project; unit assessment.)
- **HS-PS1-3** — plan and conduct an investigation to gather evidence to compare the structure
  of substances at the bulk scale to infer the strength of electrical forces between
  particles. Clarification: emphasis is on understanding the strengths of forces between
  particles, not on naming specific intermolecular forces (such as dipole–dipole). Examples of
  particles include ions, atoms, molecules, and networked materials (such as graphite).
  Examples of bulk properties include melting point, boiling point, vapor pressure, and
  surface tension. Assessment boundary: no Raoult's-law calculations of vapor pressure.
  (Lessons 04, 06; project; unit assessment.)
- **HS-PS2-6** — communicate scientific and technical information about why the molecular-level
  structure is important in the functioning of designed materials. Clarification: emphasis is
  on the attractive and repulsive forces that determine the functioning of the material.
  Examples include why electrically conductive materials are often made of metal, why flexible
  but durable materials are made up of long chained molecules, and why pharmaceuticals are
  designed to interact with specific receptors. Assessment boundary: assessment is limited to
  provided molecular structures of specific designed materials. (Lesson 05; project Part C;
  unit assessment.)

Numerical values used in this unit, and their status:

- Pauling electronegativity values (verified 2026-10-27 against the LibreTexts reference table
  of electronegativity values): H 2.20, Li 0.98, C 2.55, N 3.04, O 3.44, F 3.98, Na 0.93,
  Mg 1.31, Si 1.90, P 2.19, S 2.58, Cl 3.16, K 0.82, Ca 1.00, Br 2.96. Noble gases have no Pauling
  value on this scale and are never used in ΔEN calculations in this unit.
- Melting and sublimation points used as **published reference data** (not measured in class):
  sodium chloride 801 °C; copper 1085 °C; sucrose decomposes around 186 °C rather than melting
  cleanly; diamond sublimes around 3550 °C; graphite sublimes around 3642 °C; quartz (silicon
  dioxide, network solid) 1713 °C; water 0 °C; carbon dioxide sublimes at −78.5 °C. The project
  states explicitly which numbers are reference data and which are the learner's own
  observations, and never presents a reference value as a classroom measurement.
- Solubilities quoted as classroom approximations at about 20 °C: sodium chloride about
  36 g per 100 mL of water; sucrose about 200 g per 100 mL of water. The project does not ask
  learners to measure saturation; it asks whether a small sample dissolves readily, slowly, or
  hardly at all.
- The project's unknown samples are **fictional practice data**, labeled as such. They are
  modeled on real bond-type patterns but are not observations of real samples and must not be
  cited as data about any real substance.

## Safety

All bench work in this unit uses household solids (table salt, granulated sugar), room-temperature
water, and a low-voltage (9 V battery) conductivity tester assembled and approved by the adult.
No sample is heated, no flame is used, and no glassware is heated. The adult handles the battery
tester, checks every wire connection before it is switched on, and stops the test if any wire
feels warm. Goggles are worn whenever a solid is crushed or a solution is stirred vigorously.
If materials or supervision are unavailable, the simulation alternative and the worked dataset in
the project carry the same objectives without bench work. Never taste any sample, and never test
an unknown household substance: the project's unknowns are paper-only practice data, not real
samples to handle.
