# Lesson 02 — Drawing Lewis Structures

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can draw Lewis structures for simple molecules and
polyatomic ions — showing every valence electron as a dot, every shared pair as a line, lone
pairs where they belong, and brackets with charges for ions.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** periodic table, notebook, Practice Set B.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. How many valence electrons do carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and hydrogen have? (Use groups from
   Unit 02: group number for main-group elements, except helium.)
2. In Lesson 01, what is a shared pair? What is a lone pair — say it in your own words before
   reading on.

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read Unit 02, Lesson 03: valence electrons are the
electrons in the outermost shell, and the group number (for groups 1, 2, 13–17) gives the
valence count for main-group elements.)

## Teaching

### What a Lewis structure shows

A **Lewis structure** is a diagram of valence electrons only. Rules used in this unit:

1. **Count valence electrons** for every atom in the formula, then adjust for charge: add 1
   electron per unit of negative charge, subtract 1 per unit of positive charge.
2. **Draw the skeleton:** the least electronegative atom (never hydrogen) usually goes in the
   center; hydrogen and halogens go on the outside. For oxyacids and ions in this unit the
   skeleton is given when it is not obvious.
3. **Connect atoms with single bonds** (one shared pair = one line = 2 electrons), then
   **complete octets** (8 electrons around each atom) with lone pairs, starting with the
   outside atoms. Hydrogen needs only **2 electrons** (a duet), never an octet.
4. **If electrons remain short**, convert lone pairs on an outside atom into extra bonds
   (double or triple) until every atom except hydrogen has an octet — or until the electron
   count runs out.
5. **Check:** count all dots and lines. The total must equal the valence count from Step 1.
   For ions, draw square brackets around the whole structure and write the charge outside,
   upper right.

The **octet rule** — atoms tend toward 8 valence electrons — is a useful guide for Period 2
elements and most main-group chemistry in this unit, not a law of nature. Some elements break
it (boron compounds with 6 electrons, and Period 3+ elements that can hold more than 8);
those cases appear only as extensions, never as assessment items in this unit.

### Worked example 1 — water, H₂O

Step 1 — Valence electrons: H has 1 each (2 × 1 = 2), O has 6. Total = **8**.
Step 2 — Skeleton: O in the center, one H on each side: H–O–H.
Step 3 — Two single bonds use 4 electrons; 4 remain. Place them as 2 lone pairs on oxygen.
Step 4 — Check: O has 2 bonds (4 electrons) + 2 lone pairs (4 electrons) = 8 ✓. Each H has
1 bond = 2 electrons ✓. Total drawn = 8 ✓.

Written form: H–O–H with two lone pairs (two dot-pairs) drawn on the oxygen atom, one
above and one below it in the notebook.

### Worked example 2 — carbon dioxide, CO₂

Step 1 — Valence electrons: C has 4, O has 6 each (2 × 6 = 12). Total = **16**.
Step 2 — Skeleton: O–C–O.
Step 3 — Two single bonds use 4 electrons; 12 remain. Completing octets on the oxygens uses
12 electrons (3 lone pairs each, 6 electrons per oxygen) — but then carbon has only 4 electrons
(2 bonds) and no electrons left to give it an octet.
Step 4 — Fix by sharing more: convert one lone pair on each oxygen into a second bond.
Now: O=C=O. Each oxygen has 2 bonds (4 electrons) + 2 lone pairs (4 electrons) = 8 ✓.
Carbon has 4 bonds = 8 electrons ✓. Total drawn = 16 ✓.

The lesson here is general: **if the central atom is short of an octet after Step 3, make
multiple bonds.** Do not add electrons you do not have.

### Worked example 3 — ammonium ion, NH₄⁺

Step 1 — Valence electrons: N has 5, H has 1 each (4 × 1 = 4), total 9 — then subtract 1 for
the +1 charge: **8**.
Step 2 — Skeleton: N in the center, four H around it.
Step 3 — Four single bonds use all 8 electrons. Nitrogen has 4 bonds = 8 electrons ✓; each
hydrogen has 2 ✓.
Step 4 — Ion rule: draw brackets around the whole structure and write ⁺ outside: [H–N–H
with four H]⁺. Forgetting the brackets or the charge is the most common lost mark on ion
questions — the structure without them describes a neutral species that does not exist here.

### A common trap

**Dots are electrons, lines are pairs — never count both.** A line already represents 2
electrons. A frequent error is drawing a bond as a line and then adding its two electrons as
dots as well, which double-counts and breaks the Step 5 check. If your total does not match
Step 1, recount lines as 2 before redrawing anything.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or a partner; hints are in brackets.

1. Draw the Lewis structure for methane, CH₄. [Total valence electrons? Central atom?]
2. Draw the Lewis structure for ammonia, NH₃. [Nitrogen has 5 valence electrons; how many
   lone pairs remain on nitrogen after three bonds?]
3. Draw the Lewis structure for the hydroxide ion, OH⁻. [Adjust the electron count for the
   charge first; remember the brackets.]

## Independent practice

Complete Practice Set B (in `practice.md`). Then answer this application question in your
notebook: nitrogen gas, N₂, is famously unreactive, while oxygen gas, O₂, supports burning.
Draw both Lewis structures and explain, from the number of shared pairs, why breaking N₂
apart should take more energy than breaking O₂ apart. (Bond-breaking energy is treated
quantitatively in Unit 07; here the qualitative claim — more shared pairs, harder to pull
apart — is enough.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) how many valence electrons does CO₂ have in total, and how many
bonds does carbon form in its structure? (b) What two things must an ion structure show that
a neutral molecule structure does not? (c) Hydrogen needs how many electrons around it — and
why is that different from oxygen?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** read each structure aloud as a sentence ("carbon double-bonded
  to two oxygens, each oxygen with two lone pairs") before drawing it.
- **Alternate response mode:** build structures with counters (one counter = one electron,
  pairs grouped) on a printed skeleton, then copy the result into the notebook.
- **Extension:** draw the Lewis structure for the sulfate ion, SO₄²⁻, and explain why sulfur
  ends up with more than 8 electrons around it. State clearly that this breaks the octet rule
  and is an extension, not a Unit 03 assessment item.
