# Lesson 04 — How Bonding Explains Bulk Properties

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can predict, for a substance whose bond type you know,
whether it will melt easily or only at high temperature, conduct electricity, dissolve in water,
and bend or shatter — and you can explain each prediction from particle structure.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, the reference-data table in this lesson,
Practice Set D, project plan (Session 9).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Salt (NaCl) does not conduct electricity as a solid, but salt water does. From Lesson 01,
   what changes when salt dissolves that could explain that?
2. Copper wire bends; a salt crystal shatters when struck. What is different about how their
   particles are held? Write one sentence each before reading on.

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read Lesson 01: ionic = lattice of ions; metallic =
ions in a sea of moving electrons.)

## Teaching

### The rule that runs this lesson

**Bulk properties follow what has to be broken.** Melting means overcoming the forces that
hold particles in place; conducting means charge can move; dissolving means the solvent can
pull particles apart. So ask, for each substance: what particles are there, and what holds
them?

| Substance type | Particles | What holds them | Melting behaviour | Conductivity | Solubility in water | Hardness / bending |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionic solid (e.g. NaCl) | Ions in a lattice | Strong attractions between every ion and its neighbours | High (NaCl 801 °C) | Solid: no (ions locked); melted/dissolved: yes (ions free to move) | Often soluble (water pulls ions apart) | Hard but brittle: shifting a layer puts like charges together and the crystal splits |
| Covalent molecular (e.g. sugar, water, CO₂) | Separate molecules | Strong bonds *within* each molecule; much weaker forces *between* molecules | Low to moderate (melting breaks only the weak between-molecule forces) | No (no charged particles free to move) | Depends on polarity: polar molecules tend to dissolve in water; nonpolar ones do not | Soft; low melting points for small molecules |
| Metal (e.g. copper) | Metal ions in an electron sea | Attraction between ions and the moving sea | High (copper 1085 °C) | Yes, solid and liquid (electrons move freely) | No (water cannot pull the sea apart) | Strong but malleable: layers slide without breaking the sea |
| Network solid (e.g. diamond, quartz) | Atoms joined in one continuous network | Strong covalent bonds in all directions | Very high (diamond sublimes ~3550 °C; quartz 1713 °C) | No (electrons locked in bonds) — graphite is the exception: its layers conduct along the sheet | No | Very hard; diamond is not malleable |

Two warnings keep this table honest. First, **melting a covalent molecular solid does not
break covalent bonds**: ice melting breaks the forces between water molecules, not the O–H
bonds inside them. Saying "melting breaks bonds" without saying which bonds is a half-truth.
Second, **graphite breaks the network-solid rule in one direction only**: it conducts along
its layers (electrons move within a sheet) but not between them. Exceptions like graphite are
why this unit teaches patterns with reasons, not rules to recite.

### Reference data (published values, not classroom measurements)

| Substance | Type | Melting / sublimation point | Conducts when solid? | Dissolves readily in water? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium chloride, NaCl | Ionic | 801 °C | No | Yes (~36 g / 100 mL at ~20 °C) |
| Sucrose (table sugar) | Covalent molecular | Decomposes ~186 °C (does not melt cleanly) | No | Yes (~200 g / 100 mL at ~20 °C) |
| Copper, Cu | Metallic | 1085 °C | Yes | No |
| Diamond, C | Network covalent | Sublimes ~3550 °C | No | No |
| Graphite, C | Network covalent | Sublimes ~3642 °C | Yes, along layers | No |
| Quartz, SiO₂ | Network covalent | 1713 °C | No | No |
| Water, H₂O | Covalent molecular | 0 °C | No | — (it is the solvent) |
| Carbon dioxide, CO₂ | Covalent molecular | Sublimes −78.5 °C | No | Slightly (dissolves as a gas) |

### Worked example 1 — rank melting points from structure

*Rank these from lowest to highest melting point, with reasons: CO₂, NaCl, Cu, diamond.*

Step 1 — Identify types: CO₂ covalent molecular; NaCl ionic lattice; Cu metallic; diamond
network.
Step 2 — What must be overcome? For CO₂, only weak forces between separate molecules.
For NaCl, attractions between ions throughout the lattice. For Cu, the ion–sea attraction
throughout the metal. For diamond, strong covalent bonds in every direction.
Step 3 — Rank: **CO₂ < NaCl < Cu < diamond**. Check against reference data: CO₂ sublimes at
−78.5 °C; NaCl 801 °C; Cu 1085 °C; diamond ~3550 °C. The order matches, and the reason is
the strength and extent of what must be broken — not the size of any single particle.

### Worked example 2 — predict conductivity and solubility for magnesium oxide

*MgO is ionic (Lesson 01: ΔEN = 2.13). Predict: (a) does solid MgO conduct? (b) does MgO
dissolve readily in water? (c) is its melting point high or low?*

Step 1 — (a) Solid ionic: ions locked in the lattice, so **no** — charge cannot move.
Step 2 — (b) Ionic compounds often dissolve because water molecules pull ions apart; MgO is
a known awkward case (it dissolves only slightly, because its lattice is unusually strong —
Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ attract twice as strongly as singly charged ions). The honest prediction is
"ionic pattern says soluble, but strong 2+/2− attraction makes it slow and slight." A
prediction with a stated uncertainty beats a confident wrong answer, and the teacher guide
accepts either "slightly soluble" or "soluble but slowly" with this reasoning.
Step 3 — (c) **High** melting point: the whole lattice must be overcome (in fact MgO melts
around 2852 °C — far above NaCl, consistent with the 2+/2− charge argument; this value is
reference data, not measured in class).

### A common trap

**"Strong bonds mean high melting point" — without saying which bonds.** Diamond has strong
bonds and a very high melting point because its bonds form the whole structure. Sugar has
strong C–C, C–H, and C–O bonds inside each molecule and a low decomposition temperature
because melting only needs to overcome the weak forces *between* molecules. Always name the
forces being overcome: within-molecule bonds, between-molecule forces, ionic lattice
attractions, or the metallic sea.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or a partner; hints are in brackets.

1. A solid conducts electricity, bends without breaking, and does not dissolve in water.
   What bond type is it, and why does each property fit? [Which type has moving charge?]
2. A white solid melts at a low temperature, does not conduct when dissolved, and dissolves
   readily in water. Is it more likely ionic or covalent molecular? Give two reasons.
   [What does "does not conduct when dissolved" rule out?]
3. Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not, even though both are pure
   carbon. [What is different about their structures — layers vs. a 3-D network?]

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) why does solid NaCl not conduct but salt water does? (b) Why does
melting ice not break O–H bonds? (c) Name one property that separates a metal from an ionic
solid in a single test, and say why.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** for each prediction, write the sentence frame "Because the
  particles are ___, held by ___, the property should be ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** sort property cards (melts high / conducts solid / dissolves /
  bends) under four substance-type headings before writing any explanation.
- **Extension:** research (resource pack) why MgO's melting point is so much higher than
  NaCl's, and write a paragraph using ion charge and lattice strength. Compare with the
  worked example's reasoning.

## Teacher note

See `teacher-guide.md`, Lesson 04. Key misconceptions: melting breaks within-molecule bonds;
all ionic solids dissolve readily (MgO, CaCO₃ counterexamples); conductivity requires
electrons specifically (ions also carry charge in solution); graphite treated as a rule-breaker
rather than a layer-structure consequence. The quiz (Session 10) follows this lesson.
