# Lesson 05 — Designed Materials: Structure Serves Function

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can explain, for a provided designed material, why its
molecular-level structure makes it work for its job — using the attractive and repulsive
forces between particles, not just its name.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, Practice Set E, the three provided material
profiles in this lesson.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. From Lesson 04: why does copper conduct electricity while sugar does not?
2. A plastic bag stretches without tearing, but a ceramic plate shatters. What structural
   difference could explain that? Write one guess before reading on.

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read Lesson 04's table: conductivity needs moving charge;
brittleness comes from a lattice that cannot slide.)

## Teaching

### The question behind every designed material

HS-PS2-6 asks for communication, not memorization: given a material's molecular structure,
explain why it functions as it does. The assessment boundary matters: this unit only asks
about **provided** structures. You are never expected to invent a material's structure from
its name. The pattern to practise is always the same three steps:

1. **Name the particles and their arrangement** (from the provided structure).
2. **Name the forces** holding them (ionic lattice, covalent network, metallic sea, long
   chains with weak forces between them).
3. **Connect force to function**: what must move, stretch, break, or stay put for the job —
   and does this structure allow it?

### Provided material profiles

**Profile A — Copper wire (metallic).** Provided structure: positive copper ions in a regular
arrangement, with valence electrons delocalised through the whole piece. Function: carrying
electric current and being drawn into thin wire. Explanation: current is moving charge, and
the electron sea provides charge that moves freely without breaking the structure. Drawing
into wire works because layers of ions can slide past each other while the sea keeps holding
them — the attraction does not depend on any one ion staying beside any particular electron.

**Profile B — Polyethylene plastic bag (long-chain polymer).** Provided structure: very long
chains of carbon atoms with hydrogen atoms attached, tangled together; the bonds along each
chain are strong covalent bonds, while the forces between chains are much weaker. Function:
a bag that is flexible, stretches a little without tearing, and keeps water out. Explanation:
flexibility comes from chains sliding and uncoiling past each other (only weak between-chain
forces must be overcome); durability comes from the chains themselves being long and hard to
break (strong bonds along the chain). Water stays out because the chains are nonpolar
hydrocarbon chains, and water's polar molecules have no strong attraction to them.

**Profile C — Diamond cutting tool (network covalent).** Provided structure: every carbon atom
covalently bonded to four others in a continuous three-dimensional network. Function: cutting
and grinding hard materials. Explanation: cutting requires a surface that does not deform or
wear away, and in diamond there is no weak direction: separating any piece means breaking
strong covalent bonds in all directions. That is also why diamond is brittle rather than
malleable — it cannot slide like a metal, so under a sharp blow it fractures instead of
bending.

### Worked example 1 — why is a metal saucepan a good cooking vessel, but a plastic one is not?

Step 1 — Particles and forces: the saucepan metal has ions in an electron sea; plastic has
long chains with weak forces between them.
Step 2 — What the job needs: heat must travel quickly from the stove through the vessel wall
to the food, and the vessel must keep its shape when hot.
Step 3 — Connect: in a metal, the moving electrons carry thermal energy quickly through the
whole piece (the same freedom that carries electric current). In a plastic, heat must travel
through weak between-chain forces and the chains themselves soften and slide more as they
warm — so it heats slowly and loses its shape. Structure explains function in both cases,
and the explanation uses the same electron-sea idea as Profile A, applied to heat instead of
electricity.

### Worked example 2 — a semiconductor, qualitatively

A pure silicon crystal is a network solid: every silicon atom covalently bonded to four
neighbours, with all valence electrons locked in bonds. It conducts poorly — there is no free
charge. In a designed semiconductor, a tiny number of silicon atoms are replaced by atoms with
one extra valence electron (for example phosphorus, 5 valence electrons where silicon has 4).
The extra electron is not locked in a bond, so it can move through the crystal — and suddenly
the material conducts, in a controlled way that depends on how many replacement atoms were
added. This is a qualitative model only: no quantitative band theory, no calculations, and
the exact doping chemistry is not assessed. The point is the pattern: **change the structure
a little, change the function a lot** — which is exactly what "designed material" means.

### A common trap

**"This material is strong, so its bonds are strong."** Strength has several meanings, and
they do not always go together. A polymer bag is durable (hard to tear) but not hard (easy to
scratch and bend). Diamond is hard (hard to scratch) but brittle (breaks under a blow).
A metal is strong (hard to pull apart) and malleable (easy to bend). Always say *which*
strength you mean — hardness, durability, tensile strength, or flexibility — and tie it to
the specific forces in the provided structure.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or a partner; hints are in brackets.

1. A flexible phone screen needs a transparent conductor. Using Profile A, explain why a thin
   metal mesh can conduct while still bending. [What moves, and what slides?]
2. A raincoat is made from a long-chain polymer. Using Profile B, explain why it keeps water
   out but can still fold. [Two different forces: along the chain vs. between chains.]
3. Why would a diamond tool be a terrible choice for a hammer head, even though diamond is
   "the hardest material"? [Which strength does a hammer need that diamond lacks?]

## Independent practice

Complete Practice Set E (in `practice.md`). Then answer this application question in your
notebook: a drug designer wants a medicine molecule to fit a specific receptor in the body.
Using the HS-PS2-6 idea that pharmaceuticals are designed to interact with specific
receptors, explain in three sentences why the molecule's shape and polarity matter more than
its size alone. (This is a communication task, not a chemistry calculation: shape decides
fit, polarity decides attraction to the receptor's charged and polar regions.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) state the three steps for explaining a designed material;
(b) why does a long-chain polymer stretch but not fall apart? (c) In one sentence, why is
pure silicon a poor conductor but doped silicon a useful one?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** use the sentence frame "The structure is ___. The forces are
  ___. So for the job of ___, it works because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** match job cards (conducts, flexes, cuts, insulates) to
  structure cards (sea, chains, network, lattice) before writing any explanation.
- **Extension:** research (resource pack) one real designed material not in this lesson
  (for example, a shape-memory alloy or a hydrogel), and write a paragraph using the three
  steps. State the source and note that your structure description comes from that source,
  not from this unit.
