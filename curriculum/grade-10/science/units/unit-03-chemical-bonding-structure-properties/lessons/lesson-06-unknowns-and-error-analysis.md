# Lesson 06 — Unknowns, Exceptions, and Error Analysis

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can identify an unknown substance's bond type from
bulk evidence, explain the cases where the simple rules mislead, and correct the unit's most
common errors with reasons.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, project data (Sessions 9 and 12), Practice
Set F.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Hydrogen fluoride: EN(H) = 2.20, EN(F) = 3.98. What does the ΔEN guide from Lesson 01
   predict for the H–F bond?
2. Is HF a salt-like lattice or a gas of small molecules at room temperature? (If you are not
   sure, note the question and return to it after the teaching below.)

(If the readiness check is shaky, compute ΔEN first: 3.98 − 2.20 = 1.78, which is above the
1.7 ionic guide — and yet HF is a covalent molecular gas. That contradiction is this
lesson's starting point.)

## Teaching

### Case 1 — Hydrogen fluoride breaks the guide

HF has ΔEN = 1.78, above the 1.7 ionic cut-off, but hydrogen fluoride is a covalent molecule,
not an ionic lattice. Why does the guide fail here? Three reasons, all worth stating:

1. **The cut-off is a summary of many compounds, not a law.** Around 1.7 the character of
   bonding changes gradually; compounds near the boundary have mixed character, and small
   changes in which atoms are involved tip the balance.
2. **Hydrogen is special.** A hydrogen atom has only one electron and no inner shells, so
   when it bonds to a very electronegative atom it does not form a clean H⁺ ion sitting in a
   lattice the way sodium does. The electron pair stays shared, though very unequally — a
   strongly polar covalent bond.
3. **Bulk evidence beats a single number.** HF boils at about 20 °C (a molecular gas/liquid,
   not a high-melting lattice), which fits separate molecules with weak forces between them
   and contradicts a lattice prediction. When a ΔEN classification and bulk evidence disagree,
   trust the evidence and revise the classification — that is what HS-PS1-3 investigation is
   for.

The correct statement is: HF has a **strongly polar covalent** bond. "Predominantly ionic"
would be wrong here, and the answer key marks it wrong even though the arithmetic points that
way. This is the unit's clearest lesson that chemistry's guides serve evidence, not the other
way round.

### Case 2 — "Molecules of salt" and other errors to correct

Correct each of these statements in your notebook, with the reason:

1. *"A molecule of sodium chloride melts at 801 °C."* — Correction: NaCl has no molecules;
   it is a lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ formula units. 801 °C is the temperature at which the
   lattice attractions are overcome, not a molecule breaking apart.
2. *"Water is polar because its O–H bonds are polar."* — Half-true, corrected: bond polarity
   alone is not enough; CO₂ has polar bonds and is nonpolar. Water is polar because its
   bonds are polar **and** its bent shape prevents cancellation (Lesson 03).
3. *"Ammonia is tetrahedral."* — Correction: ammonia has four electron domains, but only
   three are bonds; the fourth is a lone pair. Its shape is trigonal pyramidal. Domains,
   not atoms, decide shape.
4. *"Sugar melts at a low temperature because its bonds are weak."* — Correction: sugar's
   within-molecule bonds are strong; what breaks on heating is the weak forces *between*
   sugar molecules, and sugar in fact decomposes around 186 °C rather than melting cleanly.
   Always name which forces break.
5. *"A double bond counts as two domains, so CO₂ is bent."* — Correction: a double bond is
   one domain. CO₂ has two domains and is linear.

### Worked example 1 — identify an unknown from bulk evidence

*Unknown X is a white solid. It does not dissolve in water. It does not conduct electricity
as a solid or when mixed with water. It melts at a very high temperature. What bond type is
most likely, and what is ruled out?*

Step 1 — Rule out ionic: an ionic solid would usually dissolve (at least somewhat) and its
solution would conduct. X does neither.
Step 2 — Rule out metallic: a metal would conduct as a solid. X does not.
Step 3 — Rule out covalent molecular: a molecular solid would melt at a low or moderate
temperature, not a very high one.
Step 4 — Remaining candidate: **network covalent** — a continuous network of strong bonds
explains very high melting, no moving charge, and no dissolution. (Quartz and diamond fit
this pattern; the project uses this exact logic on fictional unknowns.)

### Worked example 2 — a conflicting-evidence unknown

*Unknown Y dissolves readily in water and its solution conducts electricity, but it melts at
a low temperature and its solid does not conduct. Ionic or molecular?*

Step 1 — Solution conducts and dissolves: points toward ionic (free ions in solution).
Step 2 — Low melting point and non-conducting solid: also fits ionic (solid ions locked;
lattice overcome at low temperature only if the lattice is weak — possible for some ionic
compounds with large, singly charged ions, but unusual).
Step 3 — The honest answer: **probably ionic, but the low melting point is surprising, so one
more test is needed** — for example, check whether the dissolved particles are ions (the
solution conducts, which is already evidence) or test a second property such as brittleness
or crystal shape. State the uncertainty explicitly: a single surprising property does not
overturn two agreeing ones, but it does demand a further test. The answer key accepts
"ionic, with low melting point noted as surprising" and rejects a bare "ionic" with no
discussion of the surprise.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or a partner; hints are in brackets.

1. Correct this statement: "Carbon dioxide is polar because oxygen is more electronegative
   than carbon." [Which lesson decides this — bond polarity or molecular polarity?]
2. Unknown Z is a shiny solid that conducts electricity and bends without breaking. What is
   it, and which single observation rules out an ionic lattice? [What does a lattice do when
   struck?]
3. A learner writes "ΔEN = 1.85, therefore ionic" for a compound of two nonmetals. What
   should the learner check before concluding? [Think about HF: elements, bulk evidence.]

## Independent practice

Complete Practice Set F (in `practice.md`). Then answer this application question in your
notebook: a classmate claims "the ΔEN rules are useless because HF breaks them." Write a
three-sentence reply defending the rules as guides: when they work, what they are for, and
what to do when evidence disagrees with them.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) why is HF covalent despite ΔEN = 1.78 — give two reasons;
(b) correct "a molecule of salt" in one sentence; (c) state the rule for deciding between a
bare classification and a classification with stated uncertainty.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** for each error, write the correction as "Wrong because ___.
  Right version: ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** sort error cards into "wrong word", "wrong count", and "wrong
  forces" piles before writing corrections.
- **Extension:** research (resource pack) one more exception to a simple rule in this unit
  (for example, aluminium chloride's bonding, or why some ionic compounds are insoluble),
  and write a paragraph that states the rule, the exception, and the evidence.
