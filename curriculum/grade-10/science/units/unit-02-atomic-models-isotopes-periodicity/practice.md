# Practice Sets A–F — Unit 02

Learner-facing practice. Solutions are in `answer-key.md` (adult only) — do not look at them
until you have attempted every item. Items marked **Guided** include a hint; **Independent**
items do not. Show all working for calculations, including unit (u) where a mass is asked.

## Set A — Particles, notation, and ions (Lesson 01)

**Guided**

- **A1.** An atom has 11 protons, 12 neutrons, and 10 electrons. Give its element, mass number,
  charge, and full isotope notation. [Start: Z = 11.]
- **A2.** An atom has 17 protons, 20 neutrons, and 17 electrons. Give its element, mass number,
  charge, and full isotope notation. [Charge = protons − electrons.]
- **A3.** An atom has 8 protons, 10 neutrons, and 10 electrons. Give its element, mass number,
  charge, and full isotope notation. [Neutrons = A − Z, run backwards: A = 8 + 10.]

**Independent**

- **A4.** An atom has 26 protons, 30 neutrons, and 26 electrons. Give its element, mass number,
  charge, and notation.
- **A5.** An atom has 35 protons, 46 neutrons, and 36 electrons. Give its element, mass number,
  charge, and notation.
- **A6.** Sam says: "Carbon-14 and nitrogen-14 are isotopes of each other, because they both
  have mass number 14." Explain Sam's error, using the words "protons" and "element."

## Set B — Models and evidence (Lesson 02)

**Guided**

- **B1.** Dalton's model described atoms as solid, indivisible spheres. Which observation from
  Thomson's cathode-ray work contradicts "indivisible," and why? [The beam's particles came
  from every material tested.]
- **B2.** In the gold-foil experiment, most alpha particles passed straight through, a few
  deflected widely, and a very few bounced back. State the two conclusions Rutherford drew
  about the atom's structure. [Think: empty space, and a concentrated center.]
- **B3.** Use the gold-foil illustration in Lesson 02 (or its text-only alternative) and answer:
  (a) What do most particles do? (b) What do a few do? (c) What does each part of the pattern
  imply about the atom?

**Independent**

- **B4.** Bohr's model explained hydrogen's spectrum well but failed for larger atoms. What
  replaced Bohr's fixed circular orbits in the modern model, and what does that replacement
  mean for "where the electron is"?
- **B5.** Put these in order and, for each step after the first, name the evidence that forced
  the change: quantum-mechanical model, Thomson's model, Bohr's model, Rutherford's model,
  Dalton's model.
- **B6.** A student says: "Rutherford proved atoms are mostly solid, because most particles
  passed through the foil." Identify the error and correct it in one sentence.

## Set C — Electron configurations (Lesson 03)

**Guided**

- **C1.** Write the electron configuration of lithium (Z = 3) and state its valence electrons.
  [Fill 1s, then 2s.]
- **C2.** Write the electron configuration of oxygen (Z = 8) and state its valence electrons.
  [Remember: valence means the whole outer level, not just the last subshell.]
- **C3.** Write the electron configuration of calcium (Z = 20) and state its valence electrons.
  [Filling order: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s.]

**Independent**

- **C4.** Write the electron configuration of chlorine (Z = 17), state its valence electrons,
  and predict the ion it most likely forms, with a reason.
- **C5.** A neutral atom has the configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p³. Identify the element, give its
  group, and state its valence count.
- **C6.** Predict the ion formed by potassium and the ion formed by sulfur, each with a
  one-sentence valence reason.

## Set D — Isotopes and average atomic mass (Lesson 04)

Show every calculation step, including the fraction conversion and the two sanity checks
(fractions sum to 1; answer lies between the isotope masses).

**Guided**

- **D1.** Boron: boron-10, 10 u, 19.9%; boron-11, 11 u, 80.1%. Calculate the average atomic mass.
  [Whole-number masses are given, so expect a small difference from the printed table value.]
- **D2.** Chlorine (exact masses): chlorine-35, 34.96885 u, 75.76%; chlorine-37, 36.96590 u,
  24.24%. Calculate the average atomic mass.
- **D3.** Copper (exact masses): copper-63, 62.9296 u, 69.15%; copper-65, 64.9278 u, 30.85%.
  Calculate the average atomic mass.

**Independent**

- **D4.** Carbon: carbon-12, 12 u, 98.93%; carbon-13, 13 u, 1.07%. Calculate the average atomic
  mass.
- **D5.** Bromine (exact masses): bromine-79, 78.9183 u, 50.69%; bromine-81, 80.9163 u, 49.31%.
  Calculate the average atomic mass.
- **D6.** Explain, in three sentences, why the periodic table's atomic mass for chlorine is
  35.45 u rather than 35 u or 37 u — and why using whole-number masses (35 and 37 u) in the
  same calculation gives 35.48 u instead.

## Set E — The table, trends, and predictions (Lessons 05–06)

**Guided**

- **E1.** For each element, give group, period, block, and metal/nonmetal/metalloid class:
  (a) sodium, (b) selenium, (c) calcium, (d) bromine, (e) neon.
- **E2.** Rank Li, Na, K by atomic radius (smallest to largest) and by ionization energy
  (lowest to highest), giving one reason for each ranking.
- **E3.** Rank F, Cl, Br by electronegativity (lowest to highest), with one reason.

**Independent**

- **E4.** A metal (M) and a nonmetal (X) form an ionic compound. Magnesium (group 2) and oxygen
  (group 16) are M and X. Predict the ions formed and the compound's formula, showing the
  charge balance.
- **E5.** Predict the ions formed by aluminum and by oxygen, each with a valence reason. Then
  predict the formula of aluminum oxide, showing the charge balance.
- **E6.** An unknown element is in group 17, period 4. Name it, state its valence count,
  predict its likely ion, predict whether it bonds ionically with potassium (with a reason),
  and state whether it is a metal, nonmetal, or metalloid.

## Set F — Mixed practice and error analysis (whole unit)

- **F1.** How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in ²⁴₁₂Mg²⁺?
- **F2.** Write the electron configuration of sulfur (Z = 16), state its valence count, and
  predict its likely ion with a reason.
- **F3.** Lithium (exact masses): lithium-6, 6.015 u, 7.59%; lithium-7, 7.016 u, 92.41%.
  Calculate the average atomic mass, with all steps and checks.
- **F4.** A student writes: "Atomic radius increases across a period because more electrons
  are added." Correct the trend and the reasoning in two sentences.
- **F5.** For each process, state whether it is a chemical change or a nuclear change, and
  whether the element changes: (a) burning carbon in oxygen to form carbon dioxide;
  (b) carbon-14 decaying to nitrogen-14.
- **F6.** Open explanation (HS-PS1-1): potassium and calcium sit side by side in period 4.
  Predict which one loses an electron more easily (has the lower first ionization energy),
  and justify your prediction from electron patterns — valence count, nuclear charge, and
  shielding — in at least three sentences.
