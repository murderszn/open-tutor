# Lesson 01 — What an Atom Is Made Of

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can take any atom or ion — given as a particle count, a
name, or a symbol — and state its protons, neutrons, electrons, mass number, charge, and isotope
notation.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** periodic table (print or the RSC table in the resource
pack), notebook, Practice Set A.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. What do the words "proton", "neutron", and "electron" mean to you, from grade-9 science?
2. A carbon atom is often written as ¹²₆C. Which number do you think counts protons, and why?

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read the grade-9 idea first: an atom is the smallest unit
of an element that keeps that element's chemical identity, and it is made of smaller particles.)

## Teaching

### The three particles

Every atom has a tiny, dense **nucleus** at its center and electrons around it. The nucleus
holds two kinds of particles:

- **Protons:** positive charge (+1 each), mass about 1 u (unified atomic mass unit).
- **Neutrons:** no charge, mass about 1 u.
- **Electrons:** negative charge (−1 each), mass about 1/2000 u — so small that nearly all of
  an atom's mass sits in its nucleus.

Three numbers describe any atom or ion:

- **Atomic number (Z)** = number of protons. Z *defines* the element. Every atom with 6
  protons is carbon; every atom with 17 protons is chlorine. Change the proton count and you
  change the element itself.
- **Mass number (A)** = protons + neutrons. It counts the heavy particles in the nucleus.
- **Charge** = protons − electrons (because each proton contributes +1 and each electron −1).
  A neutral atom has equal protons and electrons, so its charge is 0. An **ion** is an atom
  with unequal protons and electrons: a **cation** is positive (lost electrons), an **anion**
  is negative (gained electrons).

**Isotope notation** packs all of this into one symbol:

> ᵃzxⁿ

where A (mass number) sits upper-left, Z (atomic number) lower-left, X is the element symbol,
and the charge sits upper-right (written as ⁺, ²⁺, ⁻, ²⁻, or omitted when neutral). Example:
³⁷₁₇Cl⁻ means: chlorine (Z = 17, so 17 protons), mass number 37 (so 37 − 17 = 20 neutrons),
charge −1 (so electrons = protons + 1 = 18).

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element — same Z, same protons — with different neutron
counts, hence different mass numbers. Carbon-12 (6 protons, 6 neutrons) and carbon-14
(6 protons, 8 neutrons) are isotopes of carbon. Changing neutrons never changes the element;
it changes the mass and, sometimes, the stability.

### Worked example 1 — from particle counts to notation

*An atom has 11 protons, 12 neutrons, and 10 electrons. Identify it and write its notation.*

Step 1 — Element: 11 protons means Z = 11. On the periodic table, Z = 11 is sodium (Na).
Step 2 — Mass number: A = 11 + 12 = 23.
Step 3 — Charge: 11 − 10 = +1. It has lost one electron, so it is a cation.
Step 4 — Notation: ²³₁₁Na⁺.

Check: protons (11) + neutrons (12) = 23 ✓; charge +1 matches one fewer electron than protons ✓.

### Worked example 2 — from notation to particle counts

*How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺?*

Step 1 — Z = 26, so 26 protons. The element is iron (Fe).
Step 2 — Neutrons = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30.
Step 3 — Charge is +3, so electrons = protons − charge = 26 − 3 = 23.
Answer: 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 23 electrons.

Notice the pattern used in both directions: **neutrons = A − Z**, and **electrons = protons −
charge**. If you can say those two sentences, you can solve any problem of this type.

### A common trap

"Mass number" and "atomic mass" are not the same thing. Mass number (A) is a *count* — a whole
number of protons plus neutrons in one particular isotope. Atomic mass (on the periodic table)
is a *weighted average* over all naturally occurring isotopes, which is why it is usually not
a whole number. Lesson 04 explains that average; do not round the table value and call it a
mass number.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or a partner; hints are in brackets.

1. An atom has 17 protons, 18 neutrons, and 18 electrons. Give its element, mass number,
   charge, and notation. [Start: Z = 17. Charge = protons − electrons.]
2. How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in ⁴⁰₂₀Ca²⁺? [Neutrons = A − Z. Electrons =
   protons − charge.]
3. Two atoms both have 8 protons. One has 8 neutrons, the other 10. Are they isotopes? Are
   they the same element? Explain both answers. [Same Z means same element; different neutrons
   means isotopes.]

## Independent practice

Complete Practice Set A (in `practice.md`). Then answer this application question in your
notebook: a hospital tracer uses an isotope of iodine with 53 protons and 78 neutrons. Write
its isotope notation (neutral atom) and explain, in one sentence, why it is still iodine even
though its mass number is 131, not 127.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) write the formula for neutrons from A and Z; (b) an atom has 9
protons and 10 electrons — what is its charge, and is it a cation or an anion?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** say each symbol aloud as a sentence ("mass 37, atomic number
  17, chlorine, minus one") before writing it.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the atom with counters (red = protons, grey = neutrons,
  blue = electrons) and read the numbers off the piles instead of writing notation first.
- **Extension:** research (resource pack) why technetium has no stable isotopes, and write two
  sentences on what "no stable isotope" means for its place on the periodic table.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) learners swap A and Z — drill "A is on top because the mass is the
bigger number"; (2) learners think gaining a neutron changes the element — stress that only
protons define the element; (3) learners compute charge as electrons − protons — anchor with
"protons are positive, so protons come first." Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`, Set A.
