# Lesson 02 — Evidence Forces New Models

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can retell the Dalton → Thomson → Rutherford →
Bohr → quantum-mechanical story and, for each change, name the evidence that forced it.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** generated image `../assets/gold-foil-experiment.png`,
notebook, timeline cards (Session 4 activity).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. If you could not see inside a sealed box, what kind of test could still tell you something
   about what is inside it?
2. Why would a scientist abandon a model that had worked well for years?

## Teaching

Science does not photograph atoms directly and then describe them. It *probes* them — fires
things at them, watches what comes back, and revises the model when the results do not fit.
This lesson follows five revisions. At each step, ask: "What did they expect, what did they
see, and what had to change?"

### Dalton — the solid sphere (around 1808)

John Dalton proposed that all matter is made of tiny, indivisible, solid spheres, and that all
atoms of one element are identical while atoms of different elements differ in mass. His model
explained why elements combine in fixed mass ratios (for example, water always forms from
hydrogen and oxygen in the same mass ratio). It treated the atom as a single solid ball with no
internal parts. **What it could not explain:** anything smaller than an atom — because it
claimed nothing smaller existed.

### Thomson — the "plum pudding" (1897)

J. J. Thomson worked with cathode-ray tubes: glass tubes with most of the air removed, with a
voltage across them. A glowing beam traveled from the negative end (cathode) toward the
positive end, and it bent in electric and magnetic fields in the way a stream of *negatively
charged* particles would bend. Crucially, the same beam appeared whatever gas or metal was in
the tube. Thomson concluded the beam was made of particles smaller than any atom, present in
all matter — the **electron**.

Dalton's solid sphere could not contain smaller particles, so the model had to change. Thomson
proposed a positively charged "pudding" with negative electrons embedded in it like plums —
overall neutral, with no nucleus and no empty space. **What it could not explain:** the
gold-foil result below.

### Rutherford — the nuclear atom (1909–1911)

Ernest Rutherford's team (Geiger and Marsden) fired alpha particles — small, heavy, positively
charged particles — at an extremely thin sheet of gold foil, with a detector screen around it
to catch where the particles went.

- **Expected (Thomson model):** the positive charge is spread thinly through each atom, so the
  heavy alpha particles should plow through with only tiny deflections, like bullets through
  mist.
- **Observed:** almost all particles passed straight through, but a small number deflected at
  large angles, and a very small number bounced almost straight back toward the source.

Rutherford's famous reaction was that it was like firing a shell at tissue paper and having it
bounce back at you. The only explanation: the atom's positive charge and most of its mass are
concentrated in a tiny, dense **nucleus**, and the rest of the atom is mostly empty space.
Most alpha particles miss the nucleus entirely and pass through the empty space; the rare one
that scores a near-direct hit on a nucleus is violently repelled. In 1932 James Chadwick added
the missing neutral particle in the nucleus: the **neutron**.

### Bohr — quantized orbits (1913)

Niels Bohr studied hydrogen's spectrum: when hydrogen gas is energized, it emits light only at
certain sharp colors, not a continuous rainbow. Rutherford's model could not explain why only
certain colors appear, nor why orbiting electrons — which should radiate energy and spiral
into the nucleus — do not collapse the atom. Bohr proposed that electrons occupy only certain
allowed energy levels (shells) and jump between them, absorbing or emitting exact packets of
energy. His model explained hydrogen's spectrum beautifully. **What it could not explain:**
the spectra of larger atoms, which Bohr's neat circular orbits could not predict.

### The quantum-mechanical model (1920s to today)

The modern model, developed by Schrödinger and others, drops fixed orbits entirely. Electrons
occupy **orbitals**: three-dimensional regions where an electron is *likely* to be found. An
orbital has a shape (sphere, dumbbell, and more complex shapes) and an energy, but you cannot
say exactly where an electron is or how fast it is moving at the same time. This model predicts
the spectra of many-electron atoms, chemical bonding patterns, and the structure of the
periodic table itself — which is why Lesson 03 organizes electrons by orbitals and shells.

**The pattern to remember:** a model is not a guess that gets replaced by "the truth." Each
model was the best explanation of the evidence available, and each survived until new evidence
broke it. The current model survives because it explains more evidence than its predecessors.

### Worked example 1 — reasoning from the gold-foil result

*Thomson's model predicts that alpha particles fired at gold foil deflect only slightly. The
experiment finds a few particles bouncing back. What must be true about the atom's charge?*

Reasoning: a slight deflection would mean the positive charge is spread out and weak at any
one point. A violent bounce-back means the particle met a strong repulsion concentrated in a
very small region. Conclusion: the positive charge is concentrated, not spread out — a nucleus.
This is the core move of the lesson: **the pattern of deflections tells you the arrangement of
charge**.

### Worked example 2 — which model does this observation break?

*A new experiment shows that hydrogen emits light only at four specific visible colors. Which
earlier model cannot explain this, and what replaces it?*

Reasoning: Rutherford's nuclear model has electrons that could orbit at any distance and
radiate any energy, so it predicts a continuous smear of colors, not sharp lines. Bohr's model
replaces free orbits with allowed energy levels: light is emitted only when an electron drops
between two allowed levels, so only certain colors appear. The sharp lines break Rutherford's
model and support Bohr's.

## Guided practice

1. Put these in order and name the evidence that forced each step after Dalton: Bohr,
   quantum-mechanical, Rutherford, Thomson. [Order: Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr, quantum.]
2. A student says, "Rutherford proved atoms are mostly solid, because most particles passed
   through." Identify the error and correct it. [Passing through means mostly *empty*; the
   bounce-backs prove a small dense center.]
3. Why did Thomson's cathode-ray result break Dalton's model, even though Dalton's model
   explained combining ratios well? [A model must explain *all* reliable evidence; a single
   smaller particle inside atoms contradicts "indivisible."]

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "the gold foil experiment proved the nucleus" — it *supported* the
nuclear model by falsifying Thomson's; models are supported, not proved; (2) learners draw
Bohr orbits as the "true" atom — Lesson 03 corrects this with orbitals; (3) learners think
"most particles pass through" means the foil is mostly holes — the foil is solid gold; the
empty space is *inside* each atom. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`, Set B.
