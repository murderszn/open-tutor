# Lesson 06 — Periodic Trends, Predictions, and Nuclear Change

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can predict relative size, ionization energy,
electronegativity, ion charges, and bond types for main-group elements from their table
position — justifying each prediction from electron patterns — and explain how nuclear change
differs from chemical change.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** periodic table, Practice Set E (second half) and Set F.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Sodium loses its outer electron easily; neon does not lose any electron at all. What is
   different about their outer levels?
2. Fluorine attracts shared electrons more strongly than lithium does. Which atom's nucleus
   holds its outer electrons more tightly, and why might that be?

## Teaching

### Three trends, one explanation

All three trends below have the same two causes: **nuclear charge** (more protons pull harder)
and **shielding/distance** (inner electrons shield outer electrons from the nucleus, and
farther electrons feel a weaker pull). Keep those two causes in mind and every trend becomes
a sentence you can derive, not a fact to memorize.

- **Atomic radius** (size of the atom): increases **down** a group — each step adds a new
  energy level, so the outer electrons sit farther out. Decreases **across** a period — protons
  are added while electrons join the same level, so the stronger nuclear pull draws the
  electron cloud inward.
- **Ionization energy** (energy needed to remove an electron): the *opposite* pattern.
  Decreases down a group (outer electron farther away and better shielded, so easier to
  remove); increases across a period (stronger pull on the same level, so harder to remove).
  Noble gases have the highest ionization energies in their periods: removing an electron
  from a full level is hardest of all.
- **Electronegativity** (an atom's pull on shared electrons in a bond): same pattern as
  ionization energy — decreases down a group, increases across a period. Fluorine, at the top
  right of the main groups, is the most electronegative element. (A note for precision: the
  small dips in ionization energy between beryllium/boron and nitrogen/oxygen come from
  subshell stability effects; they are extension material only and are never assessed in this
  unit — the assessed claim is the overall trend, per the HS-PS1-1 boundary of relative
  trends.)

**Reactivity** follows from the same picture but points in opposite directions for metals and
nonmetals, which confuses learners until they see why: metals react by *losing* electrons, so
the most reactive metals are those that lose electrons most easily — bottom-left (group 1,
lower periods). Nonmetals react by *gaining* electrons, so the most reactive nonmetals are
those that pull electrons hardest — top-right (group 17, upper periods, fluorine most of
all). Same electron logic, opposite corners of the table.

### From trends to predictions (HS-PS1-1)

The standard this unit serves asks you to *use the table as a model to predict relative
properties from outer-electron patterns*. A prediction is only complete with its justification.
Compare:

- Weak: "Chlorine forms Cl⁻." (True, but unjustified.)
- Complete: "Chlorine is group 17 with 7 valence electrons; gaining one electron completes
  its outer level, so it forms Cl⁻ and bonds ionically with metals such as sodium."

**Ion charges for main-group elements** follow the "nearest full level" rule: groups 1–2 lose
electrons (→ +1, +2); group 13 loses 3 (→ +3); groups 15–17 gain electrons to reach 8
(→ −3, −2, −1); group 18 typically forms no ion. Group 14 (4 valence electrons) usually
shares rather than gaining or losing outright.

**Bond types** follow from who is bonding: metal + nonmetal → **ionic** (electron transfer);
nonmetal + nonmetal → **covalent** (electron sharing), polar when the two nonmetals pull
unequally, nonpolar when they pull equally (same element, or very similar pull). Metal +
metal → **metallic** bonding (Unit 03's topic, previewed here only). You will use these
predictions in the project and the unit assessment; Unit 03 teaches bonding in full.

### Worked example 1 — ranking with reasons

*Rank Li, Na, and K by atomic radius (smallest to largest) and by ionization energy (lowest
to highest), giving one reason for each ranking.*

Radius: Li < Na < K. Reason: all are group 1 (1 valence electron), but each step down adds an
energy level, so the outer electron sits farther from the nucleus.
Ionization energy: K < Na < Li (lowest to highest). Reason: the same added levels and
shielding make the outer electron easier to remove as you go down the group. The two rankings
are mirror images — that mirror relationship is itself a check on your reasoning.

### Worked example 2 — a full prediction

*Predict the ion formed by magnesium, the bond type in magnesium oxide (MgO), and the
formula's logic.*

Magnesium is group 2 with 2 valence electrons; losing both reaches the full level below, so
it forms **Mg²⁺**. Oxygen is group 16 with 6 valence electrons; gaining 2 completes its level,
so it forms **O²⁻**. A metal (Mg) plus a nonmetal (O) gives an **ionic** bond, and the charges
balance one-to-one: Mg²⁺ + O²⁻ → **MgO**. Every step cites the electron pattern — that is what
HS-PS1-1 assessment looks like in this unit.

### Nuclear change is a different kind of change (enrichment strand, HS-PS1-8)

A **chemical change** rearranges atoms: bonds break and form, electrons move, but every
nucleus keeps its protons — so no element changes into another element. Burning carbon
(C + O₂ → CO₂) is chemical: carbon atoms stay carbon atoms.

A **nuclear change** rearranges the nucleus itself: protons or neutrons change, so the element
*can* change, and the energy involved is vastly larger per atom than in a chemical reaction.
This unit treats it qualitatively only — no energy calculations, per the HS-PS1-8 boundary:

- **Alpha decay:** the nucleus ejects an alpha particle (2 protons + 2 neutrons, a helium
  nucleus, ⁴₂He). Mass number drops by 4, atomic number by 2. Example: ²³⁸₉₂U →
  ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He. Check: 238 = 234 + 4 ✓; 92 = 90 + 2 ✓.
- **Beta decay:** a neutron becomes a proton and ejects a fast electron (the beta particle,
  ⁰₋₁e). Mass number stays the same, atomic number rises by 1. Example: ¹⁴₆C →
  ¹⁴₇N + ⁰₋₁e. Check: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓; 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓. Carbon-14 dating rests on this decay.
- **Gamma emission:** the nucleus releases energy as a gamma ray (⁰₀γ) without changing its
  protons or neutrons — the same isotope, in a lower-energy state.
- **Fission:** a heavy nucleus splits into two lighter nuclei plus neutrons, releasing large
  energy (the process in nuclear power stations). **Fusion:** two light nuclei join into a
  heavier one, releasing large energy (the process powering the Sun). Both are nuclear
  rearrangements, not chemical reactions, and neither is a laboratory activity in this course:
  you model them on paper only.

Safety note: you will never handle radioactive material in this course. Radioactivity is
studied here through equations and models only.

## Guided practice

1. Rank F, Cl, Br by atomic radius (smallest to largest) and by electronegativity (lowest to
   highest), with one reason each. [Radius: F < Cl < Br (new levels down the group);
   electronegativity: Br < Cl < F (pull weakens with distance/shielding).]
2. Predict the ion formed by aluminum and justify it. [Al³⁺: group 13, 3 valence electrons,
   loses all three to reach the full level below.]
3. Balance-check this decay: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He. Show the mass-number and atomic-number
   checks. [238 = 234 + 4; 92 = 90 + 2.]

Without looking back: (a) state the radius trend across a period and down a group; (b) predict

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** for every ranking, write the reason *before* the ranking — if
  the reason is "more levels, farther out," the ranking must follow it.
- **Alternate response mode:** explain one prediction aloud to your adult using the
  "group → valence → gain/lose → ion/bond" chain, then write it down.
- **Extension:** research the ionization-energy dips at boron and oxygen (subshell stability)
  and write two sentences explaining why the overall trend still holds even with small
  exceptions — a good lesson in how science treats exceptions.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "bigger atom = more reactive" for all elements — reactivity's
direction depends on whether the element loses or gains electrons; (2) confusing ionization
energy with electronegativity — one removes an electron, the other pulls on shared
electrons; related but not identical; (3) thinking fusion/fission are chemical because they
"release energy like burning" — the scale and the changed element are the distinguishing
marks. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`, Sets E–F.
