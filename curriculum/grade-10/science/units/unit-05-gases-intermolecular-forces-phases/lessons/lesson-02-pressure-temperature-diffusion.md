# Lesson 02 — Pressure, Temperature, and Diffusion

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can explain gas pressure as molecular collisions, use
kelvin temperatures to predict pressure changes, and predict which of two gases diffuses
faster and why.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, a balloon (optional demonstration), Practice
Set B (first half).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. In your own words, what is gas pressure, according to kinetic molecular theory?
2. If you double the kelvin temperature of a gas in a rigid box, what happens to its average
   kinetic energy? (Lesson 01.)
3. Perfume sprayed in one corner of a room is smelled across the room minutes later, with no
   wind. How did it get there?

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read Lesson 01, postulates 2 and 5, before continuing.)

## Teaching

### Pressure is collisions

Pressure is **force divided by area**: P = F/A. For a gas, the force comes from particles
colliding with the walls. Three things make the pressure go up, and each one follows from the
collision picture:

- **More particles** in the same volume → more collisions per second → higher pressure.
  (Blow more air into a tire and the pressure rises.)
- **Higher temperature** → faster particles → each collision hits harder *and* collisions
  happen more often → higher pressure.
- **Smaller volume** → the same particles have less distance to travel between walls → more
  collisions per second → higher pressure. (Lesson 03 makes this quantitative as Boyle's law.)

Pressure is measured in several units. This unit uses **atmospheres (atm)**, **kilopascals
(kPa)**, and **millimeters of mercury (mmHg, also called torr)**. The conversions are:

> 1 atm = 101.325 kPa = 760 mmHg (exactly 760 torr)

So normal air pressure at sea level is 1.00 atm, about 101 kPa, or 760 mmHg. A tire pressure of
32 psi is about 2.2 atm — tires really are at more than twice atmospheric pressure, which is
why a puncture is dramatic.

### Temperature is motion

Temperature measures the **average kinetic energy** of the particles — nothing else. Two
consequences matter for this unit:

- The kelvin scale is the only scale on which "twice as hot" means "twice the average kinetic
  energy." 200 K really is twice as energetic, on average, as 100 K. 200 °C is *not* twice as
  energetic as 100 °C (473 K vs. 373 K is a factor of only 1.27).
- At the same temperature, **all gases have the same average kinetic energy** — but not the
  same average *speed*. A light particle must move faster than a heavy one to carry the same
  energy (kinetic energy is ½mv², so for fixed energy, smaller m means larger v). That fact
  explains diffusion.

### Diffusion and effusion

**Diffusion** is the spreading of particles through a space or through another gas, driven by
random motion — the perfume crossing the room. **Effusion** is gas escaping through a tiny
hole, such as a pinhole in a balloon. Both are faster for lighter particles. Graham's law makes
it quantitative:

> rate₁ / rate₂ = √(M₂ / M₁), where M is molar mass

So hydrogen (M = 2.02 g/mol) diffuses about 4 times faster than oxygen (M = 32.00 g/mol):
√(32.00/2.02) = √15.8 ≈ 4.0. A hydrogen balloon goes soft faster than an air balloon for
exactly this reason — and a helium balloon (M = 4.00) loses lift faster than an air-filled one.

### Worked example 1 — pressure units

*A weather report gives the pressure as 745 mmHg. Express it in atm and in kPa.*

Step 1 — To atm: 745 mmHg × (1 atm / 760 mmHg) = 0.980 atm.
Step 2 — To kPa: 0.980 atm × (101.325 kPa / 1 atm) = 99.3 kPa.
Check: 745 is slightly below 760, so the answer should be slightly below 1 atm and slightly
below 101 kPa — 0.980 atm and 99.3 kPa both pass.

### Worked example 2 — Graham's law

*At the same temperature, how much faster does ammonia, NH₃ (molar mass 17.03 g/mol), diffuse
than hydrogen chloride, HCl (molar mass 36.46 g/mol)?*

Step 1 — rate(NH₃)/rate(HCl) = √(M(HCl)/M(NH₃)) = √(36.46/17.03).
Step 2 — 36.46/17.03 = 2.141. √2.141 ≈ 1.46.
Answer: ammonia diffuses about **1.46 times faster** than hydrogen chloride. (This is the
classic demonstration: ammonia and hydrogen chloride meet in a tube and form a white ring of
ammonium chloride closer to the HCl end, because the ammonia got there first.)

### A common trap

"Hot air rises because hot particles are lighter." No — the particles are the same; hot gas is
less *dense* because the same number of particles occupies a larger volume (or, at fixed
volume, pushes harder). A hot-air balloon rises because the hot air inside is less dense than
the cool air outside, so the balloon as a whole is buoyant. Do not say the particles
themselves got lighter.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or a partner; hints are in brackets.

1. A rigid tank holds gas at 2.00 atm and 300 K. It is heated to 450 K. What is the new
   pressure? [Pressure is proportional to kelvin temperature at fixed volume: P₂ = P₁ × T₂/T₁.]
2. Convert 2.50 atm to kPa and to mmHg. [1 atm = 101.325 kPa = 760 mmHg.]
3. Which diffuses faster at the same temperature, methane CH₄ (16.04 g/mol) or carbon dioxide
   CO₂ (44.01 g/mol), and by what factor? [Graham's law: lighter is faster; rate ratio =
   √(heavier/lighter).]

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) what three changes raise gas pressure, and why does each one work?
(b) 300 K is how many degrees Celsius? (c) Which is faster, a light gas or a heavy gas at the
same temperature?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** keep a units card on the desk (1 atm = 101.325 kPa = 760 mmHg)
  and read every pressure aloud with its unit until the conversions are automatic.
- **Alternate response mode:** demonstrate diffusion with a drop of food coloring in still
  water (slow, visible spreading) and contrast it with gas diffusion (fast, invisible) — then
  write the comparison in words.
- **Extension:** research (resource pack) why a scuba tank's pressure gauge reads higher on a
  hot day, and write two sentences connecting it to postulate 5.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) learners treat temperature as "how hot something feels" rather than
average kinetic energy — anchor every temperature question with "what are the particles doing?";
(2) learners invert Graham's law and make the heavier gas faster — drill "lighter is faster,
and the ratio uses the *other* gas's mass on top"; (3) learners convert pressure units by
memorizing one direction — insist on writing the conversion factor as a fraction that cancels
the old unit. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`, Set B items B1–B4.
