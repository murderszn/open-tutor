# Lesson 04 — Intermolecular Forces: What Holds Molecules Together

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can distinguish intramolecular bonds from
intermolecular forces, rank London dispersion, dipole–dipole, and hydrogen bonding, and predict
which of two substances has stronger forces from its structure.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, Unit 03 notes on polarity, Practice Set D.

## Teaching

### Inside the molecule vs. between molecules

Keep two ideas apart, because confusing them is the most common error in this unit:

- **Intramolecular bonds** are the bonds *inside* a molecule — the covalent bonds that hold
  its atoms together (e.g., the two O–H bonds inside one water molecule). Breaking them is a
  chemical change.
- **Intermolecular forces (IMFs)** are the attractions *between* molecules — what makes one
  water molecule cling to the next. Overcoming them is a *physical* change: boiling water
  separates H₂O molecules from each other; it does not split them into hydrogen and oxygen.

IMFs are much weaker than covalent or ionic bonds. That is why molecular substances melt and
boil at far lower temperatures than ionic compounds or metals — but among molecular substances,
IMF strength decides almost everything: boiling point, vapor pressure, evaporation rate, and
surface tension.

### The three kinds of intermolecular force

**1. London dispersion forces — present in all molecules.** Electrons move constantly, and at
any instant the electron cloud of a molecule can be lopsided — slightly negative on one side,
slightly positive on the other. That fleeting (temporary) dipole induces a matching lopsidedness
in a neighbor, and the two attract. The attraction is weak and brief, but it is always there.
It gets **stronger with more electrons and larger molecules**, because bigger electron clouds
are easier to distort (more "polarizable"). This is why the noble gases' boiling points rise
down the group, and why large nonpolar molecules can be liquids or solids while small ones are
gases: methane (CH₄, 10 electrons) boils at about −162 °C; octane (C₈H₁₈, 66 electrons) boils
at 126 °C.

**2. Dipole–dipole forces — in polar molecules only.** A polar molecule has a permanent
partial-positive end and a partial-negative end (Unit 03). The positive end of one molecule
attracts the negative end of another. Dipole–dipole forces are generally stronger than London
forces between similar-sized molecules — but a large nonpolar molecule can still out-pull a
small polar one, because London forces grow with size. Compare like with like, and always check
molar mass as well as polarity.

**3. Hydrogen bonding — a special, strong dipole–dipole force.** When hydrogen is bonded
*directly* to nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine (N, O, F — the three small, highly electronegative
elements), the hydrogen carries a large partial-positive charge and is attracted strongly to a
lone pair on N, O, or F of a neighboring molecule. Hydrogen bonds are the strongest of the
three IMFs — roughly 5–10 times stronger than ordinary dipole–dipole forces, but still far
weaker than a covalent bond. Water (H₂O) is the classic case: each water molecule can form up
to four hydrogen bonds, which is why water boils at 100 °C while H₂S — same shape, no hydrogen
bonding because sulfur is not electronegative enough — boils at about −60 °C.

A memory aid: **hydrogen bonding needs H attached to N, O, or F.** H bonded to carbon or
sulfur does not count. And hydrogen bonding is *between* molecules, not within one — a common
exam trap.

### Strength order and what it predicts

For similar-sized molecules, the ranking is:

> **London dispersion < dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding**

Stronger IMFs mean: **higher boiling point** (more energy needed to pull molecules apart),
**lower vapor pressure** (fewer molecules escape the surface), **slower evaporation**, and
**higher surface tension**. Weaker IMFs mean the opposite. Lesson 06 tests this directly: if
the ranking is right, acetone (dipole–dipole + London, no hydrogen bonding) should evaporate
faster than ethanol (hydrogen bonding) and water (extensive hydrogen bonding) — and the verified
data table in the unit README agrees: vapor pressures at 25 °C are acetone 229.5 mmHg, ethanol
59.0 mmHg, water 23.8 mmHg.

### Worked example 1 — ranking by structure

*Rank the following by increasing boiling point, and justify: CH₄ (methane), NH₃ (ammonia),
H₂O (water).*

Step 1 — Identify the forces: CH₄ is nonpolar → London only. NH₃ is polar, and H is bonded
to N → hydrogen bonding. H₂O is polar, and H is bonded to O → hydrogen bonding.
Step 2 — Compare the hydrogen-bonded pair: water forms more hydrogen bonds per molecule than
ammonia (two O–H bonds and two lone pairs, versus one lone pair on nitrogen), and oxygen is
more electronegative than nitrogen — so water's network is stronger.
Step 3 — Ranking (lowest to highest boiling point): **CH₄ < NH₃ < H₂O**. Check against the
reference values: CH₄ about −162 °C, NH₃ about −33 °C, H₂O 100 °C. ✓

### Worked example 2 — the size trap

*Pentane (C₅H₁₂, nonpolar, molar mass 72 g/mol) boils at 36 °C. Acetone (C₃H₆O, polar, molar
mass 58 g/mol) boils at 56 °C. A student says "polar always beats nonpolar." Is that right?*

Step 1 — The student is *mostly* right here: acetone's dipole–dipole forces beat pentane's
London forces despite pentane being the bigger molecule — 56 °C > 36 °C.
Step 2 — But the rule is not absolute: a much larger nonpolar molecule can beat a small polar
one. Octane (C₈H₁₈, nonpolar, 114 g/mol) boils at 126 °C — far above acetone — because its
large electron cloud makes its London forces very strong.
Step 3 — The correct habit: compare **both** polarity and size; never rank on one factor alone.

### A common trap

"Hydrogen bonding is a bond, so boiling water breaks bonds." No. Boiling overcomes
*intermolecular* attractions; the O–H covalent bonds inside each water molecule stay intact,
which is why steam is still H₂O. If boiling broke O–H bonds, steam would be a mixture of
hydrogen and oxygen gas. Keep the words straight: **bonds** are inside molecules;
**forces** are between them.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or a partner; hints are in brackets.

1. For each substance, name all IMFs present: (a) CO₂, (b) HCl, (c) CH₃OH (methanol).
   [Nonpolar → London only. Polar → London + dipole–dipole. H on O/N/F → add hydrogen bonding.]
2. Rank these by increasing boiling point and justify: F₂, HF, H₂O. [F₂: London only, tiny.
   HF: hydrogen bonding. H₂O: hydrogen bonding, more extensive network.]
3. Explain why ethanol (C₂H₅OH, 78 °C) boils higher than dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃, −24 °C)
   even though both have the formula C₂H₆O. [Same atoms, different arrangement: ethanol has
   an O–H group and hydrogen-bonds; the ether has no O–H group.]

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** keep a two-column card — "inside the molecule (bonds)" vs.
  "between molecules (forces)" — and sort every new term onto it before writing anything else.
- **Alternate response mode:** build molecules with a modeling kit (or balls and sticks) and
  physically show a hydrogen bond as a dotted line *between* two models, never inside one.
- **Extension:** research (resource pack) why water's hydrogen-bond network also explains its
  unusually high surface tension — enough to float a steel needle — and write two sentences on
  it.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) learners call hydrogen bonding an intramolecular bond — the
"between, not within" correction must be repeated in Lessons 05 and 06; (2) learners rank by
polarity alone and ignore size — Worked example 2 is the antidote; (3) learners think all
molecules with O or N hydrogen-bond — stress that the H must be *bonded to* N, O, or F, not
merely near one (dimethyl ether has oxygen but no hydrogen bonding). Answer-key reference:
