# Lesson 06 — Bulk Properties Investigation: Inferring Invisible Forces (HS-PS1-3)

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can plan and conduct an investigation that compares
bulk properties of liquids to infer the relative strength of the electrical forces between
their particles — and state honestly what the evidence can and cannot prove.

**Time:** ~50 minutes (plus the project sessions). **Materials:** safety goggles, three watch
glasses or ceramic tiles, dropper, water, ethanol (adult dispenses), acetone-based nail polish
remover (adult dispenses), stopwatch or clock, notebook, balance if available (optional).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. State the HS-PS1-3 performance expectation in your own words. (Unit README.)
2. Rank water, ethanol, and acetone by IMF strength, and predict their order of evaporation
   rate. (Lessons 04–05.)
3. What variables must be kept the same to make an evaporation comparison fair?

(If the readiness check is shaky, re-read Lessons 04 and 05 before starting — this lesson is
the investigation those lessons were building toward.)

## Teaching

### The question and the plan

**Question:** Do the bulk properties of three liquids — evaporation rate and (from reference
data) boiling point and vapor pressure — let us infer the relative strength of the electrical
forces between their particles?

**Prediction (from Lesson 04):** water has the strongest IMFs (extensive hydrogen bonding),
ethanol is next (hydrogen bonding, fewer bonds per molecule), acetone is weakest (dipole–dipole
and London only, no hydrogen bonding). So the predicted evaporation order, fastest to slowest,
is **acetone > ethanol > water**, and the predicted boiling-point order is the reverse.

**Variables:** independent variable — the liquid (water / ethanol / acetone). Dependent
variable — evaporation time for an identical drop, or mass lost in 10 minutes. Controlled
variables — drop size (same dropper, same number of drops), container (identical watch
glasses), surface, temperature, air movement (same room, no fan, no breathing on the dishes),
and starting time. Three trials per liquid.

### Safety — read before touching anything

- Goggles on for everyone in the room, for the whole investigation.
- The **adult dispenses the ethanol and the acetone**; the learner never handles those bottles.
- Small drops only (about 0.5 mL — 10 drops from a dropper). Work in a well-ventilated room.
  **No flames, heat, or sparks anywhere nearby** — ethanol and acetone are flammable. Cap the
  bottles immediately after use.
- **Never** substitute hexane, ether, or any other solvent: they appear in this unit only as
  reference data.
- If ventilation is poor, goggles are unavailable, or anything is uncertain, do the simulation
  version instead: the PhET "States of Matter" simulation plus the practice dataset in
  `project.md`. The objectives are identical.

### Procedure (bench version)

1. Label three identical watch glasses A (water), B (ethanol), C (acetone). Put them side by
   side on the same surface.
2. The adult places 10 drops of each liquid in its dish; start the stopwatch when the last
   dish is filled.
3. Watch without touching or blowing on the dishes. Record the time when each dish is fully
   dry (no visible liquid). If a dish is not dry after 15 minutes, record ">15 min" and move
   on — do not wait all day.
4. Repeat for two more trials with clean, dry dishes. Record all three trials in a data table
   with units (minutes, or minutes:seconds).
5. If a balance is available, weigh each dish before and after 10 minutes instead, and record
   mass lost in grams — a stronger measurement than "time to dry," because it does not depend
   on judging the last drop.

### Worked example 1 — from data to inference

*Three trials give these drying times: acetone 1.5, 1.8, 1.6 min (mean 1.6 min); ethanol 6.2,
5.8, 6.0 min (mean 6.0 min); water 14.5, 15.2, 14.8 min (mean 14.8 min). What is inferred?*

Step 1 — Order the means: acetone fastest (1.6 min), then ethanol (6.0 min), then water
(14.8 min).
Step 2 — Connect to forces: faster evaporation means molecules escape more easily, which means
weaker attractions holding them in the liquid. So the inferred IMF strength order is
**water > ethanol > acetone**.
Step 3 — Cross-check with the reference data: boiling points water 100 °C > ethanol 78.3 °C >
acetone 56.3 °C, and vapor pressures at 25 °C water 23.8 < ethanol 59.0 < acetone 229.5 mmHg.
Both independent bulk properties agree with the evaporation inference. Three agreeing lines of
evidence make the claim strong.
Step 4 — State the limit: this ranks *relative* strength. It does not measure a force in
newtons, and it does not name the force type by itself — the names (hydrogen bonding vs.
dipole–dipole) come from the structures in Lesson 04, not from the stopwatch.

### Worked example 2 — spotting a bad inference

*A student runs one trial, with different drop sizes, on a warm windowsill, and finds ethanol
drying faster than acetone. They conclude ethanol has weaker IMFs. What went wrong?*

Step 1 — Controls failed: different drop sizes mean different amounts to evaporate, and a warm
windowsill with sunlight and drafts changes the temperature and air movement between trials.
Step 2 — One trial is not enough: a single measurement cannot show whether the result is
repeatable — the three-trial means in Worked example 1 differ by only ±0.2 min within a liquid,
so a reversal would be a real signal, not noise.
Step 3 — The conclusion contradicts two independent reference datasets (boiling points and
vapor pressures). When one shaky measurement disagrees with established data, the first suspect
is the method, not the data. Fix the controls and repeat before believing the surprise.

### A common trap

"The investigation proves water has hydrogen bonds." No — it *supports* the claim that water's
interparticle forces are the strongest of the three, which is consistent with hydrogen bonding.
Proof would need structural evidence (spectroscopy, diffraction) that this investigation does
not provide. HS-PS1-3 asks for an *inference from bulk evidence*, and honest science names the
gap between inference and proof. Write the inference and the limit in the same paragraph.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or a partner; hints are in brackets.

1. List every controlled variable in this investigation and, for each one, say what would go
   wrong if it were not controlled. [Drop size, dish, temperature, air movement, timing.]
2. A group reports one trial: water 12 min, ethanol 5 min, acetone 2 min. The means are
   plausible, but what should the adult ask before accepting the inference? [How many trials?
   Were the drops equal? Was the room still?]
3. Suppose a fourth liquid dried in 0.8 min — faster than acetone. What would you infer about
   its IMFs, and what reference data would you check next? [Weaker IMFs than acetone; check
   its boiling point and vapor pressure against the table.]

## Independent practice

Complete Practice Set F (in `practice.md`) and begin the project (`project.md`, Parts A–C).
Then answer this application question in your notebook: a puddle of water and a puddle of
gasoline dry at very different rates on a driveway. Using what this investigation taught you,
predict which dries faster and what that implies about the forces between its molecules — and
name one control you would need if you tested it for real. (Do not test gasoline at home:
it is far too flammable for this investigation.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) what is the independent variable in this investigation? (b) Name
two controlled variables and why each matters. (c) What does faster evaporation imply about
IMF strength, and what can the investigation *not* prove?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** write the plan as a numbered list before starting, and check
  off each step as you do it — the list is the procedure.
- **Alternate response mode:** if the bench version is unavailable, run the simulation version
  (PhET "States of Matter": heat the same substance in each phase and compare; plus the
  practice dataset in `project.md`) and write the same inference from that evidence.
- **Extension:** design (on paper only) a second HS-PS1-3 investigation using surface tension
  — counting drops on a coin — for the same three liquids, and predict the order of drop
  counts.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) learners treat a single trial as proof — require three trials and a
mean before any inference is written; (2) learners confuse "evaporates faster" with "boils at a
lower temperature" as if they were different claims — they are two views of the same force
ranking, and the lesson should connect them explicitly; (3) learners overclaim ("this proves
hydrogen bonding") — the inference-vs-proof distinction is a grading point in the project
rubric. Safety is non-negotiable: if the adult is uncomfortable with acetone at the bench,
use the simulation version with no penalty. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`, Set F.
