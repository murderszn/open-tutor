## Practice Set A — Kinetic molecular theory

1. (a) 273 K. (b) 298 K. (c) 233 K. (d) 373 K. (K = °C + 273.)
2. (a) The average kinetic energy doubles (600 K is twice 300 K). (b) The pressure doubles
   (faster particles hit the fixed walls more often and harder).
3. Mistake: 0 °C is 273 K, not 0 K. Correct statement: at 0 °C the particles still move with
   substantial kinetic energy; motion would stop only at 0 K (−273 °C). Cooling from 373 K to
   273 K lowers the average kinetic energy to 273/373 ≈ 0.73 of its old value — about a 27%
   drop, not a stop.
4. Box Y (400 K). Its particles have twice the average kinetic energy of Box X's (400/200 = 2).
5. Postulate 1: a gas is mostly empty space, so there is room to squeeze the particles closer
   together. A liquid's particles are already close together with little empty space, so there
   is almost nothing to remove.
6. Heating the can raises the temperature, so the particles move faster and hit the walls more
   often and harder; in a fixed volume the pressure rises, and if it rises enough the can
   bursts. (The number of particles does not change.)

## Practice Set D — Intermolecular forces

1. (a) N₂: London dispersion only. (b) HBr: London dispersion + dipole–dipole. (c) H₂O:
   London dispersion + dipole–dipole + hydrogen bonding. (d) CH₄: London dispersion only.
2. Ne < HF < H₂O. Ne is nonpolar and tiny (London only). HF hydrogen-bonds, but each molecule
   has one H and one lone pair, so its network is less extensive than water's (two O–H bonds
   and two lone pairs per molecule). Reference boiling points agree: Ne about −246 °C,
   HF about 20 °C, H₂O 100 °C.
3. Both are nonpolar, so both rely on London forces — but I₂ is far larger with many more
   electrons, so its electron cloud is much more polarizable and its London forces are much
   stronger. F₂'s weak London forces are overcome at room temperature; I₂'s are not.
4. 1-Propanol has an O–H group, so its molecules hydrogen-bond; diethyl ether has oxygen but
   no O–H group, so it cannot hydrogen-bond (dipole–dipole + London only). Hydrogen bonding
   is much stronger, so 1-propanol boils far higher (97 °C vs. 35 °C).
5. Acetone has the higher vapor pressure (229.5 vs. 23.8 mmHg). Higher vapor pressure means
   molecules escape the liquid more easily, so acetone's intermolecular forces are weaker
   than water's.
6. Water's tall dome shows high surface tension: strong hydrogen bonding makes water molecules
   cohere to each other more than they spread on the wax. Hexane spreads flat because its
   London forces are weak, so its surface tension is low and adhesive forces to the surface
   dominate.

## Unit assessment

### Part A

1. The average kinetic energy doubles (400/200 = 2), and the pressure doubles (faster
   particles hit the fixed walls more often and harder).
2. Gas-law relationships are proportional to *absolute* temperature; only the kelvin scale
   starts at a true zero (absolute zero), so only kelvin temperatures can be used in ratios.
   (Celsius has an arbitrary zero.)
3. Intramolecular bond: a bond *inside* a molecule, e.g., the O–H covalent bond in a water
   molecule. Intermolecular force: an attraction *between* molecules, e.g., a hydrogen bond
   between two water molecules.
4. Vapor pressure is the pressure exerted by a liquid's vapor in equilibrium above the liquid
   (the escaping tendency of its molecules). A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the
   outside pressure, so higher vapor pressure means a lower boiling point.
5. The added energy breaks intermolecular forces — it becomes potential energy as molecules
   separate — instead of raising the average kinetic energy (temperature).
6. The single temperature and pressure at which the solid, liquid, and gas phases of a
   substance coexist in equilibrium.

### Part B

7. V₂ = V₁ × P₁/P₂ = 8.00 L × 0.500/2.00 = **2.00 L**.
8. V₂ = V₁ × T₂/T₁ = 1.20 L × 400/250 = **1.92 L**.
9. T₂ = P₂V₂T₁/(P₁V₁) = (3.00 atm)(1.25 L)(300 K)/((1.50 atm)(2.50 L)) = 1,125/3.75 =

### Part C

12. CH₄ < NH₃ < H₂O. CH₄ is nonpolar (London only). NH₃ is polar and hydrogen-bonds (H on N).
    H₂O hydrogen-bonds more extensively (two O–H bonds and two lone pairs per molecule, and
    oxygen is more electronegative than nitrogen).
13. Acetone evaporates faster: its vapor pressure at 25 °C is 229.5 mmHg against water's
    23.8 mmHg — nearly ten times higher. The higher escaping tendency means weaker forces
    holding acetone molecules in the liquid: acetone's IMFs are weaker than water's.
14. Hexane evaporates faster (weaker London-only forces; boiling point 68.7 °C vs. water's
    100 °C; vapor pressure 151.3 mmHg vs. 23.8 mmHg). Hexane is never used at the bench
    because it is flammable and a neurotoxin — it appears only as reference data.
15. Nitrogen, oxygen, and fluorine. Hydrogen bonded to carbon does not count because carbon is
    not electronegative enough: the H carries too little partial-positive charge for a strong
    attraction to a neighboring lone pair.
