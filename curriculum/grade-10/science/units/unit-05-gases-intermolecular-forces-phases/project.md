# Project — The Evaporation Race: Inferring Invisible Forces (HS-PS1-3)

**Question:** Can the bulk properties of three liquids tell us how strongly their particles
hold on to each other?

**Deliverable:** a written investigation report (question, prediction, plan, data table, graph,
inference, error discussion, conclusion) plus a one-page summary the learner can explain aloud
to the adult. Sessions 15–17 of the unit pacing.

## Background

Lesson 04 taught that stronger intermolecular forces mean higher boiling points, lower vapor
pressures, and slower evaporation. Lesson 06 showed how to test that claim with a fair
comparison. This project is the full investigation: you plan it, run it, and defend the
inference — including its limits.

## Part A — Plan (Session 15; adult approval required before any bench work)

Write a plan that states:

1. The question (above) and your prediction, with the IMF reasoning from Lesson 04.
2. The independent variable (the liquid), the dependent variable (drying time in minutes, or
   mass lost in grams over 10 minutes if a balance is available), and every controlled
   variable with its value (drop count, dish type, surface, room, no fan, no breathing on the
   dishes).
3. The three liquids: **water, ethanol, acetone-based nail polish remover** — and only these.
   (Hexane and ether are reference data, never bench liquids.)
4. Safety: goggles for everyone; the adult dispenses ethanol and acetone; small drops only
   (about 0.5 mL); well-ventilated room; **no flames, heat, or sparks**; bottles capped
   immediately. If any condition cannot be met, use the simulation version below instead.
5. How many trials (three per liquid) and how you will decide a dish is "dry."

**Simulation version (no bench work):** use the PhET "States of Matter" simulation (resource
pack): for one substance, compare how long it takes to change phase when heated from solid to
liquid to gas, and record the temperatures at which each change happens; then use the practice
dataset below as your evaporation data, labeled clearly as practice data, not your own
measurements.

**Practice dataset (fictional practice data — use only if you run the simulation version, or
to check your method):** mean drying times — acetone 1.6 min, ethanol 6.0 min, water 14.8 min
(each the mean of three trials; individual trials within ±0.3 min of the mean). This dataset is
invented for practice and must be labeled as such in your report; it is not an observation.

## Part B — Data and graph (Sessions 16–17)

1. Run three trials per liquid. Record every trial in a data table with units (minutes, or
   minutes:seconds). Compute the mean for each liquid, showing the arithmetic.
2. Draw a bar graph: liquid on the horizontal axis, mean drying time on the vertical axis,
   with units and a title. (A hand-drawn graph is fine; label the axes.)
3. Record observations: anything unusual (a draft, a spill, a dish that dried unevenly) goes
   in the notes column — it matters for Part C.

## Part C — Inference, errors, conclusion (Session 17)

Write, in your own words:

1. **Inference:** rank the three liquids by IMF strength from your data, and state the
   reasoning (faster evaporation → weaker forces holding molecules in the liquid).
2. **Cross-check:** compare your ranking with the reference data in the unit README (boiling
   points: water 100 °C, ethanol 78.3 °C, acetone 56.3 °C; vapor pressures at 25 °C: water
   23.8 mmHg, ethanol 59.0 mmHg, acetone 229.5 mmHg). Do all three bulk properties agree?
   Say so explicitly.
3. **Errors and limits:** name at least two sources of error (e.g., judging "dry," small
   differences in drop size, room temperature drift) and say how each one could shift the
   result. State what the investigation *cannot* prove (it ranks relative strength; it does
   not measure a force, and it does not by itself name the force type — the names come from
   molecular structure, Lesson 04).
4. **Conclusion:** one paragraph answering the project question, with the evidence cited.

## Rubric (40 pts)

| Criterion | 10 pts (strong) | 7 pts (adequate) | 4 pts (developing) | 0–3 pts (missing/unsafe) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Plan and safety (10)** | Complete plan with prediction, variables, controls, and safety steps; adult approval recorded | Plan present; one variable or safety step vague | Plan incomplete; controls or safety steps missing | No plan, or bench work done without adult approval / safety steps |
| **Data and graph (10)** | Three trials per liquid, table with units, means shown, labeled bar graph | Three trials; table or graph missing labels or units | One or two trials; no means; graph missing | No usable data table |
| **Inference and cross-check (10)** | Correct IMF ranking with reasoning; all three bulk properties compared explicitly | Correct ranking; cross-check partial | Ranking wrong or asserted without reasoning | No inference |
| **Errors, limits, conclusion (10)** | Two or more errors named with effects; inference-vs-proof limit stated; conclusion cites evidence | One error named; limit vague; conclusion present | Errors or limit missing; conclusion is a restatement of the prediction | No error discussion or conclusion |

**Passing bar:** 28/40 with no criterion below 7, and the safety row satisfied — a report that
skips safety approval cannot pass, however good its data is.

## What success looks like

A strong report reads like this in miniature: "Acetone dried fastest (mean 1.6 min), then
ethanol (6.0 min), then water (14.8 min). Faster evaporation means weaker forces holding the
liquid together, so the IMF strength order is water > ethanol > acetone. The boiling points
(100, 78.3, 56.3 °C) and the vapor pressures (23.8, 59.0, 229.5 mmHg) rank the same way, so
three bulk properties agree. Judging the exact moment a dish was dry was uncertain by about
±0.2 min, and the room warmed slightly during the trials; neither is large enough to change
the order. This ranks relative strength; it does not measure the forces or prove their names."
