# Lesson 06 — The Density Investigation and Lab Reports

**Goal:** You will carry out a density determination with correct significant figures and write it
up as a structured six-section lab report.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, calculator; the investigation itself runs in
Sessions 13–14 (see `project.md`).
**Readiness check (2 min):** Density = mass ÷ volume. If a bolt's mass is 27.35 g and it displaces
10.2 mL of water, what is its density — and how many significant figures does the answer deserve?

## 1. Why density is the unit's capstone investigation

Density forces every skill in this unit to work together: choosing instruments (balance +
graduated cylinder), reading them to precision, converting units, applying significant figures,
repeating trials, and reporting uncertainty. It also answers a real question — *what is this made
of?* — because each pure substance has a characteristic density. The full procedure, materials,
and safety review are in `project.md`; this lesson teaches the thinking and the write-up.

## 2. The method: water displacement

1. **Tare** the balance with an empty dry beaker; add the metal sample; record the mass to the
   balance's full precision (e.g., 27.35 g).
2. Fill a graduated cylinder about half full; read the initial water level at the meniscus bottom,
   eye level (e.g., 50.0 mL).
3. Tilt the cylinder and slide the sample in (no splashing — splashed-out water steals volume).
   Read the final level (e.g., 60.2 mL).
4. Volume = final − initial = 60.2 − 50.0 = **10.2 mL**.
5. Density = mass ÷ volume.

**Worked example 1 — the full calculation.** Mass 27.35 g (4 s.f.); volume 10.2 mL (3 s.f.).
27.35 ÷ 10.2 = 2.68137… → fewest figures is 3 → **2.68 g/mL**.
Reasoning: the subtraction (60.2 − 50.0) follows the decimal-place rule — both to tenths, so
10.2 mL to tenths ✓; the division follows the significant-figure rule — 3 figures ✓. Two
different rules, applied at the two different steps.

**Worked example 2 — identifying the metal.** Reference densities (room temperature): aluminum
2.70 g/mL, iron 7.87 g/mL, copper 8.96 g/mL, lead 11.3 g/mL.
Reasoning: 2.68 g/mL is within 1% of aluminum's 2.70 and nowhere near the others → the sample is
consistent with **aluminum**. Percent error = |2.68 − 2.70| ÷ 2.70 × 100% = **0.74%**. Note the
honest phrasing: *consistent with*, not *proven* — density alone does not prove identity.

*The numbers above are a fictional practice dataset used to model the write-up. Your own
investigation data go in your report, not these.*

## 3. The six-section lab report

| Section | Job | Sentence starter |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Question | The testable question | "What is the density of the unknown metal sample, and which metal is it consistent with?" |
| 2. Procedure | Steps in order, with apparatus and safety notes — detailed enough to repeat | "The balance was tared with the dry beaker; the sample mass was recorded to 0.01 g…" |
| 3. Data | Tables with units; every reading at instrument precision; repeated trials | (see model table below) |
| 4. Calculation | Each computation shown with the unrounded value, then the rounded answer and the rule used | "27.35 g ÷ 10.2 mL = 2.68137… → 2.68 g/mL (3 s.f., limited by volume)" |
| 5. Error analysis | Sources of error, each classified systematic/random, with the likely *direction* of effect | "Reading the meniscus from above (parallax) would systematically raise the volume and lower the density." |
| 6. Conclusion | Answers the question with evidence; states uncertainty; notes limits | "The density is 2.68 ± 0.03 g/mL (range of three trials), consistent with aluminum (2.70 g/mL; 0.74% error)…" |

Model data table (fictional practice data):

| Trial | Mass (g) | Initial volume (mL) | Final volume (mL) | Displaced volume (mL) | Density (g/mL) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 27.35 | 50.0 | 60.2 | 10.2 | 2.68 |
| 2 | 27.35 | 50.0 | 60.1 | 10.1 | 2.71 |
| 3 | 27.35 | 50.0 | 60.3 | 10.3 | 2.66 |

Mean density = (2.68 + 2.71 + 2.66) ÷ 3 = 8.05 ÷ 3 = 2.6833… → **2.68 g/mL**; range = 2.71 − 2.66 =
0.05 g/mL; report **2.68 ± 0.05 g/mL**.

Check: 27.35/10.1 = 2.70792 → 2.71 ✓; 27.35/10.3 = 2.65534 → 2.66 ✓. Mean 2.6833 → 2.68 ✓
(hundredths, matching the trial values). Range 0.05 ✓.

## 4. What makes an error analysis good

Weak: "Human error affected the results." (Everything involves humans; this says nothing.)
Strong: names the **source**, classifies it, and states the **direction**:
- "Water clinging to the sample when it was removed between trials would carry mass away,
  systematically *lowering* later mass readings." (systematic)
- "The balance's last digit flickered ±0.02 g between readings." (random — addressed by averaging)
- "Reading the meniscus slightly above eye level (parallax) would systematically *raise* the
  volume reading, *lowering* the calculated density."

## Guided practice

1. A learner's data: mass 41.20 g; volumes 22.4, 22.5, 22.3 mL displaced across three trials
   (same mass each trial). Compute each trial's density with correct significant figures, then the
   mean, range, and ± report.
2. Classify and give the direction of effect: (a) the balance was not tared with the beaker on it;
   (b) the cylinder was read with the eye below the meniscus; (c) a drop of water stuck to the
   sample added 0.05 g to one mass reading.
3. Which lab-report section does each sentence belong in? (a) "The density is consistent with
   aluminum." (b) "50.0 mL of water was poured into a 100 mL graduated cylinder." (c) "Parallax
   error may have raised the volume readings."

## Independent practice

4. Write the Question and Procedure sections (5–8 steps) for determining the density of a liquid
   (e.g., cooking oil) with a graduated cylinder and balance. Include one safety note.
5. A classmate's conclusion says "The experiment proved the metal was aluminum." Rewrite the
   sentence with honest scientific phrasing and explain what is wrong with "proved."
6. **Error analysis:** a learner reports density 2.9 g/mL for the aluminum sample above (reference
   2.70). Compute the percent error. Then propose the single most likely procedural cause and
   explain how it would push the result *upward*.

## Application

Draft your own lab report for the Sessions 13–14 investigation using the six sections above and
*your* data. The adult reviews the error analysis before you finalize: every claimed error source
must be specific, classified, and direction-stated. The finished report is the project
deliverable (see `project.md` rubric).

## Exit check

1. Name the six lab-report sections in order.
2. What is the difference between the Data section and the Calculation section?
3. Why does a good error analysis state the *direction* of each error's effect?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the sentence starters in the §3 table may be copied verbatim into the draft
  report.
- **Alternate response mode:** the procedure may be presented as a numbered flowchart rather than
  prose paragraphs.
- **Extension:** design a second investigation: determine whether a "gold" ring is really gold by
  density (gold 19.3 g/mL). What extra problem does a small, light object create for the
  displacement method, and how would you solve it? Write the plan; do not run it without the
  adult's approval of the procedure.

## Teacher note

Common misconception: learners write the conclusion first ("it's aluminum") and bend the error
analysis to defend it. Require the data table and calculations to be complete before the
conclusion is drafted. Watch significant figures in the subtraction step (decimal places) versus
the division step (figures) — learners often apply one rule to both. The simulation alternative
(PhET Density, resource pack) produces the same six-section report from sim data; accept it as a
full deliverable when bench work is not possible. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`,
Lesson 06.
