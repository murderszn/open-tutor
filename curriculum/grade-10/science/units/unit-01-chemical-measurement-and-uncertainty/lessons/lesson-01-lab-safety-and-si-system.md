# Lesson 01 — Lab Safety, Equipment, and the SI System

**Goal:** You will follow a lab-safety contract, choose the correct protective equipment and
measuring instrument for a task, and name the seven SI base units.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** safety goggles, this unit's notebook, pen.
**Readiness check (2 min):** Name one rule you already follow when using a kitchen knife or a hot
stove. Lab rules work the same way: they protect you from hazards you can predict.

## 1. Why safety comes first

A chemistry measurement is only useful if the person making it is safe. Every procedure in this
course follows one rule: **the adult previews and approves the procedure before it begins**, and
any step the adult cannot supervise becomes a simulation or a structured observation instead. You
will sign a safety contract today (see the application task) — chemists in real laboratories sign
one too.

**The non-negotiable contract:**

1. **Eyes:** splash-proof safety goggles whenever glassware, water near electrical equipment, or
   any unknown substance is in use. Regular glasses are not goggles.
2. **Hands and skin:** wash hands before and after bench work; report any spill immediately; never
   touch your face during an investigation.
3. **Mouth:** never eat, drink, or taste anything at the bench. Never smell a container directly —
   if a procedure ever asks you to note an odor, the adult demonstrates the wafting technique first.
4. **Clothing and hair:** tie back long hair; no loose sleeves; closed-toe shoes.
5. **Glassware:** inspect for chips and cracks before use; report breakage at once and let the
   adult handle cleanup — never pick up broken glass with bare hands.
6. **Unknowns:** never mix substances to "see what happens." Every step is planned, written down,
   and approved first.
7. **Stop rule:** if anything unexpected happens — a spill, a crack, a strange smell — stop, step
   back, and tell the adult. There is no penalty for stopping.

## 2. The measuring toolkit

Four quantities, four instruments. Choosing the right one is the first measurement skill.

| Quantity | Instrument | Unit you will use | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mass | Digital balance (or triple-beam balance) | gram (g) | Place the container on the pan, press **tare** to zero it, then add the sample |
| Volume of a liquid | Graduated cylinder | milliliter (mL) | Read at eye level (Lesson 03); beakers are for holding and pouring, *not* for measuring |
| Temperature | Thermometer (alcohol or digital) | degree Celsius (°C) | Never use a thermometer as a stirring rod |
| Length | Metric ruler or meter stick | centimeter (cm), millimeter (mm) | Line up the object's end with the zero mark, not the ruler's end |

**Worked example 1 — choosing instruments.** Task: *Find the density of a metal bolt.*
Reasoning: density = mass ÷ volume, so you need a mass and a volume. Mass → digital balance in
grams. Volume of an irregular solid → submerge it in a graduated cylinder and read the water-level
rise in milliliters (water displacement). A beaker's printed scale is too coarse — its markings are
approximate, so it would corrupt the volume. Correct kit: balance + graduated cylinder.

**Worked example 2 — choosing instruments.** Task: *Check whether a liquid is warming up over
ten minutes.* Reasoning: you need temperature at two times. Thermometer in °C, read at the start
and at ten minutes. A ruler cannot measure temperature; a balance cannot either. The adult
decides whether the liquid may be heated and how.

## 3. The SI system

Scientists everywhere use the **International System of Units (SI)** so a measurement made in one
country means the same in another. Seven **base units** cover everything; all other units
(**derived units**) are built from them.

| Base quantity | SI base unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Length | meter | m |
| Mass | kilogram | kg |
| Time | second | s |
| Temperature | kelvin | K |
| Amount of substance | mole | mol |
| Electric current | ampere | A |
| Luminous intensity | candela | cd |

In this course you will measure mass in **grams** and volume in **milliliters** or **liters** most
often — both are convenient-sized versions of SI units, converted with the prefixes in Lesson 02.
Two derived units to know now:

- **Volume:** 1 liter (L) = 1 cubic decimeter; 1 mL = 1 cubic centimeter (cm³). That is why
  graduated cylinders are marked in mL.
- **Density:** mass ÷ volume, in g/mL or g/cm³ for lab work (the SI derived unit kg/m³ is used in
  physics contexts; chemists at the bench use g/mL).

**Worked example 3 — base vs. derived.** Is "grams per milliliter" a base unit?
Reasoning: the table lists kilogram as the base unit of mass and meter as the base unit of
length. Grams per milliliter combines mass and length³, so it is built from base units — a
**derived** unit. Any unit with a "per" in it is derived.

## Guided practice

1. Your partner knocks a beaker off the bench and it shatters. List your first three actions, in
   order. *(Hint: stop rule, then who handles glass?)*
2. For each task, name the instrument and the unit: (a) mass of 30 mL of water; (b) length of a
   copper strip; (c) temperature change of water left in sunlight for an hour.
3. Which of these are SI base units — meter, liter, gram, second, mole, milliliter? Explain what
   makes the others non-base.

## Independent practice

4. Write, in your own words, why a beaker's printed scale is not acceptable for measuring the
   volume in a density calculation. (2–3 sentences.)
5. A procedure says "heat the liquid and record the temperature every minute." Name two contract
   rules this step triggers, and what the adult must do before you begin.
6. Sort into base or derived: kelvin, g/mL, ampere, cm³, candela, °C. (Note: °C is a convenient
   scale, but the SI base unit of temperature is the kelvin.)

## Application

Tour your actual workspace with the adult. Identify each instrument you have (or its simulation
substitute), check glassware for chips, locate the goggles, and sketch a simple map of the bench
in your notebook labeling where each instrument lives. Write one sentence per instrument stating
what it measures and in what unit.

## Exit check

1. State the stop rule in your own words.
2. Which instrument measures liquid volume precisely enough for this course, and which common
   container must you *not* use for that job?
3. Name three SI base units with their symbols.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the contract's seven rules are numbered so you can point to a rule number
  instead of restating it during scenarios.
- **Alternate response mode:** the workspace map may be drawn and labeled rather than written as
  paragraphs.
- **Extension:** research why the kilogram was redefined in 2019 in terms of a fundamental
  constant (the Planck constant) instead of a metal object in Paris. Write a half-page summary:
  what problem did the redefinition solve?

## Teacher note

Common misconception: learners treat the beaker's printed scale as "close enough." Emphasize that
in this course a measurement device is only as good as its markings — beakers are manufactured for
pouring, not measuring, and their scales can be off by 5% or more. Answer-key reference:
`answer-key.md`, Lesson 01. If the learner has no physical glassware, do the equipment tour with
the generated illustration (`assets/lab-measurement-station.png`) and the PhET Density simulation
preview (resource pack).
