# Grade 10 Science

Independent, grade-specific materials in the public OpenTutor curriculum library.
This track is a **draft in progress**: the audit and scope-and-sequence are
delivered; the eight units are planned, not yet written. See
[scope-and-sequence.md](scope-and-sequence.md).

**Do not refer a learner to another grade track.** This folder is self-contained
for one grade-10 learner at a time; a guiding adult adapts pacing, materials,
and supports. Keep completed learner work, names, photos, schedules, and grades
in private storage — never in this public repository.

See also: [Grade 10 track](../README.md), [curriculum index](../../README.md).

## What this track covers

A full-year (about 36 weeks) grade-10 science course following the **Chemistry**
pathway: safe laboratory measurement and the meaning of uncertainty; atomic
models from Dalton to the quantum-mechanical picture, isotopes, and the
periodic table as a predictive tool; ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding and
how bonding explains bulk properties; chemical reactions, equation balancing,
and mole-based stoichiometry; kinetic molecular theory, gas laws, phases, and
intermolecular forces; solutions, concentration, and aqueous chemistry;
thermochemistry, reaction rates, and equilibrium; and an acids–bases plus
oxidation–reduction capstone that revisits the whole year.

Sessions run about 50 minutes, five per week: explicit concept lessons,
adult-supervised bench investigations with household-safe reagents, PhET
interactive simulations as the default alternative whenever a procedure is
hazardous or materials are unavailable, data-analysis practice, and review.
All wet-lab work follows the safety rules in the scope-and-sequence; the adult
previews every procedure, provides goggles and gloves, and substitutes a
simulation or structured observation whenever supervision or materials are
inadequate.

## Track objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the year the learner will be able to:

1. Measure mass, volume, length, and temperature with the correct instrument;
   record readings to the instrument's precision; apply significant-figure
   rules in calculations; and distinguish accuracy from precision while
   reporting a reasonable uncertainty for a measurement.
2. Convert fluently among SI prefixes, moles, mass, particle counts, and gas
   volumes at STP; set up multi-step unit conversions that cancel correctly.
3. Describe the historical progression of atomic models (Dalton, Thomson,
   Rutherford, Bohr, quantum-mechanical) and explain what evidence forced
   each revision; write electron configurations for main-group elements and
   relate valence electrons to an element's group.
4. Calculate average atomic mass from isotopic abundances; explain
   qualitatively how nuclear changes (alpha, beta, gamma decay) differ from
   chemical changes; model fission and fusion as rearrangements of the
   nucleus with large energy release relative to chemical reactions.
5. Use position on the periodic table to predict relative reactivity, ion
   charges, and bond types for main-group elements; justify the predictions
   from electron patterns.
6. Classify bonds as ionic, covalent (polar or nonpolar), or metallic from
   the elements involved and electronegativity differences; draw Lewis
   structures for simple molecules and ions; predict molecular shape with
   VSEPR for up to four electron domains; and connect bonding type to
   observed melting point, conductivity, and solubility.
7. Write and balance chemical equations for synthesis, decomposition,
   single- and double-replacement, and combustion reactions; use mole ratios
   from balanced equations to compute reactant/product quantities, identify
   limiting reactants, and calculate percent yield.
8. Use kinetic molecular theory to explain gas pressure, temperature, and
   diffusion; apply Boyle's, Charles's, and the combined gas law to
   predict gas behavior; distinguish intramolecular bonds from
   intermolecular forces and explain how the latter control boiling point,
   vapor pressure, and phase changes.
9. Prepare and dilute solutions of stated molarity; explain dissolution at
   the particle level; predict precipitation with solubility rules; and
   classify aqueous reactions as precipitation, acid–base neutralization,
   or redox.
10. Compute reaction enthalpy from bond energies and from Hess's-law cycles;
    explain how temperature, concentration, surface area, and catalysts
    change reaction rate through collision theory; and predict how
    concentration, temperature, and pressure shifts move a system at
    equilibrium (Le Chatelier's principle).
11. Classify acids and bases (Arrhenius and Bronsted–Lowry), calculate the
    pH of strong acid and strong base solutions, describe a titration curve
    qualitatively, assign oxidation numbers, and write balanced redox
    half-reactions.
12. Plan a safe investigation: write a testable question, identify
    variables, choose apparatus, record data in tables with units, evaluate
    sources of error, and communicate conclusions with evidence — with the
    adult approving every procedure before it begins.

## Safety summary

Chemistry investigations use heat, glassware, and reactive household
chemicals, so this track's labs are adult-supervised by design. The full
rules — personal protective equipment, ventilation, chemical storage and
disposal, forbidden procedures, and simulation alternatives — are in the
scope-and-sequence (§6). No investigation in this track requires an open
flame, concentrated acids or bases, or toxic materials; anything more
hazardous is replaced by a PhET simulation or a structured observation.
