# Practice Sets A–F — Unit 03

**Grade 10 Language Arts · Unit 03: Nonfiction rhetoric, reasoning, and
source evaluation.** Work through one set per practice session (see the
unit README pacing). Solutions are in `answer-key.md` (adult only) — do
not read them before attempting a set. Each set is worth 12 points
(2 points per item) unless the adult says otherwise.

## Set A — Claim-tracing (Lesson 01)

Use [`the-library-editorial.md`](texts/the-library-editorial.md).

1. Complete the claim-tracing map for the proposal paragraph ("The
   library board is now considering…"): what does it add that paragraphs
   1–2 did not already say?
2. Complete the map row for the warehouse paragraph ("A library is not a
   warehouse for books."). What is its job in the argument's sequence?
3. Write the editorial's central idea in one sentence of your own words.
   Your sentence must contain a claim someone could disagree with.
4. Does the editorial's idea develop or only repeat? Cite two map rows
   as evidence.
5. The editorial ends: "Open the doors. Let the town find out who walks
   in." Is that sentence a new reason, a restatement, or something else?
   Explain in two sentences.
6. Error analysis: a classmate's map says the editorial's central idea
   is "libraries." Explain, in two sentences, why that answer fails and
   how to fix it.

## Set B — Objective summary (Lesson 02)

Use both editorials.

1. Write an objective summary (120–180 words) of "The Library Can Wait."
   Include its central idea and its four main claims; include no verdict
   of your own.
2. Underline every word in your summary that reports the author's
   attitude rather than the author's claim (for example, "reckless" if
   you borrowed it as fact). Rewrite any such word neutrally.
3. Write an objective summary (120–180 words) of "Keep the Doors Open."
4. Compare your two summaries: which editorial was harder to summarize
   objectively, and why? (Two sentences.)
5. A classmate's summary of "Keep the Doors Open" reads: "The editorial
   proves that evening hours will help everyone." Name two things wrong
   with that sentence as a summary.
6. Add one clearly labeled evaluation sentence ("In my view…") to your
   Set B item 1 summary. Then explain in one sentence why the label
   matters.

## Set C — Rhetoric inventory (Lesson 03)

Use [`the-library-speech.md`](texts/the-library-speech.md).

1. Ethos: quote one passage where Delgado builds credibility *by how
   she argues* (not by her biography), and explain the effect in one
   sentence.
2. Pathos: quote the passage you find most emotionally effective, and
   name the specific feeling it targets.
3. Logos: list the speech's three checkable pieces of evidence (the
   incident log, the cost comparison, the sign-up sheet). For each, say
   whether it is strong, weak, or unverifiable — and why.
4. Repetition: find a repeated word or phrase other than the "door"
   image. What changes between its first and last use?
5. Framing: the speech frames the choice as "doors open vs. doors shut."
   Reframe the same proposal as a budget decision in one sentence. What
   does your reframing make harder to see?
6. Genre contrast (150–200 words): how would this speech's rhetoric have
   to change as a budget memo to the town finance office? Name two
   strategies that must change and why.

## Set D — Fallacy identification (Lesson 04)

Each item is a short original argument. Name the fallacy (if any), quote
the move, and credit any fair point the argument contains.

1. "Either we fund the library trial or we admit we don't care about
   children at all."
2. "My cousin signed up for evening classes twice and never went, so
   sign-up sheets never predict attendance."
3. "The mayor supports evening hours, but she only moved to town last
   year, so her opinion shouldn't count."
4. "Libraries have closed at six for twenty years, so six o'clock must be
   the right closing time."
5. "Nine out of ten people I asked at the farmers' market had never heard
   of the trial, so the town doesn't want it."
6. "The trial costs little, the rooms can be counted honestly, and if the
   numbers are small we can end it — so the risk of trying is low."
   (Is there a fallacy here? If not, say what makes this reasoning
   stronger than items 1–5.)

## Set E — Seminal documents and argument evaluation (Lesson 05 + Lesson 04)

Use [`seminal-documents-excerpts.md`](texts/seminal-documents-excerpts.md)
and [`the-library-counter-editorial.md`](texts/the-library-counter-editorial.md).

1. Complete the theme table's third row (protection against tyranny):
   one quotation from each document, plus one sentence per document on
   what it does with the theme.
2. Write a 200–250-word comparison paragraph: "How does the UDHR develop,
   rather than merely repeat, the Declaration's idea of equality?" Cite
   both texts.
3. Which document's idea of equality is wider, and what is your evidence?
   (Two sentences with quotations.)
4. Argument-evaluation chart: delineate the counter-editorial's final
   paragraph into claims and evidence, then judge it on validity,
   relevance, and sufficiency (one sentence each).
5. Credit one fair point in the counter-editorial and explain why it is
   fair even though the editorial as a whole is weak. (Three sentences.)
6. Rewrite one grievance from the Declaration excerpt as a UDHR-style
   article (a general right, addressed to all governments). In two
   sentences, say what the rewrite gains and loses.

## Set F — Mediums and speaker evaluation (Lesson 06)

Use all four unit texts.

1. Answer "Did evening use grow during the trial?" using *only* the data
   table. Quote the numbers.
2. Answer "Should the trial continue?" using *only* the speech. What can
   the speech not answer about this question?
3. Answer "Should the trial continue?" using *only* the
   counter-editorial. What can it not answer?
4. Write the integrated answer (150–200 words): use each account only
   for what it can show, name one gap no account fills, and end with a
   recommendation.
5. Speaker evaluation: Delgado never mentions librarians' evening working
   conditions. Is that a fair criticism of the speech? Argue both sides
   in two sentences each, then give your verdict in one sentence.
6. Image question: which listener in
   [`town-hall-speaker.png`](assets/town-hall-speaker.png) behaves most
   like a source evaluator — the note-taker or the arms-crossed
   listener? Defend your choice in three sentences, using the text-only
   alternative if you cannot see the image. Then state one thing the
   image cannot show and why it must never be cited as evidence.
