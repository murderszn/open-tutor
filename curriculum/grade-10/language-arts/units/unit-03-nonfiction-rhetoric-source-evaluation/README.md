# Unit 03 — Nonfiction Rhetoric, Reasoning, and Source Evaluation

**Grade 10 Language Arts (world literature and composition pathway) · Weeks 11–14 · 20 sessions of ~50 minutes**

Unit 03 of [issue #48](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/48). Status: **validated
draft** — delivered for educator review, not merged. This unit is self-contained for one grade-10
learner; a guiding adult supervises all work and previews every external link before the learner
opens it. There is no bench work in this unit: the investigation is a source-evaluation dossier
built from the unit's own texts, so no materials beyond paper, a notebook, and a device for
optional video previews are required.

## Essential question

When someone is trying to persuade you, how do you tell the difference between a good argument,
a good speech, and a good reason — and what do you do when those three point in different
directions?

## What the learner will be able to do

1. State an informational text's central idea in one sentence and trace how it develops across
   sentences, paragraphs, and larger sections, identifying hinge sentences and the argument's
   sequence (RI.9-10.2, RI.9-10.3, RI.9-10.5).
2. Write an objective summary of an argumentative text that reports the author's idea, reasons,
   and development without smuggling in a verdict of the summarizer's own (RI.9-10.2).
3. Identify a speaker's or author's purpose and point of view, and name the rhetorical
   strategies that advance them — appeals (ethos, pathos, logos), repetition, framing, and
   diction — explaining what each strategy does for its specific audience (RI.9-10.4,
   RI.9-10.6).
4. Delineate an argument's claims and evidence, then judge validity (does the conclusion
   follow?), relevance (does the evidence bear on the claim?), and sufficiency (is there
   enough of it?), identifying false statements and fallacious reasoning by name, including
   false dilemma, hasty generalization, ad hominem, appeal to tradition, and appeal to
   popularity (RI.9-10.8).
5. Analyze how two seminal documents — the U.S. Declaration of Independence (1776) and the
   Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) — address related themes and concepts
   (equality, government by consent, protection against tyranny), and explain how their
   different purposes shape what each says and leaves unsaid (RI.9-10.9).
6. Compare accounts of one subject in different mediums, integrate information across them
   while evaluating each source's credibility and accuracy, evaluate a speaker's point of
   view, reasoning, and use of evidence and rhetoric, and write an evaluation essay that
   argues a verdict from evidence (RI.9-10.1, RI.9-10.7, SL.9-10.2, SL.9-10.3, W.9-10.1,
   W.9-10.9).

Objectives 1–6 above serve track objectives 1, 7, and 12 of the
[grade-10 language arts track](../../README.md) and the practices named in
[scope-and-sequence.md](../../scope-and-sequence.md) (citing strong and thorough evidence;
delineating and evaluating arguments; analyzing seminal documents; integrating information
from diverse media; evaluating speakers). Crosscutting thread: **accountability** — a claim
is checked against its reasoning, its evidence, and its source, separately.

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: Common Core State Standards for English Language Arts,
grades 9–10 band. Codes and descriptions were checked for this track's audit on 2026-10-07
against thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy (see the resource pack for the checked pages).
Descriptions below are short paraphrases for mapping purposes, not reproductions of the
standards text. **This crosswalk is a planning reference, not a claim of state adoption,
accreditation, or certification of alignment.**

- **RI.9-10.1** — cite strong and thorough textual evidence to support analysis of what
  the text says explicitly and of inferences drawn from it. (All lessons; project;
  assessments.)
- **RI.9-10.2** — determine a central idea and analyze its development over the course of
  a text, including how it emerges and is shaped and refined by specific details; provide
  an objective summary. (Lessons 01, 02; practice Sets A, B.)
- **RI.9-10.3** — analyze how the author unfolds an analysis or series of ideas or events,
  including the order in which points are made and how they are introduced and developed.
  (Lessons 01, 02.)
- **RI.9-10.4** — determine the meaning of words and phrases as used in a text, including
  figurative and connotative meanings, and analyze the cumulative impact of specific word
  choices on meaning and tone. (Lesson 03; practice Set C.)
- **RI.9-10.5** — analyze in detail how an author's ideas or claims are developed and
  refined by particular sentences, paragraphs, or larger portions of a text. (Lessons 01,
  02, 03.)
- **RI.9-10.6** — determine an author's point of view or purpose in a text and analyze how
  an author uses rhetoric to advance that point of view or purpose. (Lesson 03; practice
  Set C.)
- **RI.9-10.7** — analyze various accounts of a subject told in different mediums,
  determining which details are emphasized, attended to, or left out in each account.
  (Lesson 06; practice Set F; project.)
- **RI.9-10.8** — delineate and evaluate the argument and specific claims in a text,
  assessing whether the reasoning is valid and the evidence is relevant and sufficient,
  and identifying false statements and fallacious reasoning. (Lesson 04; practice Sets D,
  E; assessments.)
- **RI.9-10.9** — analyze seminal U.S. documents of historical and literary significance,
  including how they address related themes and concepts. (Lesson 05; practice Set E.)
- **RI.9-10.10** — read and comprehend literary nonfiction in the grades 9–10 text
  complexity band proficiently, with scaffolding as needed at the high end of the band.
  (Whole unit; the unit's original texts are written at the high end of the band with
  adult support, and the seminal excerpts are primary sources read with notes.)
- **SL.9-10.2** — integrate multiple sources of information presented in diverse media or
  formats, evaluating the credibility and accuracy of each source. (Lesson 06; project.)
- **SL.9-10.3** — evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and use of evidence and
  rhetoric, identifying any fallacious reasoning or exaggerated or distorted evidence.
  (Lessons 03, 06; practice Set F.)
- **W.9-10.1 / W.9-10.9** — write arguments with precise claims, valid reasoning, and
  relevant and sufficient evidence, drawing evidence from informational texts. (Unit
  assessment Part B; project memo.)

Texts and their status:

- The four Alder Creek texts (editorial, counter-editorial, speech, data table) are
  **original to this unit and fictional**: the town, the library, the speakers, and every
  number are invented. The data table is labeled fictional practice data in its own file
  and must not be cited as facts about any real library. The counter-editorial was written
  to be evaluated, not agreed with: its weak reasoning is deliberate and is named in
  Lesson 04.
- The Declaration of Independence excerpts are **public-domain text** (1776), quoted from
  the National Archives transcription with original spelling and punctuation.
- The UDHR passages are **short attributed quotations** (preamble clause; Articles 1, 2,
  3, 7) from the 1948 declaration adopted as General Assembly resolution 217 (III); the
  full text is linked in the resource pack. No other copyrighted material is reproduced:
  all other passages in this unit are original.

## 20-session pacing

Five sessions per week. Lessons are written for ~50 minutes; practice, discussion,
investigation, review, and assessment sessions fill the unit as shown — six lessons alone do
not fill twenty sessions.

| Session | Week | Activity | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 11 | Lesson 01 — Tracing a central idea | Learner states the editorial's idea as a claim, not a topic |
| 2 | 11 | Practice Set A + claim-tracing map | Map rows paraphrased; development verdict defended |
| 3 | 11 | Lesson 02 — Unfolding and objective summary | Hinge sentences identified; summary has no verdict |
| 4 | 11 | Practice Set B + summary swap | Summaries pass the smuggled-verdict check |
| 5 | 11 | Reading + retrieval: both editorials; Sessions 1–4 questions | Reading notes recorded; retrieval ≥ 80% |
| 6 | 12 | Lesson 03 — Rhetoric and purpose | Inventory rows quote + explain effect on this audience |
| 7 | 12 | Practice Set C + rhetoric inventory | All six inventory rows complete |
| 8 | 12 | Genre-contrast paragraph + discussion | Two strategies named with reasons for change |
| 9 | 12 | Lesson 04 — Evaluating arguments | Counter-editorial delineated before judged |
| 10 | 12 | Practice Set D + fallacy set | Fallacies named with quoted moves; fair points credited |
| 11 | 13 | Lesson 05 — Seminal documents (Declaration) | Theme table rows quoted; purpose difference stated |
| 12 | 13 | Seminal documents part 2 (UDHR) + theme notes | Comparison paragraph cites both texts |
| 13 | 13 | Practice Set E + argument-evaluation chart | Chart judges all three tests separately |
| 14 | 13 | Project Part A — account inventory | Inventory checked for quotation accuracy |
| 15 | 13 | **Formative quiz** (`assessments/week-13_quiz.md`) + review | Gaps identified; reteach plan set with teacher guide |

| 17 | 14 | Project Part B — ratings + memo draft + peer review | Memo reasons cited; gap real, not decorative |

Retrieval practice is built into Sessions 5, 10, and 15 (each opens with 3 questions from an
earlier lesson). The adult gives feedback on every written product before the next session
begins.

## Safety

No bench work, heat, glassware, chemicals, or outdoor activity is used in this unit. The
investigation is a paper-and-discussion dossier. The adult previews every external link
and video search result before the learner opens it, and confirms the device's browser has
no account sign-in requirement for the linked pages. The seminal-documents discussion
includes the historical fact that the Declaration's 1776 practice excluded enslaved people
and women from the rights it proclaimed; the adult leads that discussion directly (see
the teacher guide) rather than smoothing it over. If a video search is unavailable, the
text-only lessons carry the same objectives without it.
