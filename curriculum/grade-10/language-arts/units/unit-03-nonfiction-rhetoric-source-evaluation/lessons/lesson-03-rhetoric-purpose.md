# Lesson 03 — Rhetoric and Purpose: How a Speaker Moves an Audience

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can identify a speaker's purpose
and point of view, name the rhetorical strategies used to advance them —
appeals, repetition, framing, diction — and explain how word choice
shapes meaning and tone.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** [`the-library-speech.md`](../texts/the-library-speech.md),
Practice Set C, the rhetorical-triangle diagram
([`rhetorical-triangle.svg`](../assets/rhetorical-triangle.svg)).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. The speech opens with a childhood memory (learning to read in the
   library, a librarian staying late unpaid). Before analyzing anything:
   what does that opening make you *feel*, and what does it make you
   *believe about the speaker*? Those are two different effects.
2. What is the speaker's purpose — to inform, to persuade, to inspire, or
   some mix? What is the one action she wants the audience to take?

## Teaching

### The rhetorical situation: speaker, audience, purpose

Every persuasive text sits inside a situation: a speaker with a purpose,
an audience with worries, and a moment that makes the argument urgent.
Here the speaker is a council member speaking to neighbors at a town
hall; her purpose is to win a vote for a six-month trial; her audience
worries about cost, trouble, and empty rooms. Every strategy below is
chosen for *that* audience, not for readers in general. A strategy that
works on neighbors at a town hall might fail in a budget spreadsheet —
and that is the first thing to notice when you evaluate rhetoric: it is
always aimed at someone.

### The three appeals

Classical rhetoric names three appeals (see Purdue OWL, "Classical
Argument," in the resource pack):

- **Ethos — credibility.** The speaker shows she is knowledgeable,
  honest, and fair. Delgado builds ethos by naming her thirty-one years
  in town, by answering objections directly instead of ignoring them,
  and by promising to vote to end the trial herself if the rooms sit
  empty.
- **Pathos — emotion.** The speaker connects the argument to feelings
  the audience already holds. The childhood memory, the nurse, the
  student, the father reading to his daughter — these are pathos: they
  make an abstract schedule feel like a human stake.
- **Logos — logic and evidence.** The speaker gives reasons and facts
  that can be checked: the community center's six-year incident log, the
  cost comparison ("the price of one repaved block"), the sign-up sheet
  that filled in four days.

A strong evaluation never says "this speech uses pathos" and stops.
Name the appeal, quote the move, and say what it does *for this
audience*.

### Repetition, framing, diction

- **Repetition:** "who is this library for?" appears twice — once as a
  question to the audience, once as a challenge ("If it is only for those
  of us with free afternoons…"). Repetition turns a question into the
  speech's spine.
- **Framing:** Delgado frames the choice as *doors open vs. doors shut*,
  not as *spending vs. saving*. The same proposal framed as a budget item
  ("two clerks, six months") sounds smaller; framed as a door, it sounds
  like a value. Framing is not lying — it is choosing which true aspect
  of a thing to put in front.
- **Diction:** compare "a chair, a lamp, and quiet" with "a handful of
  night owls" (the counter-editorial's phrase for the same people). Both
  describe evening users; one dignifies them, one shrinks them. Word
  choice is never neutral in persuasive writing — and noticing that is
  half of RI.9-10.4.

### Worked example 1 — one paragraph, three appeals

*The paragraph: "I have heard the worry about cost. Two part-time
clerks, three evenings a week, for six months. That is the price of one
repaved block of Maple Street. We repave blocks without a town hall
debate. Are we really saying that asphalt deserves a faster yes than a
child's reading?"*

- **Logos:** the cost is made concrete and comparable (one repaved
  block) — a number the audience can picture.
- **Pathos:** "a child's reading" against "asphalt" — the comparison
  carries an emotional charge (children vs. road surface).
- **Ethos:** the speaker shows she has done the arithmetic and is willing
  to name the trade-off plainly, which makes her sound candid rather
  than evasive.
- **Diction note:** "asphalt" is doing heavy lifting — a cold, gray word
  set against "a child's reading." If the speaker had said "road
  maintenance," the paragraph would lose its sting. That is word choice
  as strategy, not decoration.

### Worked example 2 — repetition as structure

*The speech's closing line repeats its opening line's image: "Thirty-one
years ago, someone kept a door open for me. Tonight, I ask you to keep it
open for them."*

This is not mere decoration. The repetition closes a circle: the
speaker's personal story becomes the audience's decision. A listener who
remembers the opening librarian hears the ending as a debt coming due.
Note also the pronoun shift: "for me" becomes "for them" — the speaker
hands her story to the audience. When you inventory repetition, always
ask what changed between the first use and the last.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Build a rhetoric inventory of the speech: one row each for ethos, pathos,
logos, repetition, framing, and diction. Each row needs a short quotation
and one sentence saying what the move does for this audience.

## Independent practice

Practice Set C, items 1–6: complete the inventory rows the guided session
left open, then write a genre-contrast paragraph (150–200 words): how
would this speech's rhetoric change if the same proposal were written as
a budget memo to the town finance office? Name two strategies that would
have to change and why.

## Applied task

Choose one rhetorical move from the speech that you find *least*
convincing, and explain why it is weak for this audience — in the
speaker's own terms, not yours. (Weak rhetoric is still rhetoric; Lesson
04 will judge it as argument.)

## Exit check

Name the speaker's purpose in one sentence, and give one example each of
ethos, pathos, and logos from the speech. (Answer-key reference:
`answer-key.md`, Lesson 03.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** use the triangle diagram: put the speaker at one corner
  (ethos), the audience at another (pathos), and the argument itself at
  the third (logos). Ask which corner each paragraph leans on.
- **Extension:** find one place where the speech's pathos might
  *undermine* its ethos (for example, does the childhood story risk
  sounding like a personal favor rather than a public reason?). Argue it
  in three sentences.

## Teacher note

Misconception to watch: learners often label every emotional passage
"pathos" and every fact "logos" without asking what each *does*. Insist
on the second half of every inventory row: the effect on this audience.
Also watch for the reverse error — assuming pathos is dishonest. Emotion
is a legitimate appeal; the question is whether it is attached to a real
reason, which is Lesson 04's job.
