# Lesson 02 — How an Author Unfolds an Analysis, and How to Summarize It

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can describe how an author unfolds
an analysis across sentences, paragraphs, and larger sections, and write
an objective summary of an argumentative text that reports what the author
says without agreeing or disagreeing.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** [`the-library-editorial.md`](../texts/the-library-editorial.md),
[`the-library-counter-editorial.md`](../texts/the-library-counter-editorial.md),
Practice Set B, a notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Lesson 01's map showed the editorial moving problem → proposal →
   objections → principle ("a warehouse keeps people out") → test ("count
   the visitors"). What does that sequence do for a reader that a list of
   reasons in random order would not?
2. Write one sentence that summarizes the editorial *without* saying
   whether you agree with it. (This is harder than it looks.)

## Teaching

### Unfolding: the shape of the argument

Authors of informational text unfold ideas the way a lawyer builds a
case: each part prepares the next. In "Keep the Doors Open," the shape is:

1. **Problem in human terms** (who is excluded) — earns attention.
2. **Proposal in concrete terms** (three evenings, six months, two
   clerks) — makes the idea testable instead of vague.
3. **Objections answered** — removes the reader's easiest exits.
4. **Principle stated** ("a library keeps things useful by letting
   people in") — lifts the argument from one town's schedule to a claim
   about what libraries are.
5. **Test proposed** ("count the visitors… if the numbers are small, end
   the trial") — answers the charge of recklessness by building in a way
   to be wrong.

Notice the order matters: the principle lands *after* the evidence, so it
feels earned; the test comes *last*, so the author cannot be accused of
dodging accountability. When you analyze unfolding, always ask: what
would break if this part moved earlier or later?

### Objective summary: report, don't join

An objective summary answers three questions and nothing else: What is
the author's central idea? What are the main reasons or evidence? How
does the author develop them? It does not say "the author is right," does
not add your own reasons, and does not borrow the author's persuasive
words ("quietly," "reckless") as if they were facts. Compare:

- **Not objective:** "The editorial brilliantly proves that the library's
  unfair hours hurt working families."
- **Objective:** "The editorial argues that Alder Creek's 6 p.m. closing
  time excludes night-shift workers, students, and working parents, and
  proposes a six-month trial of 9 p.m. closing three evenings a week. It
  supports the proposal by citing the community center's evening safety
  record and a study-room sign-up sheet that filled in four days, and it
  proposes judging the trial by evening visitor counts."

### Worked example 1 — tracing one sentence's job

*The sentence: "A library is not a warehouse for books."*

This sentence sits between the objection answers and the trial proposal.
Its job is *reframing*: it replaces the reader's mental picture (library
as storage) with a new one (library as a useful room). One sentence does
three things at once — it summarizes the objection section ("we already
answered the practical worries"), states the principle, and sets up the
test ("count the visitors" only makes sense if usefulness, not storage,
is the point). In an analysis of unfolding, a sentence like this is a
hinge: everything before it leads in, everything after it follows out.

### Worked example 2 — summarizing without smuggling a verdict

*Task: summarize the counter-editorial's third paragraph ("Third, we
already know evening programs fail…") in one objective sentence.*

A smuggled verdict would read: "The author correctly points out that
only twelve people came to a lecture." The word "correctly" is a verdict,
and "only" is the author's spin, not a fact. An objective version: "The
author cites a Thursday night lecture series at the community center
that drew twelve people to its first talk as evidence that evening
programs attract too few people to justify funding." The evidence is
reported; whether twelve is "too few" is left for the reader — and for
Lesson 04 — to judge.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Working in pairs or with the adult, mark the hinge sentences of "Keep the
Doors Open" — sentences whose removal would break the argument's
sequence. Aim for three, and defend each choice in one sentence.

## Independent practice

Practice Set B, items 1–6: write an objective summary of the full
counter-editorial (120–180 words), then write a second summary of "Keep
the Doors Open" in the same length. Swap with the adult: the adult checks
each summary for smuggled verdicts (praise words, "only," "clearly") and
for missing main reasons.

## Applied task

Take your objective summary of the counter-editorial and add one sentence
at the end that is clearly labeled as your own evaluation ("In my view…").
The point of the exercise: you should be able to see exactly where
reporting stops and judging starts.

## Exit check

In two sentences, explain the difference between a summary and a
retelling. (Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`, Lesson 02.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** use the Lesson 01 map as your summary skeleton — one
  sentence per map section, in order.
- **Extension:** rewrite one paragraph of the editorial so its hinge
  sentence comes first instead of last. Does the paragraph get stronger
  or weaker? Explain in three sentences.

## Teacher note

Misconception to watch: learners often think "objective" means "boring"
or "without opinion words at all." It does not — it means the opinions
reported are the author's, clearly attributed, and yours are kept out
until you label them.
