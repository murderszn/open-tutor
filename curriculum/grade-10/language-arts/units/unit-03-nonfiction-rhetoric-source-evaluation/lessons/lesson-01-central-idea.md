# Lesson 01 — Tracing a Central Idea

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can state an editorial's central
idea in one sentence and trace how it develops across the text, pointing
to the sentence or paragraph where each part of the idea appears.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** [`the-library-editorial.md`](../texts/the-library-editorial.md),
Practice Set A, a notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. In your own words, what is the difference between a text's *topic*
   (what it is about) and its *central idea* (what it says about that
   topic)?
2. The editorial's title is "Keep the Doors Open." What claim do you
   expect it to make, before you read a word?

## Teaching

### Topic is not the idea

The topic of "Keep the Doors Open" is library hours. That is not yet a
central idea. A central idea is a *claim* — something that could be
argued with: "The Alder Creek library should stay open three evenings a
week for a six-month trial, because its current hours exclude the people
who need it most." A reader who can only name the topic has not yet read
for argument.

### The claim-tracing map

Readers of argument trace a claim the way a hiker follows a trail: mark
each place the trail appears, and check whether it goes somewhere new or
just circles back. Use this four-column map:

| Where (paragraph) | What the author says (short paraphrase) | What it adds to the idea | Evidence or reasoning? |
|---|---|---|---|

A strong central idea *develops*: it starts narrow, gains reasons, meets
an objection, and ends wider or sharper than it began. If every paragraph
only repeats the first sentence, the idea has not developed — it has only
been repeated.

### Worked example 1 — the opening move

*Paragraphs 1–2 of the editorial: the author lists who the library
serves before six o'clock (schoolchildren, retirees, day-shift workers)
and who it does not serve (the night-shift nurse, the student sharing one
computer, the father working two jobs).*

The central idea's first part is: **the current hours quietly choose who
the library is for.** Notice what the author does not do: she does not
start with the proposal. She starts with the *problem the proposal
solves*, told through three specific people. That is a deliberate
sequence — problem first, proposal second — and it makes the proposal
feel like an answer rather than a whim. In your map: "Paragraphs 1–2 —
hours exclude night workers, students, working parents — establishes the
problem; evidence type: illustrative examples (not statistics)."

### Worked example 2 — the objection paragraph

*The paragraph beginning "Opponents of the trial will tell you…"*

Here the author does something a careless reader misses: she states the
other side's case herself, then answers each objection in turn (the
community center stays open until ten with fewer incidents; the sign-up
sheet filled in four days). In your map: "Objection paragraph — answers
'trouble' and 'nobody will come' objections — develops the idea by
showing it survives disagreement; evidence type: comparison (community
center) and a specific observation (sign-up sheet)."

A common error: calling the objection paragraph "the author changing her
mind." She is not. Anticipating objections is part of building an idea,
not abandoning it.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Complete rows of a claim-tracing map for the editorial's remaining
paragraphs: the proposal paragraph ("The library board is now
considering…"), the warehouse paragraph ("A library is not a warehouse…"),
and the closing paragraphs. For each row, write the paraphrase in your own
words — copying sentences into the map is not tracing.

## Independent practice

Practice Set A, items 1–6: finish the map, then write the editorial's
central idea in one sentence of your own (not the author's words), and
answer: does the idea develop, or only repeat? Give two map rows as
evidence for your verdict.

## Applied task

Choose any two paragraphs of the editorial and swap their order in your
head. Would the argument get stronger, weaker, or stay the same? Write
three sentences defending your answer with reference to what each
paragraph does.

## Exit check

Write the editorial's central idea in one sentence, and name the
paragraph where the idea is most fully developed. (Answer-key reference:
`answer-key.md`, Lesson 01.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the editorial feels long, read paragraphs 1–2 and the
  closing paragraph first, then fill the middle — the map works backwards
  as well as forwards.
- **Extension:** find one sentence in the editorial that does *not*
  advance the central idea (a sentence that is only decoration or
  repetition). Defend your choice in two sentences; the adult judges
  whether your case is arguable, not whether it matches one "right"
  sentence.

## Teacher note

Misconception to watch: learners often write the topic ("library hours")
as the central idea, or copy the title. Insist on a sentence with a verb
and a claim — something a reasonable person could disagree with.
