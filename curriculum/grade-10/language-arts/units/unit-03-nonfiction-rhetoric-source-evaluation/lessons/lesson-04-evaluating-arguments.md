# Lesson 04 — Evaluating an Argument: Validity, Relevance, Sufficiency, Fallacies

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can delineate an argument's
claims, test whether its reasoning is valid, judge whether its evidence
is relevant and sufficient, and identify false statements and fallacious
reasoning by name.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** [`the-library-counter-editorial.md`](../texts/the-library-counter-editorial.md),
Practice Set D, a notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. What is the difference between "this argument is wrong" and "this
   argument is weak"? (Hint: an argument can reach a true conclusion
   with bad reasoning, and a false conclusion can be argued well.)
2. The counter-editorial says: "Libraries are quiet places for study
   during the day." Is that a claim, a fact, or a definition? Why does
   the answer matter for evaluation?

## Teaching

### Delineate first, judge second

Evaluation has an order, and skipping the first step is the most common
error. First **delineate**: list the author's claims in your own words,
numbered, with the evidence offered for each. Only then judge. A critic
who judges a claim the author never made is arguing with a ghost.

The counter-editorial's claims, delineated:

1. Evening library hours will bring noise, supervision problems, and
   loitering — with no middle ground.
2. The sign-up sheet proves nothing, because signing costs nothing.
3. Evening programs are known to fail (one lecture drew twelve people).
4. The proposal's supporters are mostly non-taxpayers, so residents'
   wishes should prevail.
5. Therefore the proposal is reckless, wasteful, and unfair, and should
   be rejected.

### Three tests

- **Validity — does the conclusion follow?** A valid argument is one
  where *if* the premises were true, the conclusion would have to be
  true. Claim 1 fails here: even if evening hours bring some noise, it
  does not follow that there is "no middle ground" — security, staffing,
  and rules are middle grounds the author never rules out. That leap is
  the fallacy of **false dilemma**: presenting two options (hire security
  or accept consequences) as if no others existed.
- **Relevance — does the evidence bear on the claim?** Claim 4's
  evidence (who pays taxes) is irrelevant to whether evening hours work.
  Who supports a proposal says nothing about whether it is good; judging
  the arguer instead of the argument is **ad hominem** reasoning.
  (Note: it is not automatically fallacious to mention a speaker's
  interests — it becomes fallacious when interest is used *instead of*
  evidence about the proposal.)
- **Sufficiency — is there enough evidence?** Claim 3 rests on one
  lecture, one night, twelve people. One case cannot carry a general
  claim about "evening programs" — that is **hasty generalization**.
  Claim 2's marathon analogy is also insufficient: one neighbor's
  unfulfilled marathon sign-up does not show that *this* sign-up sheet's
  signers will not come. An analogy proves only as much as the similarity
  between the two cases supports, and a marathon is not a study room.

### False statements vs. weak reasoning

A statement can be false without being fallacious, and fallacious without
being false. "The community center lecture drew twelve people" is
presented as fact inside a fictional editorial — within the fiction we
treat it as given, and the problem is what the author *infers* from it.
In real-world evaluation, you would also check the fact itself (was it
really twelve?). Lesson 04's rule: check the reasoning first, then flag
any factual claim you cannot verify as "unverified — needs a source,"
rather than calling it false without evidence.

### Worked example 1 — delineating and testing one paragraph

*Claim 1's paragraph: "Libraries are quiet places for study during the
day. Everyone knows that a library at night is a different creature
entirely — noisier, harder to supervise, and open to exactly the kind of
loitering that daytime hours avoid."*

- **Delineated claim:** night hours cause noise, supervision difficulty,
  and loitering.
- **Evidence offered:** none — only "everyone knows," which is an
  **appeal to popularity** (a claim is true because many believe it).
  "Everyone knows" is not data; it is a way of skipping data.
- **Validity:** even if some trouble occurred, "no middle ground" does
  not follow (false dilemma, as above).
- **Verdict:** weak on all three tests — no evidence, irrelevant
  authority ("everyone"), and an invalid leap.

### Worked example 2 — a fair point inside a weak argument

*Claim 2's core: a sign-up sheet is not a promise of attendance.*

This point is actually reasonable — and a good evaluator says so. Signing
does cost little, and some signers will not come. The fallacy is not the
point itself; it is the *inference* the author draws from it: that the
sheet therefore "proves nothing." A sign-up sheet is weak evidence, not
zero evidence: 200 names still tell you more than 2 names. The honest
evaluation: "The author is right that sign-ups overstate attendance, but
wrong to treat weak evidence as no evidence. The fair response is to
discount the sheet, not discard it." Evaluating well means giving credit
where it is due — that is what makes your criticism trustworthy.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Delineate the counter-editorial's final paragraph ("The library works…")
into claims and evidence, then run the three tests on it. Is "it has
worked for twenty years" a reason against change, or just a description
of the past? (This is the **appeal to tradition**: old therefore good.)

## Independent practice

Practice Set D, items 1–6: the fallacy-identification set — six short
original arguments, each with one named fallacy to find and one fair
point to credit where one exists.

## Applied task

Write a four-sentence evaluation of the counter-editorial as a whole:
one sentence crediting its strongest point, one naming its weakest
inference and its fallacy, one judging sufficiency overall, and one
verdict (accept, reject, or revise-and-retest).

## Exit check

Define validity, relevance, and sufficiency in one sentence each, and
name the fallacy in: "Either we keep the library exactly as it is, or we
destroy everything that makes it special." (Answer-key reference:
`answer-key.md`, Lesson 04.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the three tests on a card: "Does it follow? Does it
  bear on the claim? Is there enough of it?" Run every argument through
  the card before naming a fallacy.
- **Extension:** find one argument in the *pro*-trial editorial that is
  also weak under these tests (for example, does the community-center
  comparison really prove the library will be safe?). Fair evaluation
  cuts both ways.

## Teacher note

Misconception to watch: learners often use fallacy names as insults
("that's a straw man!") without showing the misrepresentation. Insist on
the two-step form: quote the move, then explain why it fails the test.
Also watch for over-labeling: not every weak argument contains a named
fallacy, and "weak evidence" is a sufficient verdict on its own.
