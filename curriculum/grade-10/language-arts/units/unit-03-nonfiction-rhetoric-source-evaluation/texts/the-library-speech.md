# A Speech at the Town Hall

*An original speech written for this unit. The speaker, the town of Alder
Creek, and every number named here are fictional. The speech is a text to
analyze, not a record of a real event. Any resemblance to a real person or
place is coincidental.*

*Delivered by Council Member Rosa Delgado at a town-hall meeting on the
library-hours proposal. Read aloud by the guiding adult in Lesson 06, or
read silently with the same attention to pauses and emphasis.*

Good evening. I have lived in Alder Creek for thirty-one years. I learned
to read in that library, in the children's corner, sitting on a rug that
smelled faintly of old paper. My mother worked the night shift at the
clinic, and she could not take me in the afternoons — so the librarian,
Mrs. Adeyemi, stayed late for me twice a week, off the clock, unpaid,
because she believed a child's reading could not wait for a convenient
hour. I am standing here because someone once kept a door open for me.

Now I am asked: should we keep that door open for others?

I have heard the worry that evening hours will bring trouble. Let me
answer it plainly. The community center, two blocks from the library, has
kept evening hours for six years. Its incident log is public. In those six
years it has recorded fewer incidents than the library parking lot records
in a single summer of daytime use. Fear is not evidence. A logbook is.

I have heard the worry about cost. Two part-time clerks, three evenings a
week, for six months. That is the price of one repaved block of Maple
Street. We repave blocks without a town hall debate. Are we really saying
that asphalt deserves a faster yes than a child's reading?

And I have heard the worry that nobody will come. Last spring, the library
posted a sign-up sheet for evening study rooms. It filled in four days.
There was a waiting list. Those were not signatures collected by a
campaign. Those were names written by hand, by people who wanted a chair,
a lamp, and quiet — people who are asking for almost nothing, and who are
used to being told to wait.

So I ask you tonight: who is this library for? If it is for all of us,
then its hours must reach all of us — the nurse finishing at seven, the
student sharing one computer with three siblings, the father who reads to
his daughter only after his second job ends. If it is only for those of
us with free afternoons, then let us say so honestly, and stop calling
it public.

I am not asking you to fund a palace. I am asking you to fund a trial:
six months, three evenings a week, counted honestly at the end. If the
rooms sit empty, I will vote to end it myself. But do not tell me the
rooms will sit empty before we have ever unlocked them.

Thirty-one years ago, someone kept a door open for me. Tonight, I ask
you to keep it open for them.
