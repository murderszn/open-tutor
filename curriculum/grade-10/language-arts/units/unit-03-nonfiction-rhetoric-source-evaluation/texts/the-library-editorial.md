# Keep the Doors Open

*An original editorial written for this unit. The town of Alder Creek, the
library, and every person and number named here are fictional. Any resemblance
to a real place is coincidental.*

For twenty years, the Alder Creek Public Library has closed its doors at six
o'clock. For twenty years, that closing time has quietly decided who the
library is for.

It is for the people who can get there before six: schoolchildren with a
parent free in the afternoon, retirees with open days, and workers on a day
shift. It is not for the night-shift nurse who finishes at seven and wants a
quiet room to study for her licensing exam. It is not for the high-school
student who shares one computer with three siblings and needs a place to
finish an essay after dinner. It is not for the new father working two jobs
who can only read to his daughter at eight, in the children's corner, where
someone else turns the pages for a change.

The library board is now considering a modest proposal: keep the building
open until nine o'clock on weeknights, three nights a week, for a six-month
trial. The cost is two part-time evening clerks and the electricity to keep
the lights on. The board already owns the building, the books, and the
computers. What it has not owned, until now, is the evening.

Opponents of the trial will tell you that the library is a daytime place,
as if that were a law of nature rather than a habit. They will tell you
that evening hours invite trouble, though the community center two blocks
away stays open until ten and reports fewer incidents than the parking lot
outside the library at noon. They will tell you that nobody will come,
though the sign-up sheet for evening study rooms — posted as an experiment
last spring — filled in four days, with a waiting list.

A library is not a warehouse for books. A warehouse keeps things safe by
keeping people out. A library keeps things useful by letting people in. Every
hour the doors stay shut is an hour in which a nurse, a student, or a father
is told, politely and without a word, that this public room is not theirs.

The trial costs little, and it can be measured honestly. Count the evening
visitors. Count the study-room bookings. Ask the night-shift workers whether
the room helped. If the numbers are small after six months, end the trial
and say so plainly. But do not refuse to try on the grounds that the
library has always closed at six. "Always" is not an argument. It is only
the sound a closed door makes.

Open the doors. Let the town find out who walks in.
