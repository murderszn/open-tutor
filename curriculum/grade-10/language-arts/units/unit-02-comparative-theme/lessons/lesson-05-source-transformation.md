# Lesson 05 — Drawing On and Transforming: How a Retelling Changes Its Source

**Goal (student-readable):** I can analyze how a later author draws on source material and transforms it — naming what is kept, what is changed, and how the theme itself is transformed. (RL.9-10.9)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, [texts/tortoise-hare-and-rematch.md](../texts/tortoise-hare-and-rematch.md) · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Retell "The Tortoise and the Hare" in two sentences — but as the *Fox*, the judge, telling it to a friend that evening. What changes when the teller changes? Note one change.

## Transformation is not repetition

RL.9-10.9 asks readers to analyze **how an author draws on and transforms source material in a specific work**. "The Rematch" draws on Aesop's fable — same Hare, same Tortoise, same race in the backstory — but it is not a *retelling* in the sense of saying the same thing again. It is a **transformation**: it keeps the raw material and changes what the material *means*.

Analyze a transformation in three layers:

1. **Kept** — what the new work carries over unchanged (characters, premise, key events).
2. **Changed** — what is altered: point of view, form, time, which characters matter, what is shown vs. told.
3. **Transformed** — the deepest layer: how the *theme itself* moves. A transformation can keep every plot point and still change the theme — or change every plot point while keeping it. The question is always: *what does the new work claim that the source did not?*

A weak analysis stops at layer 2 ("it's a letter instead of a fable"). A complete analysis reaches layer 3.

## Worked example 1 — The three-layer inventory

| Layer | "The Rematch" vs. the source |
|---|---|
| **Kept** | The Hare, the Tortoise, the race, the nap, the outcome (Tortoise wins). The backstory facts are untouched — Joren never denies he slept. |
| **Changed** | *POV:* third-person fable → first-person letter from the Hare. *Form:* compact fable → epistolary confession. *Time:* the race is over before line one; the story is all aftermath. *Cast:* the Fox vanishes; the Tortoise shrinks to two quoted lines; a new character (Sela, the sister) becomes the audience. *Moral:* the stated moral ("Slow but steady wins the race") is quoted — and then argued with. |
| **Transformed** | The source's theme: *how to win — steadiness beats pride.* The retelling's theme: *who gets to tell the story of your worst day, and what the telling costs.* The race hasn't changed; its *meaning* has. The fable is about the runners; the letter is about the audience — every telling since. |

The key insight: Joren *accepts* the source's facts and *rejects* its frame. That is what transformation looks like — not contradiction, but re-framing.

## Worked example 2 — Modeling a transformation paragraph

> "The Rematch" transforms its source most decisively in its treatment of the moral. Aesop's fable *states* its theme outright — "Slow but steady wins the race" — closing interpretation down. Joren's letter *quotes* that moral ("You know the story the way everyone knows it… 'Slow but steady wins the race.' The leverets chant it") and then shows its human cost: "To have your worst morning turned into everybody's lesson." The transformation is structural as well as thematic: where the fable's meaning arrives in a single closing line, the letter's meaning accumulates across eleven declined rematches — "I have taken over the telling." The source teaches how to race; the retelling asks who owns the story of the race. By keeping the facts and changing the frame, the author transforms a lesson about *winning* into an inquiry about *narrative power*.

Notice the paragraph's anatomy: names the transformation site (the moral), quotes both texts, explains the structural change, and lands on the transformed theme. Every transformation analysis you write should have all four moves.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Find **two more** layer-2 changes the inventory missed (look at: what the Tortoise is allowed to say; what happened to the race as an *event* vs. as a *memory*). For each, say in one sentence whether it also reaches layer 3.
2. With the adult, debate: does "The Rematch" *contradict* Aesop's moral, or *accept and reframe* it? Cite one line for your side. (There is a defensible answer on each side — that is the point.)
3. Name one thing the retelling *could not* do if it changed the race's outcome (if Joren claimed he actually won). What does keeping the facts enable?

## Independent practice

1. Write a three-layer inventory for a transformation you know (a film adaptation, a modern retelling of a myth or fairy tale, a song cover that changes a song's meaning). All three layers, with specifics.
2. Write one transformation paragraph (modeled on Worked example 2) about **the Fox's disappearance**: what does removing the judge do to the story's meaning? Quote or cite.
3. Answer in three sentences: why is "the theme was transformed, not just the plot" the real test of RL.9-10.9? Use "The Rematch" as your evidence.

## Application

Take any story you know well. Change its teller (a minor character, the villain, an object) and write the opening three lines. Then write one sentence on how the theme would have to move.

## Exit check

On an index card: one kept element, one changed element, and the transformed theme of "The Rematch" — each in a single phrase or sentence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the inventory may be built orally; quotations may be paraphrased with line references.
- **Alternate response mode:** the three-layer inventory works as a three-column drawing or a dictated list; the paragraph may be dictated.
- **Extension:** write a 150-word counter-retelling — the *Tortoise's* reply to Joren's letter (she has read it). What does her POV transform *now*? (Whose frame wins?)

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners stop at "it's different" (layer 2) and call it analysis — always push to layer 3: *what does the new work claim that the source did not?* Another trap: treating transformation as *correction* ("the retelling fixes the fable") — redirect to *reframing*: Joren never says the fable lied. Watch for the reverse error too: claiming *everything* changed and missing what was kept — the kept facts are what make the transformation legible. If a learner can't name what's kept, they can't see what's transformed. Answer key: § L5.
