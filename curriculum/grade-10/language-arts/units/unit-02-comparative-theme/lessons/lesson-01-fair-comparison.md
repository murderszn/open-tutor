# Lesson 01 — What Makes a Comparison Fair

**Goal (student-readable):** I can judge whether a comparison is fair (shared question, comparable evidence, named differences) and organize a comparison point-by-point or in blocks. (Foundations for RL.9-10.2/5/7/9 comparative work)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, [texts/the-salt-road.md](../texts/the-salt-road.md), [texts/the-harbor-light.md](../texts/the-harbor-light.md) · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L1](../answer-key.md#lesson-01)

**Readiness check (5 min):** In your notebook, answer: *Is a bicycle better than a train?* Write your answer in two sentences. Then answer this: *what would you need to know before your answer could be fair?* Share one thought with the adult.

## Comparison is a method, not an opinion

This unit compares stories: how two works handle the same human concern, how their structures create different effects, how a retelling transforms its source, how a scene changes across mediums. Every one of those tasks can go wrong in the same way — by comparing unfairly.

A **fair comparison** has three parts:

1. **A shared question.** Both things are measured against the *same* question. "Which is better?" is not a shared question — better *at what?* "Which gets me to school faster on a rainy day?" is. In this unit our shared questions will look like: *What does each story claim we owe a stranger?* or *How does each text's structure shape its theme?*
2. **Comparable evidence.** Evidence from each side must be the *same kind* of evidence. Comparing one story's ending to another story's opening sentence is like comparing a bicycle's top speed to a train's paint color — the evidence doesn't meet.
3. **Named differences.** A fair comparison says what is *different*, not just what is similar, and names the differences precisely. "Both stories are about hospitality" is a weak comparison because it hides the differences. "One story treats hospitality as a debt the road enforces; the other treats it as a custom no one repays" names them.

If any of the three is missing, the comparison is unfair — and an unfair comparison always favors whichever side the comparer already liked.

## Worked example 1 — Fair vs. unfair

**Unfair:** "The Salt Road is more exciting than The Harbor Light, because Sef faces a life-or-death choice and Mara just remembers things."

What's wrong: no shared question (exciting *to whom*? measured how?), and the evidence isn't comparable — a present-tense crisis scene is being measured against a memory scene, and the difference in *structure* (in medias res vs. retrospective) is being mistaken for a difference in *quality*.

**Fair:** Shared question: *How does each story build tension?* Comparable evidence: the opening 200 words of each story. Named differences: "The Salt Road opens in medias res — a stranger stumbles into the firelight in the first line — so tension comes from immediate danger and unanswered questions (who is he? will Sef share water?). The Harbor Light opens with a sixty-year-old narrator stating a routine ('I keep the light the way my mother kept it'), so tension is deliberately withheld; the story's pull comes from the slow approach of a known loss (the coat, the ingratitude). The two stories build opposite kinds of tension on purpose."

Notice the fair version never says which is better. It says what each *does*.

## Worked example 2 — Two ways to organize: point-by-point vs. block

Once the comparison is fair, it needs a shape. Two standard shapes:

**Point-by-point** alternates between the texts under each point:

- *Point 1 — What is owed:* In "The Salt Road," Sef owes water by law ("A man at your fire is owed water, no questions, until the third watch"); refusing has a history (Tam's death). In "The Harbor Light," Mara's mother owes nothing by law; she gives "because we are the kind of people who keep a light" — the obligation is identity, not rule.
- *Point 2 — Whether repayment matters:* In "The Salt Road," repayment is the whole engine — Sef settles the company's debt with the salt cake. In "The Harbor Light," repayment never comes (no thanks, no returned coat) and the story insists that changes nothing.
- *So what:* the same concern — what we owe a stranger — takes opposite shapes: enforced debt vs. unpriced custom.

**Block** finishes one text, then the other, then compares:

- *Block 1 — "The Salt Road":* hospitality as enforced debt; water is owed by road-law; the ending settles an old account.
- *Block 2 — "The Harbor Light":* hospitality as unpriced custom; the coat is given with no expectation; the ending refuses the idea of accounts entirely.
- *So what (comparison block):* ...

Point-by-point is better when the points are sharp and paired; block is better when each text needs its own full picture first. Both are fair *if* the three rules hold.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Each of these comparison attempts breaks one fairness rule. Name the broken rule and fix it in one sentence:
   a. "The Salt Road's ending is stronger than The Harbor Light's beginning."
   b. "Both stories show that people should be kind to strangers."
   c. "The Harbor Light is better because it's about a mother and mothers are more sympathetic than guards."
2. With the adult, choose: for the shared question *"What does each story claim we owe a stranger?"*, would you organize point-by-point or in blocks? Give one reason tied to the evidence, not to preference.

## Independent practice

1. Write a shared question for comparing "The Salt Road" and "The Harbor Light" on the topic of *structure* (not theme). It must be answerable with comparable evidence from both stories.
2. List two pieces of comparable evidence — one from each story — that answer your question. Quote or closely paraphrase.
3. Name one difference your comparison must not hide, in a single precise sentence.

## Application

Pick two things from your own life that people compare unfairly (two games, two schools, two ways of traveling, two family rules). Write the unfair version in one sentence, then rewrite it as a fair comparison with a shared question and named differences.

## Exit check

On an index card: in your own words, the three rules of a fair comparison — plus one named difference between "The Salt Road" and "The Harbor Light" that a lazy comparison would hide.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read either story aloud; the comparison rules are the work, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** the fairness diagnosis may be given orally; outlines may be dictated or drawn as two-column charts.
- **Extension:** take the unfair example in Worked example 1 and rewrite it twice — once point-by-point, once in blocks — then argue which shape serves that comparison better.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often think comparison means *ranking* ("which is better") — redirect to the shared question every time: *better at what, measured how?* Another common error is comparing a *feature* of one text to a *different feature* of the other (ending vs. beginning, dialogue vs. description); insist the evidence be the same kind on both sides. Watch for the similarity trap: "both stories are about X" stated as if it were a finding — press for the named difference, which is where all comparative insight lives. Answer key: § L1.
