# Lesson 06 — One Scene, Two Mediums: What Text Emphasizes and What Image Leaves Out

**Goal (student-readable):** I can analyze how the same subject is represented in two artistic mediums — naming what each emphasizes and what each leaves out — and use that analysis to plan my comparative essay. (RL.9-10.7)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, [texts/the-keeper-at-the-crossroads.md](../texts/the-keeper-at-the-crossroads.md), the illustration below · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L6](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Read "The Keeper at the Crossroads" once. Close it. Draw the scene from memory in 90 seconds — stick figures welcome. Open the story again: what did your drawing emphasize that the text barely mentions? What did it leave out that the text stresses?

## A medium is a set of affordances

RL.9-10.7 asks: **analyze the representation of a subject or a key scene in two different artistic mediums, including what is emphasized or absent in each treatment.** Every medium can do some things easily and other things barely at all:

- **Prose** does *time, interiority, and sense-data* well: the hiss of a taper, a memory of busy roads, the meaning of a dark fourth lantern. It does *simultaneous space* poorly — it must describe things one after another.
- **A still image** does *simultaneous space, color, and composition* well: amber against indigo, the keeper's bent posture, the visible emptiness of two roads at once. It does *time and interiority* poorly — it cannot show "every night" or "she remembers" except by suggestion.

Neither is "better." The analysis is in the **trade**: what each medium gains by its strengths and loses by its limits.

## Worked example 1 — Emphasized and absent, in each medium

| | Prose passage | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **Emphasizes** | *Sound and smell:* "The taper hisses," "oil smells of sesame and smoke" — senses an image cannot render. *Time and habit:* "Every night at dusk" — the image shows one instant; the text shows years. *Interiority:* the keeper's unspoken sentence ("The light is not for them") and the final thought about lighting the fourth lantern — the image can only imply. | *Color as meaning:* amber lanterns against indigo dark — warmth literally surrounded by cold, visible all at once. *Posture:* the keeper's bent back and worn cloak *show* age and labor without a word. *Simultaneous emptiness:* both roads visible *at once*, empty in both directions — the text must say "empty" twice; the image says it in one glance. |
| **Leaves out / cannot do** | The exact visual arrangement — a reader could never reconstruct the shrine's shape, the roads' angles, the branches from the words alone. The *felt* scale of the dark. | The hiss, the smell, the memory of the busy roads, the traveler's visit last spring, the meaning of the fourth lantern ("for the traveler who is not coming") — the image shows a dark lantern but cannot say *why* it is dark. |

The comparative insight: the passage's power is in **duration and interiority** (what the keeper remembers and means); the image's power is in **immediacy and space** (what solitude looks like, all at once). The fourth lantern is the perfect test case — in prose it is a *meaning* (hope, withheld); in the image it is a *shape* (dark among lit). Same object, different work.

## Worked example 2 — From medium comparison to thesis

> "Although the prose passage and the illustration depict the same night at the crossroads shrine, each medium makes a different claim about the keeper's solitude. The passage builds solitude through *time* — 'every night at dusk,' the remembered busy roads, the traveler's visit 'last spring' — so that loneliness becomes a *history*. The illustration builds solitude through *space* — the small amber shrine centered in vast indigo dark, both roads empty in a single glance — so that loneliness becomes a *condition*, visible all at once. The difference reveals what each medium is for: prose explains why solitude persists; the image shows what solitude looks like from the outside."

Note the structure: shared subject → what each emphasizes → what the difference reveals. The same three-part thesis shape from Lesson 02, now applied across mediums.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Name one detail the illustration emphasizes that the passage *never mentions* (look at the keeper's clothing, the branches, the shrine's construction). What does that emphasis add that the text doesn't?
2. Name one passage detail the illustration *cannot* show (sound, smell, memory, or the fourth lantern's meaning). How would you convey it if you had to *draw* it — and what would be lost?
3. With the adult, test this claim: "The illustration makes the keeper look lonelier than the passage does." Fair or unfair comparison — and which fairness rule from Lesson 01 applies?

## Independent practice

1. Write a two-medium comparison paragraph (modeled on Worked example 2) about **light itself**: how the passage *describes* the lanterns lighting vs. how the image *shows* them. Quote the passage once.
2. Begin your comparative essay plan (due with the unit assessment): choose your two texts (the paired stories, the fable + retelling, or passage + illustration), write your shared question, and draft a three-part thesis. List the four strongest evidence pairs (quotation + quotation) you will use.
3. For your chosen pair, decide: point-by-point or block? Write two sentences justifying the choice from the *evidence*, not from habit.

## Application

Find any illustrated scene you know (a book cover, a film still of a book scene, an album cover for a song). In four sentences: what the image emphasizes, what it leaves out, and what that trade reveals about the medium.

## Exit check

On an index card: one thing the *text* does that the *image* cannot, and one thing the *image* does that the *text* cannot — each with a specific detail.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the comparison may be done from adult read-aloud plus guided looking; the text-only alternative above carries the image's content.
- **Alternate response mode:** the emphasized/absent chart may be drawn as two overlapping circles (Venn) with details placed by medium; the essay plan may be dictated.
- **Extension:** the passage says the keeper "did not finish the sentence" — the traveler supplies the ending. Write the traveler's *own* 100-word account of that dusk (a second transformation, cf. Lesson 05): what does his POV emphasize that the passage leaves out?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners judge the image as "accurate" or "inaccurate" to the text — redirect: the question is never fidelity but *emphasis*: what does each treatment choose to stress, and what does the trade reveal? Watch for learners listing only visual details of the image without connecting them to meaning (amber vs. indigo *as* warmth-surrounded-by-cold). The guided-practice item 3 is a fairness check in disguise: "lonelier" is not a shared question with comparable evidence — press them to name the rule it breaks. This lesson also launches the essay; check the plan's evidence pairs now, while revision is cheap. Answer key: § L6.
