# Lesson 03 — First Each Text, Then the Comparison: Dual Theme Tracking

**Goal (student-readable):** I can track how a theme emerges and is refined by specific details *within* each text before comparing across texts — and revise my theme statement when the details demand it. (RL.9-10.2)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, both unit stories · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write a one-sentence theme for "The Salt Road" as fast as you can — your *v1*. Now write one sentence about what in the story your v1 does *not* explain. That gap is today's work.

## Compare only what you have actually read

The most common comparative failure is not unfairness — it is *thinness*. The writer compares two half-read texts and produces a half-true comparison. The rule of this lesson: **analyze each text's theme development fully before a single comparative claim is made.**

A **theme tracker** is a running record with four columns:

| Point in the text | Detail (quoted or closely paraphrased) | What it adds to the theme | Theme statement, revised |

The last column is the discipline: after each detail, you restate the theme *as the text has so far earned it*. A theme statement that never changes across a whole story was never really tracking — it was a guess wearing a tracker's clothes.

Theme here means the U01 distinction: not the *topic* (hospitality) but the *claim the text makes about the topic* — what the story says is true about what we owe a stranger.

## Worked example 1 — Tracking "The Salt Road"

| Point in the text | Detail | What it adds | Theme statement, revised |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | "A man at your fire is owed water, no questions, until the third watch." | Hospitality is stated as *law*, not kindness — owed, not given. | v1: The story claims hospitality is a strict obligation. |
| Flashback | The company captain: "Our charter is coin first"; Tam dies. | The law has an opposite — refusal is also a system, and it kills. Obligation is not universal; it is *chosen by communities*. | v2: The story claims hospitality is an obligation communities choose — and the choice defines them. |
| Climax | Sef breaks the salt cake, returns half: "The road keeps accounts, but not in coin. Tell your company the debt is paid." | The obligation becomes *justice*: Sef uses the company's own debt-logic against it, settling Tam's death without cruelty. | v3: The story claims hospitality is a binding debt — and debts can be collected as justice, not only paid as duty. |

Notice v1 → v3: the theme got *narrower and stranger* — that is refinement. A theme that stays "hospitality is good" through all three rows has not been tracking.

## Worked example 2 — Tracking "The Harbor Light"

| Point in the text | Detail | What it adds | Theme statement, revised |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening frame | "I keep the light the way my mother kept it" — sixty-year-old Mara, routine. | Hospitality is *inherited practice*, not a decision made fresh each time. | v1: The story claims hospitality is a family tradition. |
| The coat | Mother gives the father's wool coat; Tomas takes it, never thanks her, never returns it. | The tradition *costs* — and the cost is never recovered. The story refuses to soften this. | v2: The story claims hospitality is a tradition that costs the giver and expects nothing back. |
| Closing | Adult Mara, the fleet now with radios, still lights the lamp; "The light is not a bargain." | The tradition survives its own uselessness — the light is kept when no one needs it. So it was never *for* the stranger at all. | v3: The story claims hospitality is an identity — "the kind of people who keep a light" — complete in the giving, not in the result. |

Now the comparison has something to stand on: v3 vs. v3. "The Salt Road" ends with debts *settled*; "The Harbor Light" ends with accounts *refused*. Same topic — opposite claims. That is a comparative insight earned by tracking, not by guessing.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Add one more row to the "Salt Road" tracker: use the stranger's line "Name your price. The company pays its debts." What does it add, and does v3 survive it or need a v4?
2. Find the detail in "The Harbor Light" where nine-year-old Mara is "furious" about the coat. Add it as a tracker row: what does the child's anger add to the theme? (Hint: the story includes the *objection* to its own theme — why?)
3. With the adult, test this claim: "You cannot fairly compare the two stories' themes until each has a v3." True or false — and what would a v1-vs.-v1 comparison get wrong?

## Independent practice

1. Write a v1 theme statement for "The Keeper at the Crossroads" (text only, no illustration yet) from a first read. Then re-read the last three lines and write v2. What changed?
2. Choose one story and add two tracker rows the worked examples skipped. Each row must quote or closely paraphrase, and each must *change or confirm* the current theme statement — say which.
3. Write three sentences: the fair comparative claim your dual tracking now supports about the two stories' themes. (This is raw material for the essay — save it.)

## Application

Take a film or book you know well. Write a v1 theme, then find one detail that forces a v2. One paragraph: what the detail added and why v1 was too thin.

## Exit check

On an index card: your v3 theme statement for ONE of the two stories, plus the single quoted detail that forced the biggest revision from v1.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** tracker rows may be built from adult read-aloud; quoting may be replaced by close paraphrase with page/paragraph reference.
- **Alternate response mode:** the tracker may be a spoken back-and-forth (adult scribes), or a two-column drawing with arrows showing revision.
- **Extension:** track a *second* theme in one story (e.g., memory, justice, identity) through three details, and write two sentences on how the two themes interact — which one the story finally serves.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners confuse topic with theme ("the theme is hospitality") — require the *claim* form: "the story claims *that*…" Another failure: the tracker where every row "supports" an unchanged v1 — that is confirmation, not tracking; require at least one revision and ask what forced it. Watch for comparison-before-tracking: if the learner's comparative claim could have been written before reading (e.g., "both are about kindness"), send them back to the tracker. The guided-practice item 2 matters: the story *includes* Mara's childish fury as an objection it then answers — strong theme-tracking notices when a text argues with itself. Answer key: § L3.
