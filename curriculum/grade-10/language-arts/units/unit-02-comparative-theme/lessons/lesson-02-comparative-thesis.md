# Lesson 02 — The Comparative Thesis: An Arguable Claim About Two Texts

**Goal (student-readable):** I can write a thesis that makes an arguable comparative claim — saying not just that two texts are alike or different, but *what the likeness or difference reveals*. (W.9-10.2, W.9-10.9)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, both unit stories · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write this sentence in your notebook and finish it three different ways: *"The Salt Road" and "The Harbor Light" are both about hospitality, but __________.* Share your strongest finish with the adult.

## A thesis is an argument, not a topic

A **comparative thesis** answers the reader's real question: *so what?* It has three parts:

1. **The texts and the shared question** — what is being compared, and on what grounds.
2. **The difference or likeness, named precisely** — not "they're different" but *how*.
3. **The "so what"** — what the difference or likeness *reveals* about the theme, the traditions, or the authors' choices.

A thesis that lacks part 3 is a **topic sentence wearing a thesis costume**. "This essay will compare how 'The Salt Road' and 'The Harbor Light' treat hospitality" announces a topic. It argues nothing. A reader can agree with it without reading the essay.

## Worked example 1 — From topic to thesis

**Weak (topic only):** "Both 'The Salt Road' and 'The Harbor Light' are about showing hospitality to strangers."

**Stronger (difference named, no "so what"):** "While 'The Salt Road' treats hospitality as a debt enforced by the road's law, 'The Harbor Light' treats it as a custom no one is required to repay."

**Thesis (all three parts):** "While 'The Salt Road' treats hospitality as a debt the road's law enforces — Sef gives water because the law demands it and settles an old account with the salt cake — 'The Harbor Light' treats hospitality as an unpriced custom — Mara's mother gives the coat expecting nothing, and the story insists the giving is complete without thanks. The difference reveals two opposite answers to the same question: whether what we owe a stranger can ever be settled."

Note the moves: *while X…, Y…* sets up the contrast; each half carries its key evidence; the final clause says what the contrast *reveals*. That last clause is the arguable part — a reader could disagree with it, which is exactly what makes it a thesis.

## Worked example 2 — Revising a "so what"-less thesis

**Draft:** "The two stories have different structures: 'The Salt Road' jumps around in time and 'The Harbor Light' goes in order."

Diagnosis: difference named (part 2 ✓), but no shared question is stated and there is no "so what" — it reports a fact any reader can see.

**Revision:** "Although 'The Salt Road' fractures time — opening in medias res, flashing back to Tam's death — while 'The Harbor Light' moves chronologically from one winter night to a lifetime of lamp-keeping, both structures serve the same end: they make the theme *unavoidable*. Sef cannot escape the past because the story keeps returning to it; Mara cannot escape the custom because the story never leaves the present long enough to forget it. The authors' opposite structural choices reveal that a theme can be built by disruption or by patience."

The revision keeps the observed difference but attaches it to an argument about *what the structures do*. That is the step from description to analysis.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Revise each weak thesis. Keep what is true, add the missing part(s), and read the result aloud to test whether a reasonable reader could disagree:

1. "This essay compares the points of view in 'The Salt Road' and 'The Harbor Light.'"
2. "'The Salt Road' is told by a limited third-person narrator and 'The Harbor Light' is told in first person, so they are very different."
3. "Both stories end with the main character continuing a tradition, which shows hospitality is important in both cultures."

*(Hint: ask of each draft — "Could someone disagree with this? If not, it is not yet a thesis.")*

## Independent practice

1. Write a comparative thesis on this shared question: *How does each story's point of view shape what the reader is allowed to know?* (Consider: Sef's limited third-person present vs. Mara's first-person retrospective.) Include all three parts.
2. Write a second thesis on a shared question of your own choosing about the two stories. Underline the "so what" clause.
3. Trade with the adult: the adult plays skeptic and tries to agree with your thesis *without reading any evidence*. If they succeed, revise — a thesis should need its evidence.

## Application

Take the fair comparison you wrote for Lesson 01's application (two games, schools, routes, or rules). Write a three-part thesis about it: the shared question, the named difference, and what the difference reveals. (Yes — theses work outside literature. That is the point.)

## Exit check

On an index card: one comparative thesis about the two unit stories, with the "so what" clause underlined. Below it, in five words or fewer, name which part was hardest to write.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** theses may be built from the adult's read-aloud; the argument is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** thesis drafts may be dictated; the "so what" test may be done as a spoken debate with the adult.
- **Extension:** write an *anti-thesis* — the strongest arguable claim *against* your Lesson 02 independent-practice thesis — then write two sentences on which thesis the evidence actually favors and why. (This is the seed of the counterargument skill U06 will develop.)

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The most common failure is the announcement thesis ("In this essay I will compare…") — treat it as a missing "so what," not a formatting error. Learners also write theses that are true but unarguable ("the stories are different"); the skeptic test in independent practice is the fix — if agreement costs nothing, there is no argument. Watch for theses where the "so what" merely repeats the difference in fancier words; press: *what does the difference reveal that I couldn't see from either story alone?* Answer key: § L2.
