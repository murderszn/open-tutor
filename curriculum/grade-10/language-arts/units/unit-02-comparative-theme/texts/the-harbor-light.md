# The Harbor Light

*An original story written for this unit. Coastal fishing-village tradition.*

I am sixty now, and I keep the light the way my mother kept it: a lamp in the front window, lit at dusk, burning until the boats are in or the night is done. People in the village ask me why I bother — the harbor has electric beacons now, and the fishing fleet has radios. I tell them what my mother told me when I asked her, at nine years old, the same question.

"The light is not a bargain, Mara."

It was the winter I turned nine that I learned what she meant. The cold came early that year and stayed. The fleet was out for the late mackerel, and the wind had been wrong for three days — wrong in the way that makes old men check the sky and say nothing.

The strange boat came in after dark. Not one of ours: a low trading sloop from the southern islands, driven north by the storm, its mast cracked, its sail in ribbons. My mother saw it from the window before the harbor bell rang. She put on her coat and mine, and we went down to the dock with the lantern.

The sailor was half-frozen. That is not a figure of speech; his hands were white to the wrist, and he could not make his fingers close around the rope my mother threw him. She got him up the dock with her shoulder under his arm — he was a big man and she was not — and brought him home, and sat him by the stove, and wrapped him in blankets, and fed him soup one spoonful at a time the way you feed a child.

He slept for a day and a night. When he woke, he ate everything we put in front of him and said very little. He had a name — Tomas — and a wife in the southern islands, and that was all we learned. On the third morning the wind turned fair, and he went down to his boat, and my mother gave him my father's coat — the good wool one, the one she had kept folded in cedar since my father drowned — because his own coat had gone overboard with the mast.

"Your father would want a man to go home warm," she said.

Tomas took the coat. He nodded once. He did not thank her — not then, not in any letter after. The sloop went out with the tide, and we never saw him again, and the coat never came back.

I was furious. Nine years old, and I understood perfectly well that we had been robbed — that the coat was worth something, that gratitude was owed, that the world had rules and Tomas had broken them.

My mother listened to all of it. Then she trimmed the lamp, and lit it, and set it in the window, though every boat was safe in harbor and there was no one left to guide.

"Was it a bargain, Mara?" she said. "Did I give the soup on condition of thanks?"

"No," I said, because even at nine I knew a trap when I heard one.

"Then nothing was broken. The light is not a bargain." She looked out at the dark water. "Someday you will give something that costs you, and no one will thank you, and you will want to take up the lamp and smash it. On that day, remember: we do not light it for them. We light it because we are the kind of people who keep a light."

I am sixty now. The fleet has radios. The coat is forty years gone. Tomas is long dead, I expect, or very old.

Every dusk, I trim the lamp, and light it, and set it in the window.

Last week a boat I did not know limped in after dark, running before a squall. I put on my coat. I went down to the dock with the lantern.

The light is not a bargain.
