# The Salt Road

*An original story written for this unit. Desert-caravan tradition.*

The stranger came out of the dark between one breath and the next.

Sef had been watching the fire eat the last of the thornwood, and then there was a shape at the edge of the light — a man on his knees, one hand raised, the other clutching a waterskin so empty it folded like cloth. Behind him the dunes went on and on, white under the moon, and there was no camel, no companion, no track Sef could see.

"Water," the man said. It was not a question.

Sef's spear was in his hand before he knew he had lifted it. That was the training: anything that moves at the edge of the fire gets the spear first and the questions after. But the road-law was older than the training, and it spoke first. *A man at your fire is owed water, no questions, until the third watch.*

Sef lowered the spear. "Sit," he said. "Slowly."

---

Three years before, Sef had learned what the road-law costs when it is broken.

He had been nineteen, riding guard for his uncle's salt caravan on the northern crossing, and his brother Tam had come along for the wages — sixteen, all elbows, certain he would see the sea. On the ninth day the second water camel went down in the heat, and by the eleventh their jars were down to the dregs, brown and warm. On the twelfth day a company caravan passed them going north, twenty camels, water sloshing in every jar — and the company's captain would not unseal a single one.

"Our charter is coin first," the captain said, not unkindly. "I cannot open the company's water on a guardsman's word."

Tam died before sunset. He died quietly, which Sef had not expected; he had expected his brother to fight it. After, old Ilya — the caravan master who had ridden the salt road forty years — sat with Sef beside the small cairn of stones and said the thing Sef would carry the rest of his life.

"The road keeps accounts, boy. Not in coin — coin is the company's fiction. In water. You will be owed, and you will owe. The law is not kindness. The law is the road refusing to let us become the kind of men who watch a boy die for a charter."

Sef had nodded because there was nothing else to do with his hands.

---

The stranger drank the way drowning men are said to breathe — in desperate, disbelieving pulls. Sef watched the man's throat work and counted the swallows, and at twenty he took the cup away and gave it back, the way Ilya had taught him: a little, then a little more, or the water itself will kill him.

"Your name?" Sef said.

"Call me what you like," the man said. "The road took my name two days ago with my camel." He fished inside his robe and brought out a cake of pressed salt, white as bone, stamped with a wheel. "For the water. The company's mark — it's good salt."

Sef knew the mark. The wheel was the charter-sign of the Meridian Salt Company — the same company whose captain had ridden past Tam with sealed jars.

The stranger saw him recognize it. "You know it," he said. "I ride dispatch for them. Or I did." He pushed the salt cake across the sand toward Sef, and then, because Sef did not move, he added the pouch from his belt — coin, by the weight of it. "For the water," he said again. "Name your price. The company pays its debts."

Sef looked at the salt cake. He looked at the pouch. He looked at the man's cracked lips, already healing at the corners from the water.

Then Sef broke the salt cake in half.

He put one half in his own pack. He pressed the other half into the stranger's hand and closed the man's fingers over it, the way you close a child's fingers over something precious.

"The road keeps accounts," Sef said, "but not in coin. Tell your company the debt is paid."

The stranger stared at him. "What debt?"

Sef stood, took up his spear, and walked back to his post at the edge of the firelight — because some things are not for the telling, and the road, which had taken his brother, had this once given something back: the exact shape of the refusal, returned to its makers, with interest compounded in water.

Behind him, he heard the stranger break the half-cake and eat, weeping quietly, the way men weep when they have been spared by a law they do not understand.
