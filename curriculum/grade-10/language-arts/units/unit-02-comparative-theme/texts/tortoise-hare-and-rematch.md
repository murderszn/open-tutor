# Source and Transformation: Aesop's "The Hare and the Tortoise" and "The Rematch"

## The source — Aesop's "The Hare and the Tortoise"

Public domain. George Fyler Townsend's 1867 translation, wording verified
2026-10-20 against the Townsend version printed at fablesofaesop.com
(which cites the Townsend text). Fablesofaesop.com labels this the
"Townsend version":

> A Hare one day ridiculed the short feet and slow pace of the Tortoise,
> who replied, laughing: "Though you be swift as the wind, I will beat
> you in a race." The Hare, believing her assertion to be simply
> impossible, assented to the proposal; and they agreed that the Fox
> should choose the course and fix the goal. On the day appointed for the
> race the two started together. The Tortoise never for a moment stopped,
> but went on with a slow but steady pace straight to the end of the
> course. The Hare, lying down by the wayside, fell fast asleep. At last
> waking up, and moving as fast as he could, he saw the Tortoise had
> reached the goal, and was comfortably dozing after her fatigue.
>
> *Moral: Slow but steady wins the race.*

**Source notes for comparison work:** third-person narration; the whole
race told in a single forward-moving sequence; both racers characterized
by one trait each (the Hare's mockery, the Tortoise's steadiness); the
Fox exists only to start the race; the theme is stated outright as a
moral. The story is about *the race* — who wins and why.

## The transformation — "The Rematch"

*An original retelling written for this unit. A letter, years later, from
the Hare to his younger sister.*

Dear Sela,

You asked me yesterday, in front of the whole warren, why I never raced
the Tortoise again. Everyone laughed. I laughed too — I have had years of
practice at laughing at the right moment.

Here is the true answer, for you alone: I have raced her eleven times
since. Eleven rematches, on eleven spring mornings, and she has declined
every one. "I have nothing left to prove, dear," she says, "and you have
nothing left to prove either, if you would only stop trying to prove it."

She is wrong about the second part. That is the part I need you to
understand.

You know the story the way everyone knows it. The Hare mocked the
Tortoise. The Hare napped. The Tortoise won. *Slow but steady wins the
race.* The leverets chant it when they play at racing. The old ones tell
it when some young buck gets too pleased with himself.

What the story does not say — what no telling of it has ever said — is
what it is like to be the Hare in that story. To have your worst morning
turned into everybody's lesson. To walk into a meadow and hear your own
name used as a warning. I was nineteen, Sela. I am forty-one now, and I
am still, in every telling, nineteen and asleep by the wayside.

So I do what the story will not let me do inside it: I change the frame
around it. Every spring I challenge her, publicly, politely, where
everyone can see — and every spring she declines, graciously, where
everyone can see. Do you see what that does? It writes a new last line.
The story used to end with me asleep. Now, in the warren's telling, it
ends with me standing at the starting line, ready, and *her* walking
away. The young ones have started saying a new thing: "Even the Hare
keeps showing up."

I have not beaten her. I will never beat her; she will never race me.
But I have done the only thing a character in someone else's moral can
do: I have taken over the telling.

Do not pity me. Pity is another kind of moral. Just remember, when they
tell you the story — and they will — that the Hare in it was a boy who
made one mistake on one morning, and that the Tortoise won the race, but
the *telling* is still being run, every spring, and I intend to keep
running it.

Your brother,
Joren

**Transformation notes for comparison work:** first-person epistolary
form (a letter, not a fable); the race itself is over before the first
line — the story is about the *aftermath*; the Hare is no longer a flat
embodiment of arrogance but a narrator managing his reputation; the
Tortoise barely appears; the Fox is gone entirely; there is no stated
moral — the theme has moved from "how to win a race" to "who gets to tell
the story of your worst day, and what the telling costs." The source's
theme (steadiness beats pride) is *replaced*, not repeated: the retelling
draws on the source's characters and premise and transforms them into a
story about reputation, shame, and narrative control.
