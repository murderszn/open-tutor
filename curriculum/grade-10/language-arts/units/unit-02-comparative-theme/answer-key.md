# Answer Key — Unit 02: Comparative Theme Structure and Perspective

Every item solved independently; acceptable variation noted. Open
responses: the reasoning shown is what earns credit, not exact wording.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. a. Breaks *comparable evidence* (an ending vs. a beginning are
   different kinds of evidence). Fix: "Compare the two stories' *endings*
   on the shared question of how each resolves its theme."
   b. Breaks *named differences* (hides differences under similarity).
   Fix: "Name how each story's hospitality differs — e.g., 'While both
   stories value hospitality, one treats it as enforceable debt and the
   other as unpriced custom.'"
   c. Breaks *shared question* ("better" at what? plus smuggled
   preference). Fix: "On the shared question of how POV shapes sympathy,
   the first-person retrospective gives access to motive while the
   limited third-person withholds it."
2. Accept either organization with an evidence-based reason. Sample:
   point-by-point — "because the claim 'what we owe a stranger' breaks
   cleanly into paired sub-claims (what is owed / whether repayment
   matters), so alternating keeps each pair side by side." Block is
   acceptable if justified (e.g., "each story's system needs full
   explanation before the contrast lands").

**Independent practice**

1. Sample: "How does each story order its events, and what effect does
   the order create?" (Answerable with: Salt Road's in-medias-res +
   flashback vs. Harbor Light's chronology — same kind of evidence.)
2. Sample evidence: Salt Road — "The stranger came out of the dark
   between one breath and the next" (opening in medias res); Harbor
   Light — "I am sixty now, and I keep the light the way my mother kept
   it" (chronological frame announced up front).
3. Sample: "The Salt Road makes the past interrupt the present; The
   Harbor Light makes the past *become* the present through memory."

**Exit check:** Three rules in own words (shared question, comparable
evidence, named differences) + one hidden difference, e.g., "One story
ends by settling a debt; the other ends by refusing the idea of debts."

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice** (sample revisions; any arguable three-part version
earns credit)

1. → "Although both stories ask what we owe a stranger, 'The Salt Road'
   answers with enforceable debt while 'The Harbor Light' answers with
   unpriced custom — revealing that 'hospitality' names opposite
   obligations in the two traditions."
2. → "While 'The Salt Road''s limited third-person locks the reader to
   Sef's present-tense ignorance and 'The Harbor Light''s retrospective
   first-person hands the reader the ending up front, both POVs control
   *when* the reader understands — showing that point of view is a
   timing device, not just a camera angle."
3. → "Both stories end with a continued tradition, but 'The Salt Road'
   continues a *law* (the road's accounts, settled) while 'The Harbor
   Light' continues an *identity* ('the kind of people who keep a
   light') — so the same gesture, continued, means opposite things."

**Independent practice**

1. Sample: "While Sef's limited third-person present withholds the
   stranger's identity until the wheel-stamp reveal — manufacturing
   surprise — Mara's retrospective first-person gives away the ending in
   line one, shifting interest from *what happens* to *what it meant*;
   the contrast shows POV decides not what the reader sees but *when the
   reader is allowed to understand*." ("So what" underlined.)
2. Learner's own question + thesis; credit for all three parts and an
   underlined arguable clause.
3. Skeptic test: credit if the learner can articulate why the thesis
   needs evidence (a skeptic *cannot* agree for free). If they can,
   revision required.

**Exit check:** One three-part thesis with underlined "so what" + the
hardest part named (usually the "so what").

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. The stranger's "Name your price. The company pays its debts" adds:
   the *company's* logic (everything settles in coin) vs. the *road's*
   logic (accounts kept in water). v3 survives — this line is the foil v3
   already answers — but a v4 is defensible: "…and the story stages the
   two accounting systems side by side so the road's can defeat the
   company's."
2. Nine-year-old Mara's fury adds the *objection*: the story voices the
   reader's protest ("we had been robbed") before answering it ("The
   light is not a bargain"). A theme that survives its own objection is
   stronger; the anger proves the cost is real, not sentimental.
3. True. A v1-vs.-v1 comparison ("both about hospitality/kindness")
   mistakes the topic for the claim and misses that the stories make
   *opposite* claims (debts settled vs. accounts refused).

**Independent practice**

1. v1 sample: "The passage claims keeping faith matters even when no
   one sees." v2 (after the last lines): "…and the kept faith is
   *hope held in reserve* — the dark fourth lantern means the ritual is
   incomplete on purpose, waiting." What changed: v1 was generic; the
   fourth lantern forced specificity about *withheld* hope.
2. Credit for two new quoted rows that change or confirm the theme
   statement, with the change/confirm named.
3. Sample: "Where 'The Salt Road' claims what we owe a stranger is a
   debt the community enforces, 'The Harbor Light' claims it is an
   identity no one can collect on. The same topic yields opposite
   obligations — so 'hospitality' is not one virtue but two."

**Exit check:** One v3 statement + the detail that forced the biggest
revision (e.g., Salt Road v3 + the salt-cake split; Harbor Light v3 +
"the light is not a bargain").

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**

1. Sample chart cells — Order: Salt Road, "The stranger came out of the
   dark between one breath and the next" (in medias res) / Harbor Light,
   "I am sixty now…" (chronological frame). Pacing: Salt Road, ~150
   words on the arrival vs. one sentence on three years / Harbor Light,
   ~400 words on one night vs. one paragraph on forty years. POV: Salt
   Road, third-person limited on Sef ("Sef's spear was in his hand before
   he knew") / Harbor Light, first-person retrospective ("I am sixty
   now").
2. Agree (defensible either way with reasoning): the flashback's
   placement *after* the stranger arrives but *before* Sef decides makes
   Tam's death the lens for the choice; told chronologically, the reader
   would meet the law as history, not as pressure. The structural feature
   doing the work is *order as timing of understanding*.
3. Sample: "The light is not a bargain, Mara." — no nine-year-old
   narrates her own story with that aphoristic compression; the sentence
   is the sixty-year-old's *interpretation* laid over the memory. The POV
   adds retrospective meaning: the adult names what the child lived.

**Independent practice**

1. Sample: The flashback converts the stranger from a *problem* (who is
   he?) into a *test* (will Sef repeat the captain's refusal?). Because
   we learn "A man at your fire is owed water" only after seeing the man,
   the law arrives as judgment on the scene we've just read — the reader
   re-reads the arrival with Tam's death in mind. Quote required.
2. Sample: Gained — immediacy and uncertainty (would the reader know the
   light survives? would the coat's loss feel more shocking?). Lost —
   the frame's authority: "I am sixty now" tells us the custom *lasted*,
   which is the story's real claim; without it, the ending is just a
   lamp, not a lifetime.
3. Credit for three paired observations with one quotation per story per
   pair.

**Exit check:** Two "*choice* → *effect* (evidence)" lines, e.g.,
"In-medias-res opening → mystery/tension ('between one breath and the
   next')" / "Chronological frame → inevitability/intimacy ('I am sixty
now…')".

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. Sample additions: (a) The Tortoise's dialogue shrinks from a speaking
   challenger to two quoted lines — changed; reaches layer 3 because the
   story is no longer *about* the Tortoise at all, but about the Hare's
   reputation. (b) The race changes from *event* to *memory* — changed;
   reaches layer 3 because a race can be won, but a memory must be
   *managed*, which is the letter's whole subject.
2. Either side earns credit with a cited line. *Accept-and-reframe:*
   "Joren never denies he slept — 'I have had years of practice at
   laughing at the right moment' accepts the facts and changes the
   frame." *Contradict:* "'she has declined every one' rewrites the
   moral's ending — the story now ends with the Hare showing up, which
   contradicts 'slow but steady wins' as the last word."
3. Keeping the outcome is what makes the transformation *legible*: if
   Joren claimed victory, the letter would be a different story, not a
   transformation. The kept facts are the fixed point against which the
   reframing shows.

**Independent practice**

1. Credit for a complete three-layer inventory of the learner's chosen
   transformation with specifics in each layer.
2. Sample paragraph: "Removing the Fox removes the *judge* — and with
   him, the idea that the race had an official meaning. In the fable the
   Fox 'marked the distance,' giving the contest a sanctioned frame; in
   the letter there is no judge, only an audience (Sela, the warren),
   which shifts judgment from the race's officials to its *tellers*.
   The transformation: meaning is no longer declared by authority but
   negotiated by retelling."
3. Sample: "RL.9-10.9 is about what a new work *does* with source
   material, not just what it borrows. 'The Rematch' borrows the plot
   but transforms the claim — from 'steadiness beats pride' to 'the
   telling is still being run.' If only the plot moved, it would be an
   adaptation; because the *theme* moved, it is a transformation."

**Exit check:** Kept (e.g., "the Hare, the Tortoise, the nap, the
outcome") / changed (e.g., "letter form; Hare as narrator") /
transformed (e.g., "from how to win to who owns the story").

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice**

1. Sample: the keeper's *tattered cloak* / the *bare branches* / the
   shrine's rough stonework — the passage never describes her clothing
   or the branches. The emphasis adds visible *labor and exposure*: we
   see the cost of the nightly ritual on her body, which the text only
   implies through "knotted as roots" hands.
2. Sample: the taper's *hiss* or the oil's *smell* — to draw it you
   would need motion lines or a label, and the sensory immediacy would
   be lost; an image shows the flame but cannot make you *hear* it
   catch. What is lost: the passage's intimacy — sound and smell put the
   reader *beside* the keeper.
3. Unfair — it breaks the *shared question* rule: "lonelier" is not
   measured the same way in both (the passage's loneliness is temporal,
   the image's spatial). A fair version: "On the shared question of how
   each medium conveys solitude, the passage uses duration and the image
   uses composition."

**Independent practice**

1. Sample paragraph: "The passage *narrates* the lighting — 'The taper
   hisses when it catches… The first lantern takes the flame the way a
   sleeper takes waking' — so lighting is a *process in time*, each
   lantern an event. The image *shows* three lit lanterns and one dark
   simultaneously, so lighting is a *state*: three suns already burning,
   one absence already visible. The passage makes you wait with the
   keeper; the image makes you see what waiting looks like."
2. Credit for: chosen pair, shared question, three-part thesis draft,
   four evidence pairs (quotation + quotation).
3. Credit for a deliberate choice justified from the evidence (e.g.,
   "point-by-point, because the light-description vs. light-depiction
   pairs are sharpest side by side").

**Exit check:** Text-only: e.g., "the hiss of the taper / the smell of
oil / the memory of busy roads." Image-only: e.g., "amber-vs.-indigo
color as simultaneous meaning / the keeper's bent posture / both roads
empty in one glance."

## Practice sessions

**A.** Credit for two outlines on the same shared question with
comparable evidence, plus the planted violation found, named, and
repaired.

**B.** Sample revisions:
1. → "This essay argues that while 'The Salt Road' enforces hospitality
   as law, 'The Harbor Light' practices it as identity — and that the
   difference shows 'hospitality' naming opposite obligations."
2. → "Although 'The Salt Road''s flashback withholds the reason for the
   road-law until the moment of decision while 'The Harbor Light''s
   chronology never withholds anything, both orders serve their themes:
   one builds tension around a choice, the other weight around a loss."
3. → "Both stories value hospitality" is a topic; arguable version: "…
   but 'The Salt Road' values it as *justice* (the debt is collected)
   while 'The Harbor Light' values it as *grace* (the debt is refused)
   — so the shared value produces opposite endings."
4. → Breaks the shared-question rule ("better for action fans" smuggles
   preference); fair version compares *effects*: "…the first builds
   tension through disruption, the second through patience — opposite
   structures for opposite claims about obligation."
Skeptic test applied to the strongest.

**C.** Credit for two three-row trackers on *memory* with v1→v3
revision. Sample v3s — Salt Road: "the story claims memory is a *debt
record* — the past is kept so it can be collected (the salt cake settles
Tam)." Harbor Light: "the story claims memory is *custody* — the past
is kept so it can be *continued* (the lamp, lit nightly)." Comparative
claim: "One tradition uses memory to settle accounts; the other to keep
them open — memory as ledger vs. memory as lamp." The sentence on what
this reveals beyond the hospitality comparison: e.g., "It shows the
difference isn't only about strangers — it's about what the past is
*for*."

**D.** Credit for the six-cell chart, two choice→effect paragraphs with
quotations, and the error analysis: the claim breaks the *fitness* rule
— "The Harbor Light" needs inevitability and intimacy, not tension; a
flashback would manufacture mystery the story deliberately refuses. Its
power is patience; disruption would unmake it.

**E.** Credit for the 150-word transformed opening, a complete
three-layer inventory, the theme-shift paragraph, and the adult's
restatement of the layer-3 shift (revision if the adult can't find one).

**F.** Process session: credit for handled quotations, a conclusion that
answers the "so what," a counter-thesis sentence, and every quiz miss
re-solved with its rule named in the learner's own words.

## Week-10 quiz

1. Breaks the *shared question* rule ("better" at what? "harder" by
   whose measure?). Fair question: "How does each passage portray the
   cost of helping an enemy?" (2)
2. Sample: "What does each passage claim we owe an enemy, and what does
   the giver receive in return?" (1)
3. Sample completion: "…the difference reveals two economies of mercy:
   Marta's keeps accounts (the arrow, kept and hung — the debt recorded
   but uncollected), while L.'s refuses them (the mended shutter, given
   without witness — the debt erased). One mercy remembers; the other
   forgets." Any arguable "so what" earns credit. (2)
4. v2 sample: "The story claims mercy can be given *without breaking
   the law* — and that the enemy's answer (the unbroken arrow, laid
   down) is a message Marta keeps but cannot read: mercy creates meaning
   even when it changes nothing." (2)
5. Sample: chronological order → *inevitability*: we watch the night
   unfold in real time, so the arrow lands as quiet fact. Letter form →
   *intimacy and risk*: L. confesses as she writes ("which troubles me
   more than the scolding did"), so the reader becomes the brother —
   the confidant. Compared: one structure observes mercy from outside;
   the other confesses it from inside. (2)
6. Sample: the letter lets L. *address* someone — "You will scold me" —
   making the reader a participant in her moral reasoning; the Gate's
   narration can only report Marta's uncertainty ("She was not sure
   herself") from outside it. (1)
7. (a) kept — the bread on a rope is a source fact; (b) changed — the
   form/POV is altered; (c) transformed — the theme moves from the
   giver's act to the receiver's debt. (3)
8. Sample: the keeper's tattered cloak / the bare branches arching
   overhead / the shrine's stonework — none described in the passage;
   they add visible wear, age, and exposure. (1)
9. Sample: "Marta's mercy is *institutional* — given within the law's
   shadow, recorded in the kept arrow — while L.'s mercy is *secret*,
   given against her mother's law and confessed only to her brother."
   (2)
10. It states a topic ("kindness is good") that no reader could disagree
    with — there is no arguable claim and no "so what." Adding one
    arguable clause fixes it, e.g., "…*but* 'The Gate' suggests mercy
    needs no gratitude to be complete, while 'The Door' suggests mercy
    needs a witness — even a secret one." (2)

## Unit assessment

**Part A**

1. Sample question: "What does each passage claim a promise to a dying
   person *requires* of the living?" Comparable evidence: the oath's
   wording and the diary's dated entries — both are *records of the
   promise kept over time* (same kind: textual evidence of fulfillment).
   (3)
2. v2 sample: "The story claims a promise can be *perfectly kept and
   completely unnoticed* — Joss runs through fire to deliver a message
   command files 'with a hundred others.' The oath mattered absolutely
   to the swearer and not at all to the institution." (3)
3. Sample: in-medias-res → *urgency without context*: we meet the dying
   before we know the war, so the oath feels larger than its cause.
   Dated entries → *duration as devotion*: three months of dated labor
   make the promise visible in time. Comparative claim: "One structure
   makes a promise *igniting* — sworn in crisis; the other makes it
   *enduring* — kept in routine. The contrast reveals promises have two
   lives: the swearing and the keeping." (3)
4. Sample: the diary shows the promise *decaying and renewing* across
   entries — "My back aches… It is a ridiculous garden. I am planting it
   anyway" — private complaint inside public fidelity, which third-person
   narration of Joss (locked to action) cannot render. It reveals that
   keeping can be reluctant and still count. (3)
5. Sample completion: "…the contrast reveals that a promise is two
   different things: *a moment* (the oath, sworn once, in the wrong
   order, under fire) and *a practice* (the garden, weeded across three
   months). One story honors the swearing; the other honors the
   keeping." (3)
6. Breaks the *shared question / comparable evidence* rule: "emotional"
   is not measured the same way across a diary (which performs feeling
   in dated entries) and an action narrative (which withholds feeling
   for momentum). It also smuggles ranking ("better"). A fair version
   compares what each form *does with* feeling, not how much it has.
   (3)

**Part B — essay:** score against the rubric in

Notes: "handled" = introduced, cited, explained — a dropped quotation
caps Evidence at 1. The counter-thesis sentence must be *answered*, not
