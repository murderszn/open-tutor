# Answer Key — Unit 01: World Literature: Cultural Context and Interpretation

*Adult-only. Solutions for every practice item, quiz question, and
assessment task, solved independently with reasoning. Open responses
note acceptable alternatives and common misconceptions. Do not show
this key to the learner. — Reconciled 2026-10-14.*

---

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. Historical moment — e.g., "two coppers" (coin economy);
   "the ledger at the ferry house" (paper record-keeping, pre-digital).
   Social norms — e.g., "the fare is the fare, and a ferryman who gouges
   a passenger in a storm is the sort the other boatmen talk about"
   (fixed fares; reputation policing); "merchants give a ferryman's
   regulars a small courtesy" (reciprocity custom).
2. Sorts:
   - a. **Textual evidence** — "the toll schedule written in the
     ledger," "the fare is the fare," and "merchants give a ferryman's
     regulars a small courtesy" directly state the norm. Acceptable
     alternative: borderline if the learner notes "values" is an
     interpretive word — accept with defense.
   - b. **Reader's cultural assumption** — the text never compares
     river-town and city honesty. This is the stereotype-projection
     error the lesson warns against.
   - c. **Reader's cultural assumption** — the text shows she writes;
     it never says *why* her culture values it. "Because her culture
     values record-keeping" is the reader's explanation, not the
     text's.
3. (6d) is tricky because the text *does* show the practice (merchants
   give courtesies; the bread stall gives an extra roll), so it feels
   stated — but "expect prices to be honored" generalizes from
   instances to a *system-wide expectation*, which is the reader's
   inference. Good answers name the instance-vs.-system gap.

**Independent practice**

1. Accept any belief-system detail with quotation, e.g., "the river
   keeps its own accounts" (river as moral accountant); "the circle
   holds" (reciprocity as cosmic order).
2. Model paragraph (alternatives vary): *The traveler treats Hal's
   refusal as personal generosity — "People here seem to pay in stories
   instead of coin" — but the text shows the refusal was professional:
   "the fare is the fare." Her frame (kindness = giving things up)
   overwrites his frame (the fare is the fare), so she leaves believing
   she received a gift when she received correct service.*
3. Accept any clearly argued case, e.g., "Hal is a simple man" — the
   text never calls him simple; "simple" is the reader importing a folk
   stereotype. The difference: evidence points at words; assumption
   points at the reader's head.

**Exit check:** accept any correctly labeled pair with a real quotation.

**Misconception watch:** learners sorting (2b) as evidence — press:
"point at the words that compare river-town and city honesty."

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice**

1. Model — traveler to a friend: *"You won't believe this — the
   ferryman wouldn't take my money! It rained and everything, and he
   just pushed my hand back and told me to tell the market he sent me.
   People here are incredible."* Hal to a boatman: *"Had a girl today,
   city boots, offered three coppers for the rain before I'd finished
   the joke. Sent her on with the word for the stalls. Ledger says two;
   I took none."* Accept alternatives that keep each voice's frame
   (wonder vs. routine; "incredible" vs. ledger language).
2. Traveler: "wouldn't take my money" / "incredible" — only makes sense
   if refusing payment is remarkable (her frame). Hal: "before I'd
   finished the joke" / "Ledger says two" — only makes sense inside the
   fixed-fare professional frame.
3. Accept: the two-half structure lets the reader *share* the
   traveler's misreading first, then experience the correction as
   discovery — alternating would give away the correction too early and
   kill the dramatic irony. Alternatives about immersion or sympathy
   accepted if argued.

**Independent practice**

1. Accept any faithful rewrite; check that events are unchanged and
   diction shifts (e.g., "gentle" → "proper," "kindness" → "the fare").
2. Narrator knowledge beyond both characters: (a) the toll joke's
   history ("a joke his father had told"); (b) that the girl "was the
   first who seemed to think it was a gift" (the narrator reads her
   mind *and* Hal's history); (c) the ledger margin entry's seasonal
   count ("the seventh no-fare passage"). Neither character could know
   all three.
3. Model: *Yes — she is unreliable the way an outsider is unreliable,
   not the way a liar is. "People here seem to pay in stories instead
   of coin" states as observation what is actually her frame's
   invention; the second half proves no such payment system exists.
   "Seem" is the tell: she reports her impression as ethnography.*
   Accept "no, she's honest about uncertainty" if argued from "seem" —
   a defensible alternative; note it.

**Exit check:** accept any pair revealing frame, e.g., traveler: "the
kindest man on the river"; Hal: "a girl who didn't hear the joke's end."

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. Townsend foregrounds the ants ("The Ants and the Grasshopper") —
   the industrious collective first; sympathy tilts toward order. Jones
   foregrounds the grasshopper — the individual first; the reader meets
   the underdog before the judges. Title order is a framing choice.
2. Plot: unchanged — the exchange and outcome are identical. Sympathy:
   may shift; accept honest answers *if* the learner identifies the
   source. Model: *"I felt slightly sorrier for Jones's 'her' — and
   caught myself: the text gives no reason a female grasshopper
   deserves more pity. The reaction came from my assumptions about
   vulnerability, not from the fable."* Misconception watch: learners
   who insist the gender "obviously" changes the meaning without
   examining why.
3. Model: *Jones adds the cause to make the ants' winter work
   intelligible to a reader who has never dried grain — a readability
   choice ("How Translators Work": fidelity vs. readability trade-off).
   Townsend trusts the image alone.* Accept: scene-setting for a less
   agrarian 1912 audience.

**Independent practice**

1. Accept any two well-argued pairs. Key points: (a) "earnestly
   begged" (formal, dignified) vs. "I'm simply starving" (direct
   speech, visceral — Jones lets the grasshopper speak, Townsend
   narrates); (b) "treasure up" (biblical/archaic register) vs.
   "collect a store" (plain); (c) "perishing with famine" (elevated,
   distant) vs. "I'm simply starving" (immediate, embodied).
2. Accept any invented note with translator name, 2026 date, and a
   defended choice.
3. Accept any verdict with a quoted word as cause.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**

1. Model tracker rows: Beginning — "her prices were written on a
   chalkboard" → supports (records have public power). Middle — Milo's
   ledger entry "Pepper, damp shipment, sold at full price. Customer
   not told" → refines: records have power *over the recorder* —
   writing it down forces the reckoning. End — "Do not sell the damp
   shipment at full price again" → the record now *commands future
   action*. Refined theme: *"Written records don't just describe what
   people do — they compel what people do next."*
2. The heaped saffron pinch: complicates — Ren *breaks* the board
   price (gives extra) out of kindness, and the story approves. So
   records/prices are not absolute: the theme must allow deliberate,
   kind deviation. Refined further: *"...except when a person knowingly
   and openly departs from them for kindness's sake."* Accept:
   supports (it shows the board is a floor, not a cage) — either
   verdict accepted if argued from the text.
3. Accept any honest comparison grounded in differing details.

**Independent practice**

1–2. Accept any second candidate fully tracked with a real revision.
   Check: final theme is a sentence with a verb; version numbers
   change.
3. Error-analysis diagnosis model: *"Pepper" is a topic, not a theme —
   it names a thing in the story, not a claim about it. It has no verb
   and argues nothing; no detail could support or contradict it because
   it says nothing. Fix: turn it into a sentence that claims
   something, e.g., "The story suggests that pepper — ordinary goods
   honestly priced — becomes a test of character."*

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. Model beginning summary: *"Spice seller Ren runs a corner stall at
   a night market with her fourteen-year-old apprentice Milo, who
   weighs, wraps, and keeps the ledger. Prices are posted on a
   chalkboard in neat handwriting. Milo admires the match between the
   board's neatness and Ren's habit of heaping a saffron pinch for a
   shaking-handed customer."* Judgment-word hunt: "admires" is
   borderline (inference about Milo's feeling) — accept if flagged;
   "neat" is the story's own word ("so neat it looked printed").
2. Objective: *"Milo disagrees with Ren about selling the damp pepper
   at full price and records the sale in the ledger."* Shorter: *"Milo
   records the damp-pepper sale, disputing it."* Both keep facts, drop
   "hero/bravely/greedy/disgusting."
3. Accept either verdict argued: YES — it establishes the board/book
   contrast the theme needs; NO — the summary survives without it (the
   ledger scene carries the contrast alone).

**Independent practice** — score against the checklist; the adult
verifies no invented facts (quantities: twenty/ten/five; Milo is
fourteen).

**Adult scoring (objectivity / completeness), 4 points each** (see
Lesson 05): objectivity 4 = zero opinion/judgment words, no invented
facts; completeness 4 = beginning, middle, and resolution all present
with load-bearing facts kept.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice:** check claim arguability ("a smart reader could
disagree"); check the four quotations are real with five-word proofs;
check the complication is genuine (e.g., Hal's "the fare is the fare"
as a counter to the generosity reading).

**Independent practice:** verify each highlighted quotation is
introduced/cited/explained/connected; verify the skeptic's objection is
real and answered.

**Exit check:** claim + three real quotations.

**Misconception watch:** the summary-essay (no claim) and the dropped
quotation — both must be caught at the exit-check stage, not after
drafting.

## Practice A

1. Historical moment: chalkboard prices, coin economy, "ledger" book,
   oil lamps ("lit the lamps"). 2. Social norms: posted board prices
   honored; apprentices keep ledgers; caravan buyers accept board
   price. 3. Belief systems: "the price is what the board says. The
   truth is what the book says" (Milo's line) — writing makes things
   accountable. 4. Genre: realist folk episode / market tale; promises
   an ethical test and a reckoning — keeps it. 5. Accept *belief
   systems* (the ledger-truth assumption drives Milo's rebellion) or
   *social norms* (the board-price custom creates the conflict) —
   either with reasoning. 6. Sorts: a. **Assumption** — text says lamps
   are lit; "no electric lights" is the reader inferring a pre-electric
   era (defensible inference, but still the reader's, not stated). b.
   **Evidence** — "Milo, was fourteen" stated. c. **Assumption** — text
   says "since spring"; "many years" is invented. d. **Tricky — leans
   evidence**: the merchant says "I'll take it at your board price"
   and Ren honors posted prices throughout; but "expect... honored" as
   a *system-wide merchant expectation* generalizes — accept either
   pile with a defense naming the instance/system gap. 7. Model: *It
   feels like evidence because we watch it happen twice, but two
   instances are still instances — the text never states the rule. The
   line between is whether the text asserts it or I assembled it.*

## Practice B

1–3. Accept in-voice retellings; frame phrases: Ren — e.g., "the boy
means well but the board price is the board price" (frame: the stall's
survival depends on margin); merchant — e.g., "bought at a fair posted
price, good pepper" (frame: a transaction completed correctly; he
never learns otherwise). 4. Ren's telling omits: the musty smell (she
never acknowledges it aloud) and Milo's ledger line (she reads it but
wouldn't volunteer it) — omissions reveal her frame: the sale was
*procedurally correct*, so the rest is noise. 5. Model: *Milo is
reliable about facts — his ledger line ("Pepper, damp shipment, sold
at full price. Customer not told") matches what the narration showed —
but his frame shows in "Lesson: the price is what the board says. The
truth is what the book says," which is his moral, not everyone's.
Reliable witness, interested moralist.* 6. Check underlining and
correct usage. 7. Error-analysis model: *The error is treating
"biased" as "wrong" and "unbiased" as a real option. Both tellings are
framed — Ren's by the stall's economics, Milo's by his moral
education; neither is a view from nowhere. Replace "biased/unbiased"
with "whose frame": ask what each teller's position lets them see and
hides.*

## Practice C

1. Townsend: "If you were foolish enough to sing all the summer, you
   must dance supperless to bed in the winter." Jones: "If you spent
   the summer singing, you can't do better than spend the winter
   dancing." 2. Diction: "foolish enough" (direct insult, moral
   condemnation) vs. no adjective (irony does the work); "supperless
   to bed" (concrete punishment, archaic cadence) vs. "spend the
   winter dancing" (light surface over a death sentence). 3. Townsend
   colder — "derision," "foolish," "supperless" are contempt +
   punishment vocabulary; Jones warmer — "chuckled," no insults, the
   sting hidden under lightness. 4. See Lesson 03 key. 5. Accept any
   correctly styled note. 6. Check underlining. 7. New difference:
   Townsend "spending a fine winter's day drying grain" vs. Jones
   "busy drying their store of corn, which had got rather damp during
   a long spell of rain" — Jones explains and modernizes ("store of
   corn," causal clause); Townsend is spare and formal. Effect: Jones
   domesticates; Townsend monumentalizes.

## Practice D

Model tracker: Traveler's half — "he closed her fingers over them
gently, pushing her hand back" → supports v1 (looks like generosity).
"People here seem to pay in stories instead of coin" → supports v1
(she reads it as generosity). Hal's half — "the fare is the fare, and
a ferryman who gouges a passenger in a storm is the sort the other
boatmen talk about" → **breaks** v1: the refusal was duty, not
generosity. "Tell them the ferryman sent you... the circle holds" →
refines: what looked like a gift was *system maintenance*. v2: *"What
looks like generosity is often someone doing their job inside a
system the outsider can't see."* Competing candidate test: the
traveler romanticizes ("a kindness" in her journal) vs. Hal's
"ordinary Tuesday" — well supported; verdict on "better developed"
accepted either way if argued (generosity/job candidate has the
ledger-entry clincher; romanticize/ordinary candidate has the
two-endings structure).

## Practice E

1. Model: *"Hal ferries a young traveler across a low river for the
   posted fare of two coppers. Mid-crossing, rain forces them to
   shelter; Hal lends her his cloak and later refuses her offer of a
   third copper, telling her instead to name him at the market stalls.
   The traveler reads the refusal as extraordinary generosity and
   writes it in her journal as the local way. Hal's half reveals the
   day as routine: the fare is fixed, the 'three coppers' was his
   father's joke, and naming him at stalls is standard practice — he
   logs it as 'one passage, no fare' and loads firewood."* Checklist:
   arc ✓; objectivity ✓ ("extraordinary" is borderline — it's *her*
   reading, attributed; accept if attributed, flag if stated as fact);
   facts ✓; followable ✓. 2–6. Per instructions; slanted-sentence
   fixes: *"The traveler misreads Hal's routine as generosity because
   she doesn't know the ferry customs"* / shorter: *"The traveler
   mistakes routine for generosity."*

## Week-06 quiz

1. **b** — the toll schedule in the ledger is the direct statement.
   (a) is setting; (c) shows kindness, not fare policy; (d) is
   atmosphere.
2. **c** — clues ≠ proof; "wealthy" is the reader's assembly. (d) is
   wrong: fictional texts still support evidence-based claims.
3. **b** — the line is her frame's invention; the second half
   disproves stories-as-payment. (a) misreads literally; (c)
   contradicts Hal's half.
4. **b** — it explicitly marks the boundary of his perception: he
   cannot see how his world looks from outside. The others show his
   competence, not his limit.
5. **b** — full sentence, verb ("means," "costs"), arguable. (a) is a
   topic; (c) is plot; (d) is description.
6. **b** — Ren uses the *form* of honesty to defend a dishonest sale;
   it breaks "honesty is always rewarded" by showing honesty can be
   performed. (c) is the resolution, not the complication.
7. **a** — names the words and the tone effect. (c) repeats the
   older=truer fallacy; (d) is false — the moral temperature *is* part
   of the meaning.
8. **b** — verifiable in the texts ("him"/"He replied" vs. "her"/"she
   said"). (a) is the older=truer fallacy; (c)/(d) are unstated.
9. Model (accept alternatives with one phrase per half): *The traveler
   reads the pushed-back hand through a frame where kindness and
   commerce are separate, so refusal of money becomes "a kindness"
   ("People here seem to pay in stories instead of coin"). Hal reads
   the same hand through the fixed-fare frame, so the refusal is
   unremarkable duty ("the fare is the fare"). Same gesture; the
   meaning comes from the frame, not the hand.*
10. Accept any theme-as-sentence + genuine refinement. Model: *Theme:
    "The story suggests that honesty means making the public price
    match the private record, whatever it costs." Refinement detail:
    Ren's "Damp pepper is still pepper, and the board price is the
    board price" — it first looked like a defense of the sale, then
    became the sentence the ending answers: the story redefines "board
    price" from excuse to obligation.*

## Unit assessment

**A1.** Strongest: "not because the work needed singing, but because
their grandmothers had sung, and the grandmothers before them" — it
explicitly chains generations, proving repetition. Weaker candidates:
"Every village in the valley sent its daughters... on the first
morning of the planting season" (states scheduling, but a scheduled
event could be new); "the first morning" alone proves nothing about
repetition.

**A2.** Reader assumption. Evidence: "Maren laughed — not unkindly"
directly contradicts dislike; "an unheard-of visit" + "she smiled"
show regard. The reader imports "resents the notebook" from Maren's
skepticism about writing, which is a *position on method*, not a
feeling about Sarella. Accept "partially textual" defenses that
distinguish Maren's criticism of the notebook from her warmth toward
Sarella.

**A3.** Model: *Sarella's schooling trained her to convert experience
into record — "she had been taught to write down what she saw" — so
she notices times, verses, and disputes ("6:10 — jars filled"). What
she misses is the unrecordable: "all of us being there before the sun
got hot" — the communal, bodily knowledge her notebook has no column
for.*

**A4.** Model: *Maren means Sarella captured the facts (times,
verses) but missed the meaning the facts served — the being-together
that the song exists to make. It reveals Maren's point of view: what
matters is participation ("the song isn't for remembering. It's for
doing"), not preservation; the event's value is in the doing, which a
record can describe but not hold.*

**A5.** Accept any sentence-with-verb theme, e.g.: "The story suggests
that some knowledge lives in doing together and cannot be written
down." / "The story suggests that recording an event and understanding
it are different acts." Reject topic words ("tradition," "notebooks").

**A6.** Model refinement: *Lina's "Maybe the notebook is your song"
complicates the doing-vs.-writing split — it suggests Sarella isn't
outside the tradition but inside it differently, her own form of
participation. The theme refines from "writing misses the point" to
"each participant has their own song, and the tradition is big enough
to hold Sarella's too" — which Maren's final visit confirms.*

**Essay (Part B):** score with the 12-point rubric in
[assessments/unit-assessment.md](assessments/unit-assessment.md).
Notes: strong claims name a mechanism (e.g., "Sarella's school frame
converts communal doing into data, and the story's arc is her
learning the conversion loses something — then finding her own form
of doing"). Expect quotations like "the song doesn't go in the book
the way water doesn't go in a sieve," "You got the morning. You didn't
get the singing," "Maybe the notebook is your song." The genuine
complication is usually Lina's line or Maren's closing generosity
("the book held *you* being there") — both soften a simplistic
"writing bad / doing good" claim. Watch for essays that judge Sarella
("she was disrespectful") instead of interpreting the text — redirect
per the Lesson 06 teacher note.
