# The Night Market

*Original — written for this unit. Fictional setting; no real-world culture is depicted.*

The market woke at dusk. Vendors rolled up the awnings, lit the lamps, and the whole long lane turned the color of honey. Ren, the spice seller, had the corner stall by the well, and her prices were written on a chalkboard in a hand so neat it looked printed: *cumin, one spoon; saffron, a pinch; cardamom, by the handful.*

Her apprentice, Milo, was fourteen and had been with her since spring. His job was to weigh, to wrap, and to keep the ledger. The ledger was a thick book bound in blue cloth, and Ren checked it every night with a pencil behind her ear.

On the third Friday of the month, a merchant from upriver came to Ren's stall. He was buying for a whole caravan: twenty bags of pepper, ten of cumin, five of saffron. A fortune, by lane standards. He looked at the chalkboard, nodded, and said, "I'll take it all at your board price."

Milo had seen caravan buyers before. They always haggled — it was half the sport of the lane. This one didn't haggle at all, which told Milo the board prices were fairer than Ren let on when she complained about the stall rent. He filed that away, the way he filed everything, in the ledger of his head.

Milo weighed and wrapped while Ren counted the coin. Halfway through, Milo leaned over and whispered, "The pepper is off. The morning shipment — it got damp in the storeroom. It smells musty."

Ren's hand did not pause in the counting. "It's still pepper," she said quietly. "He didn't ask about the smell."

"But he'll serve it to his whole caravan. He'll taste it."

"He'll taste pepper," Ren said. "Damp pepper is still pepper, and the board price is the board price."

Milo kept wrapping. His hands were steady, but his face was hot. He thought about the ledger, and the pencil, and the neatness of Ren's hand on the board. He thought about the saffron pinch he'd given the old woman on Tuesday — Ren had told him to heap the pinch, "because her hands shake and it cheers her." He thought about that heaped pinch and then about the damp pepper, and the two things would not sit together in his head.

When the merchant had gone, Milo opened the ledger to a fresh page. Ren watched him.

"What are you writing?" she asked.

"I'm writing: *Pepper, damp shipment, sold at full price. Customer not told.*"

Ren put down her pencil. "Why?"

"So we remember," Milo said. "So if his caravan comes back complaining, we know what we did. And so —" He stopped. "So I can look at the board tomorrow and not feel like the handwriting is lying."

For a long moment the market noise went on around them: the oil sizzling, the lantern strings creaking in the wind, a child laughing by the well.

Then Ren took the pencil from behind her ear, and in her own hand, under Milo's line, she wrote: *Apprentice right. Send a boy after the merchant with two sound bags and the difference in coin. Do not sell the damp shipment at full price again.*

She looked up at Milo. "There. Now the board and the book match."

Milo nodded, and his face cooled. He wrote one more line, smaller: *Lesson: the price is what the board says. The truth is what the book says.*

Ren read it, snorted, and said, "You'll do."

She sent him to fetch the sound pepper himself, so he'd remember where it was stored. He ran through the market with the lamps going gold all around him, and for the first time that evening, the honey-colored lane looked honest to him.

He was back before the supper hour, the two sound bags on his shoulders and the difference in coin counted twice in his fist. Ren weighed the bags herself, nodded once, and handed him the pencil.
