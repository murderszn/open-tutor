# The Ant and the Grasshopper — in Two Public-Domain English Translations

*Both translations below are U.S. public domain, first published well before 1929: George Fyler Townsend's* Three Hundred Æsop's Fables *(London: George Routledge and Sons, 1867) and V. S. Vernon Jones's* Æsop's Fables: A New Translation *(London: William Heinemann, 1912; first published 1912). Townsend's text follows the Project Gutenberg edition of the 1867 Routledge printing. Jones's text was reproduced from the 1912 Heinemann edition and cross-checked 2026-10-14 against the University of Pittsburgh folktexts archive (sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type0280a.html, which records "First published in 1912") and the Wikisource transcription of the 1912 Heinemann edition — both match word for word. Source editions and dates are cited under each text.*

## Translation 1 — George Fyler Townsend (1867)

**Source:** George Fyler Townsend, *Three Hundred Æsop's Fables* (London: George Routledge and Sons, 1867). Text verified against the Project Gutenberg edition as republished by the Internet History Sourcebooks Project, Fordham University (`sourcebooks.fordham.edu/ancient/aesop-fables.asp`), checked 2026-10-14.

### The Ants and the Grasshopper

THE ANTS were spending a fine winter's day drying grain collected in the summertime. A Grasshopper, perishing with famine, passed by and earnestly begged for a little food. The Ants inquired of him, "Why did you not treasure up food during the summer?" He replied, "I had not leisure enough. I passed the days in singing." They then said in derision: "If you were foolish enough to sing all the summer, you must dance supperless to bed in the winter."

## Translation 2 — V. S. Vernon Jones (1912)

**Source:** V. S. Vernon Jones, *Æsop's Fables: A New Translation*, with an introduction by G. K. Chesterton and illustrations by Arthur Rackham (London: William Heinemann, 1912; Heinemann/Doubleday edition, 1916, p. 125). Text verified via D. L. Ashliman's folktexts archive, University of Pittsburgh (`sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type0280a.html`), checked 2026-10-14.

### The Grasshopper and the Ants

One fine day in winter some ants were busy drying their store of corn, which had got rather damp during a long spell of rain. Presently up came a grasshopper and begged them to spare her a few grains, "For," she said, "I'm simply starving."

The ants stopped work for a moment, though this was against their principles. "May we ask," said they, "what you were doing with yourself all last summer? Why didn't you collect a store of food for the winter?"

"The fact is," replied the grasshopper, "I was so busy singing that I hadn't the time."

"If you spent the summer singing," replied the ants, "you can't do better than spend the winter dancing."

And they chuckled and went on with their work.

## Reading notes for Lesson 03

- **Title difference:** Townsend says "The Ants and the Grasshopper"; Jones says "The Grasshopper and the Ants." Which character is foregrounded, and does it change the story's sympathy?
- **The grasshopper's excuse:** Townsend: "I had not leisure enough. I passed the days in singing." Jones: "I was so busy singing that I hadn't the time." Compare the tone — formal/confessional vs. conversational.
- **The ants' reply:** Townsend ends in "derision" — mocking, moralizing, cold: "you must dance supperless to bed in the winter." Jones's ants "chuckled and went on with their work" — amused, almost gentle, and the moral sting lands softer.
- **The grasshopper's gender:** Jones's grasshopper is "her" (female); Townsend's is "him." Check whether this changes how you read the exchange. (It shouldn't change the plot — but notice if it changes your sympathy. That noticing is the lesson.)
- **Added detail:** Jones explains *why* the ants are drying corn ("rather damp during a long spell of rain"); Townsend gives no cause. A translator's small addition is still a choice.
