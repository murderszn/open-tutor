# The Ferryman's Toll

*Original — written for this unit. Fictional setting; no real-world culture is depicted.*

The river was low that season, and the current ran thin and gray over the stones. Hal the ferryman stood at the head of his flat-bottomed boat, coiling a rope that smelled of wet hemp, and watched the traveler come down the path with her pack high on her shoulders.

She was young, and her boots were the wrong kind — city boots, soft at the heel. She stopped at the water's edge and counted coins in her palm twice before she spoke.

"What's the toll?" she asked.

Hal nodded at the far bank, a smudge of green. "Two coppers. Three if the rain comes before we land."

"That's highway robbery," she said, but she was smiling. She placed two coppers in his hand and climbed in. The boat rocked and settled.

Halfway across, the clouds broke open the way clouds do here, without apology. Rain struck the water hard enough to sting. Hal steered the boat into the bend where the current eased, and they slid into a pocket of calm beneath an overhanging bank. He handed her his own oilcloth cloak without a word, and she took it, surprised.

"Three coppers, then," she said, laughing a little. "For the rain."

Hal shook his head. "Sit out the worst of it first. Pay me after, or don't. The river keeps its own accounts."

When the rain thinned, he rowed them the last stretch. At the far bank she stood, held out her coins — three now — and he closed her fingers over them gently, pushing her hand back.

"Keep them," he said. "Spend them in the market and tell them the ferryman sent you. That's worth more to me than copper."

"Why?" she asked.

"Because the man who runs the ferry owes the river a passenger now and then," Hal said. "Today it was you. Tomorrow, who knows."

She frowned, not understanding, and he laughed — a big, unbothered sound — and waved her on up the path. She looked back twice. The second time, he was already loading firewood for someone waiting on the far side, and the boat was gone into the mist like it had never been there.

At the market she did as she was told, though it embarrassed her. "The ferryman sent me," she said at the bread stall, and the vendor gave her an extra roll and asked after Hal's knees. At the lantern stall, she said it again, and got directions she hadn't asked for and a cup of something hot. The words were a key she hadn't known she was carrying.

That night, in the lodging house, she wrote in her journal: *Crossed the river. The ferryman refused payment. People here seem to pay in stories instead of coin.*

She meant it as praise. She would remember it for years as a kindness.

---

The same day, told differently, was an ordinary Tuesday for Hal.

The rain had come, as it comes. He had shared his cloak, as anyone would. He had refused the coins, because the toll schedule written in the ledger at the ferry house said *two coppers*, and Hal had never once taken three for a crossing, rain or shine — the three-copper talk was a joke his father had told, and he told it to every traveler, and they laughed, and then they paid two.

But the girl had not waited to hear the joke's end. She had offered three, laughing, before he'd explained. And when she'd insisted on paying for the rain, he'd refused, not from generosity but from principle: the fare is the fare, and a ferryman who gouges a passenger in a storm is the sort the other boatmen talk about at the dock.

"Tell them the ferryman sent you" was not generosity either. It was the practice of the river towns: merchants give a ferryman's regulars a small courtesy, and the ferryman brings the merchants travelers, and the circle holds. He had said it to hundreds of people. She was the first who seemed to think it was a gift from him personally.

He did not write anything in a journal. He had firewood to load.

That evening he entered her two coppers — no, he crossed it out. She had not paid. He entered instead, in the ledger's margin where he kept such things: *one passage, no fare. Courtesy of the river.* It was the seventh no-fare passage that season. The ledger had seen hundreds. He blew the ink dry and went inside to his supper, and the river went on keeping its own accounts.
