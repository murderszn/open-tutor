# Lesson 06 — The Interpretive Essay: Claim, Theme, and Thorough Evidence

**Goal (student-readable):** I can plan and draft a 3–4 paragraph interpretive essay that makes a claim about a cultural point of view or experience in a story, shows how a theme develops, and supports everything with strong, thorough textual evidence. (W.9-10.9)
**Time:** ~50 minutes (planning + drafting; revision in Practice F) · **Materials:** this page, notebook, both stories, both Aesop translations, Lessons 01–05 notes · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L6](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Look back at your Lesson 04 theme tracker and your Lesson 02 point-of-view notes. Write one sentence answering: *what is the most interesting thing you now believe about one of these stories that you did not believe before reading closely?* That sentence is your essay's seed.

## What an interpretive essay does

An interpretive essay is not a summary (Lesson 05) and not a review ("I liked it"). It is an *argument about meaning*: you claim something the story *does* — how a point of view shapes it, how a theme develops through it — and you prove it with the text's own words. The reader should finish your essay thinking, "I see the story differently now," even if they disagree with you.

**The essay frame (3–4 paragraphs):**

1. **Paragraph 1 — Claim + map.** Name the story, state your interpretive claim in one sentence, and map the essay: what you will show in paragraphs 2 and 3 (and 4, if you write one).
2. **Paragraph 2 — Evidence set one.** Your first body of proof: 1–2 quotations, each introduced and explained. Show *how* the detail supports the claim — don't drop quotations and run.
3. **Paragraph 3 — Evidence set two (the complication).** Your second body of proof — and the stronger essay puts the *hardest* detail here: the one that could argue against the claim, and your answer to it. (This is Lesson 04's revision habit, inside the essay.)
4. **Paragraph 4 (optional but recommended) — So what.** Why does this interpretation matter? What does it reveal about the story's cultural point of view that a surface reading misses?

**Evidence standard:** "strong and thorough" means: quotations are *introduced* (not dropped), *cited* (story title + paragraph or line), *explained* (what the words show), and *connected* (how they prove the claim). Two well-handled quotations beat five dropped ones.

## Worked example 1 — A claim, built from the unit's work

Weak claim (topic, not argument): *"The Ferryman's Toll" is about cultural misunderstanding.*

Strong claim (argues something): *"In 'The Ferryman's Toll,' the traveler's misunderstanding is not a failure of attention but a product of her cultural frame: she reads Hal's professional routine as personal generosity because her world separates kindness from commerce, while his world weaves them together.*

Why the strong version works: it *names the mechanism* (her frame vs. his frame), it *predicts* what the evidence must show (a moment she misreads + a detail proving his frame), and it can be *argued against* (someone could counter that she simply wasn't listening). A claim nobody could disagree with is a summary wearing a costume.

**Planning steps (do these before drafting):**

1. **Choose** your claim from your Lesson 02/04 notes. Write it as one sentence. Test: could a smart reader disagree? If no, sharpen it.
2. **Collect** 4–6 candidate quotations. For each, write what it proves in five words or fewer.
3. **Sort** them into paragraph 2 (direct support) and paragraph 3 (the complication + your answer). Cut the weakest two.
4. **Order** within each paragraph: strongest evidence last (it lands hardest), context-setting detail first.
5. **Draft** without stopping. **Revise** with the checklist below.

## Worked example 2 — A body paragraph, modeled

Claim (from above). Paragraph 2 draft:

> *The traveler's frame shows first in the coin scene. When Hal pushes her hand back, she does not hear a professional refusing an overcharge; she sees "gentleness" — the story says he closed her fingers over the coins "gently" ("The Ferryman's Toll," par. 8) — and converts it into generosity. Her journal entry proves the conversion is complete: "People here seem to pay in stories instead of coin" (par. 11). Nothing in the story supports stories-as-payment; the sentence is her frame writing over the facts. The quotation matters because it is the exact moment her cultural assumption replaces textual evidence — the error Lesson 01 trained us to catch.*

Why this paragraph works: the quotation is *introduced* ("the story says"), *cited*, *explained* ("converts it into generosity"), and *connected* to the claim and to the unit's vocabulary ("her frame writing over the facts"). Now the complication paragraph would take up Hal's half — "the fare is the fare" — and answer: *does his correction erase her feeling, or only her explanation of it?*

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Choose your story and draft your claim sentence. Read it aloud. The adult plays skeptic: *"Could a smart reader disagree?"* Sharpen until the answer is yes.
2. Collect four candidate quotations for your claim. For each, write the five-words-or-fewer "what it proves" note. Cut the weakest one together.
3. Sort the remaining three into paragraph 2 vs. paragraph 3 (complication). If none of them complicates your claim, find one that does — the essay needs it.

## Independent practice

1. Draft the full essay: 3–4 paragraphs, following the frame. (Rough is fine today; Practice F is revision.)
2. Highlight every quotation in your draft. For each highlighted quotation, check: introduced? cited? explained? connected? Fix any "dropped" quotation — one that sits in the paragraph without your own sentence explaining what it proves.
3. Write a two-sentence "skeptic's objection" to your own claim — the strongest counterargument — and one sentence answering it. If the answer is already in paragraph 3, note that; if not, plan where it goes.

## Application

Write a three-sentence interpretive claim about anything you read this week outside this unit (news, a novel, lyrics — lawful sources), plus the two quotations you would use to prove it. The habit transfers; the format is the same.

## Exit check

On an index card: your claim sentence + your three chosen quotations (with what each proves, five words or fewer). The adult checks that the claim is arguable and the quotations are real.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** planning may be done entirely orally — claim spoken, quotations dictated — before any drafting.
- **Alternate response mode:** the essay may be dictated and scribed; the planning steps are the assessed work.
- **Extension:** draft the optional paragraph 4 ("so what") as a standalone 150-word piece: what does your interpretation reveal about how *readers* — not just characters — bring cultural frames to stories? Connect to Lesson 01's evidence-vs.-assumption sort.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Three recurring failures: (1) **The summary essay** — all plot, no claim. Check paragraph 1: if the "claim" is "this story is about X," send the learner back to the planning steps. (2) **Dropped quotations** — the quotation sits alone as its own sentence, unexplained. Require the "what this shows" sentence every time; no exceptions while learning. (3) **The strawman complication** — paragraph 3 "answers" an objection nobody would raise. Teach the honest version: the best complication is a detail the learner *genuinely* found troubling for their claim (Lesson 04's tracker is full of them). Also: learners often write the essay about the *characters'* morality ("Ren was wrong") instead of the *story's* meaning — redirect from judging people to interpreting the text.
