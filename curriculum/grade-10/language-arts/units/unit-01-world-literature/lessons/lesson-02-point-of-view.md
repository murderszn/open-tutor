# Lesson 02 — Point of View and Cultural Experience: Whose Eyes Is This?

**Goal (student-readable):** I can analyze how a narrator's point of view and cultural experience shape a story — what gets noticed, what gets misunderstood, and what gets left out — by comparing how the ferryman and the traveler each experience the same river crossing. (RL.9-10.6)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, [texts/the-ferrymans-toll.md](../texts/the-ferrymans-toll.md) · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Two people watch the same dog knock over a trash can. One says "what a menace." The other says "he's starving." Write two sentences: *what is each person noticing, and what in their life might make them notice it?*

## Point of view is not just grammar

You know the terms: first person ("I"), third person ("he/she"). But point of view is bigger than pronouns. It is the question: **whose cultural experience is filtering this scene?** A narrator's background decides what counts as *worth noticing*. The traveler notices that Hal *refused money* — because in her world, kindness and money are separate, and refusing money is remarkable. Hal doesn't notice the refusal at all; in his world, the fare is the fare, and the remarkable thing would have been taking three coppers in a rainstorm.

Narrators can also be *unreliable* — not liars, necessarily, but limited. The traveler is unreliable the way every outsider is unreliable: she reads a normal day through her own world's rules and gets it half wrong. Many storytelling traditions build this in on purpose: the stranger who marvels, the insider who shrugs. The story's truth often lives in the *gap* between the two views.

## Worked example 1 — The same scene, two experiences

The coin scene at the far bank:

**Through the traveler's experience:** "She held out her coins — three now — and he closed her fingers over them gently, pushing her hand back." She reads gentleness + refusal of payment → *generosity*. Her journal: "People here seem to pay in stories instead of coin." She is genuinely moved. She is also wrong about what happened: nothing about stories-as-payment occurred. Her cultural frame (kindness = giving things up) turned a *routine professional courtesy* into a *personal gift*.

**Through Hal's experience:** He reads offered-three-coppers → *a passenger who didn't hear the joke's end and now wants to overpay for rain*. Refusing is not generosity; it is *doing his job correctly*. His frame (the fare is the fare; reputation among boatmen matters) turns the same moment into an ordinary Tuesday. The one thing that *does* surprise him — "She was the first who seemed to think it was a gift from him personally" — reveals his frame as clearly as her journal reveals hers.

Reasoning step to name aloud: *neither view is "the facts." The facts are the boat, the coins, the hand. The views are what each character's world makes of the facts.*

## Worked example 2 — What the narrator leaves out

Look at what each half of the story *doesn't* contain:

- The traveler's half never mentions the ledger, the toll schedule, or the boatmen's reputation. She has no access to the professional world Hal lives in — and the narration doesn't invent access she doesn't have. That restraint is a craft choice: we experience her misunderstanding *as* misunderstanding.
- Hal's half never mentions the extra roll at the bread stall or the hot drink at the lantern stall as *kindnesses*. To him they're the circle holding — routine. A narrator from his world wouldn't think to marvel at them.

A useful test for any narrator: **what would this person never think to explain?** The answer shows you the shape of their world.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Re-read the rain scene ("Halfway across, the clouds broke open..."). Write two sentences as the traveler would *tell* it later to a friend, and two sentences as Hal would *tell* it to another boatman. Keep each in character — the words each would choose.
2. For each retelling in (1), underline one word or phrase that only makes sense inside that character's cultural frame. Explain the frame in one sentence.
3. The story gives the traveler the first half and Hal the second. Why not alternate paragraph by paragraph? Propose one effect of this structure. *(Hint: think about when the reader is allowed to learn she was wrong.)*

## Independent practice

1. Choose one paragraph from the traveler's half. Rewrite it as Hal would narrate it — same events, his frame. Then write two sentences: what changed, and what the change reveals about his world.
2. List three things the *narrator* (not either character) seems to know that neither character knows. For each, explain how you can tell the narrator knows it. *(Example to start: the narrator knows the toll joke's history — "a joke his father had told.")*
3. Write a paragraph: is the traveler an *unreliable* narrator of her own half? Defend your answer with two quotations.

## Application

Pick a news or sports event you saw covered by two different outlets (or two people you know). Write six sentences comparing what each chose to emphasize and one sentence proposing what cultural or professional frame explains the difference. No names of real people are needed — "Outlet A" and "Outlet B" is fine.

## Exit check

On an index card: one sentence the traveler would use to describe Hal, and one sentence Hal would use to describe the traveler — each revealing the speaker's frame, not just the facts.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the two halves aloud in different voices; the frame-analysis is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** the two retellings may be spoken and recorded, or drawn as two-panel comics with captions.
- **Extension:** read the ending of each half again. The traveler's ends on "a kindness"; Hal's ends on "the river went on keeping its own accounts." Write 150 words on how the *last line* of each half is a point-of-view statement — what each world counts as a satisfying ending.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners tend to crown one view "right" (usually Hal's, since his half comes last and corrects her). Push back: the story does not say the traveler's feeling was *false* — only her explanation of it. A feeling can be real while its cause is misunderstood; that's the whole point of point-of-view analysis. Also watch for "Hal is the reliable narrator" — he is reliable about fares and blind about how his world looks from outside, which the story flags explicitly ("She was the first who seemed to think it was a gift").
