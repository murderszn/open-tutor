# Lesson 04 — Tracking a Theme as It Emerges: "The Night Market"

**Goal (student-readable):** I can track a candidate theme through a full story — stating it, testing it against details as they appear, and refining it when the text pushes back — and explain how the theme is developed, not just what it is. (RL.9-10.2)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, [texts/the-night-market.md](../texts/the-night-market.md) · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L4](../answer-key.md#lesson-04)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write a one-sentence "candidate theme" for "The Ferryman's Toll" from Lesson 01 — a claim about what the story *says*, not what happens. Then write one detail from the story that *complicates* your sentence. (A theme that survives a complication is a theme worth keeping.)

## Themes are tracked, not found

A theme is not hidden in the text like a coin in a sofa. It is *built* — by you, across the whole story, out of details the author keeps returning to. The method:

1. **Propose** a candidate theme early (a full sentence with a verb: "The story suggests that...").
2. **Test** it against each new detail: does the detail support it, refine it, or break it?
3. **Refine** the sentence when the text pushes back. Version 2, version 3 — the tracker below makes this visible.
4. **Confirm** at the end: the final version should account for the beginning, the middle, *and* the ending — especially details that first seemed to contradict it.

Read [the-night-market.md](../texts/the-night-market.md) now, all the way through, before continuing.

## Worked example 1 — A theme tracker, filled in

Candidate starting point: *"The story suggests that honesty matters more than profit."*

| Story section | Key detail (quoted) | What it does to my theme | Theme version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginning | "her prices were written on a chalkboard in a hand so neat it looked printed" | Supports v1: the neat board = public honesty, prices in the open. | v1: Honesty matters more than profit. |
| Middle | "Damp pepper is still pepper, and the board price is the board price." | **Complicates** v1: Ren uses the *form* of honesty (the board price) to defend a *dishonest* sale. The theme needs the difference between looking honest and being honest. | v2: Honesty means the public price and the private truth match — the board and the book must agree. |
| Middle | Milo writes: "Pepper, damp shipment, sold at full price. Customer not told." | Supports v2 strongly: the ledger (private truth) now records what the board (public price) hid. | v2 holds. |
| End | "Send a boy after the merchant with two sound bags and the difference in coin. Do not sell the damp shipment at full price again." | Refines v2 into v3: honesty isn't just *recording* the truth — it's *acting* on it, at a cost (two sound bags + coin). | v3: Honesty means making the public price and the private truth match — and paying the cost when they don't. |

Notice what happened: the middle *broke* v1 (Ren's defense of the damp pepper), and the theme got better because of it. A tracker that never revises is a tracker that stopped reading halfway.

## Worked example 2 — A competing candidate, tested and kept or dropped

Competing candidate: *"The story suggests that apprentices learn by watching their masters."*

- **Beginning:** Milo has "been with her since spring"; his job is weigh, wrap, keep the ledger → supports: he is learning the trade by watching.
- **Middle:** The complication — Milo learns the *wrong* lesson first (Ren's "damp pepper is still pepper") and then *corrects* his teacher by writing in the ledger. The theme as stated is too simple: he doesn't just absorb; he judges.
- **End:** "She sent him to fetch the sound pepper himself, so he'd remember where it was stored." Ren *does* teach — but what she teaches in the end is the corrected lesson, the one Milo wrote.

**Verdict:** the candidate survives, but refined: *"Apprentices learn by watching — including learning when to correct what they see."* A competing theme isn't an enemy; testing two candidates against the same details is how you find the stronger one. (You may keep both if both survive — stories can carry more than one theme.)

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Start your own tracker for this candidate: *"The story suggests that written records — boards, ledgers — have power over what people do."* Fill in beginning / middle / end rows with quotations. Does the candidate survive? Refine it once.
2. Test this detail against your v-final from (1): "Ren had told him to heap the pinch, 'because her hands shake and it cheers her.'" Does the heaped saffron pinch support your theme, complicate it, or break it? Write two sentences.
3. Compare your refined theme with a partner's (or the adult's). Where you differ, point at the *detail* that moved each of you — not the conclusion.

## Independent practice

1. Complete a full tracker for a *second* candidate of your choice (examples: "doing the right thing costs something"; "admitting a mistake in writing makes it real"). Three rows minimum, with quotations and a version history (v1 → v2 → final).
2. Write a paragraph (6–8 sentences): which of your two tracked themes is *more* developed by the story — meaning the story returns to it in more scenes and refines it further? Cite two details.
3. Error-analysis: below is a weak theme statement. Diagnose it in three sentences — what makes it weak, and how would you fix it?
   > *"The theme of 'The Night Market' is pepper."*

## Application

Take a film, song, or game you know well. State a candidate theme in one sentence, then name three moments (beginning / middle / end) that test it, and give the refined final version. If a moment broke your first version, say so — that's the interesting part.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the story aloud in sections; the tracking is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** the tracker may be spoken, drawn as a path with revision flags, or built as a table on paper.
- **Extension:** track the *same* candidate theme ("honesty = board and book matching") through "The Ferryman's Toll" — does Hal's ledger margin ("one passage, no fare. Courtesy of the river.") fit? Write 150 words on whether the theme travels between stories or belongs to only one.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The big one: stating a *topic* ("honesty," "pepper," "apprenticeship") and calling it a theme. A theme is a *sentence that claims something* — it needs a verb and a point. Press every topic-word answer: "Honesty *does what* in this story?" Second: learners often lock v1 and cherry-pick supporting details while ignoring the damp-pepper complication. The tracker exists to make revision visible — check that version numbers actually change. Third: "the moral" ≠ "the theme" — a fable's stated moral is the author's summary; a theme is the reader's tracked claim about what the details develop.
