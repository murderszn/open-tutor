# Lesson 05 — Objective Summary vs. Retelling: The Summary Workshop

**Goal (student-readable):** I can write an objective summary of "The Night Market" — covering the whole story, leaving out my opinions and judgments, and keeping only what matters to the plot and theme. (RL.9-10.2)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, [texts/the-night-market.md](../texts/the-night-market.md); the adult scores with the Lesson 05 rubric in the teacher materials · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Summarize "The Ferryman's Toll" in exactly three sentences — no opinions, no "I think." Then circle any word in your three sentences that is secretly a judgment (like "kind," "selfish," "weird"). Most first drafts have at least one.

## A summary is not a retelling, and not a review

Three different jobs:

- **Retelling:** "First this happened, then this, then this..." — everything in order, including the lantern strings creaking and the child laughing by the well. Complete, but not selective.
- **Review:** "This story is about how Milo is braver than Ren..." — your judgment, your take. Useful elsewhere; poison here.
- **Objective summary:** the whole arc, *only* the load-bearing events, *zero* opinion words. It answers: what happened, to whom, what changed — in the fewest sentences that still cover beginning, middle, and end.

The test for every sentence: **would someone who disagrees with my opinion of the story still agree this sentence is true?** If yes, it belongs. "Ren sells damp pepper at full price" — yes, both her defenders and critics agree. "Ren is greedy" — no; that's a verdict, not a fact.

## Worked example 1 — Shrinking a retelling into a summary

A learner's first-draft retelling of the middle (too long, too much color):

> *"Then this rich merchant from upriver comes in, and he's buying a TON of stuff, like twenty bags of pepper and a bunch of other spices, which is a huge deal. Milo is weighing everything and he whispers to Ren that the pepper got damp and smells musty, which is pretty gross. Ren just keeps counting money and says it's still pepper, which is kind of shady honestly."*

Diagnosis, sentence by sentence:

1. "rich merchant" — *rich* is an inference (he buys a lot; the text never says rich). Cut or replace with "a merchant buying for a caravan."
2. "a TON of stuff... huge deal" — color, not load-bearing. The quantities matter (they raise the stakes); "huge deal" is judgment. Keep: "twenty bags of pepper, ten of cumin, five of saffron."
3. "pretty gross" / "kind of shady honestly" — opinions. Cut both. The facts (damp, musty, sold at full price without telling) already do the work — a summary trusts the facts.

Revised into summary sentences:

> *"A merchant buying for a caravan orders twenty bags of pepper, ten of cumin, and five of saffron at the board price. While wrapping, Milo warns Ren that the pepper shipment got damp and smells musty. Ren tells him damp pepper is still pepper and completes the sale without telling the merchant."*

Three sentences, whole middle, zero judgments. Notice: the summary is *shorter* than the retelling but *more* informative about what matters.

## Worked example 2 — A full objective summary, annotated

> *"At a night market, spice seller Ren runs a corner stall with her apprentice Milo, who weighs, wraps, and keeps the ledger. (1: sets scene and roles) A merchant orders a large quantity of spices at the posted prices; Milo discovers the pepper is damp and musty but Ren sells it at full price without telling the buyer. (2: the conflict, with the key facts) Milo records the sale and its concealment in the ledger; Ren reads it, agrees he is right, and sends sound pepper and the price difference after the merchant. (3: the turn and resolution) Milo closes the ledger with the lesson that the price and the truth must match. (4: the ending's meaning, stated as what happens — he writes the line — not as my praise of him.)"*

Check it against the three summary rules:

- **Whole arc?** Beginning (stall, roles) → middle (damp pepper sale) → end (correction). Yes.
- **Objective?** Scan for adjectives of judgment: "large" (quantity — factual), "sound" (the story's own word for the replacement pepper). No "brave," "dishonest," "kind." Yes.
- **Selective?** The heaped saffron pinch, the lamps, the child by the well — all cut. They enrich the story; they don't carry the summary. (A longer summary might keep the saffron pinch as contrast — selectivity is judgment about *importance*, and that's allowed.)

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Write a three-sentence objective summary of the *beginning* of "The Night Market" (before the merchant arrives). Then trade with the adult: each circles any judgment word in the other's draft.
2. Take this sentence and fix it twice — once to remove the opinion, once to shorten it without losing facts: *"Milo is the real hero because he bravely stands up to his greedy boss about the disgusting pepper."*
3. Decide: does an objective summary of this story need the saffron-pinch detail? Argue yes or no in two sentences — citing what the detail *does* for a reader who only gets the summary.

## Independent practice

1. Write a full objective summary of "The Night Market": 5–8 sentences, whole arc, no opinion words.
2. Write a two-sentence "retelling fragment" of one scene (full color, in-order detail), then write the one summary sentence that replaces it. Label which is which.
3. Self-score your summary from (1) with this checklist — mark each yes/no and fix every no:
   - [ ] Covers beginning, middle, and end?
   - [ ] No opinion adjectives ("brave," "shady," "kind," "greedy")?
   - [ ] No invented facts (check quantities and names against the text)?
   - [ ] A stranger could follow the plot from my sentences alone?

## Application

Summarize — objectively, 4–6 sentences — a news article, podcast episode, or chapter you read this week. Then write two sentences: what was hardest to leave out, and was the hardest thing a fact or an opinion?

## Exit check

On an index card: your one-sentence summary of the *ending* of "The Night Market" (Ren's correction through Milo's last ledger line). The adult checks it for smuggled judgments.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the story aloud; the selecting-and-shrinking is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** summaries may be dictated, or built as "keep / cut" card sorts before drafting.
- **Extension:** write two summaries of the same story — one in 3 sentences, one in 10. Then write 100 words: what did the 10-sentence version keep that the 3-sentence version cut, and which kept item is most defensible as load-bearing?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners confuse *short* with *objective* — a three-sentence summary can still smuggle "bravely" and "greedy." Score objectivity and completeness separately (see rubric): a summary can be complete but slanted, or objective but missing the ending. The most common failure is the missing ending — learners summarize the conflict and stop, because the conflict is the exciting part. Require the resolution sentence explicitly. Also watch invented precision: "twenty-five bags" for "twenty bags" — summaries must not invent facts; check quantities against the text.

**Adult scoring (objectivity / completeness), 4 points each:**

- *Objectivity 4:* zero opinion/judgment words; no invented facts. *3:* one borderline word. *2:* two+ judgment words or one invented fact. *1:* summary is a review in disguise.
- *Completeness 4:* beginning, middle (conflict), and resolution all present; load-bearing facts kept. *3:* all three present, one thin. *2:* missing one arc section. *1:* covers one section only.
