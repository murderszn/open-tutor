## Cultural context, in plain words

Every story sits inside a world — not the physical world of rivers and boats, but the *cultural* world: what people in that setting believe, what they consider normal, what the times they live in make possible. Readers call this **cultural context**, and it has four parts:

1. **Historical moment** — the time the story happens in. What technology, laws, and events shape life? (A world with ledgers and market stalls is a world before spreadsheets.)
2. **Social norms** — what people are *expected* to do: the unwritten rules. Refusing payment can be an insult in one place and a courtesy in another.
3. **Belief systems** — what people assume is true about how the world works: debts the river "keeps," debts to ancestors, ideas about fairness.
4. **Genre traditions** — the kind of story it is and what that kind *promises*. A fable promises a moral; a tall tale promises exaggeration; a folk episode promises that the same trick works every time.

You never need to know all four to enjoy a story. But knowing them keeps you from a specific mistake: reading the story as if it happened in *your* world, with *your* rules.

## Worked example 1 — Context-mapping "The Ferryman's Toll"

Read the story, then map it:

| Context layer | What's in the story's world | How I know (words on the page) |
|---|---|---|
| Historical moment | Pre-industrial river economy: coins ("two coppers"), ledger books, market stalls, no bridges or engines | "two coppers," "the ledger at the ferry house," "loading firewood" |
| Social norms | The fare is fixed and public; overcharging in a storm is shameful; merchants do small courtesies for a ferryman's passengers | "the toll schedule written in the ledger," "the three-copper talk was a joke," "merchants give a ferryman's regulars a small courtesy" |
| Belief systems | Hal believes the river "keeps its own accounts"; generosity and business are woven together, not separate | "the river keeps its own accounts," "the circle holds" |
| Genre traditions | Folk episode: a traveler meets a local, a custom is shown, and the ending loops back to the river's "accounts" | The second half retells the same day as routine, deflating the traveler's wonder |

Notice: every entry ends at the words. The map is built *from* the text, not from our guesses.

## Worked example 2 — Sorting evidence from assumption

The lesson's sorting task. Each statement goes in one of two piles:

| Statement | Pile | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| "Hal refuses the traveler's coins at the end." | Textual evidence | It happens on the page: "he closed her fingers over them gently, pushing her hand back." |
| "The traveler's world is wealthier and more urban than Hal's." | Reader's cultural assumption | The text gives "city boots" and a journal — clues, not proof. I am adding my own picture of cities vs. river towns. The text never says this. |
| "Ferrymen in this world are expected to charge a fixed fare." | Textual evidence | "the fare is the fare, and a ferryman who gouges a passenger in a storm is the sort the other boatmen talk about" — stated outright. |
| "The traveler is naive and Hal is wise." | Reader's cultural assumption | This is a *judgment* my reading makes. The text shows her misunderstanding; calling it "naive" is my word, not the story's. |
| "Hal has refused fares before." | Textual evidence | "She was the first who seemed to think it was a gift from him personally" and "the seventh no-fare passage that season." |

**The rule:** an inference is fine — readers infer constantly. But label it. *What the text says* and *what I assume* are different piles, and confusing them is how stereotypes sneak in. A background note can *inform* your reading; it never *decides* it. And no reader is entitled to project a stereotype onto a story's people just because they seem unfamiliar.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Find two more details in the story's first half and place each on the context map: one for *historical moment*, one for *social norms*. Quote the words.
2. Sort these three statements into evidence vs. assumption, and say why:
   a. "Hal's community values reciprocity — favors circulate rather than being settled in cash."
   b. "River-town people are more honest than city people."
   c. "The traveler writes things down because her culture values record-keeping over memory."

*(Hint: ask of each one — "Could a skeptic point at the exact words? If not, it's my addition.")*

## Independent practice

1. Add a fifth row to your context map: pick a *belief system* detail the guided practice didn't cover, with quoted words.
2. Write a four-sentence paragraph explaining one way the traveler misunderstands Hal's world, using at least two quotations. Start with the text's words, end with what they reveal.
3. Find one statement a careless reader might treat as fact about Hal's world that is really an assumption. Explain the difference in two sentences.

## Application

Take a story or film you already know well. Map one layer of its cultural context (your choice of the four) with three text details. Then write: *what would a reader from a different world get wrong about this story, and why?*

## Exit check

On an index card: one textual-evidence statement about "The Ferryman's Toll" and one assumption a reader might make, labeled, with the quoted words that make the evidence *evidence*.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the story aloud while the learner follows; the sorting is the work, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** context-map rows and sort piles may be dictated, drawn as a labeled diagram, or sorted as written cards.
- **Extension:** map the *traveler's* cultural context (her world: journals, city boots, tipping-for-kindness logic) in the same four layers, and show how the two maps collide in the coin scene.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often hear "cultural context" as permission to explain a story by stereotyping it ("people like that are generous"). Redirect every generalization to the words: *who in the text does this, and where?* Also watch the reverse error — treating background notes as verdicts ("the ledger means the ending is only about economics"). Background informs; the text decides. Press the evidence-vs.-assumption sort physically: *point at the words.*
