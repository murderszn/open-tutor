# Lesson 03 — Translation as Interpretation: Two Grasshoppers, Two Worlds

**Goal (student-readable):** I can compare two translations of the same fable, explain how specific word choices change tone and meaning, and read an edition/translator note to say whose English I am reading. (RL.9-10.4)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, [texts/aesop-ant-grasshopper-two-translations.md](../texts/aesop-ant-grasshopper-two-translations.md), [texts/how-translators-work.md](../texts/how-translators-work.md) · **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Read the two translations of "The Ant and the Grasshopper" once each, straight through, no notes. Then write: *which grasshopper do you feel more sorry for — and can you point at a word that made you feel it?*

## A translator is an interpreter wearing a disguise

Last lesson, point of view belonged to characters. Today it belongs to the *translator* — the invisible narrator between you and the story. Two translators can face the same ancient Greek sentences and produce two different grasshoppers, because every word is a choice, and choices carry tone.

Before comparing, read [how-translators-work.md](../texts/how-translators-work.md). The key idea: **translation is interpretation.** When two editions disagree, the question is never "which one is right?" It is: *"what did each translator choose, and what does each choice do to the meaning?"*

And read the edition notes at the top of the translations file. They tell you *whose English* you are reading and *when*: George Fyler Townsend, 1867, London — and V. S. Vernon Jones, 1912, London. A 150-year-old translation is itself a historical document, showing how one era read the story. That is cultural context applied to the *reading*, not just the story.

## Worked example 1 — Word choice and tone: the grasshopper's excuse

Same moment in the story — the ants ask why the grasshopper stored nothing — two renderings:

- **Townsend (1867):** "I had not leisure enough. I passed the days in singing."
- **Jones (1912):** "I was so busy singing that I hadn't the time."

Reasoning through the difference:

1. **Diction register:** "I had not leisure enough" is formal, almost legal — the language of an excuse offered to a judge. "I hadn't the time" is conversational, the language of a friend caught out. Townsend's grasshopper sounds like he is *defending himself*; Jones's sounds like she is *confessing*.
2. **"Passed the days in singing" vs. "so busy singing":** "Passed the days" is neutral, nearly elegant — time spent. "So busy" is defensive and a little whiny; *busy* claims the singing was *work*, which is exactly what the ants dispute. Jones gives the grasshopper a worse argument and a more human voice at the same time.
3. **Effect on sympathy:** The formal grasshopper keeps his dignity and loses your pity; the casual one loses dignity and gains it. A translator's vocabulary is a sympathy dial.

Neither is "the real Aesop." The ancient text is gone; what we have are English performances of it, 45 years apart, and the performances disagree about what kind of creature a grasshopper is.

## Worked example 2 — The ending: derision vs. a chuckle

- **Townsend:** "They then said in derision: 'If you were foolish enough to sing all the summer, you must dance supperless to bed in the winter.'"
- **Jones:** "'If you spent the summer singing,' replied the ants, 'you can't do better than spend the winter dancing.' And they chuckled and went on with their work."

Reasoning:

1. **"In derision" vs. "chuckled":** Townsend *tells* you the ants are mocking — the narrator takes the ants' side and makes sure you do too. "Derision" is contempt. Jones's "chuckled" is amusement — the ants find the grasshopper funny, not despicable. The moral temperature drops from *condemnation* to *a rueful joke*.
2. **"Foolish enough" vs. no adjective:** Townsend's ants name the grasshopper a fool outright; Jones's ants let the sentence do the work. The reader of Townsend is handed the judgment; the reader of Jones has to decide whether to join the chuckle.
3. **"Supperless to bed" vs. "spend the winter dancing":** "Supperless" is a concrete punishment — hunger, named. Jones's ending is almost gentle: dancing through winter sounds nearly pleasant until you remember it's a death sentence. Jones lets the horror sit *under* the lightness.

Same plot. Different fable — one a sermon, one a shrug. The translator chose both.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Compare the titles: "The Ants and the Grasshopper" (Townsend) vs. "The Grasshopper and the Ants" (Jones). Who is foregrounded in each? Write two sentences on how the title order nudges the reader's sympathy before the story starts.
2. Jones's grasshopper is "her"; Townsend's is "him." Does the gender change the plot? Does it change *your* reading? Write three sentences — and check yourself: is your reaction coming from the text, or from your own assumptions about who "her" and "him" are? (This is Lesson 01's sort, applied to translation.)
3. Jones adds an explanation Townsend skips: the corn "had got rather damp during a long spell of rain." Why would a translator add a cause the original doesn't state? Propose one reason, using an idea from "How Translators Work."

## Independent practice

1. Pick any *two* of these pairs and write a four-sentence comparison for each, naming the word-choice difference and its tone effect: (a) "earnestly begged for a little food" vs. "begged them to spare her a few grains, 'For,' she said, 'I'm simply starving'"; (b) "Why did you not treasure up food during the summer?" vs. "Why didn't you collect a store of food for the winter?"; (c) "perishing with famine" vs. "I'm simply starving."
2. Write the edition note for a *third, imaginary* translation — dated 2026, translated for teenagers — in two sentences: translator's name (invented), date, and one choice you'd make differently from both Townsend and Jones. Then defend the choice in two more sentences.
3. One-sentence verdict, with a quotation: which translation's ants would you rather meet, and what word made you decide?

## Application

Find any two English versions of the same short public-domain text (two Bible translations of one verse, two versions of a nursery rhyme, two editions of a Poe stanza — all lawful sources). Write six sentences comparing one word-choice difference and its effect. Cite both sources.

## Exit check

On an index card: one word-choice difference between the two translations, quoted from each, and one sentence on what the difference does to tone or meaning.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read both translations aloud; the comparison is the work, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** comparisons may be spoken, drawn as labeled "tone dials," or built as a two-column chart.
- **Extension:** research (adult-supervised, lawful sources only) when Townsend and Jones were writing — 1867 and 1912 Britain. Write 150 words: how might each translator's era show in the ants? (Consider: what did "industry" and "leisure" mean to a Victorian schoolroom vs. an Edwardian general reader?)

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat the older translation as "more accurate" (older = closer to the original). Correct this directly: Townsend is not closer to Aesop than Jones is; he is closer to *1867*. Both are performances. Also watch for "the translator was biased" as a dismissal — the lesson's point is the opposite: every translation *must* choose, so the skill is reading the choices, not resenting them. Finally, some learners will call Jones's grasshopper "nicer" as if niceness were in the Greek; redirect to the words.
