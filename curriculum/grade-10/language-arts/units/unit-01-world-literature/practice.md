# Practice Tasks — Unit 01: World Literature: Cultural Context and Interpretation

*Each task names its session, its activity, and its success criterion, and names the lesson skill it reinforces. The adult reviews written practice the same day. Vocabulary recycled across sessions: cultural context, point of view, textual evidence, assumption, inference, theme, objective summary, translation, interpretation, tone, diction.*

## Practice A — Session 4: context-map drill (Lesson 01)

Build a full four-layer context map for **"The Night Market"** (historical moment / social norms / belief systems / genre traditions).

1. Historical moment: name two details placing the story's time, each with quoted words.
2. Social norms: name two unwritten rules of the market lane, each with quoted words.
3. Belief systems: name one assumption the characters hold about how the world works (e.g., about ledgers, debts, or prices), with quoted words.
4. Genre traditions: name the story's kind and one promise it keeps (or breaks).
5. Write two sentences: which layer most shapes the damp-pepper conflict, and why?
6. Sort these into TEXTUAL EVIDENCE or READER'S ASSUMPTION, with one-sentence defenses:
   a. "The market lane has no electric lights."
   b. "Milo is fourteen years old."
   c. "Ren has run the stall for many years."
   d. "Merchants in this world expect posted prices to be honored."
7. Write three sentences explaining why (6d) is tricky — what makes it *feel* like evidence.

**Success criterion:** four layers mapped with quotations; six statements sorted with defenses; the tricky-case explanation names the evidence/assumption line. (Reinforces L1.)

## Practice B — Session 7: point-of-view rewrite (Lesson 02)

1. Re-read the market-stall scene where Milo whispers about the damp pepper. Write it as **Ren** would tell it later to a fellow vendor (4–6 sentences, in her voice).
2. Write the same scene as **the upriver merchant** would tell it to his caravan that night (4–6 sentences, in his voice).
3. For each retelling, underline one phrase that only makes sense inside that character's frame, and name the frame in one sentence.
4. List two things **Ren's** telling leaves out that **Milo's** telling would include, and explain what the omission reveals about her point of view.
5. Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences): is Milo a reliable narrator of the ledger scene? Defend with two quotations.
6. Vocabulary recycle: use *point of view*, *cultural experience*, and *textual evidence* correctly in your paragraph from (5) — underline each use.
7. Error-analysis: a learner writes, *"Ren's version is biased so it's wrong, and Milo's version is unbiased so it's right."* Diagnose the error in three sentences: what is wrong with "biased vs. unbiased" here, and what should replace it?

**Success criterion:** two in-character retellings; frame phrases identified; omission analysis with reasoning; error diagnosis replaces "biased/unbiased" with frame-based language. (Reinforces L2; error-analysis task.)

## Practice C — Session 10: translation comparison lab (Lesson 03)

Using both Aesop translations:

1. Copy out Townsend's ants' final line and Jones's ants' final line. Label each with its translator and date.
2. Write four sentences comparing the two lines' **diction**: name one specific word choice in each and what it does to tone.
3. Write three sentences on the **endings'** moral temperature: which is colder (more condemnatory), which is warmer — and which *words* set the temperature?
4. The grasshopper's gender differs (him/her). Write three sentences: does it change the plot? Does it change your sympathy? Where is the change coming from — the text or you?
5. Write a two-sentence edition note for the translation you *preferred*, in the style of the notes in the translations file (translator, date, one choice they made).
6. Vocabulary recycle: use *interpretation*, *tone*, and *diction* in your answers above — underline each use.
7. Find one more word-choice difference the lessons didn't discuss (hint: compare how each translation describes the ants' work in the first sentence). Write three sentences on its effect.

**Success criterion:** both final lines quoted and labeled; diction comparison names specific words; gender question answered with the text-vs.-reader distinction; one new difference found. (Reinforces L3.)

## Practice D — Session 12: theme-tracker workshop (Lesson 04)

Track a **new** candidate theme through "The Ferryman's Toll": *"The story suggests that what looks like generosity is often just someone doing their job well."*

1. Fill a tracker table (section / quoted detail / effect on theme / version) for the traveler's half — at least two rows.
2. Fill at least two rows for Hal's half.
3. Show at least one revision (v1 → v2): name the detail that forced it.
4. Write a paragraph (6–8 sentences): does the candidate survive the whole story? If yes, state the final version; if no, state what replaced it and why.
5. Competing candidate, quick test: *"The story suggests that travelers romanticize what locals find ordinary."* Test it against one detail from each half (two sentences each). Which candidate is better developed? Say why in two sentences.
6. Vocabulary recycle: use *theme*, *inference*, and *assumption* correctly in your paragraph from (4) — underline each use.

**Success criterion:** tracker with 4+ rows, quotations, and a visible revision; verdict paragraph with a final theme sentence; competing candidate tested. (Reinforces L4.)

## Practice E — Session 14: summary clinic (Lesson 05)

1. Write a 5–8 sentence objective summary of **"The Ferryman's Toll"** (whole arc, no opinion words).
2. Self-score it with Lesson 05's checklist (arc / objectivity / no invented facts / followable). Fix every "no."
3. Take one sentence from your summary and expand it into a four-sentence *retelling fragment* (full color, in-order detail). Label both.
4. Rewrite this slanted sentence two ways — once objective, once shorter but still objective: *"The silly traveler totally misreads poor hardworking Hal because she's a snob about river people."*
5. Vocabulary recycle: in two sentences, explain the difference between *objective summary* and *retelling* using both terms correctly — underline them.
6. Trade summaries with the adult (or re-read yours after an hour). Circle any judgment word that slipped through and replace it.

**Success criterion:** summary passes all four checklist items after revision; slanted sentence rewritten twice with zero judgment words; keep/cut judgment demonstrated. (Reinforces L5.)

## Practice F — Session 17: essay revision studio (Lesson 06)

Bring your Lesson 06 draft.

1. Highlight every quotation. For each, verify: introduced, cited, explained, connected. Repair any dropped quotation.
2. Read your paragraph 3 (complication). Write the skeptic's objection in one sentence. If your paragraph doesn't answer it, add two sentences that do.
3. Cut 10%: mark every sentence that repeats an idea already stated, and delete or combine. (Strong essays are dense, not long.)
4. Check your claim sentence against the "smart reader could disagree" test. If it fails, sharpen it and adjust paragraph 2's topic sentence to match.
5. Write a 3-sentence "so what" closing (the optional paragraph 4): what does your interpretation reveal that a surface reading misses?
6. Vocabulary recycle: use *claim*, *textual evidence*, and *cultural context* in your closing — underline each use.
7. Final polish: read the essay aloud. Fix any sentence you stumble over. Check every quotation's citation format.

**Success criterion:** all quotations handled (introduced/cited/explained/connected); complication answered; claim arguable; closing written; read-aloud polish done. (Reinforces L6; prepares the unit assessment essay.)
