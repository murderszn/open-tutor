## The Well at the Crossroads

*Original — written for this unit. Fictional setting.*

Every village in the valley sent its daughters to the well at the crossroads on the first morning of the planting season. They came with clay jars, and they came singing — not because the work needed singing, but because their grandmothers had sung, and the grandmothers before them.

This year, Sarella came with a jar and a notebook.

She was the schoolteacher's daughter, home from the town school for the planting, and she had been taught to write down what she saw. While the others sang, she wrote: *6:10 — jars filled. 6:25 — procession forms. Song: "The Water Remembers" (third verse disputed).*

Old Maren, who led the singing, watched her write and said nothing until the jars were full. Then she said, "Child, the song doesn't go in the book the way water doesn't go in a sieve."

Sarella looked up, pen still moving. "But if I don't write it, how will anyone remember the third verse?"

Maren laughed — not unkindly. "Ask the well," she said, and the other girls laughed too, the easy laugh of people sharing a joke Sarella had not been told.

On the walk back, Sarella's cousin Lina fell into step beside her. "She didn't mean it cruel," Lina said. "It's just — the song isn't for remembering. It's for doing. You sing it while your hands are busy, and your hands remember."

"My hands don't remember anything," Sarella said. "That's why I have the notebook."

Lina was quiet for a few steps. Then she said, "Maybe the notebook is your song."

Sarella wrote that down too.

That evening, Maren came to the schoolteacher's house — an unheard-of visit — and asked Sarella to read back what she had written. Sarella read the times, the verses, the dispute about the third verse. Maren listened with her eyes closed. When Sarella finished, Maren said, "You got the morning. You didn't get the singing."

"What did I miss?"

"The part where it wasn't about the verse at all," Maren said. "It was about all of us being there before the sun got hot. The book can't hold that. But —" and here she smiled — "the book held *you* being there. That's something."

After Maren left, Sarella turned to a new page and wrote, not times or verses, but this: *The song is not for remembering. It is for doing. The notebook is my song. I was there before the sun got hot.*

She underlined the last line twice.

## Part A — Short items (RL.9-10.1, RL.9-10.2, RL.9-10.6)

**A1. (RL.9-10.1)** Quote the single strongest line of textual evidence that the well visit is a *repeated tradition* rather than a one-time event, and explain in one sentence why it is stronger than the other candidates.

**A2. (RL.9-10.1)** A reader claims: "Maren dislikes Sarella and resents the notebook." Is this textual evidence or reader assumption? Defend your answer in two sentences with one quotation.

**A3. (RL.9-10.6)** Explain in two to three sentences how Sarella's *cultural experience* (town school, notebook, "write down what she saw") shapes what she notices at the well — and what it keeps her from noticing. Quote one phrase.

**A4. (RL.9-10.6)** Old Maren says, "You got the morning. You didn't get the singing." In two to three sentences, explain what Maren means — and what this line reveals about *Maren's* point of view on what matters.

**A5. (RL.9-10.2)** State one theme of "The Well at the Crossroads" as a full sentence with a verb (a claim, not a topic).

**A6. (RL.9-10.2)** Name one detail in the story that *complicates or refines* your theme from A5, and explain in two sentences how the detail changed or deepened it.

## Part B — Interpretive essay (W.9-10.9)

Write a 3–4 paragraph interpretive essay making a claim about **a cultural point of view or experience in "The Well at the Crossroads"** (Sarella's, Maren's, or the village's) **and** how a theme develops through the story's details. Support the claim with strong, thorough textual evidence: quotations introduced, cited (paragraph numbers), explained, and connected to the claim. Include at least one detail that complicates your claim and your answer to it.

**Essay scoring rubric (12 points):**

| Criterion | 3 — Strong | 2 — Developing | 1 — Beginning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim (arguable, about POV/theme) | Arguable interpretive claim naming a mechanism | Claim present but vague or summary-like | No claim; retells plot |
| Evidence (strong, thorough) | 3+ quotations, each introduced, cited, explained, connected | Quotations present; some dropped or unexplained | Fewer than 2 quotations or none cited |
| Theme development | Shows theme emerging/refined across details | Theme stated but not tracked | Topic word instead of theme |
| Complication + conventions | Addresses a genuinely complicating detail; clear prose, few errors | Complication mentioned but unanswered; some errors | No complication; errors impede meaning |
