# Lesson 02 — Phrase Variety: Saying More With Less

**Goal (student-readable):** I can use different phrase types to add
precise detail and variety to my sentences. (L.9-10.1.b)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook
· **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write this flat sentence in your
notebook: *The boat came in.* Now add one detail about *when*, one
about *how*, and one about *the boat itself* — each as a separate
short phrase. Read your expanded sentence to the adult.

## A phrase adds detail without a new clause

A **phrase** is a group of words that works as one part of a
sentence but has no subject-verb pair of its own. The phrase types
this unit uses:

- **Noun phrase** — a noun plus its modifiers, acting as a subject,
  object, or complement: *the old stone quay*.
- **Verb phrase** — a main verb plus its helpers: *had been waiting*.
- **Adjectival phrase** — describes a noun: *with a cracked lantern*
  (prepositional, used as an adjective).
- **Adverbial phrase** — tells when, where, how, or why: *at dusk*,
  *without a sound*.
- **Participial phrase** — begins with a present participle (*-ing*)
  or past participle (*-ed* form) and describes a noun: *carrying a
  lantern* (describes the keeper); *tired by the long crossing*
  (describes the crew).
- **Prepositional phrase** — begins with a preposition (*in, on, at,
  through, without, beside*) and ends with a noun: *through the
  narrow channel*.
- **Absolute phrase** — a noun plus a participle (and any modifiers),
  describing the whole sentence rather than one word: *The tide
  turning, the boats rocked gently.* Here *the tide turning* is not
  the subject of *rocked*; it sets the scene for the whole sentence.
  Its noun (*tide*) is different from the sentence's subject
  (*boats*) — that difference is the test for an absolute phrase.

Why vary phrases? Because each type puts the detail in a different
place and gives it a different weight. A participial phrase tucks an
action beside its actor (*The keeper, carrying a lantern, crossed
the quay*). An absolute phrase opens a wide shot before the action
(*Lanterns lit, the quay glowed*). A prepositional phrase pins down
place or manner (*through the narrow channel*). Same facts, different
sentences — and the difference is meaning, not decoration.

## Worked example 1 — Expanding a flat paragraph

Flat paragraph: *The boat arrived. The crew was tired. The harbor
was quiet.*

Expansion, one phrase type at a time:

- *At dusk* (adverbial phrase — when), *the boat, its sails torn by
  the storm* (absolute phrase — the boat's state, described before
  the main action), *came through the narrow channel* (prepositional
  phrase — where).
- *The crew, tired by the long crossing* (participial phrase —
  describes the crew), *worked without a sound* (prepositional
  phrase used adverbially — how).
- *The harbor, a bowl of dark water and lamplight* (noun phrase in
  apposition — renames the harbor), *lay quiet.*

Expanded paragraph: *At dusk, the boat, its sails torn by the storm,
came through the narrow channel. The crew, tired by the long
crossing, worked without a sound. The harbor, a bowl of dark water
and lamplight, lay quiet.* Nothing new happened — but the reader
now knows when, how, and what condition everything was in. Note the
commas: a phrase that interrupts or opens a sentence is usually set
off by commas; a phrase that is essential to identifying its noun
(*the lamps that lined the quay* is a clause, not a phrase, and
*the boat with the torn sail* needs no commas around the phrase
because it identifies which boat) is not.

## Worked example 2 — Combining choppy sentences

Choppy: *Mara stood on the quay. She held a lantern. The wind was
cold. She waited.*

Combined with phrases: *Mara stood on the quay, holding a lantern,
waiting in the cold wind.* Three short sentences became one sentence
in which *holding a lantern* and *waiting in the cold wind* are
participial and prepositional phrases attached to *Mara stood*.
The combination works only because all the actions belong to Mara;
if the wind had its own actor, a clause — not a phrase — would be
needed (*While the wind cut across the quay, Mara waited* is a
clause combination, from Lesson 01).

A warning: a participial phrase must describe the noun next to it.
*Carrying a lantern, the quay was dark* is wrong — the quay was not
carrying the lantern. This is a **dangling modifier**. Fix it by
naming the actor: *Carrying a lantern, the keeper crossed the dark
quay.*

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Name the phrase type in each: (a) *through the narrow channel*
   (b) *tired by the long crossing* (c) *The tide turning, the
   boats rocked gently* — the phrase *the tide turning*.
2. Fix this dangling modifier two ways: *Waiting on the quay, the
   boat finally appeared.*
3. Combine these three sentences into one using at least two phrase
   types: *The scholar opened the letter. Her hands shook. The
   archive was silent.*

## Independent practice

1. Expand this sentence with one participial phrase and one
   absolute phrase (label both): *The travelers reached the river.*
2. Rewrite this flat paragraph with at least three phrase types
   (label each): *The market opened. Sellers arranged goods. Buyers
   arrived. The morning was busy.*
3. Identify the phrase type of each named group: (a) *without
   hesitation* in "She spoke without hesitation." (b) *its windows
   dark* in "The house, its windows dark, stood empty." (c)
   *exhausted by the climb* in "The runner, exhausted by the climb,
   sat down."
4. Find and fix the dangling modifier: *Reading by lamplight, the
   book's pages glowed.*

## Application

Rewrite one paragraph of your U01–U03 writing (or the paragraph you
labeled in Lesson 01) so that every sentence uses a different phrase
type as its main detail-carrier. Underline each phrase and label its
type in the margin. Keep the meaning; change only the packaging.

## Exit check

On an index card: write one sentence containing a participial phrase
and one sentence containing an absolute phrase. Label both phrases.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the example sentences aloud;
  phrase labeling is the work, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** expansions may be dictated; phrase
  labels may be spoken or drawn with brackets.
- **Extension:** take a paragraph from a text you are reading and
  rewrite it twice — once with most detail in participial phrases,
  once with most detail in absolute phrases — then explain in two
  sentences how the two versions feel different to a reader.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often label every *-ing* group a participial phrase, even
when it is part of a verb phrase (*was carrying*). Test: remove the
group — if the sentence still has a complete verb, the group is a
phrase; if the sentence loses its verb, it was the verb phrase.
Absolute phrases are often mistaken for fragments; remind learners
that an absolute phrase is a deliberate sentence part, not a
sentence, and must attach to a complete clause. Watch dangling
modifiers in the learner's own revisions all unit. Answer key: § L2.
