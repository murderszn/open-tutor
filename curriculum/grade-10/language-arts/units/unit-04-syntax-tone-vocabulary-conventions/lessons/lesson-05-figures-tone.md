## Figures of speech: meaning beyond the literal

A **figure of speech** uses words in a non-literal way to create an
effect. Two to master in this unit (L.9-10.5.a):

- **Euphemism** — a mild, indirect, or vague expression substituted
  for one considered harsh or blunt: *passed away* for *died*,
  *downsizing* for *layoffs*, *the boat was retired* for *the boat
  was scrapped*. A euphemism's role in a text is never just
  politeness: it can soften, evade, flatter, or deceive, depending on
  who uses it and why. Ask: *what is being softened, and who
  benefits from the softening?*
- **Oxymoron** — two contradictory terms joined: *deafening
  silence, bitter sweet, living dead, organized chaos, clearly
  confused*. An oxymoron forces two incompatible ideas into one
  phrase, and the tension between them is the point: the silence
  after the boats leave is *deafening* precisely because it is so
  complete that it feels loud.

Related tools: **denotation** is a word's literal dictionary meaning;
**connotation** is the feeling and association a word carries
(*house* vs. *home*, *stubborn* vs. *determined*, *cheap* vs.
*thrifty*). Tone is not made by one word. **Tone** is the writer's
attitude toward the subject, built by the *cumulative* impact of
word choices (RL.9-10.4): many small choices, all leaning the same
way. One *gloomy* does not make a gloomy passage; ten gloomy choices
do.

## Worked example 1 — A euphemism's role

Passage: *After the storm, the company announced it would "rightsize
the fleet." Three boats never returned to the quay.*

*Rightsize* is a euphemism: it replaces a harsher truth (boats
scrapped, crews dismissed) with a managerial-sounding word that
suggests the fleet is simply being made the correct size. Its role
here is evasion — the sentence after it supplies the fact the
euphemism hides. A reader who accepts *rightsize* at face value
misses the passage's real claim: that the company is disguising loss
as order. The analysis move is always the same: name the plain term,
name the substitute, and ask what the substitution gains for the
speaker.

## Worked example 2 — An oxymoron doing real work

Sentence: *When the last boat left, a deafening silence settled over
the quay.*

*Deafening* (extremely loud) and *silence* (absence of sound)
contradict each other. The contradiction is not a mistake: it says
the quiet was so total, after a day of noise, that it pressed on the
listener like sound. Remove the oxymoron — *When the last boat left,
the quay was quiet* — and the sentence becomes accurate but flat.
The figure carries the feeling the plain version cannot.

**Text-only alternative (same information as the image):** A small
wooden fishing boat with a single glowing lantern hanging from its
mast moves across calm water toward a stone quay on the right. Gulls
perch on the quay's wooden pilings. Behind the quay, stone cottages
with lit windows stand on a grassy slope; dark hills rise behind
them under a dusky blue sky. The water reflects the amber light of
the lantern and the cottage windows. The scene is quiet and still;
no people are shown in close detail.

Now compare two descriptions of this same scene:

- **Version A (terse):** *The boat came in. The water was calm. The
  lights were on.*
- **Version B (lush):** *Lantern-lit and unhurried, the little boat
  slipped through the glassy water toward the quay, where the gulls
  waited in patient rows and the cottages glowed against the
  darkening hills.*

Both versions are factually true. Version A uses short simple
sentences, plain nouns, and no modifiers: its tone is flat,
businesslike, almost bored — the scene matters little to this
speaker. Version B uses a participial phrase (*Lantern-lit*), a
prepositional phrase (*toward the quay*), a relative clause
(*where the gulls waited*), and connotative words (*unhurried,
glassy, patient, glowed*): its tone is watchful and fond. The facts
did not change. The syntax and the word choices did — and tone is
exactly that difference, accumulated.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Name the figure and its role: *The old lighthouse keeper was
   "let go" after forty years.* (Plain term? What does the
   substitute gain for the speaker?)
2. Name the figure and what its contradiction expresses: *Her
   farewell was bitter sweet: she was proud to leave and sorry to
   go.*
3. Which version's tone is warmer — A or B above — and which two
   word choices most create that warmth? Quote them.

## Independent practice

1. Write one sentence using a euphemism, then one sentence stating
   the plain fact it softens. Name what the euphemism gains for its
   speaker.
2. Write one sentence using an oxymoron that expresses a real
   tension (not a random contradiction). Explain the tension in one
   sentence.
3. Describe the harbor illustration in two sentences with a
   *hurried, anxious* tone. Use at least one participial phrase and
   one word with a negative connotation. (Do not copy Version A or B.)
4. Read these two sentences and name the tone of each with two
   quoted words as evidence: (a) *The committee, after months of
   careful study, released its findings.* (b) *The committee,
   after months of foot-dragging, finally dumped its findings.*

## Application

Take the paragraph you expanded in Lesson 02. Rewrite it twice:
once so its tone is calm and certain, once so its tone is uneasy.
Change only word choice and syntax — not the facts. Then write
three sentences explaining which choices created each tone, quoting
your own words.

## Exit check

On an index card: define *euphemism* and *oxymoron* in your own
words, with one original example of each.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the versions aloud; the
  tone analysis is the work. The text-only alternative above carries
  the image's content for any learner who cannot see it.
- **Alternate response mode:** tone descriptions may be dictated;
  figure identifications may be given orally.
- **Extension:** find a euphemism in a real public text (a news
  report, a company notice, a government form — adult-vetted) and
  write a paragraph analyzing what it softens and who benefits.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often label any contradiction an oxymoron, including
accidental ones (*pretty ugly* as a casual intensifier is not an
oxymoron doing analytical work — though it is a borderline case
worth discussing). Insist on the test: does the contradiction
*express* something the plain version cannot? Euphemisms are often
judged as simply "polite"; press for the speaker's gain. Tone is
often attributed to a single word; require cumulative evidence —
two or more quoted choices pulling the same way. Answer key: § L5.
