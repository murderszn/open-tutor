# Lesson 03 — Semicolons and Colons: Punctuation With a Reason

**Goal (student-readable):** I can use semicolons and colons correctly
and explain why I chose each mark. (L.9-10.2.a)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook
· **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Punctuate this sentence two ways and
tell the adult which version you prefer and why: *The storm passed
the harbor lay still.*

## The semicolon: a period's close cousin

A **semicolon** links two or more closely related independent
clauses (L.9-10.2.a). Think of it as a soft period: it separates
fully, but it tells the reader the two thoughts belong together.

- No connector: *The tide turned; the keeper lit the lamps.* The
  two clauses are closely related (cause and response), so the
  semicolon shows the link a period would hide.
- With a **conjunctive adverb** (a word that joins clauses and
  shows a relationship — *however, therefore, moreover,
  consequently, nevertheless, thus, meanwhile, otherwise*): the
  semicolon goes *before* the adverb and a comma goes *after* it:
  *The boat was late; however, the gulls waited on the pilings.*
  The adverb alone cannot do the joining — *The boat was late,
  however, the gulls waited* is a comma splice.
- In a complex list: when list items already contain commas, use
  semicolons between items so the reader can tell items apart:
  *The crew visited Harrow, on the north coast; Selby, upriver; and
  Dunmere, by the marsh.*

Never use a semicolon with a coordinating conjunction (*and, but,
or, for, nor, so, yet*): *The tide turned; and the keeper lit the
lamps* is wrong. Never use a semicolon before a dependent clause
or a phrase: *The keeper lit the lamps; when the tide turned* is
wrong, because *when the tide turned* is not an independent clause.

## The colon: a drumroll

A **colon** introduces something the first clause promises: a list,
a quotation, an explanation, or an example. The part before the
colon must be a complete independent clause.

- List: *The keeper carried three things: a lantern, a key, and a
  coil of rope.* (Not: *The keeper carried: a lantern…* — the part
  before the colon must stand alone.)
- Quotation: *The logbook ends with a single warning: "Trust no
  tide you cannot read."*
- Explanation: *The harbor was silent for a reason: every boat was
  already home.*

Colon or semicolon? A colon *announces* what follows; a semicolon
*balances* two equal thoughts. *The harbor was silent; every boat
was already home* balances two clauses. *The harbor was silent for
a reason: every boat was already home* announces the reason. Both
are correct; they mean slightly different things. That choice is
style, and Lesson 06 returns to it.

## Worked example 1 — Punctuating a set

Unpunctuated: *Mara kept the light no ship came that night she
trimmed the wick and waited until dawn.*

Step 1 — Count independent clauses: (1) *Mara kept the light*,
(2) *no ship came that night*, (3) *she trimmed the wick and waited
until dawn* (one clause with a compound verb).

Step 2 — Choose the joins by relationship. Clauses 1 and 2 contrast
(keeping the light vs. no ship): a semicolon with *however* fits:
*Mara kept the light; however, no ship came that night.* Clauses 2
and 3 are a sequence in time, best separated by a period: *…
no ship came that night. She trimmed the wick and waited until
dawn.*

Result: *Mara kept the light; however, no ship came that night. She
trimmed the wick and waited until dawn.* A period between clauses
1 and 2 would also be correct — but it would hide the contrast the
semicolon shows.

## Worked example 2 — When the mark is wrong

- *The committee published its notes; including three dissenting
  opinions.* Wrong: *including…* is a phrase, not an independent
  clause. Fix with a comma (*…its notes, including three dissenting
  opinions*) or a colon if the notes are then listed.
- *The archive holds letters, maps, and photographs: the letters
  date from 1910.* Wrong: the colon promises a list or explanation
  but the sentence then wanders into a new clause. Fix with a
  semicolon (*…photographs; the letters date from 1910*) or a
  period.
- *The travelers reached the river, they crossed at dawn.* Wrong:
  comma splice. Fix with a period, a comma plus *and*, or a
  semicolon (*…the river; they crossed at dawn*).

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Punctuate with a semicolon and a conjunctive adverb:
   *The evidence was thin the argument still convinced the room.*
2. Punctuate with a colon: *Bring three things a notebook a pen and
   patience.*
3. Name the error and fix it: *The scholar copied the letter;
   before the archive closed.*

## Independent practice

1. Punctuate each pair with a semicolon (add a conjunctive adverb
   where one fits naturally): (a) *The wind dropped the gulls
   settled.* (b) *The first translation was literal the second
   caught the tone.* (c) *The town needed water the council argued
   for a month.*
2. Punctuate each with a colon or explain why a colon is wrong:
   (a) *She named her reasons cost time and safety.* (b) *The
   reasons were cost time and safety.* (c) *He quoted the captain
   "Hold fast."*
3. Correct each sentence or write OK: (a) *The harbor lay still;
   and the boats rocked gently.* (b) *The keeper lit the lamps;
   the tide had turned.* (c) *Mara waited, the ship never came.*
4. Rewrite this paragraph using one semicolon and one colon, each
   justified in a margin note: *The expedition packed food water
   and tools. The route was long. The team left before dawn.*

## Application

Take a paragraph from your U01–U03 writing. Find one place where a
period hides a close relationship between two clauses and replace it
with a semicolon (with or without a conjunctive adverb), and one
place where a list or explanation deserves a colon. Write one
sentence justifying each change.

## Exit check

On an index card: write the semicolon rule in one sentence and the
colon rule in one sentence, each with a correct example of your own.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the practice sentences
  aloud; the punctuation reasoning is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** punctuation choices may be dictated
  or marked on printed sentences.
- **Extension:** rewrite Worked example 1's paragraph three ways —
  all periods, all semicolons, and the mixed version — and explain
  in three sentences how each version changes the reader's sense of
  pace and relationship.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat the semicolon as a fancy comma and sprinkle it
before phrases, lists, and conjunctions. Drill the test every time:
*can both sides stand alone as sentences?* If not, no semicolon.
The conjunctive-adverb pattern (semicolon before, comma after) is
the most common error site — have learners say the pattern aloud:
"semicolon, however, comma." Colon errors usually come from putting
the colon after a fragment (*The things are:*); require the
before-colon test: *could this part end with a period?* Answer key:
§ L3.
