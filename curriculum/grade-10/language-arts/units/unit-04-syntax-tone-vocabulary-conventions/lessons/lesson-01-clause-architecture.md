# Lesson 01 — Clause Architecture: What a Sentence Is Made Of

**Goal (student-readable):** I can take a sentence apart into its
clauses, name its sentence type, and repair fragments and run-ons.
(L.9-10.1.b)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
[clause-structure diagram](../assets/clause-structure-diagram.svg)
· **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L1](../answer-key.md#lesson-01)

**Readiness check (5 min):** In your notebook, write one sentence
about the last thing you read. Underline its verb (or verbs). Circle
the word or words doing the action. Share one thought with the adult:
how many complete thoughts does your sentence contain?

## A clause is a subject plus a verb

A **clause** is a group of words with a subject and a verb. There are
two kinds, and the difference is whether the group can stand alone:

- An **independent clause** can stand alone as a sentence. It
  expresses a complete thought: *The keeper lit the lamps.*
- A **dependent clause** has a subject and a verb but cannot stand
  alone. It leans on an independent clause: *when the tide turned* —
  "when" leaves the thought unfinished; the reader waits for the rest.

A **phrase** is different from both: a group of words with no
subject-verb pair of its own, such as *carrying a lantern* or *at
dusk*. Phrases add detail; they never stand alone as sentences.
(Lesson 02 works with phrases in full.)

Dependent clauses come in three kinds, named by the job they do:

- **Noun clause** — acts as a noun (subject, object, or complement).
  It often starts with *that, what, whether, who, why*: *The keeper
  knew **that the tide would turn**.* (object of *knew*).
- **Relative clause** — acts as an adjective, describing a noun. It
  usually starts with *who, whom, whose, which, that*: *the lamps
  **that lined the quay*** (describes *lamps*).
- **Adverbial clause** — acts as an adverb, telling when, where, why,
  or how. It often starts with *when, while, because, although, if*:
  ***When the tide turned**, the keeper lit the lamps.* (tells when).

## Worked example 1 — Taking the anchor sentence apart

Anchor sentence: *When the tide turned, the keeper, carrying a
lantern, lit the lamps that lined the quay.*

Step 1 — Find the verbs: *turned, lit*. ( *carrying* is a participle
in a phrase, not a clause verb; *lined* is the verb of the relative
clause — so there are three clause verbs in total: *turned, lit,
lined*.)

Step 2 — Group each verb with its subject:

- *When the tide turned* — subject *tide*, verb *turned*. It starts
  with *when* and cannot stand alone: **adverbial clause**.
- *the keeper lit the lamps* — subject *keeper*, verb *lit*. It can
  stand alone: **independent clause**.
- *that lined the quay* — subject *that* (standing for *lamps*),
  verb *lined*. It describes *lamps*: **relative clause**.
- *carrying a lantern* — no subject-verb pair: **participial phrase**
  describing *keeper*, not a clause.

Step 3 — Name the sentence type. One independent clause plus at
least one dependent clause makes a **complex sentence**. The four
sentence types are: **simple** (one independent clause),
**compound** (two or more independent clauses), **complex** (one
independent clause plus one or more dependent clauses), and
**compound-complex** (two or more independent clauses plus at least
one dependent clause). Our anchor sentence is **complex**. The
[diagram](../assets/clause-structure-diagram.svg) shows the same
breakdown in boxes; a text version of the breakdown is in the
paragraph above, so nothing depends on seeing the diagram.

## Worked example 2 — Two common breaks, and how to repair them

**Fragment** — a group of words punctuated as a sentence but missing
a subject, a verb, or a complete thought:

- *Because the boat was late.* — This is an adverbial clause with no
  independent clause to lean on. Repair by attaching it: *Because the
  boat was late, the gulls settled on the empty pilings.* Or by
  completing the thought: *The boat was late.*
- *The keeper carrying a lantern.* — This is a phrase, not a clause;
  there is no main verb. Repair: *The keeper, carrying a lantern,
  crossed the quay.*

**Run-on** — two independent clauses joined with no punctuation or
with only a comma (a **comma splice**):

- *The tide turned the keeper lit the lamps.* — Two complete
  thoughts crashed together. Repair with a period (*The tide turned.
  The keeper lit the lamps.*), a comma plus a coordinating
  conjunction (*The tide turned, and the keeper lit the lamps.*), or
  a semicolon (*The tide turned; the keeper lit the lamps.* — Lesson
  03 teaches when a semicolon is the best choice).
- *The tide turned, the keeper lit the lamps.* — A comma alone
  cannot join two independent clauses. Same three repairs apply.

The repair rule: first count the independent clauses. One clause
needs no joiner. Two clauses need a period, a comma plus a
conjunction, a semicolon, or a subordinating word that turns one
clause dependent.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. In *The letter that arrived at dawn changed everything*, name the
   relative clause and the noun it describes.
2. Is *Although the road was closed* a sentence? If not, repair it
   two different ways.
3. Repair this comma splice two different ways: *Mara kept the
   light, no ship came that night.*

## Independent practice

1. Label each clause in: *What the map showed surprised the
   travelers, because the road they trusted ended at the river.*
   (Name each clause's kind, and name the sentence type.)
2. Label each clause in: *The scholar who found the letter copied it
   before the archive closed.*
3. Identify each item as a sentence, a fragment, or a run-on, and
   repair every non-sentence: (a) *Running to catch the last train.*
   (b) *The wind dropped the harbor grew still.* (c) *After the
   meeting ended, the committee published its notes.*
4. Write one compound-complex sentence about a harbor at dusk. Label
   its clauses.

## Application

Take one paragraph from your U01–U03 writing (or write a new
four-sentence paragraph about a journey). Label every clause in it.
Then rewrite the paragraph so it contains at least one sentence of
each type: simple, compound, complex, compound-complex. Do not change
the paragraph's meaning.

## Exit check

On an index card: define *dependent clause* in your own words, and
give one example of each: a noun clause, a relative clause, and an
adverbial clause.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the sentences aloud; the
  clause work is the task, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** clause labels may be given orally or
  by drawing brackets and labels over printed sentences.
- **Extension:** find a compound-complex sentence in a text you are
  reading, label its clauses, and explain what the sentence gains by
  combining its clauses instead of splitting them into short
  sentences.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often call any long group of words a clause, or mistake a
participial phrase (*carrying a lantern*) for a clause because it
contains a verb-like word. Insist on the test: a clause needs a
subject *and* a finite verb. Another common error is treating *that*
as always relative: in *She knew that the tide would turn*, *that*
starts a noun clause (the object of *knew*), not a relative clause.
Ask: does the *that* group describe a noun, or fill a noun's job?
