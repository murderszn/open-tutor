# Lesson 04 — Vocabulary Systems: How to Learn a Word for Good

**Goal (student-readable):** I can figure out a new word's meaning
with context clues, word-change patterns, and reference materials —
and then verify it. (L.9-10.4.a–d, L.9-10.6)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
dictionary access (print or free digital)
· **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L4](../answer-key.md#lesson-04)

**Readiness check (5 min):** In your notebook, write what you think
*reluctant* means, and write one sentence showing how you guessed.
Then check a dictionary. Were you right? What did the dictionary add
that your guess missed?

## Four moves, in order

Unknown words are not a guessing game. Use the four moves in order,
and never stop at the first guess:

1. **Context clues (L.9-10.4.a).** Use the sentence, paragraph, or
   text around the word — its position, its job in the sentence,
   nearby contrasts, examples, or restatements. Clue types:
   definition (*a quay, a stone landing for boats*), contrast (*not
   hasty but meticulous*), example (*tools such as a sextant and a
   compass*), and cause/effect (*because the road was closed, the
   travelers were delayed* — *delayed* follows from the closure).
2. **Word-change patterns (L.9-10.4.b).** Words change form to
   change meaning or part of speech: *analyze* (verb) → *analysis*
   (noun) → *analytical* (adjective); *advocate* → *advocacy*.
   Prefixes, suffixes, and roots narrow the guess: *re-* (again),
   *un-* (not), *-tion* (the act or result of), *-able* (capable of).
   A pattern never proves a meaning by itself — *inflammable*
   famously does not mean "not flammable" — so it feeds the next
   move, not the conclusion.
3. **Reference materials (L.9-10.4.c).** Consult a dictionary (print
   or digital) for pronunciation, part of speech, precise meaning,
   and etymology; a thesaurus for nearby words — with care, since
   synonyms are rarely exact. For a word with several meanings, pick
   the sense that fits *this* sentence's part of speech and context.
4. **Verification (L.9-10.4.d).** Check the preliminary meaning back
   against the sentence: substitute your definition and re-read.
   If the sentence stops making sense, the guess was wrong. Then
   use the word in a new sentence of your own — a word you cannot
   use is a word you have not learned (L.9-10.6).

## Worked example 1 — From guess to verified meaning

Sentence: *The archivist was meticulous: every letter was dated,
copied, and filed in order.*

- Context: the colon introduces an explanation — dating, copying,
  and filing in order. So *meticulous* must mean something like
  "extremely careful and precise about details."
- Pattern: *meticulous* ends in *-ous* (full of, characterized by),
  like *generous* or *cautious* — consistent with a character trait,
  not an action.
- Reference: a dictionary gives, for *meticulous* (adjective):
  "showing great attention to detail; very careful and precise."
- Verification: substitute — *The archivist was very careful and
  precise about details: every letter was dated, copied, and filed
  in order.* The sentence still makes sense, and the explanation
  after the colon confirms it. Word-bank entry complete.

## Worked example 2 — When context misleads

Sentence: *Her candid answer surprised the committee.*

A quick guess might be "kind" — the committee was surprised, and
kind answers can surprise. But check: *candid* comes from the Latin
*candidus* (white, pure, shining) — the dictionary's first sense is
"truthful and straightforward; frank." Substitute: *Her truthful and
straightforward answer surprised the committee.* That fits better:
the surprise is that she did not soften the truth. The lesson:
contrast and cause clues help, but a guess built on the *feeling*
of a sentence, without a definition clue, must be verified before it
is trusted. Record the correction in the word bank — wrong first
guesses, kept honestly, are the best study material.

## The unit word bank

From this lesson through Session 20, keep a bank of 12 words:
*candid, diligent, ambiguous, coherent, reluctant, substantiate,
meticulous, sustain, precise, interpret, evident, compose*. For each
word, record: (a) the sentence where you met it, (b) your context
guess and clue type, (c) a word-change pattern or reference note
(such as *precise → precision*, *interpret → interpretation*,
*sustain → sustainable*), (d) the verified meaning, and (e) a new
sentence of your own using the word correctly. Practice D starts the
bank; the Session 15 studio finishes it.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Use context to guess *diligent* in: *Diligent through the whole
   winter, the keeper repainted every piling before spring.* Name
   the clue type.
2. Use a word-change pattern: if *compose* means "to put together,"
   what do *composition* and *composer* most likely mean? Verify
   with a dictionary.
3. Verify or correct this guess: a learner guessed *ambiguous*
   means "unclear in a bad way" from *The ending was ambiguous: some
   readers saw hope, others saw loss.* Is the guess right, and what
   should the bank entry say?

## Independent practice

1. For each sentence, guess the italic word's meaning, name the
   clue type, then verify with a dictionary and record the verified
   meaning: (a) *The instructions were coherent: each step followed
   the last without gaps.* (b) *He was reluctant to sign, and asked
   for a day to think.* (c) *She could substantiate her claim with
   letters, dates, and receipts.*
2. Write the word-change family for *sustain* (verb): give its noun
   and adjective forms and use each in a sentence. Verify the forms
   with a dictionary.
3. A thesaurus lists *frank, blunt, open* for *candid*. Explain in
   two sentences why *blunt* is not always a safe substitute for
   *candid*.
4. Write a new sentence using *evident* correctly, then verify your
   use by substituting the dictionary definition.

## Application

Choose any three words from this lesson's bank that you did not
already know. Write a single paragraph (4–6 sentences) that uses
all three correctly and gives each one a context clue a reader could
use. Exchange paragraphs with the adult: can the adult recover each
word's meaning from your clues alone?

## Exit check

On an index card: list the four vocabulary moves in order, and
write one sentence explaining why verification (move 4) is not
optional.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the practice sentences
  aloud; the clue analysis is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** word-bank entries may be dictated;
  verification may be shown by substitution read aloud.
- **Extension:** research one word's etymology (its history) and
  explain in a paragraph how its history explains a meaning that its
  modern spelling hides. (*Candidate* words: *candid, meticulous,
  diligent* — the adult vets the source.)

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often stop at a plausible guess, or treat a thesaurus
synonym as an exact replacement. Require the substitution test on
every entry: a meaning that breaks its sentence is wrong, however
confident the guess felt. Watch part-of-speech errors in word-change
families (*analysis* used as a verb, *analytical* used as a noun).
For multiple-meaning words, insist the learner name the sense that
fits the sentence, not the first dictionary sense. Answer key: § L4.
