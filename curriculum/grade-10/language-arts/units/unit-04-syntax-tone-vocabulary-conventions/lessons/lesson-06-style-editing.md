# Lesson 06 — Style and Editing: Choosing Language for the Occasion

**Goal (student-readable):** I can edit a piece of writing for
structure, language, and conventions, and explain my choices for its
audience and purpose. (L.9-10.3, L.9-10.3.a, W.9-10.5)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook, one
piece of your U01–U03 writing · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key § L6](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

**Readiness check (5 min):** In your notebook, write the same
message two ways: (1) asking an adult for an extra day on an
assignment, and (2) telling a friend the same thing. Read both to
the adult. What changed — and what stayed the same?

## Language changes with context

**Knowledge of language** (L.9-10.3) means knowing that language
functions differently in different contexts, and making effective
choices for meaning or style. The same facts wear different clothes
for different audiences, purposes, and occasions — linguists call
this **register**. A formal letter, a lab report, a story, and a
text to a friend are not better or worse versions of each other;
they are different tools. Style is choosing the right tool on
purpose, not accidentally.

A **style manual** is a book of agreed rules for a kind of writing
— how to punctuate, capitalize, cite sources, and format pages.
L.9-10.3.a asks writers to write and edit so their work conforms to
a style manual appropriate to the discipline and writing type (for
example, the *MLA Handbook* for literary writing). This unit only
previews that skill: U06 and U07 will require MLA conformity for
full essays. Here, the preview is three habits: consistent
punctuation (Lessons 01–03 rules), consistent citation of sources
(quoted words attributed, not dropped in), and consistent
formatting (one way of writing titles, dates, and quotations
throughout a piece).

## The three-pass edit

Good editing happens in passes, in this order — never all at once:

1. **Structure pass.** Does each sentence say one thing clearly?
   Are clauses joined correctly (Lesson 01)? Is any sentence a
   fragment or a run-on? Would splitting or combining a sentence
   help the reader follow the thought?
2. **Language pass.** Is the phrasing varied (Lesson 02)? Is the
   vocabulary precise (Lesson 04 — is *nice* hiding a more exact
   word)? Does the word choice create the intended tone (Lesson
   05)? Is any figure of speech accidental or misleading?
3. **Conventions pass.** Are semicolons and colons used by rule
   (Lesson 03)? Is spelling consistent? Are quotations introduced
   and punctuated correctly? Does the piece follow one style
   throughout?

Editing in this order matters: there is no point perfecting the
punctuation of a sentence you are about to delete.

## Worked example 1 — Editing a flawed paragraph

Flawed paragraph: *The harbor was busy, boats came and went all
morning. The keeper, he watched from the quay. He was tired, he had
worked all night, the boats they were late. It was a nice day
though.*

- Structure pass: The first sentence is a comma splice (*The harbor
  was busy, boats came and went*) — split it or use a semicolon.
  *The keeper, he watched* repeats the subject (a double subject) —
  delete *he*. *He was tired, he had worked all night, the boats
  they were late* is a run-on with another double subject (*the
  boats they*) — split into sentences.
- Language pass: *nice* is vague — the paragraph's evidence (busy
  harbor, long night) suggests *bright* or *clear* fits the morning;
  choose *bright* and keep it consistent with the quay scene.
  *though* dangles at the end; fold the contrast into a sentence
  (*Although he was tired, the bright morning kept him on the
  quay* is one option, using an adverbial clause from Lesson 01).
- Conventions pass: after the structure fixes, check each
  semicolon/colon by rule (none needed here once periods do the
  work — a semicolon is a choice, not a requirement).

Edited paragraph: *The harbor was busy; boats came and went all
morning. The keeper watched from the quay. He was tired, for he had
worked all night, and the boats were late. Although he was tired,
the bright morning kept him on the quay.* Wait — the last two
sentences repeat *tired*. A final language pass catches that: merge
them. Final version: *The harbor was busy; boats came and went all
morning. The keeper watched from the quay. Although he had worked
all night and the boats were late, the bright morning kept him on
the quay.* The worked reasoning above is deliberately shown
mid-edit, including a caught repetition — editing is re-reading,
not one clean pass.

## Worked example 2 — Same message, two registers

Message: a student will miss tomorrow's meeting and wants the notes.

- Formal (to an adult): *I am writing to let you know that I will
  be unable to attend tomorrow's meeting. I would be grateful if
  you could share the notes afterward, and I will complete any
  follow-up tasks promptly.*
- Informal (to a friend): *Hey — can't make tomorrow's meeting.
  Can you send me the notes after? I'll catch up on anything I
  miss.*

Same facts (absence, request for notes, promise to catch up). What
changed: sentence length and completeness (full clauses vs.
fragments acceptable in a note), vocabulary (*unable to attend* vs.
*can't make*), and politeness markers (*I would be grateful* vs. a
bare request). Neither version is "more correct" — each fits its
audience. A formal version sent to a friend would sound stiff; an
informal version sent to a supervisor would sound careless. Style is
fit, not fanciness.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Name the pass (structure, language, or conventions) that catches
   each flaw, and fix it: (a) *The archive was quiet, scholars
   worked in silence.* (b) *The report was nice.* (c) *The team
   finished early; and celebrated.*
2. Rewrite this sentence for a formal letter: *Can't come Friday,
   send me what I missed?*
3. A paragraph uses *however* three times in four sentences. Which
   pass catches that, and what is one fix?

## Independent practice

1. Edit this paragraph with the three passes, and list each change
   with its pass and reason: *The letter arrived late, it changed
   everything. The scholar, she read it twice. It was interesting
   though. She copied it before the archive closed, the copy was
   careful.*
2. Rewrite this message for two audiences (formal letter; note to
   a friend) without changing the facts: *We need to move our
   meeting from Tuesday to Thursday because the room is booked.*
3. A writer's draft mixes *theatre* and *theater* and dates written
   two ways (*Nov. 3* and *3 November*). Which pass catches this,
   and what is the fix, in style-manual terms?
4. Explain in three sentences why the structure pass comes before
   the conventions pass. Use the Lesson 06 reason, in your own words.

## Application

Run the three-pass edit on the U01–U03 piece you chose in Session
4. Mark every change with its pass (S, L, or C) in the margin and
write a half-page editor's note: the three most important changes
you made and why each one serves the piece's audience and purpose.
This draft becomes Step 3 of the Editor's Portfolio project.

## Exit check

On an index card: list the three editing passes in order, and write
one sentence explaining what a style manual is for.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the flawed paragraphs
  aloud; the editing reasoning is the work.
- **Alternate response mode:** edits may be dictated or marked on
  printed text; the editor's note may be spoken and scribed.
- **Extension:** take a published paragraph (adult-vetted, public
  domain or lawfully linked) and write a style-manual note for it:
  which of its punctuation, citation, and formatting choices would
  MLA-style editing keep, and which would it change? Do not
  reproduce the paragraph in your answer beyond short quotations.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often edit backwards — fixing commas before deciding
whether a sentence should exist. Hold the pass order firmly for the
whole unit; it is the unit's most transferable habit. Another common
error is treating formal register as "better English" rather than
"fitting English": correct it whenever it appears, using Worked
example 2. Watch for edits that change meaning while improving
style (especially *nice* → a specific word that the evidence does
not support); the language pass must serve the facts, not decorate
them. Answer key: § L6.
