# Answer Key — Unit 04: Syntax, Tone, Vocabulary, Conventions

**Teacher only. Never place this file beside a student question.**
Every lesson item, practice item, quiz question, assessment task,
and project criterion is solved or scored here. Open responses give
a sample response plus acceptable variation; the adult accepts any
response meeting the stated criteria.

## Lesson 01

**Guided:** 1. Relative clause: *that arrived at dawn*; it describes
*letter*. 2. Not a sentence — it is an adverbial clause fragment
with no independent clause. Repairs: *Although the road was closed,
the travelers waited.* / *The road was closed.* 3. Comma splice.
Repairs: *Mara kept the light. No ship came that night.* /
*Mara kept the light, but no ship came that night.* / *Mara kept
the light; no ship came that night.*

**Independent:** 1. *What the map showed* — noun clause, subject of
the main clause. Main independent clause: *What the map showed
surprised the travelers* (it contains the noun clause as its
subject). *because the road they trusted ended at the river* —
adverbial clause (why the travelers were surprised). *(that) they
trusted* — relative clause with omitted *that*, describing *road*.
Sentence type: **complex** (one independent clause plus dependent
clauses). 2. Independent: *The scholar copied it*. Relative:
*who found the letter* (describes *scholar*). Adverbial: *before
the archive closed* (when). Type: **complex**. 3. (a) Fragment
(phrase, no subject-verb pair): *She was running to catch the last
train.* (b) Run-on: *The wind dropped. The harbor grew still.*
(period, comma + *and*, or semicolon all correct). (c) Sentence —
OK (adverbial clause + independent clause). 4. Sample
compound-complex: *When the tide turned, the keeper lit the lamps,
and the boats came home.* Labels: adverbial *When the tide turned*;
independent *the keeper lit the lamps*; independent *the boats came
home*. Accept any learner sentence with two or more independent
clauses plus at least one dependent clause, correctly labeled.

**Application:** Accept when every clause in the paragraph is
labeled, the rewrite contains one simple, one compound, one
complex, and one compound-complex sentence, and the meaning is
unchanged. **Exit check:** A dependent clause has a subject and a
verb but cannot stand alone; examples: noun — *what the map
showed*; relative — *that lined the quay*; adverbial — *when the
tide turned*.

## Lesson 02

**Guided:** 1. (a) Prepositional phrase. (b) Participial phrase.
(c) Absolute phrase (*the tide turning*). 2. Dangling: the boat did
not wait on the quay. Fixes: *Waiting on the quay, she finally saw
the boat appear.* / *While she waited on the quay, the boat finally
appeared.* 3. Sample: *Opening the letter, the scholar, her hands
shaking, read in the silent archive.* (Participial *Opening the
letter*; absolute *her hands shaking*; prepositional *in the
silent archive*.) Accept any combination with two or more phrase
types and no dangling modifier.

**Independent:** 1. Sample: *The travelers, exhausted by the
journey, reached the river, the sun setting behind them.*
(Participial: *exhausted by the journey*; absolute: *the sun
setting behind them*.) 2. Sample: *At dawn* (prepositional,
adverbial), *the musicians, tuning their instruments* (participial),
*began to play, the square filling with children* (absolute):
*At dawn, the musicians, tuning their instruments, began to play,
the square filling with children. The morning, a rush of music and
voices* (noun phrase in apposition), *grew busy.* Accept any rewrite
with three or more labeled phrase types and unchanged facts.
3. (a) Prepositional phrase (used adverbially). (b) Absolute phrase.
(c) Participial phrase. 4. Dangling: the book did not read. Fixes:
*Reading by lamplight, she saw the book's pages glow.* / *As she
read by lamplight, the book's pages glowed.*

**Application:** Accept when every sentence uses a different phrase
type as its main detail-carrier, each phrase is underlined and
correctly labeled, and meaning is unchanged. **Exit check:** Sample:
participial — *Carrying a lantern, the keeper crossed the quay.*;
absolute — *The tide turning, the boats rocked gently.*

## Lesson 03

**Guided:** 1. *The evidence was thin; however, the argument still
convinced the room.* (Semicolon before *however*, comma after.)
2. *Bring three things: a notebook, a pen, and patience.*
3. Error: semicolon before a dependent clause — *before the archive
closed* cannot stand alone. Fixes: *The scholar copied the letter
before the archive closed.* / *The scholar copied the letter; the
archive was about to close.*

**Independent:** 1. (a) *The wind dropped; the gulls settled.*
(b) *The first translation was literal; the second caught the tone.*
(c) *The town needed water; however, the council argued for a month.*
(Plain semicolons without adverbs are also correct in (a) and (b).)
2. (a) Colon: *She named her reasons: cost, time, and safety.*
(The before-colon part is a complete clause.) (b) NO COLON — *The
reasons were* is not a complete clause (it needs its complement);
correct: *The reasons were cost, time, and safety.* (c) Colon:
*He quoted the captain: "Hold fast."* (Colon introduces a
quotation after a complete clause.) 3. (a) Wrong — never a
semicolon with *and*. Fix: *The harbor lay still, and the boats
rocked gently.* or *The harbor lay still; the boats rocked gently.*
(b) OK — two related independent clauses joined by a semicolon.
(c) Comma splice. Fix: period, comma + *and*, or semicolon.
4. Sample: *The expedition packed three things: food, water, and
tools. The route was long; nevertheless, the team left before dawn.*
Margin notes: colon introduces the list after a complete clause;
semicolon + *nevertheless* links two closely related independent
clauses with a contrast adverb.

**Application:** Accept when one period is replaced by a justified
semicolon (both sides independent, relationship named) and one list
or explanation gains a justified colon (before-colon part complete).
**Exit check:** Semicolon rule — links closely related independent
clauses, with a conjunctive adverb taking semicolon-before/comma-
after. Colon rule — follows a complete clause and introduces a
list, quotation, or explanation. Each with a correct example.

## Lesson 04

**Guided:** 1. *Diligent* = working with steady, careful effort.
Clue type: example — repainting every piling through the whole
winter exemplifies sustained careful work. (Dictionary check:
*diligent* — "having or showing steady, earnest, and energetic
effort.") 2. *Composition* = the act of putting together, or a work
so composed (e.g., a piece of music or writing); *composer* = a
person who composes. Verify both in a dictionary before banking
them. 3. The guess is half right and must be corrected:
*ambiguous* means open to more than one interpretation — neutral,
not necessarily bad. The sentence's evidence (some readers saw hope,
others saw loss) shows two readings, not a defect. Bank entry:
meaning "open to more than one interpretation"; clue: the colon's
explanation (two different readings); verification: substituting
"open to more than one interpretation" keeps the sentence true.

**Independent:** 1. (a) *Coherent* = logical and consistent,
forming a unified whole. Clue: definition/explanation after the
colon (each step followed the last without gaps). Verified by
dictionary; substitution keeps the sentence true. (b) *Reluctant* =
unwilling, hesitant. Clue: cause/effect — he asked for a day to
think instead of signing at once. (c) *Substantiate* = provide
evidence to support or prove. Clue: example — letters, dates, and
receipts are the supporting evidence. 2. *Sustain* family:
adjective *sustainable* ("able to be sustained"); noun
*sustenance* ("the means of sustaining; nourishment") — and, for
the quality of being sustainable, *sustainability*. Sample
sentences: *The crew carried sustenance for a week at sea.* /
*The village chose a sustainable fishing limit.* / *Sustainability
was the council's stated goal.* Verify forms in a dictionary.
3. *Blunt* adds harshness and lack of tact that *candid* does not
carry: *candid* means truthful and straightforward, which can be
kind; *blunt* suggests the truth was delivered without care for
feelings. Substituting *blunt* therefore changes the claim about
how the truth was told. 4. Sample: *It was evident from the empty
quay that the boats had left.* Verification by substitution:
*It was clearly seen/understood from the empty quay…* — the
sentence still makes sense. Accept any correct use with the
substitution shown.

**Application:** Accept when the paragraph uses three bank words
correctly and each carries a recoverable context clue (definition,
contrast, example, or cause/effect), and the adult can recover each
meaning from the clues alone. **Exit check:** The four moves in
order: context clues, word-change patterns, reference materials,
verification. Verification is not optional because a plausible guess
can still be wrong (see *candid* in Worked example 2); only the
substitution test proves the meaning fits the sentence.

## Lesson 05

**Guided:** 1. Figure: euphemism. Plain term: *fired/dismissed*.
The substitute *let go* softens the employer's action; it gains
distance and politeness for the speaker (the employer), while hiding
who acted and why. 2. Figure: oxymoron (*bitter sweet*). The
contradiction expresses mixed feeling: pride at leaving joined to
sorrow at going — one feeling cannot be stated without the other.
3. Version B is warmer. Two choices (any two of): *unhurried*,
*glassy*, *patient*, *glowed* — each quoted with the warmth it adds
(calm affection rather than flat report).

**Independent:** 1. Sample: euphemism — *The school will release
students early today.* Plain fact — *The school will close at noon
because of the storm.* Gain: the euphemism sounds routine and
reassuring, softening disruption for parents. Accept any pair where
the plain fact is named and the speaker's gain is stated. 2. Sample:
*It was a joyful sorrow to watch her go.* Tension: joy at her
opportunity joined to sorrow at her absence. Accept any oxymoron
whose contradiction names a real tension, with the tension
explained. 3. Sample (hurried, anxious tone): *Rushing across the
choppy water, the boat lurched toward the quay while the gulls
screeched overhead.* (Participial phrase: *Rushing across the
choppy water*; negative-connotation word: *lurched* or
*screeched*.) Accept any two sentences meeting the constraints
with an anxious tone. 4. (a) Tone: respectful, formal, measured.
Evidence: *careful study* (or *released*). (b) Tone: dismissive,
contemptuous. Evidence: *foot-dragging* (or *dumped*).

**Application:** Accept when the two rewrites keep the facts but
differ unmistakably in tone, and the three-sentence explanation
quotes the writer's own word choices/syntax for each tone.
**Exit check:** Euphemism — a mild/indirect substitute for a harsh
term (example: *passed away* for *died*). Oxymoron — contradictory
terms joined to express tension (example: *deafening silence*).

## Lesson 06

**Guided:** 1. (a) Structure pass — comma splice. Fix: *The archive
was quiet; scholars worked in silence.* (or period / comma + *and*).
(b) Language pass — *nice* is vague. Fix: a precise word the
evidence supports (e.g., *thorough* for a report praised for
detail — the adult checks the evidence fits). (c) Conventions pass
— semicolon with *and*. Fix: *The team finished early and
celebrated.* or *The team finished early; they celebrated.*
2. Sample formal version: *I am unable to come on Friday. Could you
please send me what I missed?* Accept any complete-sentence,
polite version with the facts intact. 3. Language pass (variety):
varying connectors is a word-choice/variety fix. One fix: replace
two of the three *however*s with *moreover*/*therefore* where the
relationship fits, or restructure a sentence so no connector is
needed.

**Independent:** 1. Sample edited version with change list:
*The letter arrived late; it changed everything. The scholar read
it twice. The letter was remarkable, so she copied it carefully
before the archive closed.* Changes: (S) comma splice split with a
semicolon — two independent clauses; (S) double subject removed
(*The scholar, she* → *The scholar*); (L) vague *interesting*
replaced with *remarkable*, supported by "changed everything";
(S/C) second comma splice repaired by joining with *so* plus a
comma (two independent clauses, cause/result). Accept any edit
removing all four flaws with each change labeled by pass and reason.
2. Sample formal: *We need to move our meeting from Tuesday to
Thursday because the room is booked for Tuesday. Please let me know
whether Thursday works for you.* Sample informal: *Hey — can we
move our meeting to Thursday? The room's booked Tuesday.* Facts
unchanged in both. 3. Conventions pass: a style manual requires
consistency — pick one spelling (*theater* in U.S. usage) and one
date format and use them throughout; mixed forms are corrected to
the chosen house style. (Also note: this is not a structure or
language error, so it must not be "fixed" by rewriting sentences.)
4. Accept when the explanation states the Lesson 06 reason: fixing
conventions first wastes work on sentences that may be cut or
rewritten, and structure changes can introduce new convention
errors; conventions are therefore checked last, on the final
wording.

**Application:** Accept when the U01–U03 piece carries S/L/C margin
marks for its changes and the half-page editor's note names the
three most important changes with audience/purpose reasons.
**Exit check:** Passes in order: structure, language, conventions.
A style manual is an agreed set of rules (punctuation, citation,
formatting) for a kind of writing, used so a piece is consistent.

## Practice A

1. Adverbial: *When the meeting ended*. Independent: *the chair
   announced what the committee had decided* (containing the noun
   clause). Noun clause: *what the committee had decided* (object of
   *announced*). Type: complex. 2. Independent: *The book was
   written by an author*. Relative: *that won the prize* (describes
   *book*). Relative: *who had never left her village* (describes
   *author*). Type: complex. 3. (a) Compound (two independent
   clauses joined by comma + *and*). (b) Complex (adverbial +
   independent). (c) Compound-complex (two independent clauses plus
   adverbial *while the rain fell*). 4. (a) Fragment (dependent
   clause alone): *Before the library opened, the line had already
   formed.* or *The library opened.* (b) Run-on: *The dog barked
   all night. The neighbors complained.* (period, semicolon, or
   comma + *and*). (c) OK — one independent clause with a compound
   verb. (d) OK — independent clause with a phrase (*tired and
   hungry*) describing the travelers. 5. Sample: *When the tide
   turned, the keeper lit the lamps, and the boats came home.*
   (Adverbial + two independent clauses = compound-complex.)
   6. *Carrying a lantern* has no subject-verb pair of its own, so
   it cannot be a clause; it is a participial phrase describing
   *keeper*. The clause test is subject plus finite verb — *carrying*
   is a participle, not a finite verb here.

## Practice C

1. (a) *The first draft was messy; however, the idea was strong.*
   (b) *The map was old; the route was still clear.* (c) *The
   committee disagreed; however, the report was published anyway.*
   (Plain semicolons without adverbs are also correct.)
2. (a) NO COLON — *The toolbox held* is not a complete clause
   (it needs its object); the list *is* the object. Correct: *The
   toolbox held a hammer, a saw, and a level.* (b) NO COLON —
   what follows is a second independent clause stating a separate
   fact, not a list, quotation, or explanation promised by a complete
   first clause; use a semicolon or period: *The toolbox held the
   tools; the tools were old.* (c) Colon: *Her advice was simple:
   "Begin."* (Colon introduces a quotation after a complete clause.)
3. (a) OK — two related independent clauses joined by a semicolon.
   (b) Comma splice — fix with a period, comma + *and*, or
   semicolon. (c) Wrong — semicolon with *and*. Fix: *The storm
   passed, and the harbor lay still.* or *The storm passed; the
   harbor lay still.* (d) OK — semicolons separate complex-list
   items that already contain commas. 4. Sample: *The class read
   three things: poems, essays, and plays. The poems were hardest;
   therefore, the teacher gave extra time.* Margin notes: colon
   introduces the list after a complete clause; semicolon +
   *therefore* links related independent clauses (cause/result).
5. Error: semicolon before a dependent clause — *because the tide
   had turned* cannot stand alone. Fixes: *The keeper lit the lamps
   because the tide had turned.* (comma not needed; the dependent
   clause follows) / *The keeper lit the lamps; the tide had
   turned.*

## Practice D

Bank entries: the adult checks each of the 12 words for (a) source
sentence, (b) context guess + clue type, (c) pattern/reference note,
(d) verified meaning, (e) a correct new sentence. Verified meanings
(minimum acceptable): *candid* — truthful and straightforward,
frank; *diligent* — showing steady, careful effort; *ambiguous* —
open to more than one interpretation; *coherent* — logical and
consistent, forming a unified whole; *reluctant* — unwilling,
hesitant; *substantiate* — provide evidence to support or prove;
*meticulous* — showing great attention to detail, very careful and
precise; *sustain* — keep going, support, uphold over time;
*precise* — exact, accurate in detail; *interpret* — explain the
meaning of; *evident* — clearly seen or understood; *compose* — put
together, create (as a work). 1. *Interpret* family: noun
*interpretation*; adjective *interpretive* (also *interpretative*).
Sample: *Her interpretation of the poem differed from mine.* /
*The guide gave an interpretive note on the translation.*
2. *Precise* family: noun *precision*; adverb *precisely*. Sample:
*The map's precision impressed the surveyor.* / *She measured the
rope precisely.* 3. Correction: *diligent* does not mean slow; the
winter-long repainting is evidence of sustained careful effort, not
slowness. Reference: "steady, earnest, energetic effort."
Substitution: *Steady and careful through the whole winter, the
keeper repainted every piling* — true; *Slow through the whole
winter…* changes the claim and is not what the sentence supports.
4. Accept any three sentences meeting the criterion: each word used
correctly and its meaning recoverable from that sentence's context
alone (the adult tests this by covering the word and guessing from
the clue).

## Practice E

1. (a) *The room was quiet.* — flat, neutral, report-like tone
   (plain statement, no connotative words). *The room held its
   breath.* — tense, expectant tone (personification: *held its
   breath*). (b) *The manager said jobs would be cut.* — direct,
   blunt tone (*jobs would be cut*). *The manager said the company
   would "streamline its workforce."* — managerial, evasive tone
   (euphemism *streamline its workforce* softens layoffs).
2. Sample: euphemism — *The school will release students early due
   to weather.* Plain fact — *The school will close at noon because
   of the storm.* Gain: sounds routine and reassuring; softens
   disruption for families. 3. Sample: *Their parting was a sweet
   sorrow.* Tension: happiness for the opportunity joined to grief
   at separation. Accept any oxymoron expressing a real tension with
   the tension explained. 4. Sample (lonely tone): *The boat, which
   no one waited for, drifted toward the empty quay.* (Relative
   clause: *which no one waited for*; negative-connotation word:
   *empty* or *drifted*.) Accept any two sentences meeting the
   constraints with a lonely tone. 5. Flatness is itself a tone:
   short plain sentences with no modifiers convey detachment or
   indifference — the speaker reports facts without feeling. "No
   tone" confuses "no strong feeling" with "no attitude"; Version
   A's attitude is businesslike distance, created by its syntax
   (short simple sentences) and plain word choice.

with the right rule named):** 1. Noun clause: *What the travelers
found* (subject). Independent: *What the travelers found surprised
them*. Adverbial: *because the inn they trusted had closed for the
winter*. Relative: *(that) they trusted* (describes *inn*). Type:
complex. 2. Accept any compound-complex sentence (two or more
independent clauses plus at least one dependent clause) with all
clauses correctly labeled; sample: *While the moon rose, the

wax*; absolute: *the messenger waiting outside*.) Accept any
expansion with both types correctly labeled. 4. Dangling: the road
did not read the map. Fixes: *Reading the map, she thought the road
seemed longer than expected.* / *As she read the map, the road
seemed longer than expected.* 5. Sample: *The first essay was
short; the second was thorough. However, the class preferred the
first.* Margin notes: semicolon links two related independent
clauses; *However* opens a new sentence (period before it, comma
after it) because it comments on the previous sentence rather than
joining two clauses. (The three-clause semicolon chain — *…short;
the second was thorough; however, the class preferred the first* —
is also correct.) 6. Colon: *The exhibit showed three things: maps,
letters, and photographs.* (Before-colon part is a complete clause;
colon introduces the list.) 7. (a) OK — semicolon between related
independent clauses. (b) Comma splice — fix with a period, comma +
*and*, or semicolon. (c) Wrong — semicolon before a dependent
clause; fix: *The team practiced daily because the recital was
near.* 8. *Ambiguous* = open to more than one interpretation. Clue:
explanation after the colon (both sides claimed it favored them —
two readings). Verification: substitute "open to more than one
interpretation" — the sentence stays true; then confirm in a
dictionary. 9. None fits: *diligent* names sustained careful effort
and persistence, while *tired, weary, exhausted* name fatigue.
Substitution fails both ways: a diligent worker need not be tired,
and a tired person need not be diligent. The thesaurus lists words
near in feeling, not equal in meaning. 10. Figure: euphemism —
*"strategic withdrawal"* softens retreat after defeat; it gains
the speaker (the general) a dignified framing and hides loss.
Tone of the second sentence: controlled, formal, composed.
Evidence: *steady* (or *flanked by aides*).

**Part B:** Sample clean revision: *The harbor woke early;
fishermen loaded crates all morning. The captain checked the ropes
twice. Although the day was bright, the boats left one by one, and
the quay grew quiet.* Changes: (S/C) comma splice repaired with a
semicolon; (S) double subject removed (*The captain, he* → *The
captain*); (L) vague *fine* replaced with *bright* (fits a morning
harbor scene; *clear* or *calm* also acceptable if consistent with
the scene); (S) comma splice and double subject repaired (*the quay
they grew quiet* → *the quay grew quiet*, joined with *and*).
Sample opposite-tone version (uneasy, 80–120 words): *The harbor
jerked awake before dawn; fishermen hauled crates in a frantic,
clattering rush. The captain, his hands shaking, checked the ropes
again and again. Although the sky was a hard, flat gray, the boats
slipped away one by one, and the quay sank into an uneasy quiet.*
Tone explanation (sample, 3 sentences): "In the calm version I used
*bright* and the steady clause *the boats left one by one* to sound
unhurried; in the uneasy version *jerked*, *frantic*, and *his hands
shaking* make the same morning feel rushed and afraid; the facts —
early harbor, crates, ropes checked, boats leaving, quay quiet —
did not change, only the words and syntax carrying them." Score
with the Part B rubric in the assessment file (20 points).

with a rule named per mark — unmarked "fixed" text does not count.
The two-tone passage passes when the adult can name each tone from
the words alone; if the adult must be told, the tone criterion is
at most Developing. The rationale passes when each of the three
changes is tied to audience/purpose (not merely "I fixed grammar").

tone-and-style criterion's rationale half; a missing rationale caps
that criterion at Developing.
