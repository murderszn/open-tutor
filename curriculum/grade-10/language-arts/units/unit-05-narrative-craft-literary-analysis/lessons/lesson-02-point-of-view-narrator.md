# Lesson 02 — Point of View, Narrator, and Multiple Plot Lines

**Goal (student-readable):** I can choose a point of view and a narrator
on purpose, explain what each choice lets the reader know (and hides),
and braid two plot lines so the cuts between them build tension.
(W.9-10.3.a, W.9-10.3.b, RL.9-10.6)

· **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

## Point of view, in plain words

**Point of view** is the position the story is told from — whose eyes and
mind the reader is allowed inside. The main choices:

- **First person ("I")** — the narrator is a character in the story. The
  reader knows what that character knows and feels, and nothing else.
  Good for intimacy and for limited knowledge: the reader discovers
  things when the narrator does.
- **Third-person limited ("she/he")** — the narrator stands outside but
  stays close to *one* character's thoughts and feelings. The reader
  knows what that character knows, plus what the narrator chooses to
  show. This is the anchor story's choice: the narrator stays close to
  Lian, and Sef's inner life arrives only through what he says and does.
- **Third-person omniscient ("she/he," knowing everything)** — the
  narrator can enter any mind and report anything. Powerful, but it
  removes mystery: if the reader already knows what Sef feels, his
  silence at the end means less.

A **retrospective narrator** tells the story from a later time, looking
back — the narrator already knows how it ended. The anchor story is not
retrospective: it stays in the present of each scene, so the reader
shares Lian's uncertainty about whether Sef will ever fly.

**Multiple plot lines** are two (or more) story threads running at once —
here, Lian's kite-building and Sef's remembering. A writer **braids**
them by cutting between threads at chosen moments. A good cut does two
things: it leaves one thread at a moment of tension, and it answers a
question in the other thread — or raises a new one.

## Worked example 1 — The same moment in two points of view

The workshop scene, as the story tells it (third-person limited, close
to Lian):

> "Will you fly the heron on festival day?"
>
> "No."
>
> "Why not? It's the best kite you ever made. Everyone says so."
>
> "Everyone says a lot of things." He wiped his hands on his apron.
> "Your grandmother flew it. I made it for her hands, not mine. Some
> things you keep by leaving them alone."

What this POV *shows*: Lian's questions, her frustration ("That's not
keeping. That's hiding."), and Sef's words and gestures. What it
*hides*: why Sef really refuses. The reader must infer his grief from
"for her hands, not mine" — and the inference is the point. If the story
were told from Sef's point of view, the reader would know his grief
directly, and Lian's question would carry no mystery.

Now the same exchange as first-person Lian might tell it:

> "I asked him if he'd fly the heron, and he said no, just like that,
> and I could feel myself getting angry in a way that embarrassed me
> even while it happened."

The facts are the same. What changes is *access*: first-person Lian can
report her embarrassment directly ("in a way that embarrassed me"), but
she cannot report Sef wiping his hands unless she saw it — and she
cannot know what his silence means. Third-person limited gives the writer
one freedom first person does not: the narrator can notice things the
character misses. The story uses that freedom in the final scene, when
the narrator reports that "the crowd had gone quiet in the particular
way crowds go quiet when they are watching something true" — a judgment
Lian, inside the moment, could not make.

**The rule:** choose the POV that gives the reader exactly the knowledge
the story needs — no more. Mystery is not missing information; it is
*chosen* missing information.

## Worked example 2 — Braiding two plot lines

The anchor story braids two threads:

- **Thread A (present):** Lian builds her kite, flies it, loses it.
- **Thread B (memory):** Sef remembers Ana and the heron kite.

The braid has three cuts:

| Cut | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hilltop opening → "Three days earlier, the workshop…" | Opens in the middle of the action (in medias res), then rewinds to show how Lian got there. The reader already knows the kite flies, so the workshop scenes carry a question: *how does this end?* |
| 2 | Workshop → "Over the next three days she built the kite the slow way…" (summary) | Compresses three days of building into one paragraph, then returns to scene for the stories about Ana. The cut says: the building matters less than what Sef *tells* her while she builds. |
| 3 | Workshop memories → "On festival morning the hill was crowded…" | Returns to the hilltop at the exact moment the reader left it. The memory thread has now answered the opening's question (why Sef won't fly) — and the story can spend its ending on the new question (will he?). |

Notice what the braid *never* does: it never cuts in the middle of a
scene to show the other thread doing something unrelated. Each cut lands
at a pause — a question asked, a task finished, a morning begun. A cut
that interrupts a scene mid-sentence is a cliffhanger; a cut that lands
at a pause is a braid.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Rewrite the string-snap moment ("Then a gust came off the sea…") in
   *first-person Sef* — three to four sentences. Then answer: what does
   your version reveal that the story's version hides, and what does it
   hide that the story's version reveals?
2. Find one place in the story where the narrator reports something Lian
   could not know herself. Quote it and explain how you can tell it is
   the narrator's knowledge, not Lian's.
3. **Braid plan.** For your own story seed (Lesson 01), name two plot
   threads and mark two cut points: where you will leave thread A, and
   what question the cut leaves open.

*(Hint: if both of your threads are about the same character doing the
same thing, they are one thread. A second thread needs its own question
— Sef's thread asks "will he fly again?" while Lian's asks "will she
win?")*

## Independent practice

1. **POV rewrite.** Take the final scene (from "Here," Sef said" to the
   end) and rewrite its first paragraph from *third-person limited, close
   to Sef* — what he notices, what he feels, what he decides. Keep it
   under 150 words. Then write two sentences: what did the POV change
   gain, and what did it lose?
2. **POV decision memo.** For your own story, write a short memo (about
   150 words): which point of view you will use, why it fits your
   story's central question, and one thing your reader will *not* know
   because of that choice. Name the trade-off honestly.
3. **Cut test.** Take your braid plan from guided practice and write the
   two sentences on either side of one cut — the last sentence before the
   cut and the first sentence after it. Check: does the cut land at a
   pause? Does it leave a question open?

## Application

Take a story or film you already know well. Name its point of view in
one sentence. Then describe, in two sentences, one scene that would
change completely if told from another character's point of view — and
say what would change.

## Exit check

On an index card: name the anchor story's point of view (first person,
third-person limited, or omniscient) and write one thing that point of
view hides from the reader, with a quoted line that shows the hiding.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the target passages aloud; the
  POV analysis is the work, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** POV rewrites and braid plans may be
  dictated or drawn as two parallel lines with cut marks; the memo may be
  recorded as audio and transcribed by the adult.
- **Extension:** rewrite one scene of the anchor story as a
  *retrospective* narrator ("Years later, Lian would understand…") and
  show how the later knowledge changes the scene's tension — for better
  and for worse.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Two errors recur. First, learners equate first person with honesty
("she's telling it herself, so it must be true"). Press the opposite:
a first-person narrator can misunderstand, forget, or lie — and the
reader's job is to notice the gap between what the narrator says and
what the story shows. Second, learners treat POV as a decoration ("I'll
use first person because it's more personal") rather than a knowledge
decision. The memo in independent practice forces the trade-off into
words: every POV *hides* something, and the writer should be able to
name what. If a learner cannot name what their POV hides, they have not
chosen — they have defaulted.
