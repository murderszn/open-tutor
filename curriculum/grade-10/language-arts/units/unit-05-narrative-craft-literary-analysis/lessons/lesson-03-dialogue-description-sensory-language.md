# Lesson 03 — Dialogue, Description, and Sensory Language

**Goal (student-readable):** I can write dialogue that reveals character,
choose telling details over flat description, and use precise sensory
language so the reader feels the scene instead of being told about it.
(W.9-10.3.b, W.9-10.3.d, RL.9-10.4)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
[texts/the-kite-makers-last-flight.md](../texts/the-kite-makers-last-flight.md)
· **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write down the last thing someone said to
you today that you still remember word for word. Under it, write *why*
you remember it — was it the words, the tone, what was left unsaid, or
what it revealed about the person? Share one answer with the adult.
Dialogue that sticks does work; today you will learn to tell working
dialogue from filler.

## Dialogue and description, in plain words

**Dialogue** is not a recording of how people talk. Real talk is full of
repetition, filler, and dead ends; story dialogue is talk with the dead
ends removed, so that every exchange does at least one of three jobs:

1. **Reveals character** — what a person says, and how they say it,
   shows who they are. Short sentences, long sentences, questions
   answered with questions: all of it is evidence.
2. **Advances the story** — the exchange changes something: a decision
   is made, a secret slips, a relationship shifts.
3. **Creates tension** — the two speakers want different things, so the
   conversation itself is a small conflict.

If a line of dialogue does none of these three, it is filler — cut it or
give it a job.

**Description** works the same way. A *telling detail* is a specific,
concrete image that does analytical work: it shows a character, sets a
tone, or prepares an event. "The workshop smelled of pine resin and cut
paper" tells you this is a working place with a history; "the workshop
was nice" tells you nothing. **Sensory language** is description that
reaches a specific sense — the burn of a string across a palm, the hum
of a kite line, the scrape of a knife — so the reader's body understands
the scene before the reader's mind analyzes it. Precise words matter:
"red" is a color; "the red diamond" is a kite; "the string burned a thin
red line across her palm" is a memory.

## Worked example 1 — Dialogue that reveals character

The workshop scene, flattened into filler dialogue:

> "You are gluing the kite too fast," Sef said.
> "No, I am not," Lian said. "I am being efficient."
> "You should slow down," Sef said. "It is important to do it right."

Every line states information and nothing else. Nobody's character shows;
the exchange could belong to any two people.

The story's version of the same exchange:

> "You're rushing the glue," he said without looking up.
>
> "I'm not rushing. I'm *efficient*."
>
> "Efficient is a word people use when they're rushing and want it to
> sound clever." He set down his brush. "A kite is a promise you make
> to the wind. You don't hurry a promise."

What the real version does that the flat version does not:

- **Lian's line reveals her:** she answers a criticism with a fancy
  word — she is defending herself with vocabulary, which is exactly what
  a clever sixteen-year-old does. The italics on *efficient* carry the
  tone: she knows it sounds clever and is daring him to challenge it.
- **Sef's line reveals him:** he does not argue about the glue. He
  answers with a proverb ("A kite is a promise you make to the wind"),
  which tells you he thinks in images and in rules learned over forty
  years — and that he teaches by saying something worth remembering,
  not by repeating the instruction.
- **The exchange advances the story:** Sef sets down his brush. The
  physical action marks a shift — he is no longer half-listening; the
  teaching has begun. And the line "You don't hurry a promise" becomes
  the story's hinge: Lian slows down, learns the knots, and the whole
  middle of the story follows from this conversation.

**The test:** cover the names. Can you still tell who is speaking? If
yes, the dialogue is doing character work. If no, it is filler wearing
quotation marks.

## Worked example 2 — The sensory ladder

A flat sentence climbs the ladder one rung at a time. Each rung adds a
sense or a precise word — and each rung changes what the reader feels.

| Rung | Sentence | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| 0 — flat | The kite flew. | A fact. No image, no feeling. |
| 1 — visual | The red kite flew high over the harbor. | Color and place. The reader can picture it. |
| 2 — sensory | The red diamond climbed, its ribbon tail snapping like a flag. | Sound and motion ("snapping"), a simile that gives the tail energy. |
| 3 — embodied | The red diamond climbed, its ribbon tail snapping like a flag, and the string burned a thin red line across Lian's palm. | Touch and consequence — the reader feels the string because Lian's body feels it. The kite is no longer a picture; it is an experience. |

The story's actual opening line works at rung 3: "The wind took Lian's
kite on the third try, and the whole hill seemed to lean back and watch
it go." — *took* (a verb with force), *third try* (a history of failure
in two words), *the whole hill seemed to lean back* (the landscape
itself reacting). None of those words is decorative. Each one does a job:
force, history, scale.

**The rule:** one precise sensory detail beats five adjectives. "Very
beautiful" is an opinion; "the string burned a thin red line across her
palm" is evidence the reader can feel.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Cover-the-names test.** Take the exchange beginning "Will you fly
   the heron on festival day?" Cover the names and read only the spoken
   lines. Can you tell who is speaking? Write two sentences explaining
   *how* you can tell — point to a specific word or rhythm, not just
   "the tone."
2. **Sensory ladder.** Take the flat sentence "Sef launched the kite."
   Climb it to rung 3 in three steps, writing each rung. Use at least
   one sense other than sight.
3. **What is left unsaid.** Sef never says "I miss Ana" or "I am afraid
   to fly." Find one line of his dialogue where the grief is present
   *without being stated*, quote it, and explain in two sentences what
   the line reveals by not saying it.

*(Hint for 3: look at what Sef talks about instead of his feelings —
the kite, the wind, the promise. The subject he chooses is the evidence.)*

## Independent practice

1. **Dialogue drill 1 — reveal.** Write a six-line exchange between two
   people who disagree about something small (which route to take, whose
   turn it is to cook). No narration, no "he said angrily" — the
   disagreement and the characters must show in the spoken words alone.
2. **Dialogue drill 2 — advance.** Write a four-line exchange in which
   one character changes another character's mind. The last line must
   show the change; do not narrate it afterward.
3. **Sensory ladder drill.** Take your own story seed from Lesson 01.
   Write one flat sentence about its most important moment, then climb
   it to rung 3. Label each rung and name the sense you added.
4. **Error analysis.** Read this flat paragraph and diagnose it: *"The
   festival was very exciting. The kites were very beautiful. Lian was
   very happy. Sef was very proud."* Name the two specific problems
   (hint: one is about adjectives, one is about telling vs. showing) and
   rewrite the paragraph in three sentences using one telling detail and
   one sensory detail.

## Application

Open a paragraph of your own earlier writing — your U01 interpretive
essay or your U02 comparative essay. Find one flat sentence and revise
it with one precise sensory detail and one sharper verb. Write the
before and after, and one sentence explaining what the revision gains.

## Exit check

On an index card: write one line of dialogue that reveals a character
trait, and label the trait it reveals. No narration allowed — the
dialogue must do the work alone.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the target passages aloud; the
  dialogue analysis is the work, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** dialogue drills may be performed aloud
  with the adult taking one role; sensory ladders may be built as spoken
  lists before writing.
- **Extension:** take the story's line "Efficient is a word people use
  when they're rushing and want it to sound clever" and analyze its
  *word choice* for tone (RL.9-10.4): what does "efficient" connote
  here, and how would the line change if Sef had said "fast" instead?
  Write a paragraph.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The most common error is treating dialogue as transcription: learners
write the way people actually talk, complete with "um," repetition, and
greetings, and then wonder why the scene feels slow. Teach the cut:
story dialogue is talk with the dead ends removed. The second error is
the adverb crutch — "she said angrily," "he said sadly" — which tells
the reader the emotion instead of letting the words carry it. If a
learner's dialogue needs an adverb to work, the words are not doing
their job; revise the words, not the adverb. Finally, watch for
adjective pile-up in description ("very beautiful, very bright, very
amazing"): one telling detail replaces five adjectives, and the ladder
in Worked example 2 is the corrective exercise.
