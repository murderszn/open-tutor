# Lesson 05 — Drafting and Revision: Three Passes and a Reflective Ending

**Goal (student-readable):** I can draft a complete story, then revise it
in three separate passes — structure, language, conventions — and write a
conclusion that follows from and reflects on what the story experienced.
(W.9-10.5, W.9-10.3.e, L.9-10.1.b, L.9-10.2.a)
**Time:** ~50 minutes, plus drafting studios in Sessions 12–13 ·
**Materials:** this page, notebook, your story plan from Lessons 01–04,
U04 editing tools · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key § L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Look at a paragraph you wrote earlier this
year — your U01 essay or your U02 comparative essay. Find one sentence
you would change now. Write the old sentence and the new sentence, and
one sentence explaining *why* the new one is better. That explanation is
revision thinking; today you will make it a system.

## Revision, in plain words

**Drafting** is getting the story down. A first draft is for finding out
what the story is — it is allowed to be messy, and it is supposed to be
longer than the final version. Do not edit while you draft; the part of
your brain that judges sentences will strangle the part that invents
events. Write the draft, then revise it in **three separate passes**,
in this order:

1. **Structure pass** — does the architecture hold? Ask: does each event
   cause the next (Lesson 01)? Does the want/need tension resolve
   (Lesson 01)? Is the POV consistent (Lesson 02)? Is the braid cut at
   pauses (Lesson 02)? Are scenes and summaries in the right places
   (Lesson 04)? Fix structure first, because a beautiful sentence in a
   broken structure is still broken.
2. **Language pass** — does the writing do its work? Ask: does the
   dialogue reveal character (Lesson 03)? Is there one precise sensory
   detail per scene (Lesson 03)? Are the sentences varied — short ones
   for impact, long ones for flow (U04, L.9-10.1.b)? Is there a semicolon
   joining two closely related ideas where a period would flatten them
   (U04, L.9-10.2.a)? Cut filler dialogue and adjective pile-ups.
3. **Conventions pass** — is the surface clean? Spelling, punctuation,
   quotation marks around dialogue, paragraph breaks at every speaker
   change, consistent tense. This pass comes last: there is no point
   proofreading a paragraph you are about to delete.

A **reflective conclusion** (W.9-10.3.e) is the story's final paragraph
doing the work of reflection: it follows from what happened and tells
the reader what the experience *meant* — without preaching. It is not a
moral ("the lesson is…") and it is not a summary ("and then they went
home"). It is the character's — or the narrator's — understanding,
earned by the events.

## Worked example 1 — The structure pass, demonstrated

A learner's draft paragraph, structure unchecked:

> Lian built her kite and she went to the festival and the wind was
> strong and her kite broke. Sef had a kite too. He flew it. Lian was
> happy.

The structure problems: the events only follow each other ("and… and…
and…"); Sef's kite appears without preparation; the ending ("Lian was
happy") is unearned because nothing *caused* the happiness. The
structure pass asks three questions and rewrites from the answers:

| Structure question | Answer | Revision |
|---|---|---|
| What causes the kite to break? | A gust from the sea — the same wind that lifted it. | "A gust came off the sea — a hard, sideways gust, the kind that finds every weakness in a kite at once." The wind is now a cause, not weather. |
| Why does Sef fly now? | Because Lian's loss forces the question his silence has avoided. | The launch follows the snap *because* the snap forces the question. Order is now causal, not chronological. |
| What does Lian understand at the end? | That flying is not winning. | The ending earns "she did not mind" by showing the understanding first: "She had come to the hill wanting a prize, and she was going home with something else: the weight of a string in her hand, her grandfather beside her, and a kite in the sky that was not hers and was hers now, both at once." |

After the structure pass, the paragraph is not prettier — it is
*truer*. The language pass comes next, and only then the conventions
pass. (Compare the revised shape with the story's actual ending
paragraphs; the architecture is the same.)

## Worked example 2 — The language pass, demonstrated

The same events, language unchecked:

> Lian was very sad when her kite broke. It was very disappointing.
> Sef was very kind. He flew the old kite and it was very beautiful.

Language problems: four "very"s, three telling adjectives ("sad,"
"disappointing," "kind," "beautiful"), zero sensory detail, zero
dialogue. The language pass replaces telling with evidence:

> Lian stood with the broken string in her fist, blinking. Fifty
> crowns, gone. The compass, gone. She had done the arithmetic so many
> times, and none of it had included this.

What changed: "very sad" became a *physical image* (broken string, fist,
blinking) and a *thought* in her voice ("Fifty crowns, gone. The compass,
gone.") — short sentences that sound like a mind doing arithmetic and
finding nothing. "Very kind" became an *action* (Sef kneeling, unfolding
the kite "the way you unfold a letter you have read a hundred times").
"Very beautiful" became a *sensory image* (the patched wing "bright
against the amber sky"). And one semicolon — none needed here; the short
sentences do the work, which is itself a language decision: fast, broken
rhythm for a breaking moment.

**The rule:** revise in order, and never let the conventions pass do the
structure pass's job. A perfectly punctuated wandering chain is still a
wandering chain.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Structure-pass drill.** Take the flat draft paragraph in Worked
   example 1 and apply the structure pass yourself: write the three
   questions (cause? preparation? earned ending?) and one revised
   sentence for each. Compare with the worked example afterward.
2. **Language-pass drill.** Take this paragraph and apply the language
   pass: *"The workshop was very messy. Sef was very busy. Lian was very
   excited to build her kite. The glue was very sticky."* Rewrite it in
   three sentences with one telling detail and one sensory detail, and
   cut every "very."
3. **Reflective-conclusion test.** Read the story's final paragraph
   ("She did not mind…"). Underline the sentence that *reflects* rather
   than reports. Then explain in two sentences why the paragraph is a
   reflection and not a moral: what does it refuse to say?

*(Hint for 3: a moral tells the reader what to think. A reflection
shows the character thinking. Look for the sentence that could only be
Lian's thought, not a rule for everyone.)*

## Independent practice

1. **Draft your story.** Using your plan from Lessons 01–04 (architecture,
   POV memo, braid plan, technique notes), write the full first draft of
   your short story: **1,500–2,500 words**. This begins now and continues
   in the drafting studios (Sessions 12–13). Do not stop to polish
   sentences; get the events down in order.
2. **Structure pass on your draft.** When the draft exists, answer the
   three structure questions in writing: (a) which event causes which?
   (b) where is your want/need tension resolved, and is it earned? (c)
   is your POV consistent? Write one revision per question.
3. **Reflective conclusion.** Draft your story's final paragraph (3–5
   sentences). It must follow from your events and reflect on what the
   experience meant — no moral, no summary. Label it "REFLECTIVE
   CONCLUSION — DRAFT" so the adult can check it separately.

## Application

Take a paragraph from your U02 comparative essay. Run the three passes
on it in order: write one structural change, one language change, and one
conventions change, each labeled. Which pass changed the paragraph most?
Write one sentence explaining why.

## Exit check

On an index card: name the one structural change and the one language
change you made (or plan to make) to your own draft, and one sentence
each on *why* the change improves the story.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the worked examples aloud; the
  revision work is the assessed skill.
- **Alternate response mode:** revision notes may be dictated; the draft
  may be handwritten or typed; the adult may scribe the draft while the
  learner composes aloud, if handwriting is the barrier rather than the
  thinking.
- **Extension:** run the language pass on the story's own opening
  paragraph ("The wind took Lian's kite on the third try…"). Is there
  anything you would change? Write the paragraph as you would revise it
  and defend one change in two sentences — or defend keeping it exactly
  as it is, with evidence. (There is no single right answer; the defense
  is the work.)

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The dominant error is revising in the wrong order: learners proofread
first, fall in love with their sentences, and then refuse to cut the
scenes those sentences live in. Enforce the order physically — structure
pass in one color, language pass in another, conventions last — and do
not accept a conventions pass until the structure pass is signed off.
The second error is the "moral" ending: "the lesson is that you should
never give up." A reflective conclusion earns its meaning from the
story's own events; if the sentence would be equally true attached to
any story, it is a platitude, not a reflection. The test in guided
practice 3 catches it: could only *this* character think this sentence
after *these* events?
