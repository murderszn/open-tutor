## Architecture, in plain words

A story is not a pile of interesting things that happen. It is a building,
and like a building it has architecture — parts that hold each other up.
Three parts do most of the work:

1. **Problem/situation** — the trouble the story opens with, or the
   situation that makes trouble inevitable. A story needs something at
   stake from the first page: a contest to win, a kite to fly, a question
   that must be answered. Without a problem, there is nothing for the
   events to *do*.
2. **Want vs. need** — what a character *wants* is the visible goal they
   can name: a prize, a victory, an escape. What a character *needs* is
   the change inside them that the story is really about: to let go of
   something, to understand someone, to stop hiding. The want drives the
   plot; the need gives the plot its meaning. A good story lets the
   character chase the want and discover the need along the way.
3. **Events that build** — each event should change something and cause
   the next event. If you can remove an event and nothing changes, it is
   decoration, not architecture. The test is a chain: *because* this
   happened, *therefore* that happened. A story that only lists events in
   order — this happened, then this happened, then this happened — is a
   diary, not a narrative.

## Worked example 1 — Want and need in "The Kite Maker's Last Flight"

Read the story, then map it. (Page references are to
[texts/the-kite-makers-last-flight.md](../texts/the-kite-makers-last-flight.md).)

| Character | Want (visible goal) | Need (inner change) | How the story shows it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lian | Win the festival grand prize — a brass compass and fifty crowns | To understand what flying is *for*, and to see her grandfather fly again | Want: "The grand prize was a brass compass and fifty crowns, and Lian had done the arithmetic in her head a hundred times: fifty crowns was a new set of spars, a roll of good paper, and a winter without mending other people's kites for pennies." Need: "a quieter want was forming, one she did not have a name for yet: she wanted to see the heron kite in the air again, just once, with her grandfather standing beside it." |
| Sef | To be left alone with his grief — to keep Ana's kite untouched | To fly the heron kite one last time and pass it on | Want: "Some things you keep by leaving them alone." Need: "Yours now," he said. "Fly it like she did." — he gives the string to Lian, which is the opposite of leaving it alone. |

Notice the pattern: Lian's want and need point in *different* directions.
The want says "win the prize"; the need says "fly with my grandfather."
The story's tension comes from that difference — and the ending resolves
it by letting the need win: Lian loses the prize and gains the flight.
If want and need were the same thing, there would be no story, only a
shopping list.

## Worked example 2 — A chain that builds vs. a chain that wanders

**The story's chain (each event causes the next):**

1. Lian wants the prize → she builds her kite fast and rushes the glue.
2. Sef corrects her ("You don't hurry a promise") → she slows down and
   learns the knots.
3. She asks about the heron kite → Sef refuses, and she stops asking —
   but the question stays open.
4. She listens to his stories about Ana → her need forms: she wants to
   see the heron fly.
5. Her red kite snaps in a gust → the prize is lost, and the open
   question is forced: what now?
6. Sef launches the heron kite and hands her the string → want and need
   resolve in one gesture.

Every step *causes* the next. Remove step 3 and step 6 has no meaning;
remove step 5 and there is no reason for Sef to act.

**A wandering chain (same setting, no architecture):**

1. Lian builds a kite. 2. She eats lunch. 3. A gull steals a sandwich.
4. She paints the kite red. 5. It rains. 6. She wins a prize.

Each event happens *after* the last one, but none *causes* the next.
Nothing is at stake, nothing changes inside anyone, and the prize at the
end is unearned because nothing led to it. "Then" is not "therefore."

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. In one sentence, state the story's problem/situation: what is at stake
   on the hilltop, and for whom?
2. Sef's want and need also point in different directions. Find one quoted
   line for his *want* (keeping the kite untouched) and one for his *need*
   (flying it and passing it on) — different from the lines in Worked
   example 1.
3. Pick one link in the story's chain (for example, step 4 → step 5) and
   explain in two sentences *how* the first event causes the second. Use
   the word "because."

*(Hint: if your explanation works with "and then" but not with
"because," you have found a decoration, not a link.)*

## Independent practice

1. **Story-seed sketch 1.** Invent a character, a visible want, and a
   different inner need. Write them as three lines: *Character / Want /
   Need*. Then write one sentence explaining how the want and the need
   point in different directions.
2. **Chain test.** Write a five-event chain for your story seed in which
   each event causes the next. Number the events and put the word
   *because* between each pair, as in "1 → 2 because…". Then write one
   wandering event that could be cut without changing anything, and say
   why it can be cut.
3. **Problem sentence.** Write the problem/situation of your story seed in
   a single sentence that names what is at stake and for whom.

## Application

Take a story or film you already know well. Name its main character's
want in one sentence and their need in another. Then name the one event
in the middle of the story without which the ending could not happen —
the load-bearing event — and explain why in two sentences.

## Exit check

On an index card: one sentence naming Lian's *want* with a quoted line,
and one sentence naming her *need* with a quoted line. Label each.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the story aloud while the
  learner follows; the architecture work (mapping, chaining) is the
  assessed skill, not the decoding.
- **Alternate response mode:** want/need maps and event chains may be
  dictated, drawn as boxes and arrows, or built as index-card chains on a
  table.
- **Extension:** map the want and need of a *secondary* character in a
  story you know (a parent, a rival, a friend) and show how their chain
  crosses the main character's chain at one event.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat "need" as a second, smaller want ("she wants the
prize *and* she wants her grandfather's approval"). Press the
difference: a want is a goal the character can name and chase; a need is
a change in the character that the story reveals through choices. Also
watch for "the character learns a lesson" as a substitute for a need —
a need must show up in what the character *does* (Sef hands over the
string), not only in what they realize. Finally, the chain test catches
the most common drafting error: events that merely follow each other.
If a learner's chain reads as "and then, and then," ask "what changed
because of that event?" until a cause appears or the event is cut.
