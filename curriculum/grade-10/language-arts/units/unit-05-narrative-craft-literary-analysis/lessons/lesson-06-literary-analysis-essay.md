# Lesson 06 — Literary Analysis Essays: From Craft Knowledge to Argument

**Goal (student-readable):** I can write a literary analysis essay that
argues — with a debatable thesis and handled textual evidence — how a
studied author's choices (complex characters, structure, time
manipulation) create specific effects, and I can present an excerpt aloud
with deliberate delivery choices. (RL.9-10.3, RL.9-10.5, RL.9-10.1,
W.9-10.9, SL.9-10.4, SL.9-10.6)
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook,
[texts/the-kite-makers-last-flight.md](../texts/the-kite-makers-last-flight.md)
· **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key § L6](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

**Readiness check (5 min):** Write one sentence that is *true* about
"The Kite Maker's Last Flight" but *boring* as an essay claim — for
example, "The story is about a girl who flies a kite." Then write one
sentence that is *arguable* — a claim a reasonable reader could disagree
with. Share both with the adult. The gap between them is the whole of
literary analysis.

## Analysis, in plain words

A **literary analysis essay** is an argument about how a text works. It
is not a summary, not a review ("I liked it"), and not a list of devices
("the author uses imagery, symbolism, and foreshadowing"). Its shape:

1. **Thesis** — one arguable sentence: *the author does X to create
   effect Y*. A thesis is arguable if a skeptic could disagree with it
   for a reason. "The story is about kites" is not arguable; "The story
   uses the heron kite to turn Sef's grief from something he hides into
   something he hands over" is — a skeptic could argue the kite is only
   a prop.
2. **Body paragraphs** — each makes *one* claim about a choice, supports
   it with **handled evidence** (a quotation introduced, quoted
   accurately, and explained — never dropped in alone), and explains the
   *effect*: what the choice does to the reader's understanding or
   feeling, and why it matters to the whole story.
3. **Conclusion** — follows from the analysis: what the choices add up
   to, and what the story means because of them. No new evidence, no
   moral.

Your five weeks of craft study are the essay's raw material. You already
know how want/need, POV, dialogue, pacing, and revision work — now you
turn that knowledge on a studied author's choices and argue about their
effects. The analysis essay is where reading becomes writing *about*
reading.

## Worked example 1 — Weak thesis vs. arguable thesis

Three thesis candidates about the anchor story, ranked:

| # | Thesis | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A | "The Kite Maker's Last Flight" is a story about a girl, her grandfather, and kites. | **Summary, not a thesis.** True, but no one could disagree, and it says nothing about *how* the story works. |
| B | "The story uses imagery and symbolism to create a vivid picture of the festival." | **A list wearing a thesis's clothes.** "Imagery and symbolism" names devices, not choices; "a vivid picture" names no effect on meaning. A skeptic has nothing to argue with because nothing specific is claimed. |
| C | "The story withholds Sef's grief until the hilltop climax, so that the heron kite's flight lands as a decision rather than a sentiment — and that delay is what makes Lian's loss of the prize feel like a gain rather than a defeat." | **Arguable.** It names a specific choice (withholding), a specific effect (decision, not sentiment), and a consequence for the ending. A skeptic could argue the delay is merely suspense, or that the ending works without it. That disagreement is exactly what the essay will adjudicate with evidence. |

**The test:** read your thesis to a skeptic (the adult plays one). If
the skeptic can only nod, the thesis is a summary. If the skeptic can
say "I disagree, because…" — and you can answer with quotations — the
thesis is arguable.

## Worked example 2 — A model analysis paragraph

Thesis C above, first body paragraph — claim, handled evidence, effect:

> The story delays Sef's grief by keeping the reader outside it. In the
> workshop scene, Sef answers Lian's question about the heron kite with
> a deflection — "Everyone says a lot of things" — and then with a
> statement that hides as much as it reveals: "Your grandmother flew it.
> I made it for her hands, not mine. Some things you keep by leaving
> them alone." The reader learns the kite's history but not Sef's
> feeling; the narration stays close to Lian, who hears a rule about
> keeping and misses the grief underneath it. The effect is a double
> one: the reader shares Lian's frustration *and* suspects, before Lian
> does, that the rule is a cover. When Sef finally launches the kite,
> the flight therefore reads as a choice he has been avoiding for three
> years — a decision — rather than a sentimental release. The delay is
> the mechanism: by withholding the grief, the story makes the reader
> feel its weight only when it is finally set down.

Notice the paragraph's shape: a claim sentence ("delays Sef's grief by
keeping the reader outside it"), a *handled* quotation (introduced with
context, quoted accurately, followed by analysis of what it hides),
a second piece of evidence (the narrator's closeness to Lian), and an
effect sentence that ties the paragraph back to the thesis ("the delay
is the mechanism"). No sentence merely retells the plot; every sentence
answers *how* and *to what effect*.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Thesis clinic.** Write two thesis candidates for your own analysis
   essay — one about the anchor story's structure/time, one about its
   characters. Test each with the skeptic test: write the skeptic's
   objection in one sentence, then write the quotation you would use to
   answer it. Keep the stronger thesis; rewrite the weaker one.
2. **Evidence handling.** Take this dropped quotation and handle it:
   *"Some kites are for keeping, she thought, and some are for letting
   go — and the trick, the whole trick, is knowing that they are the same
   kite."* Write three sentences: one introducing the quotation (what is
   happening), the quotation itself, and one explaining the effect it
   creates. Do not let the quotation stand alone.
3. **Effect naming.** Pick one choice from the story — the in-medias-res
   opening, the summary of the three days, or Sef's proverb about the
   promise. Write two sentences: what the choice is, and what effect it
   creates *on the reader* (not just "it makes it interesting").

*(Hint for 3: an effect is specific — "the reader feels the wait," "the
reader suspects before Lian does," "the reader re-reads the opening."
If your effect sentence works for any story, it is not an effect yet.)*

## Independent practice

1. **Essay plan.** Write a full plan for your analysis essay (800–1,000
   words): thesis (one sentence), three body claims (one sentence each,
   each naming a choice and an effect), and one handled quotation per
   claim. The essay may analyze the anchor story, or — with the adult's
   agreement — "The Ferryman's Toll" from Unit 01, applying the same
   craft tools to Hal's development and the story's structure.
2. **Author reading.** Choose a 150–200-word excerpt from the anchor
   story to read aloud. Mark it for delivery: where to pause, which
   words to stress, where to speed up or slow down (SL.9-10.4, SL.9-10.6).
   Rehearse it once with the adult, then read it aloud in formal English
   as the unit's closing presentation. The delivery choices are part of
   the work: pace, pause, and emphasis should match the passage's
   meaning, not decorate it.
3. **Counter-reading.** Write one paragraph (about 150 words) arguing
   *against* your own thesis — the strongest objection a skeptic could
   make, supported by one quotation. Then write two sentences: does the
   objection weaken your thesis, or does answering it strengthen it?
   (This is the seed of the counterargument work in Unit 06.)

## Application

Take the thesis from your U01 interpretive essay. Test it with the

sentence, naming a choice and an effect.

## Exit check

On an index card: your thesis sentence for the analysis essay, plus one
handled quotation with the effect it creates named in one sentence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the model paragraph aloud; the
  thesis and evidence work is the assessed skill.
- **Alternate response mode:** essay plans may be dictated or built as
  a claim/evidence/effect table; the author reading may be recorded
  rather than performed live, with the delivery marks submitted in
  writing.
- **Extension:** analyze the *same* choice (the heron kite) in two
  paragraphs — one arguing it is the story's central image, one arguing
  it is secondary to the dialogue — and decide which argument the
  evidence supports better. The comparison is the analysis.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

The signature error is the device list: "the author uses imagery,
symbolism, foreshadowing, and irony." A device is not an analysis until
it is tied to a choice *and* an effect. The thesis test in Worked
example 1 is the corrective — if the thesis could be written about any
story with the device names swapped, it is not yet an argument. The
second error is the dropped quotation: learners paste a line and move
on, assuming the quotation argues for itself. The handling drill in
guided practice 2 makes the rule physical — introduce, quote, explain —
and the adult should refuse to accept any quotation that stands alone.
Finally, watch for summary creep in body paragraphs: every sentence
that retells plot must be followed by a sentence that analyzes it, or
cut.
