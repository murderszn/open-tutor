## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. Problem/situation (acceptable variations): "On festival morning, Lian
   must fly her kite to win the grand prize — but her kite keeps failing
   and her grandfather, the best kite maker on the hill, refuses to fly
   the one kite that matters." Any one-sentence answer naming the stakes
   (prize + Sef's refusal) for Lian is acceptable.
2. Sef's want — e.g., "Some things you keep by leaving them alone."
   (keeping the kite untouched). Sef's need — e.g., "Yours now," he
   said. "Fly it like she did." (flying it and passing it on). Also
   acceptable for need: "Your grandmother flew it like it mattered and
   like it didn't, both at once. That's the whole secret, and it took me
   forty years to learn it."
3. Model link (step 4 → step 5): "Because Lian has listened to Sef's
   stories about Ana, she now understands what the heron kite means —
   so when her own kite snaps, the loss is not only about the prize; it
   forces the open question of whether Sef will fly at all. The snap
   matters *because* the listening happened first."

**Independent practice**

1. Model story seed: *Character: Nadia, 15, first chair of the school
   orchestra. Want: to win the regional solo competition. Need: to admit
   she is afraid of being heard — of playing loudly enough for anyone to
   judge her. How they differ: the want says "be the best"; the need
   says "be willing to be heard at all" — winning requires the very
   exposure she is avoiding.* Any seed with a visible want and a
   different inner need is acceptable; the difference must be stated.
2. Model chain: 1. Nadia auditions for the solo → 2. she wins the solo
   *because* the judges hear her control → 3. the solo is exposed and
   loud *because* she won it → 4. she practices quietly at home to
   avoid being heard *because* the solo frightens her → 5. her teacher
   hears her practicing quietly and asks why *because* the change in
   volume is audible. Decoration to cut: "Nadia buys new rosin" (a
   routine purchase nothing depends on).
3. Model problem sentence: "Nadia has won the solo she wanted, and now
   she must play it loudly enough for a hall full of strangers to judge
   her — the one thing she has spent a year avoiding."

**Lesson 01 misconception check:** if a learner's "need" is a second
goal ("she wants the prize *and* her grandfather's approval"), ask them
to show the need in a *choice* the character makes, not a feeling. Sef
handing over the string is the model.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice**

1. Model first-person Sef rewrite of the string-snap moment: *"The gust
   came off the sea and took her kite, and I watched the red diamond
   fold and drop. Fifty crowns, gone — I could see her doing the sums in
   her head. And I thought: she has lost the prize, and I am standing
   here holding the one kite that could put her back in the sky."*
   Gains: the reader sees Sef's decision forming (the moment he chooses
   to act). Loses: Lian's interior arithmetic ("Fifty crowns, gone. The
   compass, gone.") and her physical sensations — first-person Sef
   cannot report what Lian feels.
2. Narrator-knowledge line: "The crowd had gone quiet in the particular
   way crowds go quiet when they are watching something true." Lian,
   inside the moment with a string in her hand, could not judge the
   crowd's quiet that way; the judgment belongs to the narrator standing
   outside the scene.
3. Model braid plan: *Thread A — Nadia prepares the solo (want). Thread
   B — Nadia's mother, a former soloist, avoids the concert hall (need
   she will not name). Cut 1: after Nadia wins the solo, cut to her
   mother at home, not coming. Cut 2: after the mother refuses the
   invitation, cut back to Nadia practicing — the question left open is
   whether the mother will come after all.* Acceptable if the two threads
   have different questions and the cuts land at pauses.

**Independent practice**

1. Model third-person-limited-Sef rewrite (final scene, under 150 words):
   *"He knelt beside the heron kite and unfolded it the way you unfold
   a letter you have read a hundred times. Forty years of keeping it
   folded, and now his hands were doing the thing before his mind had
   agreed to it. He felt the old fear — that flying it would finish the
   letting go — and then, under it, something steadier: Ana would have
   laughed at a kite kept in a drawer. He stood, lifted it into the
   wind, and put the string in Lian's hand."* Gains: Sef's decision
   process is visible. Loses: Lian's reflection at the end, which only
   her closeness can carry.
2. Model POV memo: *"I will use third-person limited, close to Nadia,
   because the story's question is whether she will let herself be
   heard — and the reader should discover that answer when she does.
   What the reader will not know: what her mother feels about the
   competition. The trade-off is that the mother's absence must be shown
   through Nadia's observations, never explained directly."* Acceptable
   if the POV is named, the fit is tied to the story's question, and one
   hidden thing is named honestly.
3. Model cut test: *Last sentence before the cut: "Nadia put the violin
   back in its case and did not practice that night." First sentence
   after the cut: "Her mother stood at the kitchen window, watching the
   street, holding the old recital program."* The cut lands at a pause
   (practice finished) and leaves a question open (why is the mother
   holding the program?).

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. Cover-the-names test on the heron exchange: Lian speaks in questions
   ("Will you fly the heron on festival day?", "Why not?") and appeals
   to reputation ("Everyone says so") — the voice of someone arguing
   from what others think. Sef speaks in short deflections ("No.",
   "Everyone says a lot of things.") and proverbs ("A kite is a promise
   you make to the wind.") — the voice of someone who has already
   decided and will not be argued with. Acceptable if the learner points
   to specific words or rhythms, not just "the tone."
2. Model sensory ladder for "Sef launched the kite": Rung 1 (visual) —
   "Sef lifted the blue heron kite into the wind." Rung 2 (sound/motion)
   — "Sef lifted the blue heron kite into the wind, and it caught with
   a soft snap of patched cloth." Rung 3 (embodied) — "Sef lifted the
   blue heron kite into the wind, and the string hummed against his palm
   as the patched wing caught the sun." Senses added: sound (rung 2),
   touch (rung 3).
3. Unsaid grief: "I made it for her hands, not mine." Sef never says "I
   miss Ana" or "I am afraid to fly." Instead he talks about *whose
   hands* the kite was made for — the grief is present in the
   possessive ("her hands") and in the refusal ("not mine"), without
   ever being named. Acceptable alternatives: "Some things you keep by
   leaving them alone" (the rule is a cover for the grief).

**Independent practice**

1. Model dialogue drill 1 (reveal, no narration): *"You're taking the
   long way again." / "The short way has the hill." / "The hill is the
   point. You're supposed to climb it." / "I'm supposed to get there
   before dark." / "Then climb faster." / "Then carry my bag."* The
   speakers' traits show in the words: one is dutiful and teasing, the
   other is tired and bargaining. Any six-line exchange passing the
   cover-the-names test is acceptable.
2. Model dialogue drill 2 (advance, change of mind): *"'I'm not going.'
   / 'You already said you'd go.' / 'I said I'd think about it.' /
   'Then think faster — the bus leaves in ten minutes, and your sister
   is already on it.'"* The last line shows the change: the first
   speaker's refusal collapses into a practical question (the bus), not
   a stated decision. Acceptable if the last line shows the change
   without narrating it.
3. Model sensory ladder (own seed, Nadia): Rung 0 — "Nadia played the
   violin." Rung 1 — "Nadia played the violin in the empty hall."
   Rung 2 — "Nadia played the violin in the empty hall, and the notes
   came back off the walls like thrown stones." Rung 3 — "Nadia played
   the violin in the empty hall, and the notes came back off the walls
   like thrown stones, and the bow trembled against her knuckles."
   Senses added: sound (rung 2), touch (rung 3).
4. Error analysis: Problems — (a) four "very"s plus vague adjectives
   ("exciting," "beautiful," "happy," "proud") *tell* the reader what
   to feel instead of showing evidence; (b) no telling detail, no
   sensory detail, no action — the paragraph could describe any
   festival. Model rewrite: *"The red diamond climbed until its ribbon
   tail snapped like a flag, and Lian laughed — a short, surprised
   sound. Beside her, Sef stood with his hands in his pockets, watching
   the heron kite the way you watch a letter you have already read."*

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**

1. Pacing map, first half:
   - Hilltop opening — **scene**: quoted dialogue ("Don't grip it"),
     moment-by-moment action (the kite climbing, the string burning).
     Chosen because the opening must put the reader *inside* the
     decisive moment.
   - Workshop scene — **scene**: quoted dialogue, action in real time
     (gluing, setting down the brush). Chosen because the argument
     changes the relationship — the reader must watch it happen.
   - Three-days passage — **summary**: "Over the next three days…"
     compresses gradual change (patience learned over days cannot be
     shown in one moment). One vivid image is kept ("the way you let
     out string").
   - Festival ending — **mixture**: the string snapping and the heron
     launch are scene (dialogue, moment-by-moment); the final paragraph
     is summary-with-reflection (the rest of the afternoon compressed
     into understanding). Scene gives the turning point immediacy;
     summary gives the reflection room.
2. Model scene from "Sef taught her the knots without calling them
   lessons": *"'This one first,' Sef said, holding out the string.
   'Fisherman's loop. Watch my thumbs, not the string.' Lian watched
   his thumbs. 'Now you.' She fumbled it twice. 'Again,' he said. 'The
   knot doesn't care that you're in a hurry.'"* Better or worse? Either
   judgment is acceptable if defended: the scene is better for showing
   Sef's teaching style, worse for the story's pace — three days of
   knots would take ten pages, and the story needs those pages for the
   hilltop.
3. Withheld question: *Why is the heron kite on the ground, and why
   won't Sef fly it?* The answer arrives in the workshop scene ("Your
   grandmother flew it. I made it for her hands, not mine.") and is
   completed only at the ending, when he flies it. The wait changes the
   ending: without it, the launch would be a pleasant surprise; with
   it, the launch is the answer to a question the reader has carried
   for three days.

**Independent practice**

1. Complete pacing map — same labels as guided practice 1, plus the
   festival ending mixture described there. Acceptable if every section
   is labeled with a reason tied to the scene/summary test.
2. Model scene + summary of one event (Nadia seed — winning the solo):
   Scene (about 150 words): *"The judges' door opened, and the woman
   with the clipboard said, 'Nadia Reyes.' Nadia stood. Her bow hand
   was shaking. She walked to the chair, sat down, and looked at the
   music — and then she did not look at it at all. She played the
   opening from memory, and the first note was too loud, and the second
   note was right, and by the third she had forgotten the judges were
   there."* Summary (two sentences): *"Nadia won the solo at the
   district audition. The judges praised her control."* What scene
   gains: the shaking hand, the loud first note — the reader feels the
   fear and the recovery. What summary gains: speed; what it loses:
   the moment the fear breaks.
3. Model flashback placement (Nadia seed): insert after "Nadia put the
   violin back in its case and did not practice that night" — *flashback:
   "She was nine, and her mother was tuning the old violin in the
   kitchen, saying, 'Listen — it remembers the notes even when I
   don't.'"* Before the flashback, the scene means "Nadia is avoiding
   practice." After it, the scene means "Nadia is avoiding her mother's
   unfinished story." The flashback changes the meaning from laziness
   to inheritance.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. Model structure-pass answers for the flat paragraph: (a) Cause —
   "the kite broke *because* a gust found its weakness, not because
   time passed; add the gust." (b) Preparation — "Sef's kite must be
   introduced before it flies: the heron kite folded at his feet in
   the opening." (c) Earned ending — "Lian is happy *because* she
   understands what flying is for, not because the story says so; show
   the understanding first ('She had come to the hill wanting a prize,
   and she was going home with something else: the weight of a string in
   her hand, her grandfather beside her, and a kite in the sky that was
   not hers and was hers now, both at once')."
2. Model language-pass rewrite: *"The workshop smelled of pine resin,
   and sawdust covered the bench where Sef mended the fisherman's kite.
   Lian leaned over her own frame, tongue between her teeth, while the
   glue dried too slowly for her patience."* Zero "very"s; one telling
   detail (sawdust on the bench) and one sensory detail (pine resin).
3. Reflective-conclusion test: the reflecting sentence is "Some kites
   are for keeping, she thought, and some are for letting go — and the
   trick, the whole trick, is knowing that they are the same kite." It
   is a reflection, not a moral, because it states what *Lian*
   understands from *these* events (the prize lost, the kite flown,
   the string handed over) — it does not tell the reader what to think
   in general. A moral would read "the lesson is that letting go is
   important"; this sentence could only be thought by Lian, after this
   story.

**Independent practice**

1. Draft — process work; no key. The adult checks word count
   (1,500–2,500) and causal order at Session 13.
2. Model structure-pass answers (framework; apply to the learner's own
   draft): (a) "Event ___ causes event ___ because ___" — the learner
   must name one link that was "and then" and rewrite it as "because."
   (b) "My want/need tension resolves at ___ (quote the action); it is
   earned because ___ (name the earlier event)." (c) "My POV is ___; the
   one place it slipped is ___; the fix is ___." Accept any revision
   that names a specific change and a reason.
3. Model reflective conclusion (Nadia seed): *"She had come to the
   audition wanting a prize, and she was going home with a different
   understanding: the solo was not a test of whether she could play
   loudly enough, but of whether she could stand to be heard. The bow
   was still trembling in her hand. She let it tremble."* Acceptable if
   it follows from the draft's events, reflects rather than preaches,
   and could only belong to this story.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice**

1. Model thesis clinic: Thesis 1 (structure/time) — *"The story opens
   on the hilltop and rewinds three days, so the reader meets the heron
   kite as a mystery before learning it is Ana's — and that delay is
   what makes the ending feel like an answer."* Skeptic: "The delay is
   only suspense; the story would work in chronological order."
   Answering quotation: "Your grandmother flew it. I made it for her
   hands, not mine." — the line lands differently after the wait.
   Thesis 2 (character) — *"Sef teaches entirely through proverbs and
   actions, never through explanation, which is how the story shows
   that his grief is a practice, not a speech."* Skeptic: "Sef's
   proverbs are just folksy dialogue." Answering quotation: "A kite is
   a promise you make to the wind. You don't hurry a promise." Keep the
   stronger thesis — either is acceptable if the skeptic test is
   genuinely answered with a quotation.
2. Model handled quotation: *"When Sef refuses Lian's question about
   the heron kite, he answers with a rule that sounds like wisdom and
   works like a wall: 'Some things you keep by leaving them alone.'
   The line's effect is to close the conversation while leaving the
   reader certain something is being hidden — the sentence explains
   nothing and implies everything."*
3. Model effect naming (in-medias-res opening): "The choice is opening
   on the hilltop with the kite already flying, instead of in the
   workshop three days earlier. The effect is that the reader's first
   question is 'how did we get here?' rather than 'what happens next?' —
   the reader reads forward to find the past, not the future."

**Independent practice**

1. Essay plan — model: *Thesis: "The story withholds Sef's grief until
   the hilltop climax, so the heron kite's flight lands as a decision
   rather than a sentiment." Claim 1: the workshop dialogue hides the
   grief behind proverbs — quotation: "I made it for her hands, not
   mine." Claim 2: the three-days summary lets the need form slowly —
   quotation: "a quieter want was forming, one she did not have a name
   for yet." Claim 3: the ending resolves want and need in one gesture
   — quotation: "Yours now… Fly it like she did."* Acceptable if the
   thesis is arguable and each claim names a choice, an effect, and a
   handled quotation.
2. Author reading — process work; the adult checks the delivery marks
   (pause, stress, pace) match the passage's meaning.
3. Model counter-reading: *"A skeptic could argue the delay is only
   suspense: the workshop scene already explains the kite — 'Your
   grandmother flew it. I made it for her hands, not mine.' — so the
   ending would work even if the grief were stated on page one, and the
   flight's power comes from the image of the heron, not from the wait."*
   Then: "Answering this objection strengthens the thesis: the image
   alone cannot explain why the flight feels like a *decision* — only
   the three years of not flying, which the delay makes the reader feel,
   can do that."

## Practice A

1. Mara's want: to win the district violin audition. Mara's need: to
   play for herself rather than for her mother's unfinished story — to
   stop carrying her mother's abandoned recital. How they differ: the
   want says "win the audition"; the need says "decide whose music you
   are playing" — winning by playing her mother's piece would satisfy
   the want and betray the need.
2. Mother's want: to see Mara succeed where she stopped — to be present
   at the audition, to help. Mother's need: to let Mara's playing be
   Mara's, not a second chance at her own recital. How they differ: the
   want says "be there, help, fix it"; the need says "step back and let
   the recital belong to your daughter."
3. Decoration: **(f) the neighbor's dog barks at the mail carrier.**
   Nothing else in the chain depends on it; cut it and Mara's story is
   unchanged. Causal chain (reordered): (a) Mara practices every morning
   before school, *because* the audition matters to her → (d) her teacher
   tells her she is ready, *because* the practice has made her ready →
   (b) her mother buys her a new set of strings, *because* the teacher's
   verdict makes the audition feel real → (c) while putting the strings
   away in the attic, Mara finds the old violin case, *because* the
   strings are stored there → (e) Mara decides to play her mother's
   unfinished piece, *because* finding the case and the program forces
   the question of whose music she is playing. Acceptable if the five
   events are linked with "because" and the dog is named as the
   decoration with a reason.
4. Model problem sentence: "On the morning of her district violin
   audition, Mara finds the program for a recital her mother never
   played — and must decide whether to play the piece her mother
   abandoned or the one she chose herself." Acceptable if it names what
   is at stake and for whom in one sentence.
5. Model seed 2: *Character: Dev, 16, who runs the school's lost-and-
   found. Want: to return every lost thing to its owner before the end
   of term. Need: to admit that he keeps the one unclaimed thing — a
   silver whistle — because it is the only thing anyone ever gave him.
   Chain: 1. Dev finds the whistle → 2. no one claims it *because* its
   owner left the school → 3. Dev starts carrying it *because* it feels
   like his → 4. a new student describes the whistle exactly *because*
   it was hers → 5. Dev must choose between returning it and keeping
   it *because* she has now appeared.* Acceptable if want ≠ need and
   each event causes the next.
6. Jamal chain diagnosis: decorations are "It rains on race day" and
   "He eats breakfast" — neither causes anything that follows; the rain
   does not change Jamal's running and breakfast is routine. Missing: a
   causal link between training and winning — the chain asserts the win
   without showing *how* the training produced it. One change that
   fixes it: make the rain a cause — "It rains, so the track is slick,
   so Jamal shortens his stride the way he practiced on wet days — and
   that is why he wins." Now the rain is a cause, not weather.

## Practice B

1. Speakers: Keeper — lines 1, 3, 5. Evidence: short imperatives about
   duty ("The lamp doesn't care if you're tired"), counting as a rule
   ("Ninety-one. Count them properly or don't count them at all."),
   and a conditional promise about the work ("I'll be happy when the
   light's lit…"). Daughter — lines 2, 4. Evidence: complaints framed
   as corrections ("The lamp doesn't have to climb ninety stairs"),
   and a defensive question ("Fine. Ninety-one. Happy?") that concedes
   the number while refusing the attitude. Acceptable if each line is
   assigned with word-level evidence.
2. Model rewrite (B is not ready because she hasn't finished a letter):
   *A: "We're leaving now." / B: "One minute." / A: "You've had twenty
   minutes." / B: "And I've used nineteen of them on the first
   sentence."* The dialogue shows B is writing, not packing — no
   narration needed. Any rewrite showing *why* B is unready in spoken
   words alone is acceptable.
3. Model ladder, "The crowd cheered": Rung 1 — "The crowd cheered from
   the hill." (place added). Rung 2 — "The crowd cheered, a wave of
   sound rolling down the hill." (sound added). Rung 3 — "The crowd
   cheered, and the sound hit Lian in the chest like a hand." (touch
   added; the cheer is now something felt, not only heard).
4. Model ladder, "It was cold on the hill": Rung 1 — "Cold wind moved
   across the hill." (specific noun/verb). Rung 2 — "Cold wind cut
   across the hill, smelling of salt." (smell added). Rung 3 — "Cold
   wind cut across the hill and found the gap at Lian's collar, and she
   hunched her shoulders against it." (touch added).
5. Same as Lesson 03 independent practice 4 — see that key.
6. Model mind-change exchange: *"'I'm not going.' / 'You already said
   you'd go.' / 'I said I'd think about it.' / 'Then think faster —
   the bus leaves in ten minutes, and your sister is already on it.'"*
   The last line shows the change: refusal collapses into a practical
   question. Acceptable if the last line shows the change without
   narrating it.
7. "Fast" vs. "efficient": "Efficient" connotes cleverness and virtue —
   it is a compliment word, so Lian using it is a defense: she is
   dressing up rushing as a virtue. "Fast" is neutral, merely speed, so
   the line would lose its joke and its target: Sef is not puncturing
   her speed, he is puncturing the fancy word she hides behind. The
   change also alters Sef: with "efficient" he is a teacher who hears
   the word *behind* the word; with "fast" he would merely be a man
   correcting a fact.

## Practice C

1. Labels: (a) **summary** — "By noon the frame was done" compresses
   hours into one clause. (b) **scene** — quoted dialogue, moment-by-
   moment action, present time. (c) **summary** — "Over the winter"
   compresses months. (d) **scene** — quoted dialogue in the present
   moment.
2. Model scene from (a): *"'Measure it again,' he said. She held the
   spar up to the light, turned it once, and shook her head. 'It's a
   finger short.' 'Then cut a new one,' he said. 'We don't fly short
   spars.' She set it aside and reached for another."* Better or worse?
   Either judgment is acceptable if defended — the scene shows Sef's
   standards; the summary keeps the story moving. The trade-off must be
   named.
3. Model summary of (b): *"Sef made her measure the spar twice and cut
   a new one when it came up short."* Lost: the characters' voices, the
   exact exchange, the rhythm of instruction — the reader no longer
   hears Sef's standard, only learns it.
4. Model placement: put the memory between "The corridor smelled of
   floor polish" and "Her name was called." Before the flashback, the
   audition reads as a test of skill. After it, the audition reads as a
   test of whose music Mara is playing — her mother's unfinished piece
   or her own. The memory changes the stakes from "will she play well?"
   to "will she play as herself?"
5. Festival ending mixture: the string snapping and the heron launch are
   **scene** (dialogue, moment-by-moment: "Here," Sef said; the kite
   "caught the wind and rose"). The final paragraph is
   **summary-with-reflection** (the rest of the afternoon compressed
   into "She did not win the brass compass… She did not mind."). Scene
   gives the turning point immediacy; summary gives the reflection room
   to land.
6. Diagnosis: the pacing error is scenifying stretches where nothing
   changes — breakfast and the walk are routine, not turning points, so
   they should be summary. The audition is the change-moment and should
   carry the chapter as the scene. Rule applied: slow down for the
   moments that change something; speed up for the stretches that only
   pass time.

## Practice D

Process items on the learner's own draft; the key gives the check the
adult applies.

1. Chain check — accept if every event is marked "causes the next
   because…" or "DECORATION," the weakest link is named, and the fix
   supplies a cause (not merely a new event).
2. Earned-ending check — accept if the learner quotes the ending and
   names one earlier event without which the ending could not happen.
   Reject "the ending is earned because it feels right" — the proof must
   be an event.
3. POV consistency — accept if the leak is quoted, the problem is named
   (narrator knows what the POV character could not), and the rewrite
   earns the knowledge through observation, dialogue, or inference.
4. Braid check — accept if each cut is tested against both questions
   (pause? open question?) and one failing cut is fixed with the two
   sentences on either side written out.
5. Want/need resolution — accept if the resolution is located in an
   *action* (a choice, a gesture, a line of dialogue), not only in
   narration or realization.

## Practice E

1. Model "very"-free rewrite: *"The wind hit the hill hard, sideways
   off the sea. The kite hung high over the harbor. Lian's hands shook
   on the string. The line cut into her palm. Sef said nothing at all."*
   Zero "very"s; each replacement is a precise verb or image.
2. Model combined sentences: *"When the glue dried, Lian tested the
   balance. The kite held; she smiled."* The semicolon joins two
   closely related independent clauses (the holding and the smiling are
   cause and response). Acceptable alternatives with a stated reason.
3. Semicolon choices: (a) **semicolon** — the humming is the consequence
   of the climbing; the two clauses are closely related. (b)
   **semicolon** — contrast between not flying and watching, closely
   related. (c) **semicolon** — antithesis of want and need; a period
   is acceptable for (a) and (c) but loses the link, which the learner
   must note.
4. Dialogue punctuation — three errors: (1) `"Where is the string"
   Lian asked.` is missing the question mark: `"Where is the string?"
   Lian asked.` (2) `"There, he said.` is missing the closing
   quotation mark after "There,": `"There," he said,`. (3) `"Take the
   red one."` should be lowercase "take" because it continues the same
   sentence after the dialogue tag: `"There," he said, "take the red
   one."` Corrected passage: *"Where is the string?" Lian asked. Sef
   pointed at the spool. "There," he said, "take the red one."*
5. Language pass — process work; accept if the before/after shows a
   genuine ladder climb (flat → visual → sensory → embodied) and the
   added sense is named.
6. Model precise-word replacements: *"She hurried into the room. It
   was a sunlit room, smelling of cut paper. She felt steady about the
   kite she had made."* Reasons: "hurried" shows urgency where
   "walked" shows nothing; "sunlit" is a specific image where "nice"
   is an opinion; "the kite she had made" names the thing where "the
   thing" hides it. Acceptable alternatives with reasons.

## Practice F

1. Ranking (weakest → strongest): **(a) weakest** — a summary of the
   subject, not an argument; no one could disagree. **(b) middle** — it
   names a device ("symbol of grief") but no specific choice and no
   effect on meaning; a skeptic has little to argue with. **(c)
   strongest** — it names a choice (withholding), an effect (decision,
   not sentiment), and a consequence for the ending; a skeptic could
   argue the delay is merely suspense.
2. Model handled quotation: *"When Sef refuses to fly the heron kite,
   he answers Lian's question with a rule that sounds like wisdom and
   works like a wall: 'Some things you keep by leaving them alone.' The
   line's effect is to close the conversation while leaving the reader
   certain something is being hidden — it explains nothing and implies
   everything."*
3. Patched kite: the author patches the kite rather than making it new
   *because* the patches are its history — each patch is a repair, a
   flight, a year with Ana. The effect is that the reader sees the kite
   as a kept thing with a past, so its flight feels like a return rather
   than a debut — and Sef's decision to fly it feels like reopening a
   history, not starting a hobby.
4. Labels: (1) **C** — claim about the opening. (2) **E** — quoted
   evidence. (3) **A** — analysis of the effect (the reader's question
   changes). (4) **A** — analysis tying the effect back to the thesis
   (the question as the story's engine).
5. Model counter-reading: *"A skeptic could argue the delay is only
   suspense: the workshop scene already explains the kite — 'Your
   grandmother flew it. I made it for her hands, not mine.' — so the
   ending would work even if the grief were stated on page one, and the
   flight's power comes from the image of the heron, not from the
   wait."*
6. Thesis revision — process work; accept if the old and new theses are
   both written and the change is explained with reference to the
   counter-reading.

## Unit assessment

### Part A — "The Borrowed Coat"

1. Tomas's want: to get the boatyard job by looking like he knows what
   he is doing — quote: "I thought — if I wore his coat, I'd look like
   I knew what I was doing." Tomas's need: to be honest about being new
   — quote: "I'd rather have a boy who tells the truth than a coat that
   lies." (foreman's line names it) or "The coat was his brother's. The
   hands were his." Difference: the want says "pretend to be
   experienced"; the need says "tell the truth about being new" — they
   point in opposite directions, and satisfying the want (keeping the
   lie) would betray the need.
2. Point of view: **third-person limited, close to Tomas.** Hidden: what
   the foreman thinks — e.g., whether he will hire Tomas. Quoted line:
   "The foreman was quiet for a moment." The silence is observed, not
   explained; the reader learns the verdict only when the foreman
   speaks.
3. The interview is scene *because* the decision happens there — every
   word matters, and the reader must watch Tomas choose honesty in real
   time. The walk is summary *because* nothing changes on it except
   Tomas rehearsing a false story; compressing it keeps the focus on
   the interview, where the change happens.
4. Before the flashback, the interview reads as a boy failing to impress
   a foreman — the coat is merely too big. After it, the interview
   reads as the moment his lie catches up with him: the reader now
   knows he rolled the sleeves three times and rehearsed a false story
   ("three years on the trawlers, ask me anything"), so the coat is not
   ill-fitting but a costume he chose — and taking it off is a choice,
   not an accident.
5. Model: *"I thought — if I wore his coat, I'd look like I knew what
   I was doing."* It reveals Tomas knows the coat is a costume: the
   dash shows hesitation, and the confession (rather than a defense)
   shows he already understands the lie. Also acceptable: the foreman's
   "A coat doesn't know anything. Take it off." — it reveals he judges
   the boy, not the coat, and that he values plain speech.
6. Model: *"the sleeves, which hung past Tomas's fingertips."* The
   detail shows the coat does not fit before anyone says so; it prepares
   the foreman's judgment ("That coat's too big for you") and the
   confession that follows. Also acceptable: "the smell of paint and
   diesel" (sensory placement of the boatyard) or "the wind off the
   water went straight through it" (the consequence of honesty, felt in
   the body).

### Part B essay rubric

| Criterion | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thesis | Arguable; names a choice and an effect; a skeptic could disagree for a reason | Arguable but the effect is vague | Summary or device list in thesis form | Missing or merely true |
| Body claims | 3+ claims, each one choice + one effect, developed in order | 3 claims; one lacks a named effect | 1–2 claims, or claims are plot points | No claims; summary only |
| Evidence | Every claim has an introduced, accurately quoted, explained quotation | Quotations present; one handling step weak (dropped or unexplained) | Quotations inaccurate or mostly dropped | No quotations |
| Effect analysis | Effects named specifically and tied to the whole story | Effects named; link to the whole is thin | Effects generic ("vivid," "interesting") | No effect analysis |
| Conclusion & conventions | Conclusion follows from the analysis; no new evidence or moral; word count 800–1,000; quotations accurate | Conclusion mostly follows; word count within 100 words | Conclusion restates the thesis or adds new evidence | Missing conclusion, or word count far off |

**Scoring:** 20–17 = strong; 16–13 = proficient; 12–9 = revise one
weakest area; below 9 = rebuild with adult support. Score the essay on
argument, not on whether the adult agrees with the reading — a
well-argued minority reading earns full marks.
