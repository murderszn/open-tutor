# Practice Tasks — Unit 05: Narrative Craft and Literary Analysis Essays

*Each task names its session, its activity, and its success criterion, and
names the lesson skill it reinforces. The adult reviews written practice
the same day. Vocabulary recycled across sessions: want, need, event
chain, point of view, braid, dialogue, telling detail, sensory language,
scene, summary, pacing, flashback, structure pass, language pass,
conventions pass, reflective conclusion, thesis, handled evidence, effect.*

## Practice A — Session 4: architecture studio (Lesson 01, Lesson 02)

Read this scenario before starting:

> *Mara, 17, has practiced the violin for the district audition for a
> year. Her teacher says she is ready. Her mother, who gave up the violin
> at Mara's age, keeps offering to drive her, tune the instrument, and
> wait outside the audition room. On the morning of the audition, Mara
> finds her mother's old violin case in the attic — inside it, a program
> from a recital her mother never played.*

1. Name Mara's **want** in one sentence and her **need** in one sentence.
   The two must point in different directions — say how, in one more
   sentence.
2. Name her mother's **want** in one sentence and her mother's **need**
   in one sentence. Again: how do they point in different directions?
3. **Chain test.** Here are six events in order. One is a decoration —
   it can be cut without changing anything. Name it and explain why in
   two sentences. Then rewrite the remaining five as a causal chain with
   *because* between each pair.
   - (a) Mara practices every morning before school.
   - (b) Her mother buys her a new set of strings.
   - (c) Mara finds the old violin case in the attic.
   - (d) Mara's teacher tells her she is ready.
   - (e) Mara decides to play her mother's unfinished piece at the audition.
   - (f) Mara's neighbor's dog barks at the mail carrier.
4. Write the **problem/situation** of Mara's story in a single sentence
   that names what is at stake and for whom.
5. **Story-seed sketch 2.** Invent a second character (not Mara): give
   them a want, a different need, and a five-event chain in which each
   event causes the next. Number the events and put *because* between
   each pair.
6. **Error analysis.** A learner writes this chain: *"Jamal wants to win
   the race. He trains hard. It rains on race day. He eats breakfast. He
   wins the race."* Diagnose it in three sentences: which events are
   decorations, what is missing (what kind of link?), and what one change
   would make the chain causal?

**Success criterion:** two want/need maps with the difference named; the
decoration identified with a reason; a causal chain written with
*because*; a one-sentence problem statement; a second seed with a causal
chain; an error diagnosis naming decorations and the missing causal link.
(Reinforces L1; error-analysis task.)

## Practice B — Session 7: dialogue and sensory language (Lesson 03)

1. **Cover-the-names test.** Read this exchange (a scene between a
   lighthouse keeper and his daughter). Cover the names, then identify
   who says each line and write one sentence per line explaining *how*
   you can tell.
   - Keeper: "The lamp doesn't care if you're tired."
   - Daughter: "The lamp doesn't have to climb ninety stairs."
   - Keeper: "Ninety-one. Count them properly or don't count them at all."
   - Daughter: "Fine. Ninety-one. Happy?"
   - Keeper: "I'll be happy when the light's lit and you're still awake to see it."
2. **Dialogue rewrite.** Rewrite this flat exchange so the dialogue
   reveals character — no narration, no adverbs:
   - A: "We need to leave now."
   - B: "I am not ready yet."
   - A: "We will be late."
   - B: "I know that."
   Your version must show *why* B is not ready, using only spoken words.
3. **Sensory ladder 1.** Climb "The crowd cheered" from rung 0 to rung 3.
   Write all four rungs; each rung must add a sense or a precise word.
   Label the sense you added at each rung.
4. **Sensory ladder 2.** Climb "It was cold on the hill" from rung 0 to
   rung 3, the same way.
5. **Error analysis.** Diagnose this paragraph in three sentences, then
   rewrite it in three sentences with one telling detail and one sensory
   detail: *"The festival was very exciting. The kites were very
   beautiful. Lian was very happy. Sef was very proud."* Name the two
   specific problems in your diagnosis (one about adjectives, one about
   telling vs. showing).
6. **Dialogue that advances.** Write a four-line exchange in which one
   character changes another character's mind. The last line must show
   the change; do not narrate it afterward.
7. **Word choice and tone.** Sef says, "Efficient is a word people use
   when they're rushing and want it to sound clever." Rewrite his line
   with the word *fast* in place of *efficient* ("Fast is a word people
   use…") — then explain in two sentences how the change alters what
   the line reveals about Lian *and* about Sef.

**Success criterion:** speakers identified with word-level evidence;
flat dialogue rewritten so character shows in words alone; two ladders
reaching rung 3 with senses labeled; error diagnosis naming both
problems plus a revised paragraph; a four-line exchange with a visible
change of mind; a word-choice analysis naming connotation effects.
(Reinforces L3.)

## Practice C — Session 9: pacing studio (Lesson 04)

1. **Scene or summary?** Label each passage *scene* or *summary* and
   write one sentence explaining your label, using the definitions from
   Lesson 04.
   - (a) *"She measured the spar twice, cut it once, and set it aside.
     By noon the frame was done."*
   - (b) *"'Measure it again,' he said. She held the spar up to the
     light, turned it once, and shook her head. 'It's a finger short.'
     'Then cut a new one,' he said. 'We don't fly short spars.'"*
   - (c) *"Over the winter the town forgot about the festival, and the
     workshop went quiet except for the gulls."*
   - (d) *"'You're rushing the glue,' he said without looking up.
     'I'm not rushing. I'm efficient.'"*
2. **Summary → scene.** Take passage (a) from task 1 and expand it into
   a six-line scene with one line of dialogue. Then answer in one
   sentence: is your scene better than the summary, or worse — and why?
3. **Scene → summary.** Take passage (b) from task 1 and compress it
   into two sentences of summary. Then answer in one sentence: what did
   the summary lose that the scene had?
4. **Flashback placement.** Read this present-tense paragraph: *"Mara
   stood outside the audition room with her violin case in her hand.
   The corridor smelled of floor polish. Her name was called."* And
   this memory: *"She was nine, and her mother was tuning the old
   violin in the kitchen, saying, 'Listen — it remembers the notes
   even when I don't.'"* Write the two paragraphs in the order you
   would place them, mark the cut, and explain in two sentences what
   the memory changes about the audition-room scene.
5. **Pacing map, festival ending.** Label the story's final section
   (from "Then a gust came off the sea" to the end) as scene, summary,
   or a mixture — and write two sentences: which parts are which speed,
   and what each speed does there?
6. **Error analysis.** A learner's draft scenes *everything*: three
   pages on choosing breakfast, two pages on walking to school, one
   paragraph on the audition itself. Diagnose in three sentences: what
   is the pacing error, which stretches should be summary, and what
   single scene should carry the chapter?

**Success criterion:** four passages labeled with reasons; a summary
expanded to scene and a scene compressed to summary, each with a
gains/losses judgment; a flashback placed with its effect explained; the
festival ending mapped as a mixture; an error diagnosis naming the
pacing fault and the fix. (Reinforces L4; error-analysis task.)

## Practice D — Session 13: structure pass on your draft (Lesson 05)

Work on your own story draft from Lesson 05.

1. **Chain check.** List your draft's events in order (at least 8).
   After each event, write "causes the next because…" or mark it
   "DECORATION." Name your weakest link and write the one revision that
   fixes it.
2. **Earned-ending check.** Quote your ending's final paragraph. Write
   two sentences: which earlier event *earns* this ending, and what
   would be lost if that event were cut?
3. **POV consistency check.** Find one place where your narrator knows
   something your POV character could not know. Quote it, name the
   problem, and rewrite the passage so the knowledge is earned (the
   character observes it, is told it, or infers it).
4. **Braid check.** Mark your cut points between plot threads. For each
   cut, answer: does it land at a pause? Does it leave a question open?
   Fix one cut that fails either test and write the two sentences on
   either side of the fixed cut.
5. **Want/need resolution check.** Write your character's want and need
   as two sentences. Then write one sentence: where in the draft does
   the need *win* over the want — and is that moment shown in action,
   or only in narration?

**Success criterion:** an event list with causal marks and one fixed
weak link; an earned ending traced to one earlier event; one POV leak
found and repaired; cut points tested with one fixed; a want/need
resolution located in action. (Reinforces L5 and L1–L2.)

## Practice E — Session 14: language and conventions pass (Lesson 05, U04)

1. **"Very" hunt.** Rewrite this paragraph with zero uses of "very,"
   replacing each one with a precise word or a telling detail: *"The
   wind was very strong. The kite was very high. Lian was very nervous.
   The string was very tight. Sef was very quiet."*
2. **Sentence variety.** Combine these four choppy sentences into two
   varied sentences, using at least one semicolon with a reason you can
   state: *"The glue dried. Lian tested the balance. The kite held.
   She smiled."*
3. **Semicolon check.** For each pair, choose semicolon, period, or
   comma — and write one sentence explaining the choice:
   - (a) "The kite climbed" / "the string hummed"
   - (b) "Sef did not fly" / "he watched"
   - (c) "Lian wanted the prize" / "she needed the flight"
4. **Dialogue punctuation.** Fix all three errors in this passage (there
   are exactly three): *"Where is the string" Lian asked. Sef pointed
   at the spool. "There, he said. "Take the red one."*
5. **Language pass on your draft.** Find your draft's flattest sentence.
   Write it, then climb it to rung 3 on the sensory ladder. Write the
   before and after and name the sense you added.
6. **Precise words.** Replace the three vague words in this paragraph
   with precise ones, and write one sentence per replacement explaining
   what the precise word adds: *"She walked into the room. It was a
   nice room. She felt good about the thing she had made."*

**Success criterion:** a "very"-free paragraph; two combined sentences
with a justified semicolon; three punctuation choices with reasons;
three dialogue-punctuation errors fixed; a ladder climb on your own
draft; three vague words replaced with reasons. (Reinforces L5 and U04.)

## Practice F — Session 18: thesis clinic and handled evidence (Lesson 06)

1. **Rank the theses.** Rank these three thesis candidates about the
   anchor story from weakest to strongest, and write one sentence per
   thesis explaining your ranking:
   - (a) "The story is about kites, family, and letting go."
   - (b) "The story uses the heron kite as a symbol of grief."
   - (c) "The story withholds Sef's grief until the hilltop climax, so
     the heron kite's flight lands as a decision rather than a sentiment."
2. **Handle a dropped quotation.** This quotation is dropped — it stands
   alone with no introduction or analysis: *"Some things you keep by
   leaving them alone."* Write three sentences: one introducing it
   (what is happening), the quotation itself, and one explaining the
   effect it creates.
3. **Effect naming.** The heron kite is patched in several places.
   Write two sentences: what choice is that (why would the author patch
   it rather than make it new?), and what effect does it create on the
   reader?
4. **Model paragraph analysis.** Read this paragraph and label each
   sentence C (claim), E (evidence), or A (analysis of effect):
   - *"The story opens on the hilltop, not in the workshop. (1) 'The
     wind took Lian's kite on the third try, and the whole hill seemed
     to lean back and watch it go.' (2) The opening puts the reader in
     the middle of the action, so the first question is not 'what
     happens?' but 'how did we get here?' (3) That question is the
     story's engine: it keeps the reader reading through the workshop
     scenes to find the answer. (4)"*
5. **Counter-reading.** Write the skeptic's objection to thesis (c) from
   task 1 in two sentences, supported by one quotation from the story.
6. **Thesis revision.** Revise *your own* thesis from Lesson 06 after
   considering the counter-reading. Write the old thesis, the new thesis,
   and one sentence explaining what changed and why.

**Success criterion:** three theses ranked with reasons; a quotation
handled in three sentences; a choice's effect named specifically; four
sentences labeled C/E/A; a supported counter-reading; a revised thesis
with the change explained. (Reinforces L6.)
