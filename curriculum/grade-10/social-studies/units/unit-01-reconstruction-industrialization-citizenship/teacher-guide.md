# Unit 01 — Teacher Guide

For the guiding adult. Student materials never link here directly in a way
that exposes answers; the answer key (`answer-key.md`) is a separate file.

## Unit at a glance

| Week | Focus | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Amendments on paper | Learners conflating emancipation with equality; rushing the clause-by-clause paraphrase in Lesson 2 |
| 4 | Contested citizenship | Emotional weight of racial-violence content; single-cause explanations of Reconstruction's end |
| 5 | Industrialization | "Technology did it" determinism; misreading data (percentages without shape) |
| 6 | Synthesis | Vague "both sides" essays; confusing moral judgment with historical explanation |

## Lesson-by-lesson guidance

### Lesson 1 — Emancipation's unfinished work

- **Core move:** on-paper vs. in-practice. Say the phrase aloud; it
  recurs in every lesson.
- **Readiness check** doubles as the Lesson 2 bridge. If the learner
  cannot state the 13th's two features, re-read Part 2 together before
  continuing.
- **Misconception:** "Lincoln freed the slaves, so 1865 = equality."
  Counter with the four problems in Part 1 — especially *protection*:
  ask "who stops the Black Codes?" and let the silence do the work.
- **Timeline task (Q7):** the point is the *closing window* — land
  redistribution was conceivable in January 1865 and dead by 1866.
  If the learner lists events without the inference, prompt: "What
  could still have happened in March 1865 that couldn't by December?"

### Lesson 2 — The Reconstruction Amendments

- **Do not skip the paraphrase discipline.** Every clause gets restated
  by the learner before moving on. This is slow and it is the point:
  constitutional text rewards slowness.
- **Worked example 2 (anachronism)** is the unit's hardest conceptual
  move. If the learner resists ("but equal *should* mean integrated"),
  agree morally, then separate the questions: *what did the words mean
  in 1868?* vs. *what should they mean?* Historians answer the first;
  citizens argue the second. Both matter; they are different jobs.
- **The "what the text excludes" move** (15th Amendment gaps) — name it
  explicitly and tell the learner it returns in Lessons 3 and 5.

### Lesson 3 — Contested citizenship

- **Preview and pace.** Read Part 2 yourself first. Keep discussion
  factual; do not assign graphic detail as reading. The deliberation
  (Week 4, S4) works best with the assigned roles in the lesson's
  support box.
- **Redirect the "Northern gift" framing:** Reconstruction's engine was
  Black institution-building (Part 1). Federal power was the *shield*,
  not the source.
- **Multiple causes (Worked example 1):** require the learner's Week 4
  deliberation position to name at least three causes. Single-cause
  answers get sent back with "and what else?"

### Lesson 4 — Industrialization

- **Data discipline:** calculators allowed; interpretation required.
  For Set C1, confirm the 5.5× factor, then ask what the factor hides
  (decade shape, regional concentration).
- **Image study:** enforce the limitation check (Q3). If the learner
  treats the illustration as evidence, re-read the caption rule: AI
  reconstructions organize thinking; they prove nothing.
- **Corporation/14th Amendment connection** (end of Part 2) often
  surprises learners — let it land, then connect back to Lesson 3's
  *Plessy*: same text, different beneficiaries, different decades.

### Lesson 5 — Synthesis

- **Consider splitting** across two sessions (Parts 1–2, then Part 3 +
  debate) — this is the unit's heaviest lesson.
- **The ledger (Worked example 1)** uses rounded illustrative numbers
  ($300 crop, $110 debt). Say so explicitly: it is a *model*, not a
  real family's books. The mechanism is the lesson, not the figures.
- **Debate norm:** "redefined" is not code for "bad" — it is the
  precise claim that new legal forms did old hierarchical work.
  Demand that precision in the Week 6 argument workshop.

## Discussion facilitation (deliberations and debates)

- Evidence rule: every claim needs a named fact (law, date, event, or
  figure). "I feel" statements get converted: "What fact makes you feel
  that?"
- Steelman rule: before rebutting, restate the other position to its
  proponent's satisfaction.
- The adult may pause any discussion that treats historical actors as
  abstractions — ask "who, specifically, is harmed in the account you
  just gave?"

## Readiness and re-teach criteria

- **Week 4 quiz:** 14+/20 with no zeros on Q4/Q5 → proceed. Otherwise
  re-teach Lesson 2 + Set A before Week 5 S1.
- **Unit assessment:** 42+/60 with ≥12/25 on the essay → proceed to
  Unit 02. Otherwise re-teach Lesson 5 Part 3 (argument structure) and
  require an essay revision.
- **Project rubric:** below proficient on *Historical accuracy*
  triggers re-teach regardless of total — accuracy is non-negotiable.
- **Error log:** keep a running list of missed facts and flawed moves
  (anachronism, single-cause, text/practice confusion). Re-teach from
  the log in Week 6 S2; do not re-teach whole lessons.

## Differentiation

- **Reading load:** amendment texts may be chunked one clause per
  session; the adult reads aloud while the learner follows and
  paraphrases.
- **Writing load:** the 250–350 word essay may be dictated and then
  revised in writing; the thinking (thesis, evidence, counterclaim)
  is what's assessed.
- **Extension paths:** each lesson lists one; the Freedmen's Bureau
  school research (L1), full 14th Amendment Section 2 (L2), Harlan's
  *Plessy* dissent (L3), gauge standardization (L4), and Douglass's
  1876 Freedmen's Memorial speech (L5) can become a portfolio piece.
