# Unit 01 Practice Sets

Session-mapped practice for Weeks 3–6. Worked solutions are in
`answer-key.md` — do not open it until you have attempted every item.
Each set: 6–8 meaningful tasks. The adult assigns all or a selection; the
error log from each set feeds the next week's re-teach.

## Set A — Amendment-text analysis (Week 3, S3)

A1. Copy the 13th Amendment's Section 1 from Lesson 1. Underline the
    punishment exception. In two sentences, explain what work that
    exception did for Southern states after 1865.

A2. Paraphrase the 14th Amendment's Citizenship Clause without using the
    words "citizen," "born," or "jurisdiction." (This forces real
    comprehension, not word-swapping.)

A3. The 15th Amendment bans voting denial "on account of race, color, or
    previous condition of servitude." A state requires voters to pay a
    $2 poll tax. Explain in three sentences why this law is
    constitutional *under the 15th's text* and unjust *in its effect*.

A4. Match each enforcement policy to its amendment and explain the
    mechanism in one sentence each: Freedmen's Bureau; Civil Rights Act
    of 1866; Enforcement Acts of 1870–71.

A5. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "The 13th Amendment ended slavery, so the
    14th and 15th were unnecessary." Identify the flaw (what did the 13th
    *not* settle?) and name the specific problem each later amendment
    addressed.

A6. Rank the three amendments by *long-term* importance to American
    citizenship. Defend your ranking in 4 sentences — there is no single
    right order, but your reasons must cite text and history.

## Set B — Contrasting-accounts lab (Week 4, S2)

Read these two short accounts of the **Colfax Massacre (Easter 1873)**
— then complete B1–B6. (Both are paraphrased from the historical record;
treat them as *secondary* summaries, not primary sources.)

> **Account 1** (from a Reconstruction-era Republican newspaper
> summary): "On Easter Sunday, an armed white mob attacked the Black
> militiamen and freedmen defending the Grant Parish courthouse, where
> Republican officeholders had taken their seats after the disputed
> election. Dozens of Black defenders were killed, many after
> surrendering. The attackers sought to overturn the election by terror."
>
> **Account 2** (from a Democratic newspaper summary of the same week):
> "A riot broke out at Colfax when armed negroes seized the courthouse
> and defied the lawful Democratic claimants to parish offices. In the
> ensuing clash both sides suffered casualties. Order was restored when
> white citizens reclaimed the building."

B1. For each account, identify: probable creator, audience, and purpose.
    (One line each — nine lines total.)

B2. List two *facts* both accounts agree on. List two points where they
    conflict.

B3. Which account uses passive or vague language to obscure agency
    ("a riot broke out," "casualties")? Quote the phrases and explain
    what each hides.

B4. Neither account is a primary source. Name **two** primary sources a
    historian would want before judging what happened (e.g., trial
    testimony, military reports, coroner's records), and say what each
    could add.

B5. The Supreme Court's *Cruikshank* (1876) decision grew from federal
    prosecutions of the Colfax attackers. In two sentences, explain the
    ruling and its consequence for federal protection.

B6. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "Account 2 proves the
    violence was mutual, so both sides were equally at fault." Write a
    4-sentence correction using the creator/purpose analysis from B1 —
    without claiming Account 1 is automatically true.

B7. Connect to the unit question in three sentences: what does the
    existence of two such different accounts tell us about *whose*
    citizenship the historical record was built to defend?

## Set C — Industrial data and map study (Week 5, S2)

C1. Railroad mileage: ~35,000 (1865) → ~193,000 (1900).
    (a) Compute the growth factor (193,000 ÷ 35,000).
    (b) In one sentence, state what a 5.5× increase meant for a farmer
    deciding *where* to sell wheat.

C2. The 1880s added about 70,000 miles of track — the fastest decade
    ever. Give **two** reasons the 1880s (not the 1860s) were the peak,
    using Lesson 4 (steel rails, capital markets, western settlement).

C3. In 1877, U.S. mills made ~432,000 tons of steel rail vs. ~333,000
    tons of iron rail. Explain in three sentences why this crossover
    year mattered more than any single year's total.

C4. By ~1890 the U.S. overtook Britain in steel; by 1900 the U.S. made
    more steel than Britain and Germany combined. Write a 4-sentence
    explanation naming **three** causes (Bessemer process, natural
    resources, tariff protection / vertical integration — pick three
    and develop each in one sentence).

C5. **Map study.** On a blank U.S. outline map, mark and label:
    Promontory Summit, Utah (1869); Pittsburgh (steel); Chicago (rail
    hub/meatpacking); Birmingham, Alabama (southern steel, founded
    1871); the approximate route of the first transcontinental
    railroad. Then write two sentences: what does the *geography* of
    these sites tell you about where industrial power concentrated —
    and where it didn't?

C6. A textbook chart shows steel output rising smoothly 1870–1900.
    Using the Panic of 1873 and Panic of 1893 (depressions that
    bankrupted railroads and idled mills), write three sentences on
    what a *smooth* chart hides about workers' lives.

C7. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "Railroads caused industrialization."
    Rewrite as a multiple-cause claim with at least three causes, one
    of which is *not* technological.

## Set D — Cause/effect error analysis (Week 6, S2)

D1. Fix this chain: "13th Amendment → freedom → equality."
    Insert the missing historical steps between each arrow (at least
    two insertions), using real events/laws from Lessons 1–3.

D2. Fix this chain: "Bessemer process → cheap steel → factories
    everywhere." Add the missing *non-technological* links (capital,
    labor, law, markets — at least two).

D3. A classmate argues: "Sharecropping was basically slavery, so
    nothing changed after 1865." Using your Lesson 5 matrix, write a
    5-sentence response that names one real change, one real
    continuity, and explains why "basically the same" is bad history
    even when the moral judgment is understandable.

D4. Identify the **anachronism** in each sentence and rewrite it:
    (a) "The 14th Amendment's framers intended to desegregate schools."
    (b) "Carnegie used vertical integration to break the Homestead
    strike." (c) "The 15th Amendment gave all Americans the vote."

D5. **Counterfactual reasoning.** Suppose federal troops had stayed in
    the South through 1900. Write two paragraphs: one arguing
    Reconstruction's gains would have survived, one arguing the
    backlash would have found another path. Each paragraph needs one
    specific mechanism (e.g., continued Enforcement Act prosecutions
    vs. Supreme Court narrowing). End with one sentence: which
    paragraph convinces you more, and why?

D6. Rank these by *explanatory power* for "why Reconstruction ended":
    Northern voter fatigue, Supreme Court decisions, organized white
    violence, the Compromise of 1877, economic depression. One sentence
    of justification per rank. (There is no answer key "correct"
    order — but your justifications must be historically accurate;
    the key shows an acceptable model.)
