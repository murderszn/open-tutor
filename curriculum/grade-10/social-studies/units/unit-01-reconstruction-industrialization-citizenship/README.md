# Unit 01 — Reconstruction, Industrialization, and Contested Citizenship

Grade 10 · Social Studies · Weeks 3–6 · issue #49, section U01 (draft for review).

## Essential question

*After the Civil War ended slavery, who got to be a full citizen of the new
industrial America — and who decided?*

## What this unit teaches

Between 1865 and 1900 the United States tried to answer two linked questions
at once. The first was political: what does freedom mean after slavery, and
does the Constitution now protect the rights of Black Americans? The second
was economic: what kind of country does a continent-spanning industrial
economy produce, and who benefits from it?

The Reconstruction Amendments (the 13th, 14th, and 15th) answered the first
question **on paper** — abolition, citizenship, equal protection, and voting
rights for Black men. The learner traces what happened to those promises:
Black political power (about 2,000 Black men held public office during
Reconstruction, 16 in Congress), federal Reconstruction policies, and then
the backlash — Black Codes, racial violence, the Compromise of 1877, and the
legal architecture of Jim Crow that emptied citizenship of much of its
meaning.

In parallel, the learner studies industrialization: railroads (about 35,000
miles of track in 1865 to about 193,000 by 1900), steel (the Bessemer
process; the U.S. overtook Britain as the world's leading steel producer
around 1890), oil, and the new corporate form. The unit closes by connecting
the two stories: the shift from enslaved labor to wage labor, sharecropping
and debt peonage in the South, industrial labor conflict in the North, and
the argument over whether American citizenship was genuinely "reconstructed"
or merely redefined.

A guiding adult should preview all primary sources and topics for mature
themes — racial violence, conquest, and labor exploitation are central and
are handled through age-appropriate, adult-supervised historical inquiry.

## Prerequisites

- The diagnostic weeks (1–2) confirmed the learner can write one claim
  supported by two pieces of evidence, identify a counterclaim, and use a
  timeline. If gaps remain, the adult re-teaches before Week 3.
- Familiarity with the Civil War's basic causes and outcome (slavery,
  secession, Union victory), from the learner's prior history.
- Reading stamina for short public-domain document excerpts (10–30 lines).

## Measurable objectives

By the end of this unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain the Reconstruction Amendments:** state what each of the 13th,
   14th, and 15th Amendments changed in constitutional text and identify one
   federal policy that tried to enforce each (e.g., Freedmen's Bureau, Civil
   Rights Act of 1866, Enforcement Acts).
2. **Analyze contested citizenship:** describe Black political participation
   and institution-building during Reconstruction, explain the causes of its
   violent suppression (Black Codes, the Compromise of 1877, Jim Crow laws
   such as *Plessy v. Ferguson*), and evaluate which forces ended
   Reconstruction.
3. **Explain industrialization's drivers:** describe how railroads, steel, and
   the corporate form transformed production and national markets, using
   quantitative evidence (railroad mileage, steel output).
4. **Analyze change and continuity in labor:** compare enslaved labor,
   sharecropping, and industrial wage labor; explain at least two labor
   responses (e.g., the 1877 railroad strike, the Homestead strike of 1892)
   and their outcomes.
5. **Evaluate conflicting historical interpretations:** distinguish a
   primary-source quotation from a historian's interpretation; identify
   creator, audience, and purpose in two contrasting accounts of one
   Reconstruction-era episode; defend an evidence-based answer to the
   essential question.

## Vocabulary

**Reconstruction** — the 1865–1877 federal effort to rebuild the South and
integrate formerly enslaved people as citizens. **Radical Republicans** —
the Congressional faction that pushed for strong federal protection of Black
rights. **Freedmen's Bureau** — the 1865 federal agency providing food,
schools, medical care, and legal help to formerly enslaved people (and some
poor whites). **Black Codes** — Southern state laws (1865–66) restricting
Black labor, movement, and rights. **Sharecropping** — farming another
person's land for a share of the crop, often under debt. **Jim Crow** — the
post-1877 system of legal segregation and Black disenfranchisement.
**Corporation** — a business chartered as a legal entity separate from its
owners, able to raise large capital. **Vertical integration** — controlling
every stage of production from raw material to finished product.
**Trust** — a combination of firms controlling an industry's prices and
output. **Strike** — workers refusing to work to pressure employers.
**Scab (strikebreaker)** — a worker hired to replace strikers.
**Yellow-dog contract** — an agreement, as a condition of employment, not to
join a union. **Nativism** — hostility toward immigrants.
**Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)** — the first major federal law barring
immigration by nationality. **Industrialization** — the shift from hand and
home production to machine production in factories.

## Standards / source notes (verified)

The track's audit (A00, draft PR #120) crosswalked this unit to the C3
Framework, *College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social
Studies State Standards* (National Council for the Social Studies, 2013):

- D2.His.1.9-12 — evaluate how historical events were shaped by unique
  circumstances of time and place (Reconstruction policies; industrial
  timing).
- D2.His.2.9-12 — analyze change and continuity over time (enslaved labor
  → sharecropping → wage labor, 1860–1900).
- D2.His.6.9-12 — analyze how historical contexts shaped and continue to
  shape people's perspectives (contrasting Reconstruction accounts).
- D2.His.8.9-12 — analyze how current interpretations of the past are
  limited by the extent to which available historical sources represent
  perspectives of people at the time (underrepresented Black voices in the
  Reconstruction-era record).
- D2.His.14.9-12 — analyze multiple and complex causes and effects of
  events in the past (why Reconstruction ended; why industrialization
  accelerated after 1865).
- D2.Civ.12.9-12 — analyze how people use and challenge local, state,
  national, and international laws and policies to address public issues
  (Reconstruction Amendments, Black Codes, Jim Crow, labor law).

No state adoption or accreditation is claimed; these are working
alignment notes for the guiding adult.

## 20-session pacing

Five 50-minute sessions per week. Lessons 1–5 anchor the teaching sessions;
practice, the investigation, review, and assessments fill the rest. The adult
adapts frequency and duration to the learner.

| Week | Goal | Sessions |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | Explain Reconstruction policies and the Reconstruction Amendments | S1: Lesson 1 — the end of slavery, emancipation's unfinished work, federal questions of 1865. S2: Lesson 2 — close read the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendment texts; what each changed on paper. S3: Practice — amendment-text analysis set (practice.md A); figure-check: Black officeholding data. S4: Project launch — *The Amendment on Trial* (project.md): research plan and source list. S5: Review + retrieval check (exit-check bank). |
| 4 | Analyze contested citizenship: resistance, violence, and federal retreat | S1: Lesson 3 — Black political power and institution-building; Black Codes and racial violence; the Compromise of 1877. S2: Practice — contrasting-accounts lab: two perspectives on one Reconstruction episode (practice.md B). S3: Project session 2 — draft analysis of one contested-citizenship case. S4: Deliberation — why did Reconstruction end? Structured discussion with evidence cards. S5: Review + check. |
| 5 | Explain industrialization: railroads, factories, corporations | S1: Lesson 4 — railroads, steel, oil, the corporation; reading industrial data. S2: Practice — railroad-mileage and steel-output data tasks (practice.md C); map study. S3: Project session 3 — peer review of drafts. S4: Investigation — *Where did the railroad go?* timeline/mapping task (project.md investigation B). S5: Review + formative quiz (assessments/week-04_quiz.md). |
| 6 | Synthesize: who did industrial America include? | S1: Lesson 5 — change and continuity: enslaved labor → sharecropping → wage labor; labor's responses (1877 strike, Homestead 1892). S2: Practice — error-analysis set on cause/effect claims (practice.md D). S3: Project final session — revise and assemble deliverables. S4: Argument workshop — was citizenship "reconstructed" or merely redefined? Thesis and counterclaim drafting. S5: Unit review + culminating assessment (assessments/unit-assessment.md). |

Retrieval practice is woven into each week's S5 and into exit checks; the
adult keeps an error log and re-teaches from it in Week 6.

## Source and safety notes

- Amendment texts are quoted from the public-domain U.S. Constitution.
- Racial violence is discussed with historical specificity and without
  graphic detail; the adult previews Lesson 3 and the deliberation.
- The generated illustration is labeled an AI reconstruction, not
  historical evidence, wherever it appears.
- All learner work, names, photos, schedules, and grades stay in private
  storage — never in this public repository.
