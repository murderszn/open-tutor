# Unit 01 — Answer Key

**For the guiding adult only.** Every item solved independently; solutions
reconciled against the lesson explanations before delivery. Open-response
items show acceptable reasoning and common errors — accept any answer that
gets the mechanism right in the learner's own words.

## Lesson 1

**Guided practice**

1. Acceptable: "Section 1 bans slavery *except as punishment for a crime
   the person was convicted of.* Southern states used this by passing
   vagrancy and petty-offense laws targeting Black people, then leasing
   convicted laborers to planters and mines — forced labor continued
   under a criminal-justice label." Key mechanism: conviction → legal
   forced labor.
2. Any two pairs, e.g.: Livelihood → the Bureau negotiated labor
   contracts and distributed food/clothing. Family → (Bureau's weaker
   area; accept "the Bureau's schools and aid freed reunification
   indirectly" only if the learner notes the Bureau did not run a
   family-reunion program — the *search* was freed people's own work.)
   Protection → freedmen's courts heard disputes. Education →
   thousands of schoolhouses built with freed people's pooled money.
3. Acceptable correction hits three points: (a) the amendment's text
   bans slavery/involuntary servitude (with the punishment exception) —
   it says nothing about citizenship, voting, or equal treatment;
   (b) "equality" required the 14th/15th Amendments plus enforcement;
   (c) on paper vs. in practice: Black Codes and convict leasing began
   almost immediately.

**Independent practice**

4. Section 2 ("Congress shall have power to enforce this article by
   appropriate legislation") made protecting liberty a *federal*
   legislative power for the first time. Before it, civil rights were
   overwhelmingly a state matter; after it, Congress could pass — and
   did pass — the Civil Rights Act of 1866, the Enforcement Acts, and
   eventually 20th-century civil-rights laws under the same enforcement
   logic (via the 14th's Section 5). Accept any answer naming the
   federalization of rights-enforcement.
5. Scope: the Proclamation freed enslaved people only in areas still in
   rebellion (exempting border states and Union-held areas); the 13th
   banned slavery everywhere in U.S. jurisdiction. Permanence: the
   Proclamation was a wartime executive order, reversible by a later
   president or peacetime courts; the 13th is constitutional text,
   reversible only by a new amendment. The 13th solved the bigger
   problem (permanence + universality); accept a defense of the
   Proclamation only if it argues *timing* (it freed people during the
   war, years before ratification).
6. Accept either side with a cause-effect chain and a concession.
   Literacy chain example: reading → access to contracts, newspapers,
   ballots, and law → durable political agency across generations.
   Land chain example: land → independent income → no sharecrop debt →
   economic base for political independence. Concession must be real
   (e.g., "but literacy without land left readers economically
   trapped" / "but land without literacy left owners cheated at the
   ledger"). One-sided answers without the concession are incomplete.

**Application**

7. Timeline order and significance: Proclamation (Jan 1863) → Special
   Field Orders No. 15 (Jan 1865, land set aside) → Bureau created
   (Mar 1865) → war ends (Apr 1865) → 13th ratified (Dec 1865) →
   Johnson restores land (1865–66). Inference: the land-redistribution
   window opened with Sherman's order and closed within about a year —
   freedom arrived *without* the economic base that would have made it
   self-sustaining. Accept any two-sentence version of this.

**Exit check**

8. Accept any three distinct pairs, e.g.: "emancipation had delivered
   *legal freedom from ownership* but not yet *citizenship*"; "...
   *the end of slavery as a status* but not *protection from coerced
   labor* (punishment exception)"; "... *a federal enforcement power*
   but not *land or economic independence*."

## Lesson 2

**Guided practice**

1. Acceptable paraphrase: "Everyone delivered into life inside the
   country's borders and under its authority counts as a member of the
   nation and of their home state." It overturned *Dred Scott v.
   Sandford* (1857).
2. 13th → Freedmen's Bureau (schools, contracts, courts; enforced
   freedom's practical preconditions) — accept Civil Rights Act of
   1866 as alternate. 14th → Civil Rights Act of 1866 (declared the
   U.S.-born citizens with equal contract/property/testimony rights,
   passed over Johnson's veto). 15th → Enforcement Acts of 1870–71
   (federal crime to block voting; marshals/troops vs. the Klan).
3. Acceptable correction: (a) the text's 1868 meaning did not include
   desegregation — most framers and Northern states accepted
   segregated schools; (b) *Plessy* (1896) later read "equal
   protection" to permit "separate but equal"; (c) the clause's use
   *expanded* over the 20th century (e.g., *Brown* 1954). The error is
   anachronism: reading later meanings back into 1868.

**Independent practice**

4. Any two the text does not forbid: poll taxes (poverty/wealth),
   literacy tests (education), property requirements, and — the big
   one — sex (the amendment protects only against race/color/previous
   servitude denial). Mechanism example: a literacy test administered
   by white registrars who pass illiterate whites via "understanding
   clauses" and fail Black college graduates; the law names no race,
   so the 15th's text does not reach it.
5. Accept either verdict if it addresses enforcement *limits*.
   "Against" model: the Acts depended on federal will — troops,
   marshals, prosecutors. When the Panic of 1873 shifted Northern
   priorities, when *Cruikshank* (1876) gutted prosecutions, and when
   the Compromise of 1877 withdrew troops, the legal tools remained
   but the enforcement vanished. Laws without enforcers are paper.
   "For" is acceptable only with a serious engagement with these
   limits (e.g., "enough *while* the will lasted — the failure was
   political, not legal").
6. Error 1 (mechanism): the amendment did not *automatically* change
   behavior — it created rights enforceable (if at all) through
   Congress and courts. Error 2 (outcome): Southern states did not
   treat freed people as equals; Black Codes began within months.
   Rewrite model: "The 14th Amendment made freed people citizens on
   paper, but Southern states immediately tested that citizenship
   with Black Codes, violence, and court challenges — equality had
   to be fought for, not just declared."

**Application**

7. Table check — third column must include at least one real evasion
   per amendment: 13th → Black Codes' vagrancy provisions / convict
   leasing via the punishment exception. 14th → *Slaughter-House*
   narrowing privileges-or-immunities; *Cruikshank* limiting federal
   action to state action; jury exclusion in practice. 15th → poll
   taxes, literacy tests, white primaries, fraud and intimidation.

**Exit check**

8. 13th — 1865 — bans slavery/involuntary servitude (punishment
   exception). 14th — 1868 — birthright citizenship; due process and
   equal protection against states. 15th — 1870 — voting not denied on
   account of race/color/previous servitude.

## Lesson 3

**Guided practice**

1. Phase examples: Evasion → Black Codes (1865–66). Federal
   counter-move → Civil Rights Act of 1866 / 14th Amendment /
   Enforcement Acts. New evasion → poll taxes, literacy tests,
   *Plessy*'s "separate but equal."
2. *U.S. v. Cruikshank* (1876): the Court held the 14th Amendment
   restrained only *state* action, not private individuals — so
   federal prosecutors could not reach the Colfax murderers (a
   private mob). Consequence: organized racial violence became
   effectively unprosecutable at the federal level.
3. Fourth column — one specific per amendment: 13th → convict leasing
   under the punishment exception. 14th → *Plessy* (1896) read equal
   protection to allow segregation; *Cruikshank* blocked federal
   prosecution of private violence. 15th → Mississippi's 1890
   literacy test/poll tax; Black registration collapsed to near zero
   across the former Confederacy by 1900.

**Independent practice**

4. Four-cause model (order may vary): (1) organized white violence
   (KKK, Colfax, lynchings) made Black politics lethal; (2) Supreme
   Court narrowing (*Slaughter-House*, *Cruikshank*) removed federal
   legal tools; (3) Panic of 1873 shifted Northern voters to economic
   anxiety ("lost interest" as effect, not prime cause); (4)
   Compromise of 1877 withdrew the last troops. Interactions to
   credit: violence + *Cruikshank* (terror the courts wouldn't
   punish); depression + troop withdrawal (austerity politics made
   "let the South alone" popular). Single-cause answers are
   incomplete by design.
5. The Black Codes (1865–66) were blatant — they named freed people
   and were struck at by the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the 14th
   Amendment directly. Jim Crow devices were *race-neutral on paper*:
   because the 14th/15th banned only race-explicit denial, laws that
   achieved racial results through formally neutral means (tests,
   taxes, white-only primaries run by parties not states) were much
   harder to reach — and the post-1877 Court was unwilling to look
   past the paper. Key insight: the amendments taught evaders to be
   subtle.
6. Acceptable: A — audience likely Northern/general readers seeking
   national stability; purpose: legitimize the bargain, frame it as
   statesmanship. Omits: who paid (Black Southerners abandoned to
   Redeemer governments). B — audience likely students of Black
   history / reformers; purpose: moral reckoning. Omits: the genuine
   constitutional crisis of 1876 (two slates of electors, real risk
   of renewed conflict) that made *some* bargain likely. Neither
   account is disproven by the other; each selects facts for its
   purpose.

**Application**

7. Score the deliberation prep on structure (claim + 2 evidence +
   counterargument + survival condition), not on which position the
   learner takes.

**Exit check**

8. (a) Black political power built public schools, colleges,
   churches, newspapers — the South's first public institutions
   across the color line. (b) The backlash used assassination,
   massacres, and terror as a *political strategy* to destroy Black
   voting. (c) The Compromise traded the presidency (Hayes) for
   ending federal Reconstruction (troop withdrawal). (d) *Plessy*
   read "equal protection" to permit "separate but equal,"
   legitimizing segregation.

## Lesson 4

**Guided practice**

1. (a) ~163,000 − ~93,000 ≈ 70,000 miles. (b) Accept: steel rails,
   mature capital markets, and western settlement demand converged
   in the 1880s; the 1860s network was still war-disrupted and
   capital-poor. Any two real reasons credited.
2. Vertical integration (Carnegie): owning mines → ore ships →
   railroads → mills, controlling every production stage to cut
   middleman costs. Horizontal integration (Standard Oil): buying or
   bankrupting competing refineries at the *same* stage to control
   the market and prices.
3. Feedback-loop paragraph must show the cycle: cheap steel → cheaper,
   stronger rails → more track laid → bigger market for steel mills →
   economies of scale → cheaper steel. The loop must be *named* as
   such.

**Independent practice**

4. Evaluation must name winners (railroad investors, Carnegie,
   Rockefeller, consumers of cheaper goods), cost-bearers (track
   laborers incl. Chinese workers, displaced artisans, Native nations
   whose land grants crossed, farmers squeezed by freight rates),
   and what "everyone richer" hides (distribution, coercion, risk).
5. Land-grant → power chain: 170M+ acres made railroads the largest
   landlords → they decided town sites, freight rates, and settler
   access → members of Congress held railroad stock / took railroad
   money → legislation (more grants, favorable rates, strike-breaking
   troops) followed the money. Any 4-sentence version with the
   land→leverage→legislation mechanism.
6. Missing link: **standardization** — common gauge (4 ft 8½ in),
   standard time zones (1883, railroad-driven), interchangeable
   couplers/brakes — without which freight couldn't move seamlessly
   across company lines. Branching effect: any real one — local
   artisans undercut by national brands; Native displacement
   accelerated; farmers dependent on distant markets and railroad
   freight rates (Granger backlash — preview of U02 Populism).

**Application**

7. Briefing scored on: both datasets cited (railroad 35k→193k;
   1877 steel/iron crossover 432k vs 333k tons), one risk named
   (overbuilding / rate wars / the 1893 panic pattern), a clear
   recommendation. "Good bet" and "bad bet" both acceptable with
   reasoning.

**Exit check**

8. Corporation: a state-chartered business that is a legal entity
   separate from its owners, able to raise large capital with
   limited liability. Vertical integration: controlling all stages
   of production from raw material to finished product. Trust: a
   combination controlling an industry's output/prices. Connection
   sentence: any version of "industrialization decided *who worked
   under what terms* — which is the economic side of the
   citizenship question."

**Image-study (Part 3) acceptable answers**

1. Continuities (any three): human workers still do the work;
   metal is still shaped; daylight/workshop lighting; skill and
   tools matter; production serves markets. Changes (any three):
   scale (2 workers → ~12+); power (hand/muscle → belt-driven
   machines); division of labor (one artisan does whole job →
   each worker tends one machine); worker's relation to product
   (makes whole item → tends one step); pace set by worker →
   pace set by machine.
2. Workshop: the artisan decides method, pace, design. Factory: the
   owner/manager (via the machine's design and the foreman) decides.
   Lost: autonomy, craft knowledge value, control of pace. Gained
   (debatable — credit either): steadier wages, or "nothing but a
   paycheck." The question invites argument, not a set answer.
3. Limitation (any one): it shows no real workplace, no real people,
   no data (wages, hours, accident rates), no regional/racial/gender
   specificity. Source that could (any one): payroll records, worker
   diaries, photographs (e.g., Lewis Hine — later, note the date),
   factory inspection reports, census occupation data.

## Lesson 5

**Guided practice**

1. Ledger logic: $150 share − $110 debt = $40 for the year; next
   year's supplies require new credit; the landowner sets prices,
   weighs the crop, keeps the books; leaving means facing vagrancy
   law and convict leasing. "Free to sign, unfree to leave."
2. 1877: leverage = paralyzing the rail network (the economy's
   arteries); state responded with militias + federal troops, 100+
   dead — labor learned national coordination, capital learned to
   call the army. Homestead 1892: leverage = shutting one giant
   plant; Carnegie used Pinkertons (private army) then the state
   militia occupied the town — union broken for a generation.
   State's role in both: coercive power deployed for property.
3. Essential question restated in own words; one evidence sentence
   per side (e.g., reconstructed: amendments' text survived and was
   revived in the 1960s; redefined: sharecropping/convict leasing/
   disenfranchisement reproduced hierarchy under new legal forms).

**Independent practice**

4. Paragraph scored on structure: thesis, two specific facts (one
   per half of the unit required by the prompt — check this),
   genuine counterclaim (not a straw man), falsifiability sentence.
   See the assessment essay guide for the same bar.
5. Matrix — acceptable cell contents: Enslaved labor: landowner owns
   land, tools, and worker; cannot leave (fugitive slave law);
   enslaver sets all terms. Sharecropping: landowner owns land;
   worker owns little; can legally leave but debt/vagrancy law
   traps; landowner sets crop, prices, weighing. Wage labor (1900):
   worker owns no tools of consequence; can legally quit; employer
   sets wages, hours, pace (foreman/machine). Biggest change:
   legal freedom of person and movement. Biggest continuity: white
   owners'/employers' control of the terms and the wealth produced.
6. "For" model: *Plessy* (1896) read the 14th to *permit*
   segregation — the amendment's protections failed its intended
   beneficiaries — while courts read "person" to shield
   corporations from state regulation (*Santa Clara County v.
   Southern Pacific Railroad*, 1886, reporter's headnote treated
   corporations as persons under the 14th). The same clause served
   capital better than citizens in that decade. "Against" acceptable
   only if it engages this evidence (e.g., "corporate use was real
   but the amendment's *text* still belonged to its framers'
   purpose, later revived").

**Application**

7. Inclusion audit — one law/event/policy per group required:
   formerly enslaved Black Southerners (included: 15th Amendment
   voting, briefly; excluded: Jim Crow disenfranchisement,
   sharecropping debt); Black women (included: community
   institution-building, schools; excluded: 15th Amendment's
   sex gap — no vote until 1920); white industrial workers
   (included: wage labor, westward mobility; excluded: union
   rights — strikes crushed 1877/1892/1894); Chinese immigrants
   (included: railroad jobs; excluded: Chinese Exclusion Act 1882,
   the first nationality-based immigration bar); Native Americans
   (included: — largely *not* included; excluded: land taken for
   railroad grants, reservation confinement — bridge to U03).

**Exit check**

8. Any substantive completion with a "because" that references unit
   content. Vague answers ("it was interesting") get one prompt to
   revise.

## Unit assessment

**Part I** (4 pts each): 1 — as quiz Q1. 2 — any two: poll taxes,
literacy/understanding tests, white primaries, fraud/intimidation
(targeting without naming race). 3 — 35,000 (1865) → 193,000 (1900);
first big business because of capital scale (stocks/bonds), national
management hierarchies, and land-grant political power — not just
mileage. 4 — sharecropping defined; debt mechanism: credit for
supplies at planter prices/interest → crop share minus debt ≈
nothing → debt rolls over; vagrancy law punishes leaving. 5 — any
two: Great Railroad Strike 1877 (crushed by militias/federal troops,
100+ dead); Homestead 1892 (Pinkertons + militia, union broken);
Pullman 1894 (federal injunction + troops, strike broken).

**Part II:** 6 — the move: splitting "equal" into *legal* vs.
*social* equality, so segregation counts as equal treatment; the
work: it lets the Court uphold Jim Crow without denying the
amendment's words. 7 — error: anachronism. 1868 understanding:
legal equality as then conceived (compatible with segregation in
the framers' minds and Northern practice). What changed by 1896:
the Court *chose* the narrow reading to legitimize the Jim Crow
order then being built. 8 — A is primary (constitutional text);
B is a paraphrase of a secondary judicial interpretation.
Weight: the text binds; the paraphrase is one Court's *reading*,
reversible by later Courts (as *Brown* later showed) — so B gets
less independent weight than A.

**Part III essay:** apply the scoring guide in the assessment. A
model 22–25 response takes the "redefined" position (or
"reconstructed" — either scores if argued): thesis; four facts
spanning both halves (e.g., 15th Amendment text; Mississippi 1890
literacy test; 1877 steel-rail crossover; Homestead 1892 troop
use); one clause quoted (e.g., equal protection or the 15th's
"on account of race"); counterclaim (the text survived for the
1960s revival); falsifiability sentence ("show me sustained Black
voting and officeholding through 1900 and I would revise").
Deduct for: facts from only one half; vague clause reference;
straw-manned counterclaim; missing closing sentence.
