# Lesson 3 — Contested Citizenship: Resistance, Violence, and Federal Retreat

**Goal:** You will explain how Black political power was built during
Reconstruction, analyze the multiple causes of its suppression (Black
Codes, racial violence, the Compromise of 1877, Jim Crow law), and
evaluate which causes mattered most in ending Reconstruction.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, your Lesson 2
audit table, notebook. **Adult note:** this lesson discusses racial
violence including massacres. Preview it; keep the discussion factual and
age-appropriate; graphic detail is not used and not needed.

**Readiness check:** From memory, write the three Reconstruction Amendments
with ratification years. Check against Lesson 2.

## Part 1 — What Black political power actually built

Between 1867 and 1877, with federal troops and the Enforcement Acts backing
Black voting, roughly **2,000 Black men held public office** in the South —
sheriffs, mayors, state legislators, a state supreme court justice, and 16
members of Congress. This was not symbolic participation:

- Black-majority legislatures wrote **the South's first public-school
  systems**, open to white and Black children — a transformation whose
  effects outlived Reconstruction itself.
- Black communities built churches, colleges (Howard, Fisk, Morehouse
  were founded in this era), newspapers, and mutual-aid societies —
  institutions that survived the political reversal and carried the
  freedom struggle into the 20th century.
- At the federal level, Black congressmen pushed for civil-rights
  legislation, including the Civil Rights Act of 1875 (equal access to
  public accommodations), passed shortly before Reconstruction ended.

The key point: Reconstruction was not something done *to* Black Americans
by the federal government. It was something Black Americans *built* —
politically, economically, and institutionally — whenever federal power
gave them the space to do it.

## Part 2 — The backlash: Black Codes and organized violence

**Black Codes (1865–66).** Immediately after the war, Southern legislatures
passed laws restricting Black labor and movement: vagrancy statutes that
criminalized unemployment, apprenticeship laws binding Black children to
former enslavers, and restrictions on land ownership and gun possession.
The Codes were the first large-scale evasion of the 13th Amendment — and
Congress's answer was the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and the 14th Amendment.
This is the pattern: *evasion, federal counter-move, new evasion.*

**Organized racial violence.** The Ku Klux Klan (1866) and similar groups
used assassination, whipping, arson, and mass murder as *political* tools —
not random hatred, but a strategy to destroy Black voting and officeholding.
Two facts make the strategy visible:

- The **Colfax Massacre** (Easter 1873, Louisiana): a white paramilitary
  force murdered dozens of Black militiamen and civilians defending the
  parish courthouse after a disputed election. The Supreme Court's 1876
  *U.S. v. Cruikshank* decision then gutted federal prosecution of such
  attacks, ruling the 14th Amendment restrained only *states*, not private
  individuals — another text-to-practice gap.
- Between 1877 and 1950, racial terror lynchings killed thousands of Black
  Americans (the Equal Justice Initiative's documented count exceeds 4,400
  across that span). The violence that ended Reconstruction continued as
  the violence that *enforced* Jim Crow.

### Worked example 1 — Multiple causes, not one cause

*Question:* "Reconstruction ended because the North lost interest." What's
wrong with this answer?

*Model reasoning:* It names one real cause and ignores the others. A
D2.His.14 analysis lists **multiple, interacting causes**: (1) organized
white violence made Black political participation lethal; (2) the Supreme
Court narrowed the amendments (*Slaughter-House*, *Cruikshank*); (3)
economic depression (the Panic of 1873) shifted Northern voters' priorities
to their own hardship; (4) the Compromise of 1877 removed the last federal
troops; (5) Southern Democrats used fraud and intimidation to "redeem"
state governments one by one. "The North lost interest" is cause #3 plus
#4 dressed as the whole story. In history, single-cause explanations of
big outcomes are almost always wrong — check for the others.

### Worked example 2 — Reading a law for its gaps

*Question:* Louisiana's 1890 Separate Car Act required "equal but
separate" railcars for white and Black passengers. In *Plessy v. Ferguson*
(1896), the Court upheld it. How did the majority claim this was
consistent with the 14th Amendment?

*Model reasoning:* The majority argued the 14th guaranteed *legal*
equality, not *social* equality — and that separation did not imply the
inferiority of either race (Justice Harlan's famous dissent called this
a "thin disguise"). For the lesson's purposes: *Plessy* shows how the
same constitutional text can authorize opposite outcomes depending on
who interprets "equal." The text did not change in 1896; the *Court*
did. When you cite a constitutional clause, always ask: *who gets to
decide what it means right now?*

## Part 3 — The Compromise of 1877 and Jim Crow's legal architecture

The presidential election of **1876** was disputed: Democrat Samuel Tilden
won the popular vote, but 20 electoral votes were contested. An Electoral
Commission awarded them all to Republican **Rutherford B. Hayes**. In the
informal **Compromise of 1877**, Democrats accepted Hayes in exchange for
the withdrawal of the remaining federal troops from the South (Louisiana
and South Carolina) and other concessions. Reconstruction's federal
enforcement ended.

What followed was a systematic legal architecture — **Jim Crow**:

- **Disenfranchisement:** poll taxes, literacy tests, "understanding"
  clauses, and all-white primaries — each race-neutral on paper (recall
  Lesson 2's gap analysis: the 15th Amendment banned only race-based
  denial), each devastating in practice when administered by white
  officials.
- **Segregation law:** "separate but equal" statutes for schools, trains,
  restaurants, parks — legitimized by *Plessy*.
- **Economic control:** sharecropping and debt peonage (Lesson 5) kept
  Black labor cheap and immobile.

By 1900, Black voter registration in the former Confederacy had collapsed
to near zero in most states. The amendments remained in the Constitution —
unchanged text, emptied meaning. They would be revived by the civil-rights
movement of the 1950s–60s (Unit 06), which is why this unit's story is not
an ending but a first act.

## Guided practice

1. List the **three phases** of the backlash pattern (evasion →
   federal counter-move → new evasion) with one real example per phase
   from Lessons 1–3.
2. Explain *Cruikshank*'s role in two sentences: what the Court ruled,
   and why that ruling mattered for stopping racial violence.
3. Add the fourth column to your Lesson 2 audit table: **What actually
   happened to this protection by 1900?** Use one specific law, case, or
   event per amendment.

## Independent practice

4. Using Worked example 1's method, write a multiple-cause explanation
   (at least **four** causes, showing how two of them interacted) for
   why Reconstruction ended. Number your causes.
5. Compare the Black Codes (1865–66) with Jim Crow disenfranchisement
   devices (poll taxes, literacy tests): what made the later devices
   harder for the federal government to strike down than the Black
   Codes had been? (Hint: what had the 14th Amendment changed between
   the two?)
6. **Perspective check.** Two historians describe the Compromise of 1877.
   Historian A: "a pragmatic bargain that preserved the Union's
   electoral system." Historian B: "the abandonment of four million
   citizens to their former enslavers' heirs." For each: identify the
   likely *audience and purpose*, then write one sentence on what each
   account leaves out. Neither is automatically wrong — but each is
   incomplete.

## Application

7. **Deliberation prep.** For the Week 4 S4 deliberation ("Why did
   Reconstruction end?"), write your opening position in 5 sentences:
   a claim, two pieces of evidence, one acknowledgment of the strongest
   counterargument, and one sentence on what would have had to change
   for Reconstruction to survive.

## Exit check

8. In one sentence each: (a) what Black political power built, (b) the
   strategy behind the violent backlash, (c) what the Compromise of 1877
   traded, (d) what *Plessy* did to the 14th Amendment.
