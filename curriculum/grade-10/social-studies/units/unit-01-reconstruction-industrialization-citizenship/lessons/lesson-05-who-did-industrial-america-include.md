# Lesson 5 — Who Did Industrial America Include?

**Goal:** You will analyze change and continuity from enslaved labor to
sharecropping to wage labor, explain labor's responses to industrial
conditions, and defend an evidence-based answer to the unit's essential
question: was American citizenship "reconstructed" or merely redefined?

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, your audit table,
notebook. **Readiness check:** Write one sentence on what changed and one
on what stayed the same between the 1860s workshop and the 1900 factory
(from Lesson 4's image study).

## Part 1 — From enslaved labor to sharecropping to wage labor

**Change:** the legal status of labor. In 1860, about 4 million people
were legally property. By 1870, they were legally free persons selling
their labor.

**Continuity:** who controlled the terms. Without the land redistribution
that "forty acres and a mule" had promised (Lesson 1), most freed families
ended up **sharecropping**: farming a landowner's land for a share —
typically half — of the crop, while buying seed and tools on credit from
the landowner's store. The math was designed to produce debt:

### Worked example 1 — The sharecropper's ledger (modeled)

*Setup:* A freed family sharecrops 40 acres of cotton in 1880. The crop
sells for $300. The contract gives the family half: $150. During the year
they bought $90 of seed, tools, and food on credit from the landowner's
store at high interest, and the debt now totals $110.

*Model reasoning:* $150 (share) − $110 (debt) = $40 for a year's work —
and next year's credit starts immediately, because the family has no
savings. If the crop fails or prices fall, the debt *grows*. The landowner
controls the weighing, the prices, and the books. Now compare: under
slavery, the worker was owned and could not leave; under sharecropping,
the worker is legally free but economically bound — leaving means
abandoning the debt, which local law treated as a crime (and the 13th
Amendment's punishment exception, Lesson 1, made convict leasing the
penalty). **Changed:** legal freedom, the ability to keep a family
together, the right to a share. **Unchanged:** white landowners'
control of land, credit, and law; Black labor producing white wealth.

### Worked example 2 — Wage labor's bargain (modeled)

*Setup:* A Polish immigrant works 12-hour days in a Carnegie steel mill in
1890 for about $1.50 a day. He can quit. He cannot afford to be unemployed
long, cannot read English well enough to find other work quickly, and has
signed no union contract because the mill fires organizers.

*Model reasoning:* Compared to the sharecropper, he has **exit** — the
legal right to leave. Compared to the artisan in Lesson 4's image, he has
lost **control** over pace, method, and product. The wage is "free" labor's
great gain and its great fiction: formally voluntary, practically
compelled by hunger. Industrial America offered millions of immigrants
this bargain — better than the Russian or Sicilian village they left,
harsher than the American promise they were sold. Both things are true;
a historian holds both.

## Part 2 — Labor answers back

Workers did not accept the bargain silently:

- **The Great Railroad Strike of 1877.** When railroads cut wages during
  the depression that followed the Panic of 1873, rail workers walked out
  from Baltimore to St. Louis. State militias and federal troops crushed
  the strike — over 100 people died — but the strike proved that industrial
  workers could paralyze the national economy. It was the first nationwide
  labor uprising.
- **The Homestead Strike (1892).** Carnegie Steel cut wages at its
  Homestead, Pennsylvania plant; 3,800 workers struck. The company hired
  300 Pinkerton detectives as a private army; a gun battle left workers
  and Pinkertons dead. The state militia then occupied the town, the
  strike collapsed, and Carnegie broke the steelworkers' union for a
  generation.
- **The Pullman Strike (1894)** — previewed for Unit 02: a national rail
  boycott crushed by federal injunction and troops.

The pattern: workers discovered collective power; corporations and the
state combined greater power. Notice *who the state helped* — federal
troops that had once protected Black voters (Lesson 3) now broke strikes.
The government's enforcement muscle moved from protecting citizenship
rights to protecting property rights. That shift is the unit's deepest
continuity.

## Part 3 — Synthesis: reconstructed or redefined?

Return to the essential question. Two defensible answers:

**"Reconstructed":** The amendments permanently changed the Constitution's
theory of citizenship — birthright citizenship, equal protection, and
Black male suffrage became constitutional bedrock. Black institution-
building (schools, churches, colleges) survived Jim Crow and became the
infrastructure of the 20th-century civil-rights movement, which *revived*
the amendments. The promise was deferred, not destroyed.

**"Merely redefined":** By 1900, the amendments' protections were hollow
for the people they were written for — disenfranchisement, segregation,
convict leasing, and sharecropping reproduced racial hierarchy under new
legal forms. Meanwhile industrial citizenship — the rights of workers,
immigrants, women — was barely begun: the 15th Amendment excluded women,
labor had no constitutional protection, and the Chinese Exclusion Act
(1882) showed citizenship could still be denied by nationality. The old
order was not reconstructed; it was *translated* into industrial language.

Your job is not to pick the "right" answer — both are argued by serious
historians — but to defend one with evidence and to state clearly what
evidence would change your mind.

### Worked example 3 — Building the argument (modeled)

*Thesis (redefined side):* "Between 1865 and 1900, American citizenship
was redefined rather than reconstructed: the legal forms changed while
racial and economic hierarchy persisted."

*Evidence:* (1) The 15th Amendment's text banned race-based voting denial,
but Mississippi's 1890 constitution used literacy tests and poll taxes —
race-neutral on paper — to eliminate Black voting, exploiting the exact
gap Lesson 2 identified. (2) Sharecropping replaced slavery's legal
ownership with debt bondage enforced by vagrancy law and convict leasing,
exploiting the 13th Amendment's punishment exception. (3) When workers
struck in 1877 and 1892, federal troops protected railroads and mills,
not strikers — the state's coercive power served property, not the
citizens the amendments named.

*Counterclaim acknowledged:* The amendments' text survived and was later
revived — the 1965 Voting Rights Act enforced the 15th Amendment nearly a
century later, which would have been impossible without the constitutional
foundation.

*What would change my mind:* evidence that a large share of Black
Southerners actually voted freely and held office continuously through
1900 — i.e., that the amendments worked *in practice*, not just in text.

## Guided practice

1. Using Worked example 1's ledger method, explain in 3 sentences why a
   sharecropper was "legally free but economically bound."
2. Compare the 1877 railroad strike and the Homestead strike on two axes:
   **workers' leverage** (what could they withhold?) and **the state's
   role** (whose side did government power take?). One paragraph.
3. State the unit's essential question in your own words. Then write one
   sentence of evidence for the "reconstructed" side and one for the
   "redefined" side.

## Independent practice

4. Write a full 8–10 sentence argument paragraph defending **one** side
   of the essential question, following Worked example 3's structure:
   thesis, two evidence pieces with specific facts, one acknowledged
   counterclaim, one "what would change my mind" sentence.
5. **Change-and-continuity matrix.** Build a 3×3 table. Rows: Enslaved
   labor (1860), Sharecropping (1880), Industrial wage labor (1900).
   Columns: Who controlled the land/tools? Could the worker legally
   leave? Who set the terms of work? Fill all nine cells with specific
   facts. Then write two sentences: the biggest change and the biggest
   continuity.
6. Evaluate: "The 14th Amendment helped corporations more than it helped
   Black citizens in the 1890s." Argue for or against in a paragraph,
   using *Plessy* and the corporate-personhood reading of "person."
   (The adult may supply a one-paragraph briefing on *Santa Clara
   County v. Southern Pacific Railroad*, 1886, if needed.)

## Application

7. **The inclusion audit.** List five groups: formerly enslaved Black
   Southerners, Black women, white industrial workers, Chinese
   immigrants, Native Americans. For each, write 2–3 sentences: what
   did industrial America (1865–1900) *include* them in, and what did it
   *exclude* them from? Use at least one specific law, event, or policy
   per group. (Native Americans connect to U03's expansion story —
   note the connection for later.)

## Exit check

8. Complete: "The most important thing I learned about American
   citizenship in this unit is ___ because ___."

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Discussion support:** The essential-question debate uses a
  fishbowl: an inner circle argues, the outer circle tracks evidence
  on a shared sheet, then circles swap. No learner is put on the spot
  without preparation.
- **Alternate response mode:** The argument paragraph may be delivered
  as a recorded 2-minute speech with a written evidence outline.
- **Extension:** Read an excerpt of Frederick Douglass's 1876 speech at
  the unveiling of the Freedmen's Memorial (public domain). In 8
  sentences, explain his mixed verdict on Lincoln and emancipation —
  and connect it to the "reconstructed vs. redefined" debate.
- **Teacher note:** This lesson carries the unit's heaviest cognitive
  load; consider splitting across two sessions (Part 1–2, then Part 3
  + debate). Answer key: `../answer-key.md` (Lesson 5). Misconception
  watch: learners may treat "redefined" as simply "bad" — press them
  to see *redescription as a strategy*: new legal forms doing old
  work is a precise historical claim, not a vague complaint.
