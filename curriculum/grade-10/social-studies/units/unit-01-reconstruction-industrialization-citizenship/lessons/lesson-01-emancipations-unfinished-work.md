# Lesson 1 — Emancipation's Unfinished Work

**Goal:** You will explain what emancipation actually delivered by 1865, what
it left unresolved, and the three questions the federal government faced:
what rights do freed people have, who enforces those rights, and how does a
rebuilt Union treat the former Confederacy.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook or
document, timeline space (paper or digital). **Adult note:** preview
mentions of racial violence with the learner before beginning.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** In two sentences, state what the 13th
Amendment did. If you can't, read the text in Part 2 first — that's what
this lesson starts with.

## Part 1 — Four million people, no plan

When the Civil War ended in April 1865, roughly **4 million enslaved
people** had been freed — about one-eighth of the U.S. population. Most had
no land, no money, no formal education, and no legal protection beyond the
Emancipation Proclamation (1863), which was a wartime measure that had
applied only to areas still in rebellion.

Freedom created immediate, concrete problems:

1. **Livelihood.** Most freed people had worked in agriculture their whole
   lives. The land they worked belonged to their former enslavers. Without
   land or wages, "freedom" could mean starvation.
2. **Family.** Slavery had separated families for generations. Freed people
   immediately began searching for spouses, children, and parents — an
   enormous migration of reunion.
3. **Protection.** Former Confederate states moved fast to write new laws
   (the Black Codes, studied in Lesson 3) that tried to restore as much of
   the old order as possible. Who would stop them — and how?
4. **Political status.** Were freed people citizens? Could they vote? Own
   land? Testify in court? The Constitution, as written before 1865, gave no
   clear answer.

Congress and President Andrew Johnson gave three different answers to these
questions, which is why Reconstruction became a contest, not a plan.

## Part 2 — The 13th Amendment: what the text actually says

Ratified **December 6, 1865**, the 13th Amendment is short enough to read
in full. It is public domain.

> **Section 1.** Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a
> punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted,
> shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their
> jurisdiction.
>
> **Section 2.** Congress shall have power to enforce this article by
> appropriate legislation.

Two features matter for the rest of this unit:

- The **punishment exception** ("except as a punishment for crime") left a
  door open. Southern states soon exploited it: Black Codes criminalized
  vagrancy and minor offenses, and convicted people were leased out as
  prison labor — **convict leasing**. Notice: the amendment ended slavery
  *as a legal status* but did not by itself guarantee freedom from forced
  labor.
- **Section 2** is the first time the Constitution explicitly gave Congress
  enforcement power over a civil right. This was a structural revolution:
  protecting individual liberty became a *federal* job, not just a state
  matter. Every later civil-rights law descends from this clause.

### Worked example 1 — Reading constitutional text precisely

*Question:* Did the 13th Amendment make formerly enslaved people citizens?

*Model reasoning:* No. Read Section 1: it prohibits slavery and involuntary
servitude (with the punishment exception). Citizenship is never mentioned.
The amendment answers the question "can people be owned?" — it does not
answer "are these people citizens with rights?" That silence is exactly why
the 14th Amendment (Lesson 2) became necessary. A careful reader always
asks: *what does this text say, and what does it not say?*

### Worked example 2 — Spotting an exception clause

*Question:* A Mississippi law of 1865 made it a crime for a Black person to
be unemployed, and leased convicted "vagrants" to planters. Did this violate
the 13th Amendment?

*Model reasoning:* The planters who wrote the law argued it did not: the
amendment's Section 1 permits involuntary servitude "as a punishment for
crime," and the vagrant had been convicted. Whether this reading is honest
is a separate question from whether it is textually arguable. This is the
pattern to watch all unit long: a constitutional promise, then a legal
mechanism that narrows it. The 13th Amendment ended slavery; it did not end
*coerced labor*.

## Part 3 — The Freedmen's Bureau: the federal government tries something new

In March 1865 Congress created the **Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and
Abandoned Lands** — the Freedmen's Bureau. It ran until 1872. The Bureau
was the first large-scale federal social-welfare agency in American history:

- Distributed food and clothing to freed people *and* destitute whites.
- Ran hospitals and vaccination campaigns.
- **Founded schools** — freed people pooled money and filled classrooms;
  the Bureau helped build thousands of schoolhouses. By 1870, hundreds of
  thousands of Black children and adults were learning to read.
- Negotiated labor contracts between freed people and planters, and ran
  "freedmen's courts" to hear disputes.

Its limits were built in from the start. Congress gave it no permanent land
program: General Sherman's Special Field Orders No. 15 (January 1865) had
set aside coastal land for freed families ("forty acres and a mule"), but
President Johnson ordered most of it returned to former Confederates.
Without land, most freed people had to work for their former enslavers for
wages — or sharecrop (Lesson 5).

**The key insight:** the Bureau showed that emancipation without
enforcement was not enough, and enforcement without economic independence
was fragile. Both lessons echo through Reconstruction's collapse.

## Guided practice

1. In your own words, explain the punishment exception in the 13th
   Amendment's Section 1. Why did it matter for convict leasing? *(Hint:
   reread Worked example 2.)*
2. Name **two** concrete problems freed people faced in 1865 and, for each,
   one thing the Freedmen's Bureau did about it.
3. A classmate says: "The 13th Amendment gave freed people full equality."
   Write a 3-sentence correction using the amendment's text and the
   concept of "on paper vs. in practice."

## Independent practice

4. Explain why Section 2 of the 13th Amendment mattered *structurally* —
   not just for slavery. What new power did Congress gain, and why does
   that power matter for civil-rights law even today?
5. Compare the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) with the 13th Amendment on
   two axes: **scope** (whom/what each covered) and **permanence** (how
   hard each was to reverse). Which solved the bigger problem? Defend
   your answer in 4–5 sentences.
6. The Bureau built schools but could not deliver land. Argue which
   mattered more for long-term freedom: literacy or land ownership. Use
   one cause-and-effect chain for your side and acknowledge one strength
   of the other side.

## Application

7. **Timeline task.** Place these on a single timeline with one-sentence
   significance notes: Emancipation Proclamation (Jan 1, 1863), Special
   Field Orders No. 15 (Jan 1865), Freedmen's Bureau created (Mar 1865),
   Civil War ends (Apr 1865), 13th Amendment ratified (Dec 6, 1865),
   Johnson returns most "forty acres" land (1865–66). Then write two
   sentences: what does the *order* of these events tell you about how
   fast the window for land redistribution opened and closed?

## Exit check

8. Complete this sentence three ways (each must be a different fact from
   the lesson): "By the end of 1865, emancipation had delivered ___ but
   had not yet delivered ___."

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading support:** Read the two amendment sections aloud twice; the
  adult paraphrases "involuntary servitude" as "being forced to work
  against your will."
- **Alternate response mode:** The timeline task may be built as a labeled
  strip of index cards instead of a written page.
- **Extension:** Research one Freedmen's Bureau school in your state and
  write a 6-sentence report: who founded it, who attended, what survives.
  (Start at the National Park Service's Reconstruction sites.)
- **Teacher note:** Learners often conflate emancipation with equality.
  The on-paper/in-practice distinction introduced here is the unit's
  load-bearing concept; return to it in Lessons 2, 3, and 5. Answer key:
  `../answer-key.md` (Lesson 1). Likely misconception: "the 13th
  Amendment ended all forced labor" — press on the punishment exception.
