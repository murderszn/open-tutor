# Unit 04 — The 1920s, the Great Depression, and the New Deal

Grade 10 · Social Studies · Weeks 15–18 · issue #49, section U04 (draft for review).

## Essential question

*When prosperity collapsed into the Great Depression, Americans argued
over what government is for — did the New Deal rescue American
democracy, remake it, or leave its deepest inequalities in place?*

## What this unit teaches

In 1920 the United States looked like a country that had solved
production: factories turned out cars, radios, and refrigerators at
falling prices, and installment credit put them in millions of homes.
By 1933 a quarter of the labor force was unemployed, about 9,000 banks
had failed since 1930, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average had lost
about 89% of its 1929 peak value. This unit follows that arc in four
moves. **Week 15:** the 1920s boom — mass production, credit,
speculation, and a cultural flowering — and who it left out: farmers
facing falling crop prices, Black Americans facing Jim Crow and a
resurgent Ku Klux Klan, immigrants facing new restriction laws, and
women whose new vote did not yet mean equal work. **Week 16:** how the
crash of October 1929 became a decade-long depression — not one cause
but interacting ones: overproduction, fragile credit, a banking system
with no deposit insurance, the gold standard's discipline, and global
links through war debts and tariffs — and what it cost in human terms:
unemployment, Hoovervilles, the Bonus Army's dispersal, and the Dust
Bowl's ecological collapse and migration. **Week 17:** the First New
Deal's Hundred Days (March 9–June 16, 1933) — the bank holiday,
relief, public works, farm and industrial codes, and banking reform —
sorted by relief, recovery, and reform, and judged program by program.
**Week 18:** the Second New Deal, its exclusions, and its limits —
the WPA, the Wagner Act, and the Social Security Act (August 14,
1935); the Supreme Court's strikes against the NIRA (1935) and the AAA
(1936); the failed court-packing plan (1937); the 1937–38 recession;
and the evidence question that runs through the whole unit: what
counts as proof that a policy "worked"?

A guiding adult should preview all primary sources and topics for
mature themes — poverty, hunger, eviction, racial exclusion, and
police/military dispersal of protesters are central and are handled
through age-appropriate, adult-supervised historical inquiry. All
sources are documentary and textual; no graphic imagery is used.
Period photographs and cartoons may contain ethnic stereotypes; the
adult frames them as evidence of the era's prejudices, never as
neutral humor. Figures are given as estimates with sources wherever
historians disagree — notably unemployment (a later estimate, since
no modern monthly survey existed in 1933) and Dust Bowl migration
(about 2.5 million people left the Plains states in the 1930s, with
over 200,000 reaching California).

- Unit 02's map and data habits: reading a scaled map and a small

- Unit 03's causation habit: distinguishing long-term causes from
  triggers, and comparing two accounts of the same event (D2.His.6.9-12).
- Reading stamina for 20–40-line public-domain document excerpts;
  statutory excerpts are used in paraphrase with short attributed
  quotations. Only short, attributed phrases are quoted verbatim from
  presidential speeches (public domain); all other historical voices in
  this unit are paraphrased or clearly labeled original composites,
  never presented as real people's words.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of this unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain 1920s prosperity and its limits:** describe mass
   production, installment credit, and speculation; and explain why
   farmers, Black Americans, recent immigrants, and many women did
   not share equally in the boom, with evidence for each group.
2. **Analyze the Depression's causes:** distinguish the October 1929
   crash (a trigger) from interacting causes — overproduction,
   fragile credit, bank fragility, the gold standard, and global
   links — and compute and interpret the Dow's 1929–1932 decline
   (381.17 to 41.22, about 89%) and the unemployment series
   1929–1940.
3. **Describe the Depression's human cost:** explain unemployment,
   Hoovervilles, the Bonus Army episode (1932), and the Dust Bowl's
   causes, migration (about 2.5 million people left the Plains
   states), and human consequences, using the generated illustration
   as a reconstruction — never as evidence — and its text alternative.
4. **Evaluate the First New Deal:** sort Hundred Days programs into
   relief, recovery, and reform; explain the bank holiday and
   Emergency Banking Act (March 1933), the CCC, FERA, PWA, TVA, AAA,
   NRA, and Glass-Steagall/FDIC; and judge each program's aims
   against its results.
5. **Evaluate the Second New Deal and its limits:** explain the WPA,
   the Wagner Act, and the Social Security Act (1935) — including
   the agricultural and domestic workers it originally excluded
   (about 65% of Black workers left outside old-age insurance, and
   about half the labor force overall); explain the Court's
   invalidation of the NIRA and AAA and the failed 1937 court-packing
   plan; and argue, with stated limitations, whether the New Deal
   "worked," by what measure, and for whom.

## Vocabulary

**Mass production** — making large quantities of a standardized product
on an assembly line, which lowers the cost per item. **Installment
credit** — buying now and paying in regular parts over time, with
interest; it expands who can buy, and adds debt. **Margin buying** —
buying stock with mostly borrowed money, putting down only a fraction
of the price; a price fall can wipe out the buyer's money and leave
debt. **Bank run** — many depositors withdrawing at once because they
fear a bank will fail; before deposit insurance, even a healthy bank
could be drained. **Deflation** — a general fall in prices, which makes
debts harder to repay because incomes fall while the debt stays the
same. **Hooverville** — a shantytown of the early 1930s, named
sarcastically for President Herbert Hoover. **Dust Bowl** — the
drought-stricken southern Plains region of the 1930s where wind
erosion, after deep plowing removed native grasses, produced massive
dust storms. **Relief / recovery / reform** — the New Deal's "Three
R's": immediate help for people in need; measures to restart the
economy; permanent changes to prevent a repeat. **Collective
bargaining** — workers negotiating as a group, through a union, over
wages, hours, and conditions. **Court-packing plan** — President
Franklin D. Roosevelt's 1937 proposal to add justices to the Supreme
Court after it struck down New Deal laws; Congress did not pass it.
**Social insurance** — a government program, funded by contributions,
that pays benefits for defined risks such as old age or unemployment.

**Key figures and their sources (checked 2026-11-03):** Dow Jones
Industrial Average peak 381.17 (September 3, 1929) to 41.22 (July 8,
1932), about −89% — Federal Reserve History, "Stock Market Crash of
1929" (data via FRED). Unemployment estimates 1929–1940 (3.2%, 8.9%,
15.9%, 23.6%, 24.9%, 21.7%, 20.1%, 17.0%, 14.3%, 19.0%, 17.2%, 14.6%) —
U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics historical series (Lebergott
estimates, as republished by BLS); these are later estimates, not
figures anyone measured in 1933. Bank failures: about 9,000 banks,
roughly 30% of banks existing at the end of 1929, 1930–1933 —
Federal Reserve / Friedman and Schwartz tradition, as summarized in
Cambridge's *Banking Panics of the Great Depression* preface.
Social Security exclusions: the 1935 Act's old-age insurance left
out agricultural and domestic workers, about half the labor force
overall and about 65% of Black workers — Social Security
Administration history and the Roosevelt Institute summary; the
*reasons* for the exclusions are debated among historians (the
SSA's own historian emphasizes administrative feasibility; other
scholars emphasize racial politics), and this unit presents the
debate rather than settling it. Dust Bowl: Black Sunday, April 14,
1935; about 2.5 million people left the Plains states in the 1930s,
over 200,000 reaching California — Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Dust
Bowl." First Hundred Days: March 9–June 16, 1933 — standard
chronology; program dates are given per program in Lesson 4.
