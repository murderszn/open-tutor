# Lesson 1 — The 1920s: Mass Production, Credit, Culture — and Who Did Not Prosper

**Goal:** You will explain how mass production, installment credit, and
speculation created a 1920s boom, and why farmers, Black Americans,
recent immigrants, and many women did not share equally in it.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
timeline space. **Adult note:** this lesson covers racial violence,
nativism, and restrictive laws. Sources are documentary and textual;
frame period stereotypes as evidence of prejudice, never as humor.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Unit 03, name one way the First
World War changed the American economy (for example: it expanded
factory production and farm output for wartime demand). Keep that in
mind: much of the 1920s story is what happened when wartime demand
ended but production capacity did not shrink.

## Part 1 — Making more, cheaper, and selling it on time

In the 1910s and 1920s, American factories reorganized work around
the moving assembly line. Instead of a team of skilled workers
building one whole product, each worker repeated one small task while
the product moved past. Output rose and the cost per item fell. The
famous example is the Ford Model T: its price fell from about $850
in 1908 to under $300 by the mid-1920s, while its production ran into
the millions. Cars, radios, refrigerators, vacuum cleaners, and
washing machines moved from luxuries toward mass-market goods.

Two inventions of finance mattered as much as the inventions of
engineering. First, **installment credit**: stores let buyers take a
product home after a small down payment and pay the rest in monthly
parts, with interest. A family that could not pay $120 at once could
pay $10 a month. Second, **buying on margin** in the stock market:
investors could buy stock by putting down only about 10% of the
price and borrowing the rest from a broker, using the stock itself
as collateral. Both devices expanded who could participate — and
both added debt that had to be repaid whether incomes rose or not.

### Worked example 1 — What installment credit really costs

*Question:* A radio costs $120 cash. A store offers it for $10 down
plus $10 a month for 12 months. How much does the buyer pay in
total, and what is the extra cost of buying on time? (Practice
numbers, not a real store's terms.)

*Model reasoning:* Down payment $10 + 12 × $10 = $10 + $120 = $130
total. The extra cost is $130 − $120 = $10. That $10 is the price of
buying now instead of saving first. Multiply that logic across a
household buying a car, a refrigerator, and a washing machine on
time, and you can see how a family could owe several months of
income before any emergency arrived. Credit is not fraud — it is a
tool that makes spending easier and saving harder to notice. A
common error is to compare only the monthly payment ($10 sounds
small) instead of the total ($130) and the debt left if income falls.

### Worked example 2 — On paper vs. in practice: the new vote

*Question:* The 19th Amendment (1920) said the right to vote could
not be denied on account of sex. Did that mean American women could
all vote freely in 1925? Explain with one qualification.

*Model reasoning:* No — not all, and not freely everywhere. The
amendment removed one legal barrier, but Black women in the South
still faced poll taxes, literacy tests, and violence that also
blocked Black men, and many Native American women were not yet
recognized as citizens at all. The lesson from Unit 01 repeats here:
a constitutional change on paper is only as strong as the laws,
officials, and local power that enforce it. The amendment still
mattered enormously — millions of women voted for the first time —
but "women won the vote" is too simple a sentence. Always ask: which
women, where, and what stood in their way next?

## Part 2 — A cultural flowering — and a backlash

The 1920s were also a cultural decade. Jazz spread from New Orleans
and Chicago into dance halls and radio; the Harlem Renaissance made
Black writers, musicians, and artists central to American culture;
movies became a mass medium; radio carried sports, sermons, and
music into living rooms; and some young urban women — "flappers" —
cut their hair, shortened their skirts, smoked in public, and
claimed new freedom in manners and work.

The backlash was organized and powerful. Congress passed the
Emergency Quota Act (1921) and the Immigration Act (1924), which
sharply restricted immigration, especially from southern and
eastern Europe, and effectively barred Asian immigration. The Ku
Klux Klan, revived in 1915, grew to millions of members in the
1920s, targeting Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, and immigrants.
Prohibition (the 18th Amendment, effective 1920; repealed 1933)
banned the manufacture and sale of alcohol, which enriched
bootleggers, filled courts, and was enforced unevenly — harshly in
poor and immigrant neighborhoods, loosely among the wealthy. The
Scopes trial (1925) put evolution teaching on public trial in
Tennessee and became a national symbol of the fight between
modernist and fundamentalist America.

## Part 3 — The farm problem nobody solved

While cities boomed, farmers struggled through most of the decade.
During the war, farmers had borrowed to plow more land and grow more
wheat for Europe. When European farms recovered and wartime demand
ended, crop prices fell — but the debts stayed. A farmer who borrowed
$1,000 when wheat sold for $2 a bushel still owed $1,000 when it sold
for $1: the debt had doubled in real terms, because it now took twice
as much wheat to repay it. This is **deflation's** cruelty, and it is
the same mechanism that will crush debtors in Lesson 2. Farm distress
is the 1920s' most important warning sign: the boom's foundation —
people able to buy what factories and farms produced — was already
cracking for a large part of the country.

### Worked example 3 — Two 1920s, one decade

*Question:* A factory worker in Detroit in 1926 and a wheat farmer
in Kansas in 1926 both hear that "America is prosperous." Using
Parts 1–3, give one reason each might agree and one reason each
might disagree.

*Model reasoning:* The Detroit worker might agree because cars and
appliances are cheaper and wages in auto plants are relatively high;
she might disagree because her purchases are on installment debt,
and a layoff would leave the payments standing. The Kansas farmer
might agree because tractors and radios reach the farm; he would
more likely disagree because wheat prices have fallen while his
mortgage has not, so his real debt burden has grown. The point is
not that one of them is "right" about the decade. The point is that
"prosperity" is an average that can hide opposite experiences — and
any claim about a whole decade must name whose prosperity it means.

## Guided practice

**G1.** A washing machine costs $90 cash, or $5 down plus $10 a
month for 10 months. Compute the installment total and the extra
cost. What does the monthly figure hide?

**G2.** Explain in two sentences why margin buying (10% down) makes
a 20% price fall worse than losing 20% of the money you put in.
(Hint: work with $100 of stock bought with $10 of your own money.)

**G3.** Name two 1920s restrictions or backlashes from Part 2, and
for each, name the group most directly targeted.

## Independent practice

**I1.** Write a paragraph: was the 1920s economy healthy? Use one
piece of evidence for "yes" (production, prices, or culture) and one
for "no" (farm prices, debt, or exclusion), and end with a qualified
judgment — healthy for whom?

**I2.** A farmer borrows $2,000 when wheat is $2 a bushel. How many
bushels must she sell to repay the loan? If wheat falls to $1 a
bushel, how many bushels then? What does the change show about
deflation and debt? (Assume no interest, to isolate the price effect.)

**I3.** Error analysis: a classmate writes, "The 19th Amendment gave
all women the vote, so gender inequality ended in 1920." Identify
two errors in that sentence and correct each with a fact from this
lesson.

**I4.** Explain the connection between installment credit and the
Depression in advance: if many households already owe several
months of income, what happens to spending when one earner loses a
job? (Two to three sentences.)

## Applied task

Choose one 1920s group — Black Harlem artists, a Kansas wheat
farmer, a Detroit assembly-line worker, or a recent immigrant in a
northern city — and write a 6–8 sentence diary entry dated 1927 in
that person's voice, showing one part of the boom they enjoyed and
one part that excluded or burdened them. Label the entry
"original composite — not a real person's words." Do not copy any
real diary or memoir.

## Exit check

In one sentence, complete this claim: "The 1920s boom was real but
uneven, because ______." Your sentence must name one mechanism of
the boom and one group it left out.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "everyone got rich in the 1920s" — press
on farm prices and debt; (2) "credit is free money" — press on
totals vs. monthly payments; (3) "the vote ended inequality" —
press on enforcement gaps (Unit 01's on-paper/in-practice habit).
