# Lesson 4 — The First New Deal: The Hundred Days and the Three R's

**Goal:** You will sort the First New Deal's programs into relief,
recovery, and reform, explain what each major program did, and judge
each program's aims against its results.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
timeline space. **Adult note:** no sensitive-content warnings beyond
poverty and exclusion, which Lesson 5 treats in depth; preview the
AAA/sharecropper discussion for age-appropriate framing.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 2, name the banking
problem the New Deal had to solve first (bank runs with no deposit
insurance). Why would a president act on banks before anything else?
(If you can't: re-read Lesson 2, Part 2, cause 3 — a banking system
that cannot hold deposits cannot pay wages, farmers, or businesses.)

## Part 1 — The bank holiday: restoring trust first

Franklin D. Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, in the middle of
a banking panic: banks in all 48 states had closed or restricted
withdrawals. On March 6 he declared a national bank holiday,
closing every bank for four days while the government inspected
them. On March 9 Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act, letting
solvent banks reopen under federal supervision and reorganizing the
rest. On March 12 Roosevelt explained the plan by radio in the first
of his "fireside chats," telling listeners that reopened banks were
safe — famously urging that "the only thing we have to fear is fear
itself" in his inaugural address a week earlier (public-domain
speech; short phrase quoted). When banks reopened, deposits flowed
back in. The sequence matters: the holiday alone did nothing; the
inspection, the law, and the public explanation together restored
confidence, which is itself an economic force — people who believe
their deposits are safe do not run on banks.

Congress then worked at a pace that gave the period its name: the
**First Hundred Days**, March 9–June 16, 1933, produced more than a
dozen major laws. Roosevelt's team sorted their aims into the
"Three R's" — **relief** (immediate help), **recovery** (restarting
the economy), and **reform** (permanent changes to prevent a repeat).
Most programs mixed aims; sorting them is a thinking tool, not a
perfect filing system.

### Worked example 1 — Sorting a program

*Question:* The Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), created March
31, 1933, enrolled unemployed young men (ages 18–25) for six-month
terms planting trees, building trails, and fighting soil erosion in
national and state parks; they were paid $30 a month, of which $25
was sent home to their families. Is the CCC relief, recovery, or
reform — and why is more than one answer defensible?

*Model reasoning:* Primarily **relief**: it gave wages directly to
jobless young men and income to their families. Secondarily
**recovery**: it put money into circulation and built parks and
conservation works. Arguably **reform**: it created a lasting
conservation and parks legacy. The defensible answer names a primary
aim and one secondary aim, with evidence: "Relief first — wages for
the jobless — with recovery and conservation effects." A common
error is to force every program into exactly one box and then argue
about the box instead of the program. Use the Three R's to ask what
a program was *for*, then judge it against that purpose.

## Part 2 — The Hundred Days program by program

- **Federal Emergency Relief Administration (FERA, May 1933):**
  federal grants to states for direct relief — food, clothing, and
  cash for the jobless. Pure relief; it kept people alive but created
  no lasting jobs, which is why Roosevelt soon preferred work relief.
- **Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC, March 1933):** see Worked
  example 1. About 3 million young men enrolled over the program's
  life; its camps were segregated, a limit Lesson 5 returns to.
- **Public Works Administration (PWA, June 1933):** large-scale
  public construction — dams, bridges, schools, hospitals — contracted
  to private firms. Recovery through jobs and infrastructure, but
  slow to start because big projects take time to plan.
- **Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA, May 18, 1933):** a federal
  corporation to develop the Tennessee River valley — dams for flood
  control, hydroelectric power, fertilizer, and economic planning
  across seven states. Relief, recovery, and reform at once, and the
  most ambitious regional planning the federal government had tried.
- **Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA, May 12, 1933):**
  paid farmers to reduce production — plowing under crops and
  slaughtering livestock — to raise crop prices. It did raise farm
  income for landowners, but its payments went to owners, not to the
  sharecroppers and tenant farmers who actually worked the land,
  many of whom were displaced when owners took land out of
  production. Relief for some farmers, harm for others — the
  clearest early case of intended vs. unintended outcomes.
- **National Industrial Recovery Act / National Recovery
  Administration (NIRA/NRA, June 1933):** industry codes setting
  minimum wages, maximum hours, and fair-competition rules, with
  businesses displaying the Blue Eagle symbol. It aimed at recovery
  (raise wages so workers could buy more) and reform (end child
  labor in covered industries), but codes were hard to enforce and
  sometimes written by the largest firms for themselves.
- **Glass-Steagall Banking Act / Federal Deposit Insurance
  Corporation (FDIC, June 16, 1933):** separated commercial from
  investment banking and created federal deposit insurance, which
  began in 1934. Reform aimed directly at Lesson 2's bank-run
  mechanism: insured depositors have far less reason to panic.

### Worked example 2 — Judging the AAA honestly

*Question:* A supporter says the AAA worked because farm income
rose. A critic says it failed because sharecroppers were displaced.
Who is right?

*Model reasoning:* Both cite real facts, but they judge by
different measures. If the measure is "landowners' income," the AAA
succeeded in its first years. If the measure is "all farm people's
welfare," it failed many of the poorest — disproportionately Black
sharecroppers in the South — because payments followed ownership,
not labor. The honest evaluation states its measure before its
verdict: "The AAA raised farm income for owners and displaced many
tenants; whether it 'worked' depends on whose welfare counts."
This is the unit's evaluation discipline, and Lesson 5 applies it
to the whole New Deal. A common error is to pick the measure that
flatters your conclusion and hide it.

## Part 3 — Critics from both sides

The New Deal was attacked from the right as government overreach
and socialism, and from the left as too timid — by figures like
Senator Huey Long ("Share Our Wealth") and Dr. Francis Townsend
(old-age pensions), who argued it did not redistribute enough.
Business critics said codes and regulation strangled recovery;
labor and populist critics said relief was stingy and left
corporate power intact. Roosevelt's answer, in practice, was
experimentation: try programs, keep what seemed to work, replace
what did not. That experimental stance is itself a claim worth
testing — Lesson 5 asks whether the Second New Deal was a change of
direction or more of the same experimentation.

## Guided practice

**G1.** Sort each program into its primary R (relief, recovery, or
reform) and name one secondary aim: FERA, CCC, TVA, FDIC.

**G2.** Explain in two sentences why the bank holiday had to be
paired with inspection and a law — why closing banks alone would
not restore trust.

**G3.** Name one group the AAA helped and one group it harmed, and
state the mechanism (payments followed what?) that produced both
results.

## Independent practice

**I1.** Write a paragraph judging the NRA: give one aim it met (or
partly met), one aim it missed, and one enforcement problem from
Part 2.

**I2.** Error analysis: a classmate writes, "The Hundred Days fixed
the Depression." Identify two errors (timing and evidence) using
Lesson 2's unemployment table: what was unemployment in 1934 and
1935?

**I3.** Compare FERA and the CCC as relief: which gave more dignity
or skill to the recipient, and what is the cost of that difference?
(Two to three sentences; there is no single right answer.)

**I4.** A critic says, "The TVA was socialism." Using Part 2, give
one fact that supports the critic's worry about government power
and one fact that complicates it (what the TVA actually built and
for whom).
