# Lesson 5 — The Second New Deal, the Courts, and How to Judge a Policy

**Goal:** You will explain the Second New Deal's major laws, its
exclusions, and the Supreme Court battles over it, and you will
argue — with your measure and limitations stated — whether the New
Deal "worked."

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
Lesson 2's unemployment table. **Adult note:** this lesson covers
racial exclusion, labor conflict, and constitutional crisis. Frame
the exclusion debate (Part 2) as historians do: the fact of
exclusion is documented; its motives are debated.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 4, name one program
whose benefits followed ownership rather than labor, and one group
harmed as a result. (Answer: the AAA; sharecroppers and tenant
farmers, many of them Black Southerners, displaced when land left

## Part 1 — The Second New Deal (1935–1938)

By 1935 critics from the left (Long, Townsend) and the continuing
misery shown in the unemployment table — still 20.1% in 1935 —
pushed Roosevelt toward a "Second New Deal" aimed more at lasting
reform and workers' security:

- **Works Progress Administration (WPA, 1935):** work relief on a
  vast scale — roads, bridges, schools, parks, and also art, music,
  theater, and writing projects (Federal Project Number One). Over
  its life it employed millions of Americans (about 8.5 million
  people at various times); it was the largest work-relief program
  and the most visible New Deal presence in many towns.
- **National Labor Relations Act / Wagner Act (July 5, 1935):**
  guaranteed workers' right to organize unions and bargain
  collectively, and created the National Labor Relations Board to
  police unfair labor practices. Union membership surged, and the
  act remains a foundation of American labor law.
- **Social Security Act (August 14, 1935):** created federal
  old-age insurance (monthly benefits for retired workers in
  covered employment, beginning in the 1940s), unemployment
  insurance run through the states, and aid for dependent children,
  the blind, and others. It was the first national social-insurance
  system in the United States — and, as Part 2 shows, a partial one.
- **Fair Labor Standards Act (1938):** set a federal minimum wage,
  a 44-hour (later 40-hour) workweek standard with overtime, and
  restricted child labor. It closed the Hundred Days' unfinished
  labor agenda after the NRA's fall (Part 3).

### Worked example 1 — Relief that leaves a building

*Question:* A WPA crew builds a school in a small town in 1936.
Name one relief effect, one recovery effect, and one reform effect
of that single project.

*Model reasoning:* Relief: wages for jobless workers and their
families now. Recovery: wages spent in local shops, and a usable
school that raises the town's capacity. Reform: a precedent that
the federal government can directly employ people in a depression
— a role it had never taken at this scale. The same project can be
judged three ways, which is why Lesson 4's "state your measure"
rule matters: a critic who counts only immediate cost will miss
the building; a supporter who counts only the building will miss
the cost. Judge with all three lenses, and say which one you weigh
most.

## Part 2 — Who the New Deal left out

The New Deal's most serious limit was exclusion, and the Social
Security Act is the clearest case. Its old-age insurance originally
covered only workers in commerce and industry. Agricultural labor
and domestic service — along with government, nonprofit, and
self-employed workers — were left out. Because of that design, about
half the labor force had no old-age coverage at first, and about
65% of Black workers were excluded, since so many Black workers
were farm laborers or domestic workers, especially in the South.
Coverage was extended to farm and domestic workers in 1950 and
1954, but a generation had already worked uncovered.

Historians debate *why* the exclusions happened. The Social
Security Administration's own historian argues the design followed
administrative feasibility — payroll taxes were hard to collect
from farm and domestic employers. Other scholars argue that
Southern members of Congress, whose support the bill needed,
insisted on protecting the Southern labor system. This unit does
not settle that debate for you. What is not debated is the *effect*:
the program that became America's most beloved safety net began by
leaving out the workers most in need of one. Other exclusions ran
through the New Deal: CCC camps were segregated; AAA payments
followed owners (Lesson 4); many WPA and relief jobs were
distributed through local officials who discriminated; and New Deal
housing programs (from 1934 onward) later helped build segregated
suburbs. A New Deal evaluation that ignores exclusion is incomplete
by its own measure — "for whom?" is part of the question.

### Worked example 2 — Debating motives without inventing facts

*Question:* Two historians disagree: one says the Social Security
exclusions were about tax collection; the other says they were
about race and Southern politics. What evidence would help you
decide, and what should you *not* claim while the debate is open?

*Model reasoning:* Helpful evidence: the congressional record
(who proposed the exclusions and what reasons they gave), the
Treasury's tax-collection memos, the NAACP's testimony at the
time (Charles Hamilton Houston warned Congress the exclusions
would fall hardest on Black workers), and later coverage
expansions. While the debate is open, do *not* claim either motive
as proven fact, and do not treat the effect as in doubt — the
exclusion and its disproportionate impact are documented
regardless of motive. This is D2.His.6.9-12 in practice: writers'
perspectives shape histories, so a responsible argument separates
what happened from why people say it happened.

## Part 3 — The Court strikes back, and the plan that failed

The Supreme Court, with a conservative majority, struck down the
NIRA in *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States* (1935) — the
"sick chicken" case — ruling Congress had delegated too much
lawmaking power to the president and that the codes regulated
intrastate commerce beyond federal power. It struck down the AAA
in *United States v. Butler* (1936) on taxing-and-spending grounds.
In February 1937 Roosevelt proposed adding a justice for every
justice over 70 who did not retire — up to six new justices — a
plan universally called "court-packing." Congress, including many
Democrats, refused: even supporters of the New Deal saw the plan
as a threat to judicial independence. The Court then upheld a
state minimum-wage law in *West Coast Hotel v. Parrish* (1937) —
the "switch in time that saved nine" — and later New Deal laws
fared better. The episode teaches a constitutional lesson the
whole course returns to: in the American system, a popular
president, a Congress, and a Court can each block the others, and
reform must survive all three.

Then came the relapse. In 1937 Roosevelt, worried about deficits,
cut WPA and relief spending and the Federal Reserve tightened
credit; unemployment jumped from 14.3% (1937) to 19.0% (1938).
Whether spending cuts, monetary tightening, or both caused the
"Roosevelt recession" is debated — but the episode is strong
evidence that recovery was fragile and policy-dependent, not
automatic. Full employment did not return until wartime
mobilization in the 1940s (Unit 05's subject), which is itself part
of the evaluation: the New Deal relieved suffering, reformed
American government permanently (Social Security, labor law,
deposit insurance, securities regulation), and did *not* by itself
restore full employment.

### Worked example 3 — Writing a verdict with limitations

*Question:* Write a one-paragraph verdict: "Did the New Deal work?"
Your paragraph must (a) state your measure, (b) give two pieces of
evidence for it, (c) name one group it left out, and (d) state one
limitation of your evidence.

*Model reasoning (sample):* "Measured by lasting reform and relief
from destitution, the New Deal worked: it created deposit insurance
that ended the bank-run mechanism of Lesson 2, and Social Security
still pays old-age benefits today. Measured by full employment, it
did not: unemployment was still 14.6% in 1940. It left out most
Black farm and domestic workers from Social Security at first.
One limitation: unemployment figures for the 1930s are later
estimates, not contemporary measurements, so small year-to-year
changes should not carry large conclusions." Note the structure,
not just the conclusion: measure, evidence, exclusion, limitation.
Any verdict missing one of the four is an incomplete argument for
this unit's purposes.

## Independent practice

**I1.** Write a paragraph on the court-packing plan: why Roosevelt
proposed it, why Congress refused, and what the episode shows about

**I2.** Error analysis: a classmate writes, "The New Deal ended the
Depression because unemployment fell every year after 1933."

**I3.** Compare two measures of "worked": (a) lasting institutions
still in use today, (b) unemployment in 1940. Give one New Deal

measure you weigh more and why (two to three sentences).

**I4.** A historian writes that the New Deal "betrayed Black
Americans." Another writes that it "helped Black Americans more
than any previous federal program." Using Part 2, give one fact
supporting each historian, and explain how both can be partly true.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "the New Deal ended the Depression" —
press on 1940 unemployment (14.6%) and wartime mobilization;
(2) "exclusion means the programs did nothing" — press on real
relief *and* real exclusion coexisting; (3) "court-packing
succeeded" — it did not pass; the Court's shift and Congress's
refusal are both part of the story. Answer-key reference: Lesson 5,
G1–G3 and I1–I4 in `answer-key.md`.
