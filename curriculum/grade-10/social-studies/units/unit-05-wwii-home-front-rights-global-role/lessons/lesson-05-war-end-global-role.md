# Lesson 5 — The War's End and America's Global Role: Victory, the Bomb, and a New Order

**Goal:** You will explain how the war ended, why the atomic bomb
decision is still debated, and how the United States built a new
international order — and you will argue, with evidence, whether the
war resolved America's prewar crises or relocated them.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Adult note:** this lesson covers the atomic bombings and mass
civilian death. Sources are documentary and textual; no graphic
imagery is used. Give advance notice, allow reflection alternatives,
and keep the discussion on evidence, responsibility, and remembrance
— not on reliving the horror.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 1, recall the Atlantic
Charter's promise: a postwar world of self-government and free trade.
Now ask: when the war ended in 1945, who got to decide what that
world looked like — and who was not in the room?

## Part 1 — The last year: D-Day, the fall of Germany, and the Pacific

By 1944 the Allies were winning on both oceans. On **June 6, 1944
— D-Day** — about 156,000 Allied troops landed in Normandy, France,
in the largest seaborne invasion in history; Paris was liberated in
August, and Germany surrendered on **May 8, 1945 (V-E Day)**. In the
Pacific, American forces had turned the tide at Midway in June 1942
and then fought island by island toward Japan — Guadalcanal, Tarawa,
Iwo Jima, Okinawa — at terrible cost on both sides. The Soviet Union,
which had borne the heaviest fighting against Germany, was promised
a role in the Pacific war. In February 1945 the Allied leaders met at
**Yalta** to plan the postwar world; in July–August 1945 they met at
**Potsdam** and issued the **Potsdam Declaration** (July 26, 1945),
demanding Japan's unconditional surrender. Japan's leaders, hoping
for a negotiated peace and for Soviet mediation, did not accept.

President Roosevelt had died on April 12, 1945. His successor,
Harry S. Truman, learned about the atomic bomb only after taking
office: the **Manhattan Project** had spent nearly $2 billion and
employed more than 130,000 people to build a weapon no one had ever
used. Its first test, **Trinity**, lit the New Mexico desert on July
16, 1945.

### Worked example 1 — Two accounts, one decision

*Question:* Two historians describe the bomb decision differently.
Historian A: "Truman dropped the bomb to end the war quickly and
save American lives that an invasion of Japan would have cost."
Historian B: "Truman dropped the bomb to intimidate the Soviet Union
and shape the postwar world." Can both be partly right? How would you
decide how much weight to give each?

*Model reasoning:* Both can be partly right, because a decision can
have more than one motive, and because the two historians are using
different kinds of evidence. Historian A relies on the military
evidence available in 1945: the casualty forecasts for an invasion,
the ferocity of Okinawa, and Truman's own statements about saving
lives. Historian B relies on the diplomatic evidence: the timing of
the bomb relative to the Soviet entry into the Pacific war, and the
messages American officials sent about postwar influence. The honest
move is not to pick a winner but to ask what each account explains
and what it leaves out: A explains the timing but not why two bombs,
three days apart; B explains the diplomacy but not why the military
argument persuaded Truman's advisers. A common error is to treat a
debate as a contest with one right side. Historians disagree because
the evidence is genuinely mixed — and because the decision itself had
mixed motives.

### Worked example 2 — Numbers with names: reading casualty estimates

*Question:* Hiroshima, August 6, 1945: about 70,000 people killed
instantly, about 140,000 dead by the end of 1945. Nagasaki, August 9,
1945: about 35,000–40,000 killed immediately, about 70,000 by the
end of 1945. Why do historians give ranges and "by the end of 1945"
figures instead of one exact number?

*Model reasoning:* Because an exact number does not exist. Records
were destroyed, bodies were unidentifiable, and many people died
weeks or months later from burns and radiation sickness — deaths
that belong to the bombing but are hard to count. The ranges are
honest: they say "we know it was at least this many, and probably
about this many, and here is the date we stopped counting." A common
error is to treat the lower number as "the" death toll and the higher
number as exaggeration. The lower number is usually the immediate
deaths; the higher number includes the deaths that followed. Both
numbers are true about different things — and both describe
civilians, mostly, in cities that also held military targets. The
numbers also do not include the survivors, the *hibakusha*, who lived
with injuries and illness for decades.

## Part 2 — The new order: the UN, Bretton Woods, and the GI Bill

The war's winners tried to build institutions that would prevent the
next war and the next depression. The **United Nations** was founded
by a Charter signed in San Francisco on **June 26, 1945**, creating a
Security Council of great powers (with vetoes) and a General Assembly
of all member states — a stronger League of Nations, deliberately
designed to keep the United States inside it. At **Bretton Woods**
in July 1944, Allied delegates created the **International Monetary
Fund** and the **World Bank** to stabilize currencies and rebuild
war-torn economies. And at home, the **GI Bill** (the Servicemen's
Readjustment Act, **June 22, 1944**) gave returning veterans money
for college, low-cost home loans, and unemployment benefits — by
1956, about 7.8 million veterans had used it for education or
training, and millions bought homes. The GI Bill helped create the
postwar middle class — and, like the New Deal, it did so unevenly:
Black veterans were often steered away from the best colleges and
neighborhoods by segregation and redlining, so the bill's benefits
flowed mostly to white veterans.

## Part 3 — Did the war resolve America's crises, or relocate them?

The Depression was over: unemployment, 14.6% in 1940, had fallen
below 2% by 1943, and the economy was producing at a scale no one
had imagined in 1932. The United States emerged as the world's
strongest power, with the only atomic weapons, the largest economy,
and the central role in the UN, the IMF, and the World Bank. But the
prewar crises had not vanished. Racial inequality was intact:
segregation in the South, discrimination in the North, and the
incarceration's unanswered questions (Lesson 3). The government was
far larger and more powerful than in 1932 — and the war had shown
what that power could do to its own citizens. The labor movement
was stronger, and so were the corporations. The atomic bomb had
created a weapon that made the next war potentially unsurvivable.
In the next unit, the Cold War will pick up exactly here: a world
reshaped by this war, with its promises unkept and its dangers new.

**Teacher note (misconception):** learners often say "the bomb ended
the war" as if nothing else mattered. The Soviet declaration of war
on Japan (August 8, 1945) and the naval blockade had also brought
Japan close to surrender; historians still debate how much weight
each factor deserves. The careful sentence is: the bombs were the
immediate trigger of Japan's surrender, and they were not the only
pressure on Japan. See the answer key for the guided-practice
solutions.

## Guided practice

1. Place these events in order with dates: (a) D-Day; (b) Trinity
   test; (c) Hiroshima; (d) Potsdam Declaration; (e) Japan's formal
   surrender; (f) V-E Day.
2. ★ A classmate says, "The atomic bomb killed 140,000 people in
   Hiroshima." Correct the sentence in 2–3 sentences, using the
   distinction between immediate deaths and deaths by the end of
   1945.
3. Name one institution created in 1944–45 and one problem it was
   designed to solve.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 4–5 sentences how the GI Bill helped create the
   postwar middle class — and why its benefits were uneven.
5. The United Nations was designed to keep the United States inside
   it, unlike the League of Nations. Explain in 3–4 sentences why
   that design choice mattered, using the Unit 03 lesson on the
   League debate.
6. ★ Evaluate Historian A and Historian B from Worked example 1
   against one piece of evidence each: for A, the casualty forecasts
   for an invasion; for B, the timing of the Soviet declaration of
   war (August 8, 1945) between the two bombings. Which account does
   each piece of evidence support, and what does neither piece prove?
   Write 5–6 sentences.

## Applied task

7. Write a 100-word radio address as President Truman on August 14,
   1945, announcing Japan's surrender. Include: one fact about the
   cost of the war, one hope for the postwar world, and one thing you
   will *not* promise. Then write one sentence explaining why you
   chose not to promise that thing. (This is an original composition
   exercise, not a quotation of Truman's real address.)

## Exit check

8. In one sentence each: (a) What was the Manhattan Project? (b)
   When did Japan formally surrender? (c) Name one postwar
   institution from this lesson and what it was for.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the casualty figures are given as
  ranges with the counting date stated; the adult reads the two
  historians' positions aloud and defines *unconditional surrender*,
  *veto*, and *redlining* in context.
- **Alternate response mode:** the timeline task may use pre-written
  event cards; the radio address may be dictated to the adult.
- **Extension:** compare the Yalta and Potsdam conferences with the
  Versailles settlement in Unit 03: what did the 1945 peacemakers
  try to do differently, and did it work? Write a paragraph with one
  parallel and one difference.
