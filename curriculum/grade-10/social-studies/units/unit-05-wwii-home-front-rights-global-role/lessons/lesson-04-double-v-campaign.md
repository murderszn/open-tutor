# Lesson 4 — The Double V Campaign: Fighting Fascism Abroad and Racism at Home

**Goal:** You will explain how Black Americans used the war to press
for full citizenship — through the Double V campaign, Executive Order
8802, and distinguished military service — and why the war changed
some things and left others standing.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Adult note:** this lesson covers segregation, racial violence, and
discrimination in wartime America. Sources are documentary and
textual; frame period language about race as evidence of the era's
prejudices, never as neutral description.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 3, name one way the
government limited a group's rights during the war. Now ask the
opposite question: how did the people whose rights were limited use
the war itself as leverage to claim those rights?

## Part 1 — "Should I sacrifice to live 'half American'?"

On January 31, 1942 — seven weeks after Pearl Harbor — the
*Pittsburgh Courier*, the most widely read Black newspaper in America,
published a letter from James G. Thompson, a 26-year-old cafeteria
worker at an aircraft plant in Wichita, Kansas. Thompson asked a
question that cut through the patriotic noise: "Should I sacrifice my
life to live half American?" He proposed that Black Americans adopt a
"double V" — the first V for victory over enemies abroad, the second
V for victory over enemies of equality at home. A week later, on
February 7, 1942, the *Courier* launched the **Double V campaign**,
and it spread fast: about 200,000 people joined Double V clubs, and
the campaign's symbols appeared on buttons, bumper stickers, and
beauty pageants. The campaign's genius was its framing: it did not
ask Black Americans to choose between loyalty and protest. It said
loyalty *was* the protest — serving the country while demanding the
country live up to its promises.

The campaign had a target before the war even started. In 1940–41,
defense plants were hiring by the millions — and turning Black
workers away. A. Philip Randolph, leader of the Brotherhood of
Sleeping Car Porters, threatened a March on Washington for July 1,
1941, to protest discrimination in defense jobs and the segregated
military. President Roosevelt, fearing the march, issued **Executive
Order 8802** on June 25, 1941 — banning discrimination in defense
industries and government "because of race, creed, color, or national
origin" and creating the **Fair Employment Practice Committee (FEPC)**
to hear complaints. It was the first federal action against employment
discrimination, and the first presidential civil-rights directive
since Reconstruction. Randolph called off the march. But the order's
limits were real: the FEPC had no power to punish employers, and in
the South it was largely ignored. On paper vs. in practice, again —
and the Double V campaign was, in part, a way of keeping score.

### Worked example 1 — A lever, not just a slogan

*Question:* Why was the Double V campaign more than a patriotic
slogan? Use the threat of the 1941 march and Executive Order 8802 to
explain.

*Model reasoning:* The campaign worked as a lever because it linked
two things the government needed and one thing Black Americans wanted.
The government needed Black workers and Black soldiers for total war;
Black Americans wanted jobs, fair treatment, and citizenship. The
threatened March on Washington showed the lever in action: Randolph
did not ask politely — he threatened a mass march that would embarrass
the government, and the government answered with an executive order.
The Double V slogan then kept the pressure on by making the bargain
public: we will serve, and in return the country must change. A
common error is to treat protest as the opposite of patriotism. Here
protest *used* patriotism — the language of American ideals — as its
strongest argument.

### Worked example 2 — Service under segregation

*Question:* The Tuskegee Airmen were the first Black military
aviators; the 442nd Regimental Combat Team was the most decorated
unit of its size in American history. Did their service end
segregation in the armed forces? Explain with dates.

*Model reasoning:* No. The Tuskegee Airmen (about 1,000 Black pilots
trained at Tuskegee, Alabama, 1941–1945; the 332nd Fighter Group
flew combat missions from 1943) and the 442nd (activated February 1,
1943; Japanese American volunteers, many with families in the camps;
21 Medals of Honor) both served in *segregated* units — the military
kept Black and white soldiers apart throughout the war. Their
distinguished records became evidence *for* desegregation, but the
policy did not change until President Harry Truman's Executive Order
9981 in 1948 — three years after the war ended. A common error is to
treat heroic service as if it automatically changed policy. Service
created the argument; political action later made the change.

## Part 2 — The limits of wartime unity: riots and the home front's other face

The war brought millions of people into crowded defense cities, and
the crowding turned violent. In **June 1943**, Detroit erupted in a
race riot after a fight on Belle Isle: 34 people were killed (25
Black, 9 white), hundreds were injured, and federal troops had to
restore order. The same month, in Los Angeles, white servicemen
attacked Mexican American teenagers wearing zoot suits — wide,
flamboyant suits that the attackers treated as unpatriotic excess in
wartime — in the **Zoot Suit Riots**; police mostly arrested the
victims. In the South, Black soldiers in uniform were beaten and
sometimes killed for using the wrong waiting room or sitting in the
wrong seat. These events belong in the same lesson as the Double V
campaign, because they are the campaign's evidence: the second V was
not an abstraction. It was a response to things happening on American
streets while the country fought a war against racism abroad.

And yet the war also created openings that outlasted it. About a
million Black southerners moved north and west during the 1940s for
defense work. Black workers entered industrial jobs in numbers never
seen before, joined unions, and built organizations — the NAACP's
membership grew from about 50,000 in 1940 to about 450,000 by 1946. Black newspapers like
the *Courier* built national audiences. The war did not end Jim Crow.
But it gave the civil rights movement its organizers, its arguments,
and its audience — and the movement that won the victories of the
1950s and 1960s was built by people who had fought the Double V
fight. That is the through-line to Unit 06.

## Part 3 — Others who served: Navajo Code Talkers and the Bracero workers

Two more groups complicate any simple story. The **Navajo Code
Talkers** — more than 400 Navajo Marines — used their language to
build a code the Japanese never broke, transmitting orders in the
Pacific battles. Their service was classified for decades, so the
public did not know about it during the war. And the **Bracero
Program** (Lesson 2) brought Mexican farmworkers under government
contract — needed labor, but with weak protections, low pay, and
discrimination that Executive Order 8802's promise did not reach.
Both cases teach the same lesson as the Double V: wartime service and
wartime labor were real contributions, and the country's gratitude
was uneven.

**Teacher note (misconception):** learners often say "the war ended
racism" or "nothing changed." Both are wrong in the same way: they
treat change as all-or-nothing. The honest sentence is that the war
moved the *argument* — it made the contradiction between American
ideals and American practice visible to millions of people, and it
gave Black Americans organizations and leverage they used after the
war. Change is a process with dates, not a switch. See the answer key
for the guided-practice solutions.

## Guided practice

1. In 2–3 sentences, explain what the "double V" meant and where the
   idea came from.
2. ★ A classmate says, "Executive Order 8802 ended discrimination in
   defense jobs." Use two facts from this lesson to correct the claim.
3. Why did the military's segregation matter for the Double V
   argument? Answer in 2–3 sentences, using the Tuskegee Airmen or
   the 442nd as your example.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 4–5 sentences how the threatened March on Washington
   (1941) led to Executive Order 8802 — and why the march's
   *cancellation* was itself a result, not a failure.
5. The Detroit riot (June 1943) and the Double V campaign happened
   in the same year. Explain in 4–5 sentences how the riot supports
   the campaign's claim that a second victory was needed at home.
6. ★ Compare the Navajo Code Talkers and the Bracero workers: both
   contributed to the war effort. Name one way their treatment was
   similar and one way it was different, and explain what that
   difference shows about how the country valued different kinds of
   contribution.

## Applied task

7. Write a 100-word Double V club flyer for your own town in 1942:
   name two things your club will do (one supporting the war effort,
   one pressing for equality at home) and one thing it will *not* do.
   Then write one sentence explaining why the flyer pairs the two
   kinds of action.

## Exit check

8. In one sentence each: (a) What was the Double V campaign? (b)
   What did Executive Order 8802 do, and what was its main limit?
   (c) Name one segregated unit that served with distinction and one
   date when segregation in the armed forces finally ended.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Thompson's letter is paraphrased
  with one short attributed phrase; the adult defines *segregation*,
  *leverage*, and *redress* in context and reads the Double V
  definition aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** the flyer may be drawn as a poster
  with a caption; the comparison task may use a two-column chart
  instead of prose.
- **Extension:** read the NYPL teaching page on the Double V
  campaign (see the Resource Pack) and find one primary-source
  detail it quotes that this lesson does not use. Write a paragraph
  on what that detail adds — and what a single detail cannot prove
  on its own.
