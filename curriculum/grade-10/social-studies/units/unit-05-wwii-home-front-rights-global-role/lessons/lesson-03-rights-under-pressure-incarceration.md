# Lesson 3 — Rights Under Pressure: Executive Order 9066 and Japanese American Incarceration

**Goal:** You will explain how and why the United States incarcerated
about 120,000 people of Japanese descent during World War II, what
the Supreme Court said about it, and how later generations judged
that decision.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Adult note:** this lesson covers forced removal, incarceration,
and racial discrimination. Sources are documentary and textual; no
graphic imagery is used. Give advance notice, allow reflection
alternatives, and keep discussion focused on evidence and
responsibility.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Unit 01, recall the difference
between a right *on paper* and a right *in practice*. The 14th
Amendment says no state shall deprive any person of liberty without
due process of law. Hold that sentence in mind: this lesson is about
a moment when the government set it aside for an entire group of
people.

## Part 1 — From Pearl Harbor to Executive Order 9066

After Pearl Harbor, fear and anger fell hardest on Japanese
Americans — even though no Japanese American was ever found guilty of
espionage or sabotage for Japan during the war. The government had
already compiled lists of Japanese community leaders before the
attack, and the FBI arrested more than a thousand of them in the
days after December 7. Newspapers and politicians on the West Coast
demanded removal. On **February 19, 1942**, President Roosevelt
signed **Executive Order 9066**, which authorized military
commanders to designate military areas "from which any or all persons
may be excluded." The order never used the words "Japanese" or
"Japanese American" — but it was applied to them. In the spring of
1942, about 120,000 people of Japanese descent were forced from
their homes on the West Coast. About two-thirds were U.S. citizens —
**Nisei**, American-born, whose parents (**Issei**) were immigrants
barred by law from becoming citizens at all. Families were given days
to sell homes, farms, and businesses at whatever price they could
get, then sent first to temporary "assembly centers" — often
fairgrounds and racetracks — and then to ten remote camps run by the
War Relocation Authority (WRA) in the interior West and Arkansas. The
first camp, Manzanar in California, opened in March 1942; the
largest, Tule Lake, held nearly 19,000 people.

Life in the camps was organized around barracks, mess halls, schools,
and barbed wire. There were jobs, newspapers, baseball leagues, and
schools — and also armed guards, watchtowers, curfews, and loyalty
questionnaires. In 1943 the government asked incarcerated adults two
questions that became infamous: would they swear loyalty to the
United States, and would they serve in the armed forces? For people
whose country had locked them up, the questions were a trap — a "yes"
could mean abandoning parents who could not become citizens, and a
"no" meant being labeled disloyal. About 12,000 people who answered
"no" — or refused to answer — were moved to Tule Lake, which became a
segregation center. Meanwhile, in January 1943, the War Department
called for Nisei volunteers for a new combat unit, the 442nd
Regimental Combat Team (Lesson 4).

### Worked example 1 — Reading an order that does not say what it does

*Question:* Executive Order 9066 never mentions Japanese Americans.
A classmate says, "Then the order was neutral — it could have applied
to anyone." What is wrong with that argument?

*Model reasoning:* The argument confuses the order's words with its
use. The order gave commanders the power to exclude "any or all
persons" from military areas; General John L. DeWitt then used that
power only against people of Japanese descent on the West Coast —
including American citizens — while German and Italian Americans were
not removed as a group. This is the on-paper vs. in-practice pattern
from Unit 01 and Lesson 1: a neutral-sounding rule can be applied
discriminatorily, and the discrimination is in the application. To
evaluate the order, you must look at what officials did with it, not
only at what it said. A common error is to quote a document's
neutral language as proof that its effects were neutral.

### Worked example 2 — Majority reasoning vs. dissent

*Question:* In *Korematsu v. United States* (1944), the Supreme Court
ruled 6–3 that Fred Korematsu's conviction for refusing the exclusion
order was constitutional. Justice Hugo Black, writing for the
majority, said the exclusion was justified by military necessity and
that the Court could not second-guess the military's judgment in
wartime. Justice Robert Jackson, dissenting,
warned that the decision would lie "like a loaded weapon, ready for
the hand of any authority that can bring forward a plausible claim of
an urgent need." Which opinion looks at the case's future, and which
looks at its present — and why does that matter?

*Model reasoning:* The majority looks at the present: it asks whether
the government had a military reason *in 1942*, and accepts the
Army's claim that it did, without requiring proof of individual
disloyalty. The dissent looks at the future: it asks what rule the
decision creates for the *next* emergency — a rule that lets the
government single out a group by ancestry whenever it can claim an
urgent need. Both opinions agree the case is about more than one man;
they disagree about which danger matters more — the danger the
government claimed in 1942, or the danger the ruling itself creates.
That is why dissents matter in history: they name the cost of the
majority's choice. The same day, the Court ruled unanimously in *Ex
parte Endo* that a loyal citizen could not be detained — and the
government rescinded the exclusion orders the day before, on
December 17, 1944.

## Part 2 — What the record shows, and what it cannot show

The documentary record of incarceration is unusually complete — and
unusually one-sided. We have the orders, the camp reports, the
Supreme Court opinions, and the photographs the government allowed.
We have far fewer records of what incarcerated families thought at
the time, because their letters were censored, their newspapers were
supervised, and many families destroyed papers rather than risk
trouble. Two facts about the record matter for every source task in
this unit:

1. **No evidence of disloyalty was ever produced.** The government's
   own later review — the Commission on Wartime Relocation and
   Internment of Civilians, reporting in 1983 — concluded that the
   removal was driven by "race prejudice, war hysteria, and a failure
   of political leadership," not by military necessity. The
   commission found that no Japanese American was convicted of
   espionage or sabotage.
2. **The government hid evidence from the Court.** In 1983, Fred
   Korematsu's conviction was vacated after newly found documents
   showed that government lawyers had suppressed a military report
   contradicting the claim of military necessity. The Supreme Court's
   1944 decision has never been formally overturned, but the Court
   itself disavowed it in 2018, calling it "gravely wrong the day it
   was decided."

## Part 3 — The reckoning: apology and redress

The camps closed by the end of 1945 (Tule Lake, the last, in March
1946), and families returned to find homes sold, businesses gone, and
communities scattered. It took more than forty years for the federal
government to answer. The **Civil Liberties Act of 1988**, signed by
President Ronald Reagan, formally apologized for the incarceration
and paid $20,000 in redress to each surviving internee. The Act's
language is worth reading closely: it called the incarceration a
"grave injustice" caused by prejudice, hysteria, and failed
leadership — the same three causes the 1983 commission named. The
reckoning was partial: no payment can restore lost years, lost
property, or lost trust, and many survivors had already died. But the
apology matters as a historical source: it is the government judging
its own earlier action, in writing, with reasons.

**Teacher note (misconception):** learners often say "the camps were
necessary because we were at war" or "the camps were fine because
people survived them." Both sentences skip the question the unit
keeps asking: necessary *for what*, and judged by *whose* evidence?
The government never showed that any individual Japanese American
was disloyal, and it applied the policy by ancestry, not by conduct.
Survival is not the same as justice. See the answer key for the
guided-practice solutions.

## Guided practice

1. In your own words, what did Executive Order 9066 authorize, and
   to whom was it applied in practice? (2–3 sentences.)
2. ★ A classmate says, "The Supreme Court approved the camps, so
   they must have been legal." Use *Ex parte Endo* and the 1983
   findings to correct that sentence in 3–4 sentences.
3. Why did the loyalty questionnaire put incarcerated Nisei in an
   impossible position? Name one way a "yes" answer and one way a
   "no" answer could each be held against the person answering.

## Independent practice

4. The 1983 commission named three causes: race prejudice, war
   hysteria, and a failure of political leadership. For each cause,
   write one sentence of evidence from this lesson.
5. Explain in 4–5 sentences why the Civil Liberties Act of 1988 is
   both a meaningful reckoning and an incomplete one.
6. ★ "The government hid evidence from the Court." If that is true,
   what does it imply about the Korematsu majority's reasoning — and
   what does it *not* prove about what the justices knew in 1944?
   Write 5–6 sentences.

## Applied task

7. Write a 100-word museum label for a photograph of a camp
   barracks (the photograph itself is not shown here — describe what
   the label must explain instead). Your label must include: who was
   held there, why the government said so, what later evidence
   showed, and one question visitors should ask. Do not invent
   details about any real person.

## Exit check

8. In one sentence each: (a) What was Executive Order 9066? (b) How
   many people were incarcerated, and what fraction were U.S.
   citizens? (c) Name one Supreme Court case from this lesson and
   what it decided.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the order's key sentence is quoted
  verbatim (public domain) and then paraphrased; the adult reads
  Jackson's dissent line aloud and defines *exclusion*, *due
  process*, and *redress* in context.
- **Alternate response mode:** the museum label may be dictated or
  drawn as a labeled sketch with a caption; the timeline of the
  incarceration (February 1942 order → camps → December 1944
  decisions → 1988 Act) may be completed with pre-written event
  cards.
- **Extension:** read the National Archives page on Executive Order
  9066 (see the Resource Pack) and compare its summary with this
  lesson's account: what does the Archives page emphasize that the
  lesson does not, and vice versa? Write a paragraph.
