# Lesson 1 — The Road to War: Aggression, Isolationism, and Pearl Harbor

**Goal:** You will explain why the United States stayed out of World War
II until December 1941, how it moved step by step toward the Allies,
and why the attack on Pearl Harbor ended the debate in a single day.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
timeline space. **Adult note:** this lesson covers aggression, war,
and racial ideology in the 1930s. Sources are documentary and textual;
frame period propaganda as evidence of the era's ideas, never as fact.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Unit 03, name one long-term
cause of World War I and one trigger. Keep the distinction in mind:
this lesson asks the same question about World War II — which causes
were long-term, and what finally triggered American entry?

## Part 1 — A world drifting toward war

In the 1930s, three aggressive powers expanded by force while the
world's democracies hesitated. In Asia, Japan invaded Manchuria in
1931 and China in 1937, seeking land, resources, and an empire. In
Europe, Germany under Adolf Hitler rearmed in defiance of the World
War I settlement, took the Rhineland in 1936, annexed Austria in 1938,
and seized Czechoslovakia in 1939. Italy under Benito Mussolini
invaded Ethiopia in 1935. Each aggressor bet that the other powers
would protest and then accept the new facts. Britain and France
followed a policy called **appeasement** — giving in to demands in
the hope of avoiding a larger war. At Munich in September 1938 they
let Germany take part of Czechoslovakia; six months later Germany
took the rest anyway. In September 1939 Germany invaded Poland, and
Britain and France finally declared war. World War II had begun — but
the United States was not in it.

Why did the democracies hesitate? The memory of World War I was one
reason: the war had killed millions and solved little, and many
Americans concluded that entering it had been a mistake. The Great
Depression was another: with a quarter of the labor force unemployed
in 1933, most Americans wanted the government to fix problems at
home, not abroad. A third reason was doubt about who the "good side"
was. The Soviet Union, a future ally, was itself a dictatorship that
had signed a non-aggression pact with Germany in August 1939. Nothing
about the 1930s was a simple contest of good against evil — and
learners should resist making it one.

### Worked example 1 — Long-term cause or trigger?

*Question:* Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931. The United States
declared war on Japan in December 1941. Was the Manchuria invasion a
cause of American entry, a trigger, or neither — and why?

*Model reasoning:* It was a long-term cause, not a trigger. A trigger
is the event that directly sets off the decision — here, the attack on
Pearl Harbor. The 1931 invasion matters because it started a decade
of Japanese expansion that put Japan on a collision course with
American interests in Asia and the Pacific, and because it showed
that the League of Nations would not stop aggression. But ten years
passed between Manchuria and Pearl Harbor, and the United States did
not declare war over Manchuria. A common error is to treat the first
event in a chain as the trigger; triggers are usually the last link,
not the first.

### Worked example 2 — What "neutral" actually meant

*Question:* The Neutrality Acts of the 1930s said the United States
would not sell arms to countries at war. In 1939 Congress changed the
law to "cash-and-carry": a country at war could buy American arms if
it paid cash and carried them away on its own ships. Was the United
States still neutral after 1939? Explain.

*Model reasoning:* Not in practice. Cash-and-carry favored Britain
and France, because they controlled the seas and could carry goods
away, while Germany could not. The law looked neutral — the same rule
applied to everyone — but its effects were one-sided. This is the
unit's first on-paper vs. in-practice case: a policy can be written
neutrally and work non-neutrally. The same pattern will return with
Lend-Lease, Executive Order 8802, and the GI Bill. Always ask not
only what a policy says, but whom it helps in practice.

## Part 2 — Step by step toward the Allies, 1939–1941

President Franklin D. Roosevelt believed the United States could not
stay out of the war forever, but he also knew most Americans opposed
entry. So he moved in steps, each one defensible as something short
of war. **Cash-and-carry (1939)** let Britain buy arms. The
**destroyers-for-bases deal (September 1940)** gave Britain 50 old
American destroyers in exchange for leases on British bases in the
Atlantic — the first direct transfer of American weapons to a country
at war. In a radio address on **December 29, 1940**, Roosevelt called
the United States the **"Arsenal of Democracy"** and argued that the
best defense was to supply the democracies fighting Hitler: "We must
be the great arsenal of democracy." The **Lend-Lease Act (March
1941)** went further: the United States could lend or lease war
supplies to any country whose defense the president judged vital to
American security. Over the war, Lend-Lease aid totaled about $50
billion, most of it to Britain and the Soviet Union.

At the same time, relations with Japan were collapsing. Japan's war
in China and its move into French Indochina in 1940–41 threatened
American interests in Asia. The United States answered with economic
pressure: it froze Japanese assets and, in July 1941, cut off oil
exports to Japan. Japan imported most of its oil, so the embargo was
a strangling measure — Japanese leaders concluded they had to seize
the oil fields of Southeast Asia, and that meant war with the United
States, whose Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, stood in the way.
In August 1941 Roosevelt and British Prime Minister Winston Churchill
met and issued the **Atlantic Charter**, promising a postwar world of
self-government and free trade — a statement of war aims before the
United States was even at war.

## Part 3 — Pearl Harbor and the decision for war

On the morning of **Sunday, December 7, 1941**, just before 8:00 a.m.
Hawaiian time, 353 Japanese aircraft launched from six aircraft
carriers attacked the U.S. Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor. In about two
hours they sank or damaged 21 ships, including all 8 battleships
present, destroyed 188 aircraft, killed 2,403 Americans, and wounded
1,178. Nearly half
the dead — about 1,177 — were aboard the battleship USS *Arizona*,
which exploded and sank. The attack was a tactical success for Japan:
it crippled the Pacific Fleet's battleships. It was a strategic
failure: the aircraft carriers were at sea and survived, the oil
storage tanks and repair yards were barely touched, and the attack
unified American opinion overnight. The day after, December 8, 1941,
Roosevelt asked Congress for a declaration of war, calling December 7
"a date which will live in infamy." Congress declared war on Japan
almost unanimously; three days later Germany and Italy declared war
on the United States, and the United States was at war on two oceans.

**Teacher note (misconception):** learners often say Pearl Harbor
"forced" the United States into war, as if no choice remained. The
attack made the political choice easy, but the United States had
already chosen sides economically — Lend-Lease, the oil embargo, and
the Atlantic Charter were choices, not accidents. The honest sentence
is: the United States moved from neutrality to open support for the
Allies during 1939–41, and Pearl Harbor turned that support into
declared war. See the answer key for the guided-practice solutions.

## Guided practice

1. Place these six events in order on a timeline, with dates: (a)
   Germany invades Poland; (b) Japan invades Manchuria; (c) Lend-Lease
   Act; (d) attack on Pearl Harbor; (e) Atlantic Charter; (f)
   destroyers-for-bases deal.
2. For each of the six events, label it a long-term cause, a step
   toward the Allies, or a trigger of American entry — and write one
   sentence justifying the label.
3. ★ A classmate says, "The United States was neutral until Pearl
   Harbor." Use two facts from this lesson to correct the claim.

## Independent practice

4. Explain in 4–5 sentences why appeasement failed to prevent war,
   using the Munich example.
5. Roosevelt called the United States the "Arsenal of Democracy."
   What did he mean, and what did the phrase leave out? (Hint: think
   about who was doing the fighting in 1940.)
6. ★ The oil embargo of July 1941 is sometimes described as the
   United States "provoking" Japan. Evaluate that description: what
   did the embargo do, what choices did Japan still have, and what
   does "provoking" imply that the facts do not support? Write 5–6
   sentences.

## Applied task

7. You are a newspaper editor on December 8, 1941. Write a 100-word
   editorial answering one question: should the United States now
   fight a war on two oceans, or focus on Japan alone? Use at least
   two facts from this lesson, and name one thing your editorial does
   not know yet.

## Exit check

8. In one sentence each: (a) What was Lend-Lease? (b) What happened
   at Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941? (c) Name one way the United
   States had already taken sides before Pearl Harbor.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the Atlantic Charter and the "Arsenal
  of Democracy" address are quoted in short, attributed phrases only;
  the adult reads the excerpts aloud first and defines *appeasement*,
  *embargo*, and *infamy* in context.
- **Alternate response mode:** the timeline task may be completed by
  ordering six pre-written event cards instead of writing dates from
  memory; the editorial may be dictated to the adult.
- **Extension:** compare the 1941 debate with the 1917 debate in
  Unit 03: in each case, what changed American minds — an event, an
  argument, or an economic interest? Write a paragraph with one
  parallel and one difference.
