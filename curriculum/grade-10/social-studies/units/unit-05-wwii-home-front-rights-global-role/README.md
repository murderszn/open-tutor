# Unit 05 — World War II: Home Front, Rights, and Global Role

Grade 10 · Social Studies · Weeks 20–23 · issue #49, section U05 (draft for review).

## Essential question

*When total war came home, who paid, who served, who was left out — and
what kind of world power did America become?*

## What this unit teaches

In December 1941 the United States entered a war it had spent two
years trying to avoid, and the war remade the country in four years
more thoroughly than the Depression decade before it. This unit
follows that arc in four moves. **Week 20:** the road to war —
aggression abroad, isolationism at home, Lend-Lease, and the attack on
Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941: 2,403 Americans killed) that ended the
debate in a day. **Week 21:** mobilization and the home front — the
War Production Board, rationing, war bonds and taxes, and a production
surge that took U.S. aircraft output from 6,086 planes in 1940 to
96,318 in 1944, carried by a workforce that grew to include more than
19 million women (37% of all workers) and by a Second Great Migration
that moved more than a million Black southerners north and west.
**Week 22:** rights under pressure — Executive Order 9066 (February
19, 1942) and the incarceration of about 120,000 people of Japanese
descent, two-thirds of them U.S. citizens; the Supreme Court's 6–3
decision in *Korematsu v. United States* (1944) and its same-day
counterpart *Ex parte Endo*; and the Double V campaign, which demanded
victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at home.
**Week 23:** the war's end and America's global role — D-Day, the
atomic bomb decision and its still-debated justifications, the United
Nations, Bretton Woods, and the GI Bill, and the question that runs
through the whole unit: did the war resolve America's prewar crises,
or relocate them?

A guiding adult should preview all primary sources and topics for
mature themes — war, incarceration, racial violence, and the atomic
bomb are central and are handled through age-appropriate,
adult-supervised historical inquiry. All sources are documentary and
textual; no graphic imagery is used. Period posters and cartoons may
contain ethnic stereotypes; the adult frames them as evidence of the
era's prejudices, never as neutral humor. Figures are given as
estimates with sources wherever historians disagree — notably the
Hiroshima and Nagasaki death tolls (about 140,000 and about 70,000 by
the end of 1945) and the size of the 442nd Regimental Combat Team's
service (sources differ between about 14,000 and about 18,000 men).
The generated illustration is a labeled AI reconstruction, never
evidence for a historical claim.

## Prerequisites

- Unit 01's core habits: on-paper vs. in-practice, identifying a source's
  creator/audience/purpose, and writing a claim supported by two pieces
  of evidence. If the learner cannot do this yet, the adult re-teaches
  with Unit 01's Lessons 2–3 before starting Week 20.
- Unit 02's map and data habits: reading a scaled map and a small
  dataset, and distinguishing a figure from an interpretation of it.
- Unit 03's causation habit: distinguishing long-term causes from
  triggers, and comparing two accounts of the same event (D2.His.6.9-12).
- Unit 04's policy-evaluation habit: sorting a program's aims from its
  results, and asking what counts as evidence that a policy "worked"
  (D2.Civ.13.9-12).
- Reading stamina for longer documentary texts: by this unit the learner
  works with 20–40-line public-domain document excerpts with vocabulary
  support; statutory excerpts are used in paraphrase with short
  attributed quotations. Only short, attributed phrases are quoted
  verbatim from presidential speeches and court opinions (public
  domain); all other historical voices in this unit are paraphrased or
  clearly labeled original composites, never presented as real people's
  words.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of this unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain the road to war and U.S. entry:** describe aggression by
   Germany, Italy, and Japan in the 1930s; explain American
   isolationism, the Neutrality Acts, and the shift through
   cash-and-carry, destroyers-for-bases, and Lend-Lease (March 1941);
   and explain how the attack on Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941; 2,403
   Americans killed) moved the United States from neutrality to war.
2. **Analyze mobilization and the home front:** explain the War
   Production Board, rationing, and war finance (taxes covered about
   45% of the war's cost; the government borrowed nearly $200
   billion); compute and interpret the aircraft-production series
   1940–1944 (6,086 to 96,318) and the women-in-the-workforce change
   (about 12 million, 27%, in 1940 to over 19 million, 37%, in 1945);
   and explain the Second Great Migration and the Bracero Program
   (1942) as wartime labor movements.
3. **Analyze rights under pressure:** explain Executive Order 9066
   (February 19, 1942) and the incarceration of about 120,000 people
   of Japanese descent in ten War Relocation Authority camps; explain
   *Korematsu v. United States* (6–3, December 18, 1944) and *Ex
   parte Endo* (same day); and evaluate the 1988 Civil Liberties Act
   (apology and $20,000 redress per surviving internee) as a later
   reckoning.
4. **Evaluate the Double V campaign and wartime service:** explain
   Executive Order 8802 (June 25, 1941) and the Fair Employment
   Practice Committee; describe the Double V campaign (Pittsburgh
   Courier, February 1942) and segregated service — the Tuskegee
   Airmen, the 442nd Regimental Combat Team, and the Navajo Code
   Talkers — and explain wartime racial tensions (Detroit, June 1943;
   the Zoot Suit Riots, Los Angeles, June 1943) as evidence of the
   limits of wartime unity.
5. **Evaluate the war's end and America's global role:** explain D-Day
   (June 6, 1944), V-E Day (May 8, 1945), and the atomic bomb
   decision — Hiroshima (August 6, 1945) and Nagasaki (August 9,
   1945), with death tolls of about 140,000 and about 70,000 by the
   end of 1945 — from at least two perspectives; explain the United
   Nations (Charter signed June 26, 1945), Bretton Woods (July 1944),
   and the GI Bill (June 22, 1944); and argue, with stated
   limitations, whether the war resolved or relocated America's
   prewar crises.

## Vocabulary

**Isolationism** — the policy of avoiding alliances and wars abroad;
in the 1930s many Americans supported it after World War I.
**Lend-Lease** — the March 1941 law letting the United States lend or
lease war supplies to Britain and other allies; about $50 billion in
aid over the war. **Arsenal of Democracy** — President Roosevelt's
December 1940 phrase for the United States as the supplier of the
weapons democracies needed. **Total war** — a war in which a whole
society's economy, workforce, and daily life are mobilized, not just
its armies. **War Production Board** — the federal agency (January
1942) that directed factories to war work and allocated scarce
materials. **Rationing** — government limits on how much of a scarce
good (sugar, gasoline, meat, tires) each household could buy, enforced
with coupon books. **Double V** — "double victory": victory over
fascism abroad and victory over racism at home; the Pittsburgh
Courier's campaign from February 1942. **Executive Order 9066** —
President Roosevelt's February 19, 1942 order authorizing military
commanders to exclude people from designated areas; used to remove
Japanese Americans from the West Coast. **Incarceration** — confining
people in camps; this unit uses it for the wartime removal of
Japanese Americans, since most were U.S. citizens held without charge
or trial. **Nisei / Issei** — second-generation (American-born) and
first-generation (immigrant) Japanese Americans. **Korematsu** — Fred
Korematsu, who refused the exclusion order and challenged it to the
Supreme Court, which ruled 6–3 against him in 1944; his conviction
was vacated in 1983 after evidence of government misconduct surfaced.
**FEPC** — the Fair Employment Practice Committee, created by Executive
Order 8802 (June 25, 1941) to hear complaints of discrimination in
defense work. **Bracero Program** — the 1942 agreement bringing
Mexican farmworkers to the United States under government contracts
to fill wartime labor shortages. **Manhattan Project** — the secret
U.S. program that built the first atomic bombs (about $2 billion;
more than 130,000 workers at its peak); its first test was July 16,
1945, in New Mexico. **Potsdam Declaration** — the July 26, 1945
Allied demand for Japan's unconditional surrender. **United Nations**
— the international organization founded by the Charter signed June
26, 1945, in San Francisco. **Bretton Woods** — the July 1944
conference that created the International Monetary Fund and the World
Bank to stabilize postwar trade and money. **GI Bill** — the
Servicemen's Readjustment Act (June 22, 1944), giving returning
veterans education, housing, and business benefits.

**Key figures and their sources (checked 2026-11-13):** Pearl Harbor,
December 7, 1941 — 2,403 Americans killed, 1,178 wounded, 188
aircraft destroyed (National Park Service / U.S. Navy figures as
summarized in standard histories). Aircraft production, all types:
6,086 (1940), 19,433 (1941), 47,836 (1942), 85,898 (1943), 96,318
(1944) — *The Army Air Forces in World War II*, Vol. VI, ch. 10, comparative
table (ibiblio); consistent with John Ellis, *The World War II Databook*;
about 300,000 military aircraft in total, July 1940–August 1945.
Women in the workforce: about 12 million (27% of the workforce) in
1940 to over 19 million (37%) in 1945; 6–7 million women entered or
re-entered work 1942–1945; married women employed 15% (1940) to 25%
(1945); women in the aircraft industry from 1% (1938) to 65% (1943)
— National Women's History Museum; HISTORY; U.S. Department of
Labor/Bureau of Labor Statistics via the Yogi Berra Museum lesson
handout. About 350,000 women served in uniform (HISTORY). War
finance: taxes covered about 45% of the war's cost; the government
borrowed nearly $200 billion (LibreTexts, *A History of the United
States, 1870–Present*). Lend-Lease: about $50 billion in aid — the
standard figure in U.S. government histories. Japanese American
incarceration: about 120,000 people, about two-thirds U.S. citizens,
in ten War Relocation Authority camps, February 19, 1942–March 20,
1946; *Korematsu* 6–3 and *Ex parte Endo* unanimous, both December
18, 1944; exclusion orders rescinded December 17, 1944 (National
Archives; Densho; standard histories). Civil Liberties Act of 1988:
formal apology and $20,000 redress per surviving internee. Double V:
James G. Thompson's letter, Pittsburgh Courier, January 31, 1942;
campaign launched February 7, 1942; about 200,000 members (Library
of Congress / NYPL teaching notes). Executive Order 8802: June 25,
1941; Fair Employment Practice Committee (National Archives
milestone documents). 442nd Regimental Combat Team: activated
February 1, 1943; most decorated unit for its size and length of
service; 21 Medals of Honor; about 14,000–18,000 men served (sources
differ; Go For Broke National Education Center; U.S. Army). Tuskegee
Airmen: about 1,000 Black aviators trained 1941–1945; 332nd Fighter
Group; 99th Fighter Squadron to North Africa, April 1943. Detroit
race riot, June 1943: 34 people killed. Atomic bombs: Hiroshima,
August 6, 1945 — about 70,000 killed instantly, about 140,000 dead
by the end of 1945; Nagasaki, August 9, 1945 — about 35,000–40,000
killed immediately, about 70,000 by the end of 1945 (National
Archives; Atomic Heritage Foundation figures). Manhattan Project:
nearly $2 billion; over 130,000 workers at peak; Trinity test July
16, 1945. Japan announced surrender August 15, 1945; formal
surrender September 2, 1945. D-Day: June 6, 1944. V-E Day: May 8,
1945. GI Bill: June 22, 1944. UN Charter: signed June 26, 1945, San
Francisco. Bretton Woods: July 1944. More than 400,000 American
service members died in the war. Second Great Migration: more than a
million Black southerners moved north and west during the 1940s;
about 5 million moved between 1940 and 1970.

  table or ordered list); the aircraft-production chart is fully
  tabled in Lesson 2 and in the SVG's own text labels.

  claims and counterclaims; extended time in S5 review sessions;

  documentation.
- **Differentiation:** learners who need a lighter load answer the
  starred core items in each practice set and may respond orally;
  extension tasks in each lesson ask for a second source, a
  counterargument, or a present-day connection (with adult guidance
  on what has and has not changed since the 1940s).
- **Sensitive content:** advance notice before Week 22 (incarceration,
  racial violence) and Week 23 (atomic bomb); reflection alternatives
  for any distressing material; pacing control stays with the adult.
