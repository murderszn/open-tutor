# Unit 05 — Answer Key (adult only)

**Do not show this file to the learner.** Model answers are given
for every lesson item, practice item, quiz question, and assessment
task. Accept any answer that uses the lesson's facts accurately,
answers the question asked, and names evidence. Partial credit: half
marks for a correct fact with a missing explanation.

## Lesson 1 — The road to war

**Guided practice**

1. Correct order: (b) Japan invades Manchuria, September 1931; (a)
   Germany invades Poland, September 1, 1939; (f)
   destroyers-for-bases deal, September 1940; (c) Lend-Lease Act,
   March 1941; (e) Atlantic Charter, August 1941; (d) attack on Pearl
   Harbor, December 7, 1941.
2. Labels: (b) long-term cause — it began a decade of Japanese
   expansion that put Japan on a collision course with the United
   States; (a) long-term cause — it started the European war, but
   the United States did not enter over Poland; (f) step toward the
   Allies — a direct transfer of American weapons to Britain; (c)
   step toward the Allies — the United States became the Allies'
   supplier; (e) step toward the Allies — a joint statement of war
   aims with Britain before American entry; (d) trigger — the event
   that directly produced the declaration of war.
3. Correction (any two facts): Lend-Lease (March 1941) made the
   United States the Allies' supplier; the destroyers-for-bases deal
   (September 1940) transferred American warships to Britain; the
   July 1941 oil embargo was economic warfare against Japan; the
   Atlantic Charter (August 1941) stated joint war aims with
   Britain. The United States was neutral on paper and a
   non-belligerent supporter of the Allies in practice.
**Independent practice**
4. Appeasement failed because each concession taught the aggressor
   that the democracies would not fight: at Munich (September 1938)
   Britain and France let Germany take part of Czechoslovakia to
   avoid war, and six months later Germany took the rest anyway. The
   policy assumed Hitler had limited aims; the evidence showed his
   aims kept growing. Giving in did not satisfy the aggressor — it
   confirmed that aggression worked.
5. "Arsenal of Democracy" meant the United States would supply the
   weapons democracies needed to fight Hitler, rather than fighting
   itself. What it left out: in 1940 Britain was doing the fighting,
   and the phrase said nothing about what the United States would do
   if supplying weapons proved insufficient.
6. The embargo (July 1941) cut off oil exports to Japan, which
   imported most of its oil — a strangling measure. Japan still had
   choices: negotiate, withdraw from China and Indochina, or seize
   Southeast Asian oil. "Provoking" implies the United States caused
   Japan's decision; the facts show the embargo was one pressure
   among several, and Japan chose war over negotiation. The word
   shifts responsibility from the attacker to the country that
   applied economic pressure.
**Applied task**
7. Accept any 100-word editorial that answers the two-ocean question,
   uses two facts (e.g., Germany and Italy declared war December
   11, 1941; the Pacific Fleet was damaged; Lend-Lease already tied
   the U.S. to Britain), and names one unknown (e.g., how long the
   war will last, whether the Soviet Union will hold, what Japan
   will do next).
**Exit check**
8. (a) Lend-Lease (March 1941) let the United States lend or lease
   war supplies to allies — about $50 billion over the war. (b) On
   December 7, 1941, Japanese aircraft attacked Pearl Harbor,
   killing 2,403 Americans and destroying 188 aircraft. (c) Any one:
   Lend-Lease, the destroyers-for-bases deal, the oil embargo, or
   the Atlantic Charter.

## Lesson 2 — Mobilization and the home front

**Guided practice**

1. (a) Largest absolute increase: 1943. 85,898 − 47,836 = 38,062.
   (b) Largest percentage increase: 1941. (19,433 − 6,086) ÷ 6,086 =
   13,347 ÷ 6,086 ≈ 2.193, i.e., about 219%. (For reference: 1942
   +146.2%, 1943 +79.6%, 1944 +12.1%.)
2. The error: "sixteen times better" confuses *more* with *better*.
   Output grew about sixteen-fold (96,318 ÷ 6,086 ≈ 15.8), but the
   planes were also larger and more complex, and the growth came
   from more workers, more factories, and government spending — not
   from factories becoming sixteen times as good.
3. Any two rationed goods (sugar, coffee, gasoline, tires, meat,
   butter, shoes) and one unrationed thing (e.g., bread, milk, or
   most clothing). It shows the government rationed what the war
   effort needed most (fuel, rubber, food for troops) and left
   everyday staples alone.
**Independent practice**
4. (a) 37 − 27 = 10 percentage points. (b) 50 million × 0.37 = 18.5
   million; 50 million × 0.27 = 13.5 million; difference = 5.0
   million more women. (c) The real increase was larger (about 12
   million to over 19 million, i.e., about 7 million) because the
   total workforce itself grew — the question holds it fixed at 50
   million, which understates the change.
5. The War Production Board directed *what factories made* and
   allocated scarce materials; rationing directed *what households
   could buy*. The Board solved the production problem (converting
   industry to war work); rationing solved the distribution problem
   (sharing scarce goods fairly and keeping prices down). Neither
   could do the other's job.
6. Alike: both moved workers to where wartime jobs were, and both
   were driven by labor shortages. Different: the Second Great
   Migration was voluntary movement by American citizens seeking
   jobs; the Bracero Program was a government contract bringing
   Mexican workers with limited rights and protections.
**Applied task**
7. Accept any ration-book page with four rationed goods, weekly
   allowances, a trade-off note (e.g., less sugar means less baking;
   less gasoline means fewer trips), and one sentence naming what
   the book cannot show (e.g., the family's income, health, or
   whether they can get black-market goods).
**Exit check**
8. (a) The War Production Board (January 1942) directed factories
   to war work and allocated scarce materials. (b) 96,318 aircraft
   in 1944, about sixteen times the 6,086 of 1940. (c) Any one group
   and cost, e.g., women gained factory work but earned far less
   than men; Black workers gained jobs but faced segregated housing
   and violence; Japanese Americans lost everything (Lesson 3).

## Lesson 3 — Rights under pressure

**Guided practice**

1. Executive Order 9066 (February 19, 1942) authorized military
   commanders to exclude "any or all persons" from designated
   military areas. In practice it was applied to people of Japanese
   descent on the West Coast — about 120,000 people, two-thirds of
   them U.S. citizens.
2. Correction: the Court's approval in *Korematsu* (6–3) did not
   settle the question. The same day, *Ex parte Endo* ruled
   unanimously that a loyal citizen could not be detained — and the
   1983 commission found the removal was driven by prejudice,
   hysteria, and failed leadership, not military necessity, and that
   the government had suppressed evidence contradicting its own
   claim. A court decision can be wrong, and later evidence can show
   it.
3. A "yes" answer could be read as abandoning Issei parents who
   could not become citizens, or as volunteering for a segregated
   army; a "no" answer (or refusal) could be read as disloyalty and
   lead to segregation at Tule Lake. There was no answer that did
   not cost the person something.
**Independent practice**
4. Race prejudice: the order was applied by ancestry — Japanese
   Americans were removed as a group while German and Italian
   Americans were not. War hysteria: the removal followed Pearl
   Harbor fear, with newspapers and politicians demanding action
   before any evidence. Failure of political leadership: the
   president, Congress, and the courts all accepted the military's
   claim without requiring proof of individual disloyalty.
5. Meaningful: it was a formal apology in law, it named the causes
   honestly (prejudice, hysteria, failed leadership), and it paid
   $20,000 to each surviving internee. Incomplete: it came more
   than forty years later, many survivors had died, and no payment
   restores lost years, property, or trust.
6. The suppressed report undermines the majority's reasoning,
   because the majority accepted the government's claim of military
   necessity — and the suppressed evidence contradicted that claim.
   It does *not* prove the justices knew about the suppression in
   1944; it proves the government misled the Court, which is a
   different claim about a different actor.
**Applied task**
7. Accept any museum label that names who was held (Japanese
   Americans, about 120,000, two-thirds citizens), why the
   government said so (military necessity after Pearl Harbor), what
   later evidence showed (no espionage convictions; the 1983
   commission's findings), and one visitor question (e.g., "What
   would you have done?" or "How do we prevent this from happening
   again?"). No invented personal details.
**Exit check**
8. (a) Executive Order 9066 (February 19, 1942) authorized military
   commanders to exclude people from designated areas; it was used
   to remove Japanese Americans from the West Coast. (b) About
   120,000 people; about two-thirds were U.S. citizens. (c) Any one:
   *Korematsu* (1944, 6–3, upheld the exclusion) or *Ex parte Endo*
   (1944, unanimous, a loyal citizen could not be detained).

## Lesson 4 — Double V

**Guided practice**

1. The "double V" meant victory over fascism abroad and victory over
   racism at home. The idea came from James G. Thompson's letter to
   the *Pittsburgh Courier* (January 31, 1942), "Should I sacrifice
   to live 'half American'?"; the *Courier* launched the campaign
   on February 7, 1942.
2. Correction (any two facts): the FEPC created by EO 8802 had no
   power to punish employers, and in the South it was largely
   ignored; segregation in the armed forces continued throughout the
   war; discrimination in hiring persisted, which is why the Double V
   campaign kept pressing. The order banned discrimination on paper;
   enforcement was weak.
3. Segregation mattered because it proved the campaign's point: Black
   soldiers were asked to fight for democracy in segregated units.
   The Tuskegee Airmen flew combat missions while the military kept
   them separate, and the 442nd fought with distinction while their
   families were incarcerated — service did not buy equality.
**Independent practice**
4. Randolph threatened a March on Washington (July 1, 1941) to
   protest defense-job discrimination; Roosevelt issued EO 8802 to
   head off the march. The cancellation was a result, not a failure:
   the threat produced the order, which was the march's goal — the
   march was leverage, and the leverage worked.
5. The Detroit riot (June 1943, 34 killed) showed the second victory
   was unfinished business: while Black Americans fought fascism
   abroad, they faced deadly racial violence at home — in the same
   year, in a defense-industry city, with federal troops needed to
   restore order. The riot is evidence the campaign's "at home"
   front was real.
6. Similar: both groups' labor was essential to the war effort, and
   both were treated as lesser contributors. Different: the Code
   Talkers' service was secret and later honored, while Bracero
   workers labored under contracts with weak protections and open
   discrimination. The difference shows the country honored military
   service more readily than farm labor — and honored both unevenly
   by race.
**Applied task**
7. Accept any flyer with two actions (one war-supporting, one
   equality-pressing), one thing the club will not do, and one
   sentence explaining the pairing (e.g., "We serve and we demand,
   because the country cannot have one without the other").
**Exit check**
8. (a) The Double V campaign (Pittsburgh Courier, February 1942)
   demanded victory over fascism abroad and victory over racism at
   home. (b) Executive Order 8802 (June 25, 1941) banned
   discrimination in defense industries and government work and
   created the FEPC; its main limit was weak enforcement — the FEPC
   could not punish employers. (c) Any one segregated unit (Tuskegee
   Airmen; 442nd Regimental Combat Team) and 1948 (Executive Order
   9981).

## Lesson 5 — The war's end

**Guided practice**

1. Correct order: (a) D-Day, June 6, 1944; (f) V-E Day, May 8, 1945;
   (b) Trinity test, July 16, 1945; (d) Potsdam Declaration, July
   26, 1945; (c) Hiroshima, August 6, 1945; (e) Japan's formal
   surrender, September 2, 1945.
2. Correction: about 70,000 people were killed instantly in
   Hiroshima; about 140,000 had died by the end of 1945, including
   deaths from burns and radiation in the following months. The
   single figure "140,000" is the end-of-1945 toll, not the
   immediate toll.
3. Any one institution and problem: the UN (prevent another world
   war / keep the great powers talking); the IMF and World Bank
   (stabilize currencies and rebuild economies); the GI Bill (help
   returning veterans with education and housing).
**Independent practice**
4. The GI Bill paid for college and training and gave low-cost home
   loans, letting millions of veterans enter the middle class (about
   7.8 million used education benefits by 1956). It was uneven
   because Black veterans were often steered away from the best
   colleges and neighborhoods by segregation and redlining, so its
   benefits flowed mostly to white veterans.
5. The League of Nations failed partly because the United States
   never joined it. The UN was designed with a Security Council and
   vetoes so the great powers — including the United States — would
   stay inside; the design traded strength for membership.
6. The invasion-casualty forecasts support Historian A (they explain
   why Truman's advisers thought the bomb would save American
   lives). The Soviet declaration of war (August 8, 1945), between
   the two bombings, supports Historian B's timing argument (it
   suggests the second bomb was partly about the postwar balance).
   Neither piece proves its account: forecasts are not outcomes, and
   timing is not motive.
**Applied task**
7. Accept any 100-word address with one cost fact (e.g., more than
   400,000 American service members dead; the atomic bombings'
   toll), one hope (e.g., the UN, a lasting peace), one thing not
   promised (e.g., an easy peace, an end to all conflict), and one
   sentence explaining the refusal.
**Exit check**
8. (a) The Manhattan Project was the secret U.S. program that built
   the first atomic bombs (nearly $2 billion; over 130,000 workers).
   (b) Japan formally surrendered on September 2, 1945. (c) Any one:
   the UN (international peace and security); the IMF/World Bank
   (postwar money and rebuilding); the GI Bill (veterans' education
   and housing).

## Culminating assessment

**Part A (2 points each — 1 for identification, 1 for significance):**

1. **Lend-Lease** (March 1941): the law letting the U.S. lend or
   lease war supplies to allies; it made the United States the
   Allies' supplier before entry, about $50 billion in aid.
2. **Arsenal of Democracy:** Roosevelt's December 1940 phrase for
   the U.S. as supplier of the democracies' weapons; it stated the
   strategy of supporting the Allies without yet fighting.
3. **War Production Board** (January 1942): the agency directing
   factories to war work and allocating scarce materials; it ran
   the conversion to total war.
4. **Rationing:** government coupon limits on scarce goods (sugar,
   gasoline, meat, tires); it shared sacrifice and kept prices down.
5. **Executive Order 9066** (February 19, 1942): authorized military
   commanders to exclude people from designated areas; used to
   incarcerate about 120,000 Japanese Americans.
6. **Nisei:** American-born Japanese Americans, U.S. citizens by
   birth; about two-thirds of those incarcerated.
7. ***Korematsu v. United States*** (1944, 6–3): the Supreme Court
   upheld the exclusion orders; later disavowed as "gravely wrong."
8. **Double V:** the Pittsburgh Courier's campaign (February 1942)
   for victory over fascism abroad and racism at home; it linked
   wartime service to civil-rights demands.
9. **Executive Order 8802** (June 25, 1941): banned discrimination
   in defense industries and created the FEPC; the first federal
   action against employment discrimination.
10. **Manhattan Project:** the secret program that built the first
    atomic bombs (nearly $2 billion; 130,000+ workers); it produced
    the weapon used on Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

**Part B:**

1. 1943: 85,898 − 47,836 = 38,062 aircraft.
2. 96,318 ÷ 6,086 ≈ 15.8 — about sixteen-fold.
3. Women went from about 12 million (27%) in 1940 to over 19
   million (37%) in 1945 — a large, real expansion of women's work.
   The numbers do not show postwar lives: many women were pushed
   out of factory jobs when veterans returned, and pay stayed far
   below men's.
4. The table supports the scale claim: U.S. output dwarfed its 1940
   level and exceeded any single rival's. It cannot prove the war
   was won by production alone: training, tactics, alliances,
   geography, and the Soviet war effort also mattered, and the table
   says nothing about them.

**Part C:**

1. The order grants the Secretary of War and designated military
   commanders the power to prescribe military areas and exclude any
   or all persons from them, whenever they deem it necessary or
   desirable.
2. The order never says "Japanese" or "Japanese American." That
   absence matters because the discrimination was in the
   application: the neutral-sounding power was used against one
   group by ancestry, and the text gives no limit that would have
   prevented that.
3. The majority accepted the military's claim of necessity under
   this text: the order's broad grant of discretion ("whenever he
   deems such action necessary or desirable") was read as
   authorizing the exclusion, and the Court deferred to the military
   rather than demanding proof of individual disloyalty.
4. Jackson's dissent warns about the *rule* the decision creates:
   by validating this use of the order's broad language, the Court
   made the same power available for any future emergency whenever
   an authority claims urgent need. The dissent is about precedent,
   not just about 1942.
5. *Endo* adds a limit the majority did not: even accepting the
   exclusion, a loyal citizen could not be *detained*. It is the
   Court drawing a line the Korematsu majority did not draw — and
   it is why the exclusion orders were rescinded the day before both
   decisions.

Sample thesis (not a model essay — learners write their own): "The
war resolved the economic crisis — unemployment fell below 2% by
1943 and the GI Bill built a middle class — but it relocated the
crisis of racial inequality: segregation survived, the incarceration
was unanswered until 1988, and the atomic bomb created a new danger
that the postwar institutions could not remove."

**Part E:**

1. The historian should trust the 1983 commission more about 1942:
   it had access to the suppressed report, it reviewed the full
   record after the war, and it found no evidence of individual
   disloyalty. The 1943 report is evidence of what the War
   Department *claimed* in 1943, not of what was true — and a claim
   is not evidence for itself.
2. No. The illustration is a labeled AI reconstruction — an artist's
   imagining made in 2026. It can show what a factory *might* have
   looked like and prompt questions, but it is not a photograph and
   not a record of any real factory or worker. Proof would need a
   period photograph, a payroll record, or another primary source.

## Project scoring guidance

Score each rubric row 0–4 as written in `project.md`. For
Investigation A, a "strong" brief names its four sources with dates,
ties every claim to one, and states what would change its answer.
For Investigation B, a "strong" memo reconstructs both 1942 cases
fairly and keeps hindsight out of the memo itself; the hindsight
section must judge the decision without pretending 1942 advisers
knew the later evidence. Deduct one point per invented fact or
figure. A project that uses no source beyond the lessons can still
earn full marks if it uses the lessons accurately.
