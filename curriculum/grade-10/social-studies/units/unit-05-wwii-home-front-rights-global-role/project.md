# Unit 05 — Project: Two Investigations

Choose **one** investigation. Both are worth 20 points; both require
a written product and a short presentation to the guiding adult. Use
only the sources named here or in the Resource Pack — do not invent
quotations, figures, or "interviews."

## Investigation A — Home-front dossier: "Who paid, who served, who was left out?"

**Question:** When the United States mobilized for total war, who
paid the costs, who did the work, and who was left out of the
benefits — and what does the pattern show about wartime America?

**Materials:** Lessons 1–5; the aircraft-production table and chart;
the women-in-the-workforce figures; the Resource Pack references;
a notebook and a folder (physical or digital) for the dossier.

**Steps:**

1. **Collect four sources** (one per category), each with a one-line
   source note (who made it, when, and what kind of source it is):
   - (a) a production or finance figure from Lesson 2 (aircraft
     table, war finance, or women in the workforce);
   - (b) a home-front account — the factory-worker or farmer account
     from Lesson 2, or a rationing artifact you describe from the
     Lesson 2 applied task;
   - (c) a rights document — Executive Order 9066, Executive Order
     8802, or the Double V campaign launch;
   - (d) a postwar outcome — the GI Bill, the UN, or the 1988 Civil
     Liberties Act.
2. **Write a 600-word brief** answering the investigation question.
   Structure: one paragraph per source (what it shows), then a final
   paragraph that states your answer as a claim, names the strongest
   counter-consideration, and says what evidence would change your
   mind.
3. **Build a one-page visual** (a table, timeline, or chart) that
   shows the four sources side by side with dates. Every figure on
   the visual must match the lesson or Resource Pack source it came
   from; the visual must carry a text alternative (a written list of
   what it shows).
4. **Present** the brief to the guiding adult in 5 minutes, then
   answer two questions about evidence and limitations.

**Deliverables:** the four-source dossier (with source notes), the
600-word brief, the one-page visual with text alternative, and a
presentation.

**Rubric (20 points):**

| Criterion | 4 — Strong | 3 — Adequate | 2 — Developing | 0–1 — Missing/weak |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sources (4) | Four sources, one per category, each with an accurate source note | Four sources; one source note incomplete | Three sources, or two from the same category | Fewer than three sources, or invented material |
| Evidence use (4) | Every claim tied to a named source; figures match the lessons | Most claims tied to sources; one figure unchecked | Claims often unsourced; figures approximate without noting it | Claims asserted without sources |
| Argument (4) | Clear claim answering the question; strongest counter-consideration named | Clear claim; counter-consideration vague | Claim restates the question without answering it | No claim |
| Limitations (4) | Names what the four sources cannot show; says what evidence would change the answer | Names one limitation | Mentions limitations in general terms only | No limitations |
| Presentation & visual (4) | Visual accurate with text alternative; 5-minute presentation answers both questions | Visual accurate; presentation answers one question | Visual has an error or no text alternative | No visual or no presentation |

## Investigation B — Rights-and-security decision memo: Executive Order 9066

**Question:** You are an adviser to the War Department in February
1942. The Secretary asks: should the government remove Japanese
Americans from the West Coast? Write a memo recommending for or
against removal — and then write a second, shorter section judging
your own memo with the evidence we have now.

**Materials:** Lesson 3; the Resource Pack references on EO 9066 and
the Korematsu case; the C3 civics codes in the unit README.

**Steps:**

1. **Reconstruct the 1942 case for removal** as its supporters made
   it (fear after Pearl Harbor, the military's claims, the demand
   from West Coast politicians) — in their terms, not as a caricature.
   Then **reconstruct the 1942 case against removal** as it could
   have been made then (no evidence of individual disloyalty, the
   14th Amendment's due-process guarantee, the precedent of applying
   a rule by ancestry).
2. **Write the memo (400 words):** your recommendation, your two
   strongest reasons, the strongest objection to your recommendation,
   and one condition or safeguard you would attach. Date the memo
   February 1942 and use only evidence available then — do not use
   the 1983 commission's findings in the memo itself.
3. **Write the hindsight section (200 words):** now judge your memo
   with the later record — the 1983 commission's findings, the
   suppressed military report, the 1988 Civil Liberties Act. State
   clearly: what did the later evidence change about your memo, and
   what (if anything) would you still defend? The rule for this
   section: hindsight is for judging the *decision*, not for
   pretending the 1942 advisers knew what we know now.
4. **Present** the memo to the guiding adult in 5 minutes, then
   answer two questions: one about the 1942 evidence, one about the
   hindsight section.

**Deliverables:** the 400-word memo, the 200-word hindsight section,
and a presentation.

**Rubric (20 points):**

| Criterion | 4 — Strong | 3 — Adequate | 2 — Developing | 0–1 — Missing/weak |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1942 reconstruction (4) | Both cases fairly stated in their own terms, with dated evidence | Both cases stated; one relies on hindsight | One case is a caricature, or hindsight is used in the memo | Only one case, or invented evidence |
| Recommendation (4) | Clear recommendation with two reasons and a safeguard | Clear recommendation; one reason or no safeguard | Recommendation unclear or unsupported | No recommendation |
| Objection (4) | Strongest objection named and answered honestly | Objection named, answer weak | Objection named but not answered | No objection |
| Hindsight (4) | Later evidence used to judge the decision, not to rewrite 1942 knowledge; states what changed and what would still be defended | Later evidence used; the 1942/now distinction is blurry | Hindsight used to blame individuals for not knowing the future | No hindsight section, or hindsight confused with the memo |
| Presentation (4) | 5-minute presentation answers both questions (1942 evidence + hindsight) | Answers one question well | Answers neither question directly | No presentation |

**Adult note:** Investigation B deals with a government decision
that harmed American citizens. Keep the discussion on evidence and
responsibility. If the learner finds the material distressing, the
adult may substitute Investigation A without penalty.
