# Unit 05 — Teacher Guide (adult only)

Grade 10 · Social Studies · Weeks 20–23 · issue #49, section U05.

This guide is for the guiding adult only. It does not replace the
lessons: it explains how to teach them, where learners usually go
wrong, and when to re-teach. The answer key is a separate file;
never show it to the learner.

## How this unit fits

U05 is the pivot of the year. Units 01–04 built habits — on-paper vs.
in-practice, long-term causes vs. triggers, reading data, judging
policy. U05 uses every one of them on the hardest material in the
course: total war, incarceration, and the atomic bomb. The unit's
central move is the one the scope-and-sequence names in Week 22:
learners must hold two true things at once — the war was a genuine
national emergency, and the government used that emergency to harm
its own citizens. If a learner collapses either side, re-teach with
the worked examples in Lessons 3 and 4.

## Pacing notes

- **Week 20 (Lesson 1):** the 1930s background is the heaviest
  reading in the unit. Read Part 1 aloud together the first time.
  The timeline task (Guided practice 1) is the week's check: if the
  learner cannot order the six events, do not move on — the rest of
  the unit assumes that sequence.
- **Week 21 (Lesson 2):** the data task is the week's core skill.
  Learners who struggle with percentages should do the aircraft
  table with a calculator and a completed first row. The image study
  (Part 2) is a discussion, not a test — the third question ("can
  this image prove anything?") is the point of the whole unit's
  approach to images. Do not skip it.
- **Week 22 (Lessons 3–4 + quiz):** two lessons in one week. Lesson
  3 is the emotional center; Lesson 4 is the argumentative center.
  Give advance notice before Lesson 3 (see Sensitive content below)
  and keep the evidence lab (S2) concrete: the two facts in Lesson 3,
  Part 2 are the ones to land. The quiz covers Lessons 1–3 only —
  Lesson 4 is assessed in the unit assessment, not the quiz.
- **Week 23 (Lesson 5 + assessment):** the bomb decision needs a
  full session. Do not let the discussion become a vote ("was it
  right?"). The lesson's question is narrower and better: what did
  each side argue, what evidence supports each, and what would change
  your mind? The assessment's Part D essay is the unit's capstone;
  give the learner the rubric (in the answer key) before writing.

## Sensitive content

- **Week 22:** forced removal, incarceration, racial violence
  (Detroit riot, Zoot Suit Riots). **Week 23:** the atomic bombings
  and mass civilian death. Give advance notice before each. Sources
  are documentary and textual; no graphic imagery is used in this
  unit. Reflection alternatives: the learner may write a private
  reflection instead of discussing aloud, or substitute
  Investigation A for Investigation B in the project. Pacing control
  stays with the adult — if a session needs to stop, stop it.
- Period posters and cartoons may contain ethnic stereotypes. Frame
  them as evidence of the era's prejudices, never as neutral humor.
- The word "internment" appears in many older sources. This unit
  uses "incarceration" for the wartime removal of Japanese Americans,
  since most were U.S. citizens held without charge or trial. Explain
  the word choice once (Lesson 3 vocabulary) and use it consistently.

## Misconceptions to watch for

1. **"The United States was neutral until Pearl Harbor."** The most
   common error in the unit. Re-teach with Lesson 1's Worked example
   2 (cash-and-carry) and the Lend-Lease/embargo sequence.
2. **"A big number proves a big claim."** Re-teach with Lesson 2's
   Worked example 1: numbers support conclusions; they are not
   conclusions.
3. **"The Supreme Court approved it, so it was legal/just."**
   Re-teach with Lesson 3: *Endo*, the 1983 commission, and the
   suppressed evidence. Legality and justice are different questions.
4. **"Heroic service ended segregation."** Re-teach with Lesson 4's
   Worked example 2: service created the argument; EO 9981 (1948)
   made the change.
5. **"The bomb ended the war, so the decision needs no debate."**
   Re-teach with Lesson 5's Worked example 1: a trigger is not a
   sole cause, and an outcome is not a justification.
6. **"The war helped everyone."** Re-teach with the Lesson 2 teacher
   note: the war ended unemployment and distributed its benefits
   unevenly.

## Reteach triggers

- **Quiz below 15/20:** re-teach the missed lesson(s) with the
  worked examples before Week 23. Do not proceed to Lesson 5 until
  the Lesson 3 items are secure — the bomb debate assumes the
  Korematsu reasoning.
- **Assessment below 60:** revisit Lessons 1–5 in order before
  Unit 06. The most common gap is Part C (source analysis): re-do
  the Lesson 3 evidence lab as a pair activity.
- **Practice Set B errors:** if the learner confuses absolute and
  percentage change, re-do Set B items 1–2 with the table and a
  calculator, one row at a time.

## What "done" looks like

- The learner can order the 1939–1945 sequence from memory and
  explain one long-term cause, one step toward the Allies, and the
  trigger of American entry.
- The learner can compute a growth factor and a percentage change
  from the aircraft table and explain what the numbers do and do not
  prove.
- The learner can explain EO 9066, *Korematsu*, *Endo*, and the
  Double V campaign in their own words, with dates.
- The learner can argue the bomb decision from two perspectives and
  name what evidence would change their mind.
- The learner's project names a limitation of its own evidence.

## Answer-key use

The answer key gives model answers, not the only answers. For open
responses, accept any answer that (a) uses the lesson's facts
accurately, (b) answers the question asked, and (c) names evidence.
Partial credit: give half marks for a correct fact with a missing
explanation, and full marks only when the explanation is present.
For the Part D essay, use the rubric in the key and show it to the
learner before writing.
