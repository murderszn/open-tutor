# Unit 03 — U.S. Expansion, Imperialism, and World War I

Grade 10 · Social Studies · Weeks 11–14 · issue #49, section U03 (draft for review).

## Essential question

*Between 1865 and 1920 the United States finished conquering a continent,
took overseas colonies, and entered a world war — did expansion make
America more democratic, or did it put democracy's promises to their
hardest test?*

## What this unit teaches

In 1865 the United States was still fighting over what its own continent
would become. By 1920 it held Caribbean and Pacific colonies, had dug a
canal across Panama, and had sent two million soldiers to Europe — and
then refused to join the League of Nations it had proposed. This unit
follows that arc in four moves. **Week 11:** how railroads, federal land
policy, and war remade the transcontinental West at Native nations'
expense — from the Homestead Act (1862) and the transcontinental
railroad (completed May 10, 1869) through the Dawes Act (1887) to
Wounded Knee (December 29, 1890). **Week 12:** how the Spanish-American
War (1898) turned a republic that had once been a colony into a colonial
power — and the fierce debate that followed, from the Treaty of Paris
(December 10, 1898; $20 million paid for the Philippines) through the
Philippine-American War (1899–1902), the Platt Amendment (1901), the
Panama Canal (opened August 15, 1914), and the Roosevelt Corollary
(1904). **Week 13:** how a European war became an American war —
alliances, nationalism, and militarism as interacting causes; the
sinking of the *Lusitania* (May 7, 1915; 1,198 dead, including 128
Americans); resumed unrestricted submarine warfare (February 1, 1917);
the Zimmermann Telegram (January 1917, published March 1, 1917); and
Congress's declaration of war (April 6, 1917). **Week 14:** what the war
did at home — mobilization, the Great Migration's wartime acceleration,
the Espionage Act (1917) and Sedition Act (1918), and the peace that
failed at home: the Treaty of Versailles (June 28, 1919) and the Senate's
refusal to ratify it (November 19, 1919; again March 19, 1920).

A guiding adult should preview all primary sources and topics for
mature themes — conquest, massacre, colonial war, and wartime repression
are central and are handled through age-appropriate, adult-supervised
historical inquiry. All sources are documentary and textual; no graphic
imagery is used. Period political cartoons and wartime posters contain
ethnic stereotypes; the adult frames them as evidence of the era's
prejudices, never as neutral humor. Casualty figures are given as
ranges or labeled estimates wherever historians disagree — notably at
Wounded Knee (about 150–300 Lakota killed) and in the
Philippine-American War.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of this unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain continental expansion's consequences:** describe how
   railroads, the Homestead Act, and federal Indian policy (treaties,
   allotment, boarding schools) reshaped the West; explain the Dawes Act
   (1887) as a policy mechanism; and analyze Wounded Knee (1890) as
   both an event and a symbol, using casualty estimates honestly.
2. **Analyze the 1898 debate over empire:** explain the
   Spanish-American War's causes (including yellow journalism and the
   *Maine*); state the Treaty of Paris's terms; and compare an
   anti-imperialist argument with an expansionist argument, judging
   each by its evidence.
3. **Explain the United States as an overseas power:** describe the
   Philippine-American War, the Platt Amendment, the Panama Canal, the
   Roosevelt Corollary, and the Open Door policy; and evaluate whether
   "empire" fits the United States in 1900–1914, with evidence.
4. **Analyze WWI's causes and the U.S. entry decision:** distinguish
   long-term causes (alliances, nationalism, militarism, imperial
   rivalry) from triggers (the July Crisis, submarine warfare, the
   Zimmermann Telegram); and argue, with evidence, why the United
   States entered in April 1917 rather than in 1914 or 1915.
5. **Evaluate the war's domestic effects and the failed peace:**
   explain mobilization, wartime limits on speech (Espionage and
   Sedition Acts, *Schenck*), and the Great Migration's wartime
   acceleration; and explain why the Senate refused to ratify the
   Treaty of Versailles, distinguishing irreconcilables,
   reservationists, and Wilson's supporters.

## Vocabulary

**Reservation** — land set aside by treaty or statute for a Native
nation, usually after larger territories were taken. **Allotment** —
dividing communally held tribal land into individual parcels, as the
Dawes Act (1887) did; "surplus" land was then sold to settlers.
**Assimilation** — a federal policy goal of absorbing Native peoples
into Euro-American culture, pursued through allotment and boarding
schools. **Ghost Dance** — a Native spiritual movement of 1890
foretelling renewal; U.S. officials treated it as a threat.
**Imperialism** — extending a country's political, economic, or
military control over other peoples or territories. **Annexation** —
formally adding territory to a country. **Protectorate** — a territory
that keeps a nominal government while another power controls its
foreign affairs or finances (Cuba under the Platt Amendment is the
unit's example). **Sphere of influence** — a region where one foreign
power claims exclusive trading or political privileges (China, in the
Open Door debate). **Yellow journalism** — sensationalist newspaper
competition that exaggerated events, notably in the run-up to the 1898
war. **Jingoism** — aggressive, warlike patriotism. **Anti-imperialism**
— opposition to overseas empire, organized in the Anti-Imperialist
League (1898). **Nationalism** — devotion to one's nation, sometimes
defined against rival nations or empires. **Militarism** — glorifying
military power and building up arms. **Alliance system** — the
interlocking defense treaties that turned a Balkan crisis into a
continental war. **Unrestricted submarine warfare** — sinking merchant
and passenger ships without warning, as Germany resumed doing on
February 1, 1917. **Neutrality** — refusing to take sides in a war;
the U.S. position, 1914–1917, that grew harder to hold as trade and
submarine attacks collided. **Mobilization** — organizing a whole
economy and population for war. **Propaganda** — persuasive material
produced to shape opinion, notably by the Committee on Public
Information. **Espionage Act (1917) / Sedition Act (1918)** — wartime
laws criminalizing interference with the war effort and "disloyal"
speech. **Self-determination** — Wilson's principle that peoples
should choose their own government (Fourteen Points, Point 5 and
others). **League of Nations** — the international organization
proposed in the Treaty of Versailles's Covenant; the United States
never joined. **Reparations** — payments imposed on Germany by the
Treaty of Versailles. **Irreconcilables / reservationists** — Senate
opponents of the treaty: the first group rejected it outright, the
second would accept it only with reservations protecting U.S.
independence of action.

### Key figures used in this unit (all verified 2026-10-27)

- **Transcontinental railroad completed May 10, 1869**, at Promontory
  Summit, Utah — Central Pacific and Union Pacific joined.
- **Homestead Act (1862):** 160 acres to a settler who lived on and
  improved the claim for five years.
- **Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868):** Great Sioux Reservation of about
  60 million acres; reduced to about 21.7 million by 1877; the Dawes
  General Allotment Act (February 8, 1887) cut it further to about 12.7
  million acres.
- **Wounded Knee, December 29, 1890:** U.S. 7th Cavalry killed about
  150–300 Lakota (estimates vary; the Army buried 146 in a mass grave
  and others died later or were carried away); 25 U.S. soldiers killed.
  Chief Big Foot (Spotted Elk) was among the Lakota dead.
- **USS *Maine*:** exploded in Havana harbor February 15, 1898; 266
  crew dead. The U.S. declared war April 25, 1898 (retroactive to
  April 21). The Teller Amendment (April 1898) disclaimed any U.S.
  intention to annex Cuba.
- **Treaty of Paris:** signed December 10, 1898; effective April 11,
  1899. Spain relinquished Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the
  Philippines; the U.S. paid $20 million for the Philippines. The
  Senate ratified it February 6, 1899, by a single vote over the
  two-thirds threshold. Fighting between U.S. troops and Emilio
  Aguinaldo's forces began February 4, 1899.
- **Philippine-American War (1899–1902):** about 4,200 U.S. soldiers
  died; Filipino military and civilian deaths were far higher, with
  civilian estimates ranging into the hundreds of thousands —
  historians disagree on exact numbers, and the unit states the range
  honestly.
- **Platt Amendment (March 2, 1901):** as a condition of U.S.
  withdrawal, Cuba accepted limits on its foreign affairs and a U.S.
  right to intervene; repealed 1934.
- **Panama:** Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty (November 18, 1903) gave the
  U.S. a canal zone; the canal opened August 15, 1914.
- **Roosevelt Corollary (December 1904):** the U.S. claimed a right to
  intervene in the Western Hemisphere to keep order and protect its
  interests. **Open Door Notes:** Secretary of State John Hay's notes
  of September 1899 and July 1900 asked the powers to keep China open
  to all trade and to preserve China's territorial integrity.
- **Hawaii annexed July 7, 1898** (Newlands Resolution), after the
  1893 overthrow of Queen Liliʻuokalani.
- ***Lusitania*:** sunk by a German U-boat May 7, 1915; 1,198 dead,
  including 128 Americans. **Sussex pledge** (1916) limited submarine
  attacks; Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare February 1,
  1917. **Zimmermann Telegram:** sent January 16, 1917, offered Mexico
  an alliance and the return of Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona;
  published in the U.S. press March 1, 1917.
- **U.S. entry:** Wilson asked Congress for war April 2, 1917
  ("the world must be made safe for democracy"); Congress declared war
  April 6, 1917. **Selective Service Act (May 18, 1917):** 24 million
  men registered; more than 2 million were inducted.
- **Fourteen Points:** Wilson's January 8, 1918 address. **Armistice:**
  November 11, 1918, 11 a.m. **U.S. service:** 4,734,991 served;
  116,516 deaths (53,402 battle, 63,114 non-battle); 204,002 wounded
  (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs figures, via the Congressional
  Research Service).
- **Espionage Act (June 15, 1917); Sedition Act (May 16, 1918).**
  ***Schenck v. United States* (March 1919):** the Supreme Court
  upheld a conviction, with Justice Holmes's "clear and present
  danger" test.
- **Great Migration:** about 1.6 million Black southerners moved
  north between 1910 and 1930, with the wartime labor shortage sharply
  accelerating the flow after 1916.
- **Treaty of Versailles:** signed June 28, 1919. Senate votes:
  November 19, 1919 — the treaty with Lodge reservations failed 39–55,
  and without reservations failed 38–53; March 19, 1920 — the treaty
  with reservations failed 49–35, seven votes short of two-thirds.
  The United States never joined the League of Nations.
- **Influenza pandemic (1918–1919):** about 675,000 Americans died
  (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimate).
