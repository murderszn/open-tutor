# Unit 03 — Answer Key (adult only)

Do not give this file to the learner. Every lesson practice item,
practice-set item, quiz question, and assessment task is solved here
independently of the lessons and reconciled with them. Open responses
show model reasoning; accept any answer using the same evidence and
reasoning.

## Lesson 1 — Transcontinental West

**Guided:** 1. Railroads made land valuable by (a) providing cheap
transport to markets, which raised land prices along the lines, and
(b) receiving federal land grants they sold to settlers, which turned
speculation into settlement; they also moved soldiers quickly, making
resistance harder. 2. The settler is right that the Homestead Act
offered 160 acres for five years' residence and improvement; the
Lakota leader is right that much of that land was treaty territory.
Both are true because the government gave away land it had promised
to others — the "free" land was only free to the giver. 3.
Observations: locomotive and freight cars on a straight track;
telegraph poles; a parallel dirt road; a distant bison herd.
Inferences: fast transport and communication arrived; older travel
was being replaced; the bison food base was shrinking. Cannot prove:
that this place existed, how any real community experienced the
railroad, or how many bison remained. 4. Step 1, divide: communal
land split into individual allotments (typically 160 acres per family
head). Step 2, declare surplus: land left after allotment declared
excess. Step 3, sell: surplus sold to settlers; tribal holdings fell
from about 138 million acres (1887) to about 48 million (1934).

**Independent:** 5. Order: Homestead Act (1862) → Fort Laramie
(1868) → railroad completed (1869) → Little Bighorn (1876) → Dawes
Act (1887) → Wounded Knee (1890). Sample link: the railroad (1869)
made the Black Hills accessible, feeding the gold rush pressure that
helped shrink the reservation by 1877. 6. Revised: "Wounded Knee
resulted from several interacting causes: the Ghost Dance movement's
spread, U.S. officials' alarm at it, the killing of Sitting Bull on
December 15, 1890, and the Army's decision to disarm Big Foot's band
at gunpoint." 7. Errors: (a) the Dawes Act did not "solve" anything
— it produced massive land loss (138M → 48M acres); (b) the citizenship
claim is partial at best — the Act offered citizenship to individual
allottees who completed the process, not to Native peoples generally,
and universal Native citizenship came only with the 1924 Indian
Citizenship Act. 8. The range reflects incomplete evidence: the
Army buried 146 in a mass grave, but relatives carried away other
bodies and some wounded died later of wounds and exposure, so no
complete count exists. The range signals honest uncertainty.

**Applied task (model):** "Federal policy both claimed to help and in
practice hurt Native nations: allotment was presented as a path to
farming and citizenship, but its surplus-sale mechanism transferred
tens of millions of acres to settlers. Limitation: the acreage figures
show land loss, not individual families' experiences."

**Exit check:** Homestead Act — offered 160 acres to settlers on
land often covered by treaty claims. Treaty reservations — set aside
Native territory on paper, then shrank under pressure. Dawes
allotment — divided communal land and sold the surplus to settlers.

## Lesson 2 — Spanish-American War and empire debate

**Guided:** 1. Sample links: *Maine* (Feb 15) → war declaration
(Apr 25): the explosion, amplified by yellow journalism, created the
political pressure for war. Manila Bay (May 1) → armistice (Aug 12):
Dewey's victory opened the Philippines question that the peace
negotiations had to settle. 2. Revised: "Yellow journalism was one
context for the war: it primed public opinion, but the war also
required Cuban rebellion, American business interests, and strategic
arguments about bases; the *Maine* was the trigger, not the whole
cause." 3. Consent argument: support — Filipinos fought a three-year
war against annexation; weakness — the U.S. did govern other
territories (Puerto Rico, Guam) without full consent too, so the
principle was applied selectively. Duty argument: support — the U.S.
did build schools and sanitation in the Philippines; weakness — the
Philippine-American War itself contradicted benevolence. 4. The
Teller Amendment bound only Cuba (a wartime promise about one island);
no amendment covered the Philippines, so McKinley could choose
annexation there. Treaties and amendments bind only what they name.

**Independent:** 5. Gets right: the U.S. did defeat Spain and Cuba
became independent. Leaves out: the Philippines were taken, not
liberated, and fought a war against the U.S.; Puerto Rico and Guam
were ceded. Rewrite: "The U.S. defeated Spain in 1898; Cuba became
independent under U.S. constraint, while Puerto Rico, Guam, and the
Philippines passed to U.S. rule — the Philippines only after a
three-year war against annexation." 6. Errors: (a) the Senate was
not unanimous — ratification passed February 6, 1899, by a single vote
over the two-thirds threshold; (b) not all Americans supported empire
— the Anti-Imperialist League (Twain, Carnegie, Bryan, Gompers)
organized against annexation. 7. The war began before the treaty took
effect because fighting broke out February 4, 1899, while ratification
was still pending; it shows Filipinos rejected the transfer rather
than accepting it. 8. Model: for — the Philippines could not be left
to anarchy or another empire; against — annexation violated consent
of the governed. Vote either way is acceptable if the counterargument
is stated; a model vote: against, because the Philippine war showed
consent was absent.

**Applied task (model):** either side acceptable with two evidence
items and a stated objection (e.g., anti-imperialist letter citing
consent and the Philippine war, ending with the expansionist
objection about abandoning the islands to another power).

**Exit check:** Cuba relinquished (independent under Platt
constraints); Puerto Rico ceded; Guam ceded; Philippines ceded for
$20 million. Anti-imperialist core: a republic must not rule without
consent. Expansionist core: duty, strategy, and markets required
holding the islands.

## Lesson 3 — Overseas power

**Guided:** 1. Puerto Rico — territory (unincorporated; Insular
Cases); Philippines — colony (military then civilian commission
rule); Cuba — protectorate (Platt Amendment limits and intervention
right); Canal Zone — canal zone (Hay–Bunau-Varilla perpetual grant).
Mechanisms: Puerto Rico — territorial government under Congress;
Philippines — colonial administration; Cuba — treaty/debt veto and
intervention; Canal Zone — U.S. sovereignty over the zone.
2. Revised: "The Open Door Notes *aimed* to keep China open to all
trade, but during the Boxer Rebellion the U.S. joined an eight-nation
force to suppress a Chinese uprising — protecting foreign access, not
Chinese sovereignty." 3. Cuba was independent in form (own president
and flag) but the Platt Amendment let the U.S. veto its treaties and
debts and intervene; the U.S. intervened in 1906 (and again in 1912).
4. Philippines: decided by the U.S. (Treaty of Paris + war);
benefited U.S. strategy and trade; costs paid by Filipinos in war
deaths and colonial rule. Panama: decided by the U.S. and Panama's
new government after a U.S.-backed separation; benefited world trade
and U.S. naval mobility; costs paid by Caribbean laborers and by
Panamanian sovereignty.

**Independent:** 5. "Empire" is more defensible: the evidence is
mechanisms of control (colonial administration in the Philippines,
Platt Amendment vetoes, canal-zone sovereignty), not labels. What
would settle it further: whether the governed peoples consented — the
Philippine war answers no. 6. Error: the Corollary was not part of
the 1823 Monroe Doctrine; it was Roosevelt's 1904 extension adding an
intervention claim to Monroe's anti-colonization principle.
7. Order: Platt Amendment (1901) → Hay–Bunau-Varilla (1903) →
Roosevelt Corollary (1904) → canal opens (1914). Sample link: the
Canal Zone treaty (1903) demonstrated the interventionist approach
the Corollary then generalized in 1904. 8. Model: for — the canal cut
weeks off voyages and made the U.S. a two-ocean naval power; against
— it rested on a treaty signed for Panama by a French engineer before
Panama had a government, and on harsh labor conditions. Judgment
either way with a reason is acceptable.

**Applied task (model table):** Annexation — Philippines — colonial
commission government — war and delayed independence (1946).
Protectorate — Cuba — Platt Amendment veto/intervention — constrained
sovereignty. Canal zone — Panama — perpetual zone grant — U.S.
control of transit and territory. Open Door — China — notes demanding
equal trade access — foreign intervention against the Boxers.

**Exit check:** Annexation (Philippines), protectorate (Cuba),
canal zone (Panama), Open Door (China).

## Lesson 4 — WWI causes and entry

**Guided:** 1. Alliance system — long-term cause (turned a Balkan
crisis into continental war). Sarajevo — trigger. U.S. trade with
Britain/France — context for U.S. entry (made neutrality fragile).
Zimmermann Telegram — trigger for U.S. entry (changed politics in
1917). Resumed submarine warfare — trigger for U.S. entry (sank
American ships). 2. Revised: "The Zimmermann Telegram was one of two
1917 triggers — with resumed unrestricted submarine warfare — that
moved a divided public; neither alone explains entry, since the
*Lusitania* (1915) had not." 3. (a) "Warfare against mankind":
support — U-boats sank passenger and merchant ships without warning;
complication — Britain's blockade also violated neutral rights.
(b) "No selfish ends": support — the U.S. sought no territory;
complication — American banks and industry had large loans and sales
to the Allies. 4. "Associated" preserved freedom to pursue separate
peace terms (the Fourteen Points) rather than adopting Allied war aims
wholesale — the distinction Wilson used at Paris in 1919.

**Independent:** 5. Timeline (8+ events, July 1914–April 1917, marked
C/T/X) with the sentence: "Entry came in April 1917 because two
triggers coincided — resumed submarine warfare sinking American ships
and the Zimmermann Telegram — after the 1915–1916 pledges had kept
neutrality alive." 6. Rewrite (three sentences): "Germany's sinking
of the *Lusitania* (May 7, 1915) outraged Americans but did not end
neutrality, because Germany pledged restraint (Arabic 1915, Sussex
1916) and opinion was divided. In February 1917 Germany resumed
unrestricted submarine warfare and sank American ships, and in March
1917 the Zimmermann Telegram was published. Together these moved
Congress to declare war on April 6, 1917." 7. Two-sided answer: not
genuinely neutral — trade and loans overwhelmingly favored the
Allies and protests to Britain were milder; but formally neutral —
no troops, no alliance, and real diplomatic protests to both sides.
Judgment either way with evidence is acceptable. 8. The margins show
broad but not unanimous support: large majorities for war, with
dissent (La Follette and others) showing a real anti-war minority
whose arguments (imperial quarrel, profiteering) persisted into the
postwar debate.

**Applied task (model):** For entry: submarine warfare killed
Americans; the Zimmermann Telegram proposed attacking the U.S. via
Mexico. Against: the war was a European imperial quarrel; the blockade
shows both sides violated neutral rights. Verdict either way with the
strongest counterargument stated.

**Exit check:** Long-term causes: alliance system, nationalism,
militarism, imperial rivalry. 1917 changes: resumed unrestricted
submarine warfare; the Zimmermann Telegram.

## Lesson 5 — War at home and the failed peace

**Guided:** 1. War Industries Board — coordinated factory production
(affected manufacturers and workers). Food Administration — voluntary
conservation campaigns (affected households). Committee on Public
Information — war propaganda and enemy-demonization (affected public
opinion and German Americans). 2. Revised: "The Great Migration
brought higher wages and new freedoms, but it did not solve racism:
East St. Louis (1917) and the Red Summer (1919) showed violent backlash
in the North." 3. The test: speech may be punished when it creates a
"clear and present danger" of a substantive evil Congress may prevent
— here, obstructing recruitment in wartime. It did not protect Debs
because the Court treated wartime dissent itself as meeting the test.
4. Irreconcilable — reject outright; voted against both versions.
Reservationist — accept with reservations; voted for the treaty with
reservations, against it without (or vice versa per the two votes:
for reservations version, against unamended — see note below).
Wilson supporter — accept unchanged; voted against the reservations
version (on Wilson's orders) and for the unamended version. Note on
the two November 19, 1919 votes: the reservations version failed
39–55 and the unamended version failed 38–53; the cross-voting is why
both failed.

**Independent:** 5. Model judgment: "Wilson's refusal to compromise
and his exclusion of senators from Paris help explain failure, but so
do the Senate's genuine Article 10 concern and a treaty that
disappointed both internationalists and nationalists; no single cause
suffices." 6. Error: the U.S. never joined the League. Correct: the
Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles on November 19, 1919 (twice)
and March 19, 1920 (49–35, seven short of two-thirds); the U.S. made a
separate peace in 1921. 7. Model: expansion — women's suffrage
momentum (19th Amendment, 1920) and Black migration/political
organization; contraction — Espionage/Sedition prosecutions and Red
Summer violence. Judgment either way with a reason is acceptable.
8. Battle deaths (53,402) were fewer than non-battle deaths (63,114),
and influenza (about 675,000) dwarfed both: counting only battle
deaths understates war's human cost, and disease environments created
by war belong in the toll.

**Applied task (model):** reservationist memo: goal — protect
Congress's war power while joining the League; evidence — Article 10's
mutual-defense commitment and the Constitution's treaty/ratification
process; objections answered — to irreconcilables: reservations keep
the U.S. free to decide; to Wilson supporters: reservations save the
treaty by making it passable. Vote: for, with reservations.

**Exit check:** Irreconcilables, reservationists, Wilson supporters.
Outcomes: November 19, 1919 — both versions failed; March 19, 1920 —
failed 49–35. One lasting domestic change: women's suffrage momentum
(19th Amendment, 1920), the Great Migration's demographic shift, or
the expanded federal administrative state — any one with a reason.

## Quiz (Week 12 formative)

1. Two mechanisms: land grants to railroads (sold to settlers) and
   fast movement of soldiers, settlers, and goods (cheap freight and
   military supply). 2. About 48 million acres. 3. About 150–300.
4. No — the cause was unproven in 1898 (later evidence points to an
   internal explosion, probably accidental). 5. Puerto Rico, Guam,
   and the Philippines; $20 million for the Philippines. 6. Model:
   expansionist argument — duty/benevolence (schools, sanitation) or
   strategy (bases, markets); weakening evidence — the
   Philippine-American War showed Filipinos did not consent, and
   occupation contradicted benevolence. 7. (a) colony; (b)
   protectorate; (c) canal zone. 8. The Western Hemisphere (Latin
   America and the Caribbean). 9. No — the Notes were unilateral
   requests no power formally signed. 10. Model: "Yes, by 1914 the
   U.S. held colonies (Philippines), a protectorate (Cuba), and a
   canal zone (Panama) — control without consent is the evidence.
   Limitation: the U.S. also withdrew from Cuba in 1902 and promised
   Philippine independence eventually, so 'empire' needs the
   four-forms qualification." A "no" answer with equivalent evidence
   and a stated limitation is also acceptable.

## Assessment (Week 14)

**Part A (chronological order):** Dawes Act (1887); Wounded Knee
(1890); Treaty of Paris (1898); Platt Amendment (1901); Panama Canal
opens (1914); *Lusitania* sunk (1915); Zimmermann Telegram published
(1917); U.S. declares war (1917); Armistice (1918); Treaty of
Versailles signed (1919).

**Part B:** 1. Position 1 appeals to duty/benevolence and strategy:
support — U.S. schools/sanitation in the Philippines; Pacific bases
and markets. Position 2 appeals to consent: support — Aguinaldo's
independence declaration and the three-year Philippine war against
annexation. 2. Weakening: Position 1 — the Philippine war and
occupation tactics contradict benevolence. Position 2 — the U.S. did
rule Puerto Rico and Guam without consent too, so the principle was
not applied consistently (and the expansionist reply cites the
alternative of abandonment to another empire). 3. Either verdict
with evidence is acceptable; a strong answer names the Philippine
war as the decisive evidence for Position 2 or the strategic outcome
for Position 1, with a reason.

**Part C (essay):** Thesis (e.g., "The U.S. entered in April 1917
because two triggers coincided after neutrality's context had
changed"). Dated evidence: *Lusitania* (May 7, 1915), submarine
warfare resumed (February 1, 1917), Zimmermann Telegram published
(March 1, 1917), declaration (April 6, 1917). Counterargument: the
isolationist case (European imperial quarrel; blockade asymmetry;
cost in lives) in at least two sentences. Score per the assessment's
rubric.

**Part D:** 1. Irreconcilables — reject outright. Reservationists —
accept with reservations protecting U.S. independence of action.
Wilson supporters — accept unchanged. 2. November 19, 1919: with
Lodge reservations failed 39–55; without reservations failed 38–53.
March 19, 1920: with reservations failed 49–35, seven short of
two-thirds. 3. Structural reason: the Constitution requires a
two-thirds Senate majority for treaties, so 34 senators can block a
president's peace; Wilson's refusal to include senators in Paris and
to accept reservations turned that structural veto into an actual
defeat.

**Part E (reflection):** No single correct verdict. Full marks for:
two pieces of evidence from different weeks (e.g., Dawes allotment
land loss; the League fight), a clear thesis, and an acknowledged
counterargument.

## Project guidance

Investigation A: grade the evidence and reasoning, not the verdict;
both "empire" and "not an empire" verdicts can score full marks if the
four-forms evidence is accurate and the counterargument is answered.
Investigation B: the Article 10 challenge answer — Article 10
committed League members to preserve each other's territorial
integrity against aggression; Lodge's reservation made that
commitment conditional on Congress's action, preserving Congress's
war power. Watch for invented quotations or figures in both
investigations — return those for correction.
