# Unit 03 — Investigations and Projects

## Investigation A — The Empire-Debate Dossier (Weeks 12–13, three sessions)

**Question:** *Was the United States an empire in 1900–1914?*

**Materials:** Lessons 2–3, practice Sets B–C, the resource pack,
notebook or document file, a timeline. No account or purchase is
required; all sources are free and public.

**Steps:**

1. **Session 1 (Week 12, S4):** Choose two cases — one from the
   Spanish-American War settlement (Philippines or Cuba) and one from
   the overseas-power period (Panama or China/Open Door). For each
   case, record: the place, the form of U.S. control, one mechanism of
   control, and one consequence for the people who lived there.
2. **Session 2 (Week 13, S4):** Build the two-sided brief. For the
   "empire" side, collect two pieces of evidence (a mechanism of
   control and a cost imposed without consent). For the "not an
   empire" side, collect two pieces of evidence (a legal difference
   from European colonies and a case where the U.S. withdrew or
   promised independence). Cite the lesson or resource for each item.
3. **Session 3 (Week 13, S5 review slot, 25 minutes):** Present a
   five-minute verdict to the guiding adult: answer the question in
   one sentence, support it with your two strongest pieces of
   evidence, and name the strongest counterargument and why it does
   not overturn your verdict (or why it qualifies it).

**Deliverables:**

- A one-page dossier table (case / form of control / mechanism /
  consequence) covering both cases.
- A two-sided brief (four evidence items with citations).
- A five-minute verdict presentation (spoken or written) with a
  stated counterargument.

**Rubric (12 points):**

| Criterion | Full (3) | Partial (2) | Missing/weak (0–1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accurate cases | Both cases correct on form of control and mechanism | One case correct, or mechanism vague | Cases confused or invented |
| Evidence quality | Four items, each cited to a lesson/resource, no invented figures | Three items cited, or one figure unverified | Fewer items, or invented evidence |
| Two-sided reasoning | Both sides stated fairly before judgment | One side stated fairly | One-sided only |
| Verdict + counterargument | Clear verdict, strongest counterargument named and answered | Verdict clear, counterargument weak | No verdict or no counterargument |

**Adult note:** the verdict may reasonably go either way; grade the
evidence and reasoning, not the conclusion. Watch for invented
quotations or figures — send those back for correction.

## Investigation B — The Versailles Decision Memo (Week 14, S3, single session)

**Question:** *As a senator in March 1920, how would you vote on the
Treaty of Versailles with Lodge's reservations?*

**Materials:** Lesson 5, practice Set D, the resource pack.

**Steps:**

1. Read the three-faction summary (Lesson 5, Part 3) and practice Set
   D, item D1.
2. Choose a faction — irreconcilable, reservationist, or Wilson
   supporter — and write a one-page memo: your faction's goal, two
   pieces of evidence for your position, one objection from each
   other faction answered in one sentence each, and your vote.
3. Present the memo to the guiding adult and answer one challenge
   question about Article 10 (the League's mutual-defense commitment).

**Deliverables:**

- A one-page memo (goal, evidence, two answered objections, vote).
- An oral or written answer to the Article 10 challenge question.

**Rubric (8 points):**

| Criterion | Full (2) | Partial (1) | Missing (0) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Faction accuracy | Goal and vote match the faction's actual position | Goal roughly right, vote unclear | Faction misidentified |
| Evidence | Two accurate, cited items | One accurate item | Invented or irrelevant |
| Objections answered | Both other factions' objections answered | One answered | None answered |
| Article 10 answer | Explains the mutual-defense commitment and the reservation's effect | Partial explanation | Missing or wrong |

**Adult note:** a reservationist memo is the natural default, but an
irreconcilable or Wilson-supporter memo graded on the same rubric is
equally acceptable. The Article 10 challenge: "What exactly did
Article 10 commit the U.S. to, and what did Lodge's reservation change
about it?" (Answer: Article 10 committed members to preserve each
other's territorial integrity against aggression; Lodge's reservation
said the U.S. would assume no such obligation without Congress's
action — preserving Congress's war power.)

## Safety and access notes

- Both investigations are reading, writing, and discussion tasks;
  no fieldwork, no materials beyond paper/digital documents, no
  hazards.
- All primary sources are public-domain government documents or
  paraphrases of them; no copyrighted text is reproduced.
- Period cartoons and posters used as evidence contain ethnic
  stereotypes; the adult frames them as evidence of prejudice, never
  as neutral humor.
- Alternate response modes: both investigations may be completed
  orally with the adult recording answers, or as tables instead of
  prose, without penalty.
