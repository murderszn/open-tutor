# Lesson 2 — The Spanish-American War and the Debate over Empire

**Goal:** You will explain why the United States went to war with
Spain in 1898, what the Treaty of Paris did, and why taking the
Philippines split Americans into expansionists and anti-imperialists.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
timeline space. **Adult note:** this lesson covers war and colonial
violence. Sources are documentary and textual; no graphic imagery.
Period cartoons contain ethnic stereotypes — frame them as evidence of
prejudice, never as neutral humor.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 1, name one way a federal
policy's stated goal differed from its effect. (If you can't: re-read
Lesson 1, Part 2 — allotment was sold as help for Native farmers and
produced massive land loss. Today's debate repeats that pattern: two
sides claiming to know what is best for other peoples.)

## Part 1 — How a republic that had been a colony became a colonial power

By the 1890s Cuba had fought Spain for independence for decades, and
American newspapers covered the rebellion in lurid detail. The New York
*Journal* and *World* competed for readers with sensational stories —
"yellow journalism" — exaggerating Spanish cruelty. When the U.S.
battleship *Maine* exploded in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898,
killing 266 crew, the papers blamed Spain at once. (Later
investigations pointed to an internal explosion, probably an accident;
in 1898, the cause was unproven, but the slogan "Remember the
*Maine*" did its work.)

President William McKinley asked Congress for war, and Congress
declared war on April 25, 1898, retroactive to April 21. To calm fears
that the U.S. simply wanted Cuba, Congress added the Teller Amendment:
the United States disclaimed any intention to annex Cuba and promised
to leave the island to its people. The war itself was short. Commodore
George Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay on May 1, 1898.
U.S. forces, with Cuban rebels, took Santiago de Cuba; Theodore
Roosevelt's volunteer "Rough Riders" fought at San Juan Hill on July 1.
An armistice followed on August 12, 1898.

The peace terms, signed at Paris on December 10, 1898, went far beyond
Cuba. Spain relinquished Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the
Philippines to the United States; the U.S. paid $20 million for the
Philippines. The treaty took effect April 11, 1899, after the Senate
ratified it on February 6, 1899, by a single vote over the two-thirds
threshold. Two days before that vote, on February 4, 1899, fighting
broke out between U.S. troops and forces led by the Filipino leader
Emilio Aguinaldo, who had declared Philippine independence in 1898 and
expected the U.S. to honor it. The Philippine-American War that
followed lasted until 1902 and cost about 4,200 American lives and far
more Filipino lives.

### Worked example 1 — Did the *Maine* cause the war?

*Question:* "The *Maine* exploded, so America went to war." Is that a
complete explanation?

*Model reasoning:* No — it names a trigger, not the causes. The
*Maine* mattered because it arrived in a context already full of fuel:
Cuban rebellion, American business interests in Cuban sugar, yellow
journalism priming the public, and a strategic argument that the U.S.
needed naval bases and coaling stations to compete with European
powers. Remove the *Maine* and war was still likely in 1898; remove the
other causes and an unexplained explosion would probably have produced
an investigation, not a war. Good causal reasoning separates the
spark from the fuel.

### Worked example 2 — Reading a treaty as a decision

*Question:* The Teller Amendment promised Cuba would not be annexed,
but the Treaty of Paris took the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and Guam.
What does the contrast show?

*Model reasoning:* It shows the war's aims were contested even among
the winners. The Teller Amendment bound the U.S. on Cuba because
annexing Cuba was politically unpopular. No such amendment protected
the Philippines, so McKinley — who said he prayed over the decision
and concluded the U.S. should "uplift and civilize" the Filipinos —
chose annexation. The contrast is evidence that "the war's purpose"
was not one thing: different decisions were made for different places,
for different reasons, by different votes. A student who writes "the
U.S. fought for freedom" or "the U.S. fought for empire" without the
contrast is flattening a real debate into a slogan.

## Part 2 — The debate: should a republic hold colonies?

Annexation split the country. The Anti-Imperialist League, founded in
1898, gathered an unlikely coalition: the writer Mark Twain, the
industrialist Andrew Carnegie, the labor leader Samuel Gompers, and the
politician William Jennings Bryan. Their arguments varied. Some were
principled: a republic founded on consent of the governed had no
business ruling peoples without their consent. Some were racist: they
feared incorporating non-white peoples as citizens. Some were
practical: empire would mean endless wars and a big standing army.

Expansionists answered with their own mix. Naval strategist Alfred
Thayer Mahan argued that great powers needed bases and markets;
McKinley and others argued for duty — that the U.S. could not abandon
the Philippines to anarchy or to another empire. Business interests
saw Asian markets. And some expansionists wrapped conquest in
benevolence: American schools, sanitation, and roads would "civilize"
the islands.

Notice the structure of the debate: both sides claimed to speak for
American ideals, and both sides mixed principle with interest. Your
job as a historian is not to declare a winner but to weigh arguments
against evidence. Did annexation bring consent? No — Filipinos fought
a three-year war against it. Did it bring markets and bases? Yes — and
it also brought the costs the anti-imperialists predicted: war,
occupation, and a long argument about who counted as American.

## Part 3 — The war the treaty started

The Philippine-American War is the part of 1898 Americans most often
forget, and the part that tests the expansionists' case hardest. U.S.
forces defeated Aguinaldo's conventional army, then fought a grinding
guerrilla war marked by harsh tactics on both sides, including U.S.
"reconcentration" of civilians and reported atrocities. American
anti-imperialists publicized the conduct of the war; the Army
court-martialed some officers. The war ended in 1902 with a U.S.
victory and a colonial government that would last until Philippine
independence in 1946.

Filipino deaths are the hardest numbers in this unit. U.S. military
deaths are well documented (about 4,200). Filipino combat deaths and
civilian deaths from war, disease, and famine are estimated in the
tens of thousands of fighters and far more civilians, with civilian
estimates ranging into the hundreds of thousands. Historians disagree
because wartime record-keeping in the Philippines was incomplete and
because disease deaths are hard to attribute. State the range, name
the uncertainty, and never present a disputed estimate as a census.

## Guided practice

1. Sequence and link: *Maine* explosion (Feb 15, 1898) → war
   declaration (Apr 25, 1898) → Manila Bay (May 1, 1898) → armistice
   (Aug 12, 1898) → Treaty of Paris (Dec 10, 1898) → fighting at Manila
   (Feb 4, 1899). Pick two adjacent events and explain the causal link
   between them.
2. Worked-example style: A student writes, "Yellow journalism caused
   the Spanish-American War." Rewrite the sentence to distinguish
   trigger, context, and causes, using the fuel-and-spark distinction
   from Worked example 1.
3. Compare one anti-imperialist argument (consent of the governed) with
   one expansionist argument (duty/benevolence). For each, name one
   piece of evidence from the lesson that supports it and one that
   weakens it.
4. Explain why the Teller Amendment did not protect the Philippines.
   What does that tell you about how treaties and amendments work as
   different kinds of promises?

## Independent practice

5. A textbook says the U.S. "liberated Cuba and the Philippines from
   Spain." Identify what that sentence gets right, what it leaves
   out, and rewrite it as a two-sentence historically careful claim.
6. Error analysis: "The Senate unanimously approved the Treaty of
   Paris, so all Americans supported empire." Find two errors and
   correct each with evidence (ratification margin; the
   Anti-Imperialist League).
7. The Treaty of Paris took effect April 11, 1899. The
   Philippine-American War began February 4, 1899 — before the treaty
   took effect. Explain in two sentences why that sequence matters for
   judging the claim that Filipinos "accepted" American rule.
8. Explain-your-reasoning: If you had been a senator in February 1899,
   would you have voted to ratify the treaty? Give one reason for and
   one reason against, then state your vote and its strongest
   counterargument.

## Applied task

Write a one-paragraph "letter to the editor" from 1899 taking ONE side
of the annexation debate (expansionist or anti-imperialist). Use two
pieces of evidence from the lesson, avoid invented quotations, and end
with the strongest objection your opponent would raise.

## Exit check

State the Treaty of Paris's four territorial outcomes (Cuba, Puerto
Rico, Guam, Philippines) and the one-sentence core of each side of the
annexation debate.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "*Maine* = proven Spanish attack" — the
cause was unproven in 1898 and is still debated; teach trigger vs.
cause; (2) "the war ended in 1898" — the Philippine war ran to 1902;
(3) "everyone supported empire" — the ratification margin and the
League's breadth refute this. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`,
Lesson 2.
