# Lesson 4 — The Great War and the American Decision: Causes, Neutrality, and Entry

**Goal:** You will distinguish the long-term causes of World War I
from the events that pulled the United States in, and argue with
evidence why America entered in April 1917 — not in 1914, and not in
1915.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
timeline space. **Adult note:** this lesson covers war and submarine
attacks on passenger ships. Sources are documentary and textual; no
graphic imagery is used.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 2, explain the difference
between a trigger and a cause, using the *Maine*. (If you can't:
re-read Lesson 2, Worked example 1 — a spark is not the fuel. Today's
lesson applies that distinction to a much bigger war.)

## Part 1 — A continent wired to explode

On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the
Austro-Hungarian throne, was assassinated in Sarajevo by a Bosnian Serb
nationalist. Within five weeks Europe was at war. The assassination
was the spark. The fuel had been accumulating for decades:

- **Alliance system.** Europe had divided into two armed camps: the
  Triple Entente (Britain, France, Russia) and the Triple Alliance
  (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy — Italy would switch sides in
  1915). Treaties promised that an attack on one ally meant war with
  all, so a Balkan crisis became a continental war by automatic
  commitment.
- **Nationalism.** Rival nationalisms — Serbian, German, French
  (seeking the return of Alsace-Lorraine, lost in 1871), and the
  nationalisms inside the multi-ethnic Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman
  empires — made compromise look like betrayal.
- **Militarism and imperial rivalry.** An Anglo-German naval arms race,
  huge standing armies, and competition for colonies in Africa and
  Asia made war planners treat war as likely and plan for it — the
  German Schlieffen Plan, for example, assumed a two-front war and
  required invading neutral Belgium, which brought Britain in.
- **The July Crisis.** Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia, issued an
  ultimatum designed to be rejected, and declared war on July 28,
  1914. Russia mobilized for Serbia; Germany declared war on Russia
  (August 1) and France (August 3) and invaded Belgium; Britain
  declared war on Germany (August 4). In a week, the alliance machine
  did what it was built to do.

None of this made American entry inevitable. The United States
proclaimed neutrality in August 1914, and President Woodrow Wilson
urged Americans to be "impartial in thought as well as in action."
Neutrality, however, collided with two facts: American trade with
Britain and France was enormous, and Britain's naval blockade and
Germany's submarine campaign both threatened American ships, lives,
and commerce.

### Worked example 1 — Long-term cause, trigger, or neither?

*Question:* Classify each: (a) the Anglo-German naval race; (b) the
assassination at Sarajevo; (c) the U.S. economy's dependence on trade
with Britain and France.

*Model reasoning:* (a) Long-term cause — it built the expectation of
war and the weapons to fight it, but it did not decide the date.
(b) Trigger — it set the July Crisis in motion, but without the
alliance system it would likely have remained a Balkan crisis.
(c) Neither a cause of the *European* war nor a trigger — it is a
reason American neutrality was fragile: it explains why the U.S. had
more to lose from a British defeat and more ships in harm's way. The
method: always ask "cause of *what*, for *whom*?" A factor can be
central to one question and background to another.

### Worked example 2 — Why not 1915?

*Question:* If the *Lusitania* sinking (May 7, 1915) killed 128
Americans, why did the U.S. wait until 1917 to declare war?

*Model reasoning:* Because one outrage rarely changes a nation's
course unless the context changes with it. In 1915 Wilson demanded
that Germany stop attacking passenger ships, and Germany, fearing
American entry, backed down with the Arabic pledge (1915) and the
Sussex pledge (1916), promising to warn before sinking merchant ships.
American public opinion was also divided: many Americans had German,
Irish, or Scandinavian roots and opposed joining Britain's war, and
Wilson had just won re-election in 1916 on the slogan "He kept us out
of war." By early 1917 the context had changed twice over: Germany
resumed unrestricted submarine warfare on February 1, 1917, sinking
American ships in March, and the Zimmermann Telegram — Germany's
January 1917 offer of an alliance to Mexico, promising Texas, New
Mexico, and Arizona in return — was published on March 1, 1917. The
combination of dead American sailors *and* a German proposal to attack
the U.S. from the south moved a divided public. The lesson: entry
required both a grievance and a changed political context.

## Part 2 — The decision: April 1917

On April 2, 1917, Wilson asked Congress for war against Germany. His
speech framed the war as a moral cause: "The world must be made safe
for democracy." He argued that Germany's submarine warfare was "warfare
against mankind," that neutral rights had been violated, and that the
U.S. fought for principles, not territory — "we have no selfish ends
to serve." Congress declared war on April 6, 1917, with the Senate
voting 82–6 and the House 373–50.

Notice what Wilson did *not* claim: he did not claim the U.S. had
been attacked on its own soil, and he did not claim America was
joining the Allies' war aims wholesale — he called the U.S. an
"associated" power, not an ally, preserving freedom to pursue its own
peace terms later. That distinction mattered in 1919 (Lesson 5).

The war effort that followed was total. The Selective Service Act
(May 18, 1917) registered 24 million men; more than 2 million were
inducted. About 2 million American soldiers reached France, and
American troops helped stop Germany's spring 1918 offensives and push
back in the Meuse-Argonne campaign (September–November 1918), the
largest American operation of the war. By the Armistice of November
11, 1918, 4,734,991 Americans had served; 116,516 had died (53,402 in
battle, 63,114 from disease and other non-battle causes); 204,002 were
wounded. Disease killed more Americans than bullets — a fact worth
remembering when war is described in heroic shorthand.

## Part 3 — The isolationist case, stated fairly

Not everyone agreed with entry, and the unit requires you to state
the opposing case fairly before judging it. Isolationists and
anti-war voices — including Senator Robert La Follette, who voted
against the declaration, and the Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs, who
would be jailed under the Espionage Act — argued that the war was a
European imperial quarrel, that American bankers and munitions makers
profited from it, that submarine warfare was a response to Britain's
illegal blockade, and that entering would cost American lives for
goals America had not chosen. Some of these claims were stronger than
others: the blockade point was real (Britain did violate neutral
rights, and Wilson protested far more mildly to London than to
Berlin); the "merchants of death" claim exaggerated a real financial
interest into a conspiracy. A fair account admits the blockade
asymmetry and still asks: did it justify Germany's sinking of
passenger ships and its offer to Mexico? Most historians answer no —
but the question is the lesson's, and your answer must show its work.

## Guided practice

1. Classify each as long-term cause, trigger, or context for U.S.
   entry, with one reason: alliance system; Sarajevo assassination;
   U.S. trade with Britain/France; Zimmermann Telegram; resumption of
   unrestricted submarine warfare.
2. Worked-example style: A student writes, "The Zimmermann Telegram
   caused America to enter WWI." Revise that sentence to name the
   telegram's role *and* the submarine warfare context, using the
   fuel-and-spark distinction.
3. Compare Wilson's two claims in his war message: (a) Germany waged
   "warfare against mankind"; (b) America had "no selfish ends to
   serve." For each, name one piece of evidence that supports it and
   one that complicates it.
4. Explain why the U.S. called itself an "associated" power rather
   than an ally. What did that wording preserve for 1919?

## Independent practice

5. Build a timeline from July 1914 to April 1917 with at least eight
   dated events. Mark each event C (cause), T (trigger), or X
   (context), and write one sentence explaining why April 1917, not
   May 1915, was the entry point.
6. Error analysis: "America entered WWI because Germany sank the
   *Lusitania*." Identify what this sentence omits (the 1915–1917
   gap, the pledges, the 1917 changes) and rewrite it as a
   three-sentence causal account.
7. Explain-your-reasoning: Was Wilson's neutrality (1914–1917)
   genuinely neutral? Consider trade, loans to the Allies, and the
   different protests to Britain and Germany. Give a two-sided answer
   and a judgment.
8. The Senate voted 82–6 and the House 373–50 for war. What do those
   margins tell you about the state of American opinion in April
   1917 — and what do the dissenting votes tell you about its limits?

## Applied task

Write a two-paragraph brief for a 1917 newspaper editor: paragraph 1
argues for entry (two pieces of evidence), paragraph 2 argues against
(two pieces of evidence). End with your own one-sentence verdict and
the strongest counterargument to it.

## Exit check

Name the four long-term causes from Part 1 and the two 1917 changes
that, together, ended neutrality.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult pre-teaches *ultimatum*,
  *mobilization*, and *associated power*; Part 1 may be read as a
  cause-sorting exercise with cards.
- **Alternate response mode:** the timeline in Independent practice
  5 may be drawn as a visual timeline with icons instead of sentences.
- **Extension:** read the decoded Zimmermann Telegram text (via the
  resource pack) and write three sentences on what it does and does
  not prove about German intentions toward the United States.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "The assassination caused WWI by itself"
— teach spark vs. fuel; (2) "America entered because of the
*Lusitania*" — teach the 1915–1917 gap and the changed context;
(3) "Neutrality meant equal treatment" — the blockade asymmetry
complicates that claim. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`,
Lesson 4.
