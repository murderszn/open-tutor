# Lesson 5 — The War at Home and the Peace That Failed

**Goal:** You will explain how World War I changed life inside the
United States — mobilization, migration, and limits on speech — and
why the peace President Wilson designed failed in the Senate.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
timeline space. **Adult note:** this lesson covers wartime repression,
racial violence, and a pandemic. Sources are documentary and textual;
no graphic imagery is used.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 4, name the two 1917
changes that ended neutrality. (If you can't: re-read Lesson 4,
Worked example 2 — resumed unrestricted submarine warfare and the
Zimmermann Telegram. Today's lesson asks what the war did *after*
that decision.)

## Part 1 — Mobilization: a government big enough for total war

War transformed the federal government's size and reach. The War
Industries Board (led by Bernard Baruch) coordinated factory
production; the Food Administration (Herbert Hoover) urged voluntary
conservation ("Meatless Mondays," "Wheatless Wednesdays") rather than
rationing; the Railroad Administration ran the railroads; the
Committee on Public Information (George Creel) produced films,
posters, and speeches to sell the war — and to demonize the enemy,
including German Americans, whose language instruction and newspapers
came under attack. Liberty Bonds financed the war; women took factory
and clerical jobs in large numbers; and the draft pulled more than 2
million men from civilian life.

The war also accelerated the Great Migration. With European
immigration cut off by war and northern factories desperate for labor,
Black southerners moved north in large numbers — about 1.6 million
between 1910 and 1930, with the flow sharply accelerating after 1916.
Migrants found higher wages and new freedoms, and also crowded
housing, job competition, and violent backlash: the East St. Louis
race riot of 1917 and the "Red Summer" of 1919, when white mobs
attacked Black communities in dozens of cities, showed that moving
north did not mean escaping racism. Migration changed America
profoundly — but it was a change bought with hardship, not a gift.

## Part 2 — Speech under pressure

The war tested the First Amendment. The Espionage Act (June 15, 1917)
made it a crime to interfere with the war effort, including by
obstructing recruitment; the Sedition Act (May 16, 1918) criminalized
"disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the
government, flag, or military. The Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs was
sentenced to ten years in prison for a 1918 speech urging resistance to
the war; the government prosecuted hundreds under the Acts, and
hundreds of German-language newspapers closed under pressure.

The Supreme Court upheld the convictions. In *Schenck v. United
States* (March 1919), Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., writing for a
unanimous Court, held that Charles Schenck's anti-draft leaflets could
be punished because they created a "clear and present danger" of
obstructing recruitment during wartime. The test sounded protective —
speech could only be punished when danger was clear and present — but
in practice the Court applied it loosely, and dissenters went to
prison. Later Courts would narrow the test sharply (notably in
*Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 1969), but that is a later story: in 1919 the
law said wartime speech had narrow protection.

### Worked example 1 — On paper vs. in practice, again

*Question:* The First Amendment says Congress shall make no law
abridging freedom of speech. The Espionage and Sedition Acts punished
speech. How can both be true?

*Model reasoning:* Because a constitutional right and its judicial
interpretation are different things. The Amendment's words did not
change in 1917; the Court's reading of what they allowed changed with
the war. Holmes's "clear and present danger" test *claimed* to protect
speech except in emergencies — but the Court treated wartime dissent
itself as the emergency. The pattern should be familiar by now (Units
01–03 keep repeating it): a promise on paper, a practice shaped by
power and fear, and a later generation re-reading the promise. Your
answer should name the mechanism — judicial interpretation during
wartime — not just the contradiction.

### Worked example 2 — Two freedoms, one war

*Question:* A Black soldier returning from France in 1919 wrote that
he had fought "for democracy" abroad and found it denied at home.
Another American in 1919 said the Espionage Act protected democracy
by silencing its enemies. Can both claims be about the same war?

*Model reasoning:* Yes, because the war meant different things to
different Americans, and "democracy" was the contested word. For the
returning soldier, the war's promise (Wilson's "safe for democracy")
highlighted its denial at home — segregation, lynching, and the Red
Summer's violence. For the Act's defender, democracy meant national
unity and victory, and dissent looked like sabotage. The historian's
job is not to pick the more sympathetic voice but to explain why the
same war produced both: because Wilson's universal language collided
with an unequal country. Note the method: compare claims by their
evidence and their definitions, not by their sincerity.

## Part 3 — The peace that failed at home

In January 1918 Wilson had announced his Fourteen Points: open
covenants, freedom of the seas, free trade, disarmament,
self-determination for peoples, and — Point 14 — a "general
association of nations" to keep the peace: the League of Nations.
After the Armistice (November 11, 1918), Wilson went to Paris himself
— the first president to travel to Europe while in office — and
negotiated the Treaty of Versailles, signed June 28, 1919. The treaty
created the League, redrew Europe's map, and imposed reparations and a
"war guilt" clause on Germany. It was a compromise: Wilson got the
League but accepted much harsher terms for Germany than his Points had
promised, and colonial peoples' hopes for self-determination were
largely deferred.

Then the treaty came home. The Constitution requires two-thirds of the
Senate to ratify a treaty, and the Senate split three ways.
**Irreconcilables** (led by William Borah and Hiram Johnson) rejected
the treaty outright — many were anti-imperialists who saw the League
as another entangling commitment. **Reservationists** (led by Henry
Cabot Lodge) would accept the treaty only with reservations protecting
American independence of action, especially against Article 10, which
committed League members to defend each other. **Wilson's supporters**
wanted the treaty unchanged. Wilson, unwilling to compromise and
weakened by a stroke in October 1919, told his supporters to vote
against the treaty *with* reservations — killing the compromise. On
November 19, 1919, the Senate rejected the treaty with Lodge's
reservations (39–55) and without them (38–53). A final attempt on
March 19, 1920, failed 49–35, seven votes short of two-thirds. The
United States never joined the League of Nations, and in 1921 it made
a separate peace with Germany.

Was the failure Wilson's fault, the Senate's, or the treaty's? All
three explanations have evidence: Wilson's refusal to compromise and
his failure to include senators (especially Republicans) in the Paris
delegation; the Senate's genuine constitutional concern about Article
10; and a treaty that disappointed almost everyone. The deeper lesson
is structural: in the American system, a president can promise the
world a new order and still lose it to 34 senators. The war ended;
the argument about America's role in the world did not — it runs
straight into the 1920s ("return to normalcy") and beyond.

Meanwhile, a different killer was at work: the influenza pandemic of
1918–1919, which killed about 675,000 Americans, many of them young
adults — more than five times the war's battle deaths. The pandemic
shaped the peace indirectly: it sickened delegates and soldiers,
strained the same public-health systems the war had stretched, and
reminds us that the war's death toll is incomplete without it.

## Guided practice

1. Name three mobilization agencies (War Industries Board, Food
   Administration, Committee on Public Information) and, for each,
   state one thing it did and one group it affected.
2. Worked-example style: A student writes, "The Great Migration
   solved racism." Revise that sentence using the East St. Louis
   (1917) and Red Summer (1919) evidence.
3. Explain Holmes's "clear and present danger" test in two sentences,
   then explain why it did not protect Debs in practice.
4. Classify three senators: one irreconcilable, one reservationist,
   one Wilson supporter. For each, state what outcome they wanted and
   what vote that implied on November 19, 1919.

## Independent practice

5. A student claims, "Wilson lost the League because he was
   stubborn." Evaluate that claim: name one piece of evidence for
   it, one piece of evidence for the Senate's constitutional
   concern, and one piece for the treaty's own problems. Then write a
   three-sentence judgment that uses all three.
6. Error analysis: "The United States joined the League of Nations
   but left in the 1930s." Find the error and correct it with the
   1919–1920 vote record.
7. Explain-your-reasoning: Did World War I expand American democracy
   or contract it? Give one expansion (women's suffrage momentum, the
   19th Amendment in 1920; Black migration and political organization)
   and one contraction (the Espionage/Sedition Acts; Red Summer
   violence), then state a judgment with a reason.
8. Compare the war's two death tolls: 53,402 battle deaths vs.
   63,114 non-battle deaths, and about 675,000 influenza deaths. What
   does the comparison suggest about how wars should be counted?

## Applied task

Write a one-page "Senate decision memo" dated March 1920, from the
viewpoint of a reservationist senator: recommend ratification *with*
reservations, using two pieces of evidence (Article 10's text and the
constitutional argument) and answering the irreconcilable objection
("any League is an entanglement") and the Wilson-supporter objection
("reservations will kill the League"). No invented quotations.

## Exit check

Name the three Senate factions, the two 1919–1920 vote outcomes that
mattered, and one domestic change the war produced that outlived it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult pre-teaches *reservation* (in the
  treaty sense — a condition attached to ratification, not the
  Lesson 1 land sense), *ratification*, and *reparations*; Part 3 may
  be read as a three-faction chart.
- **Alternate response mode:** the applied task may be delivered as
  a three-column faction chart with a one-paragraph recommendation.
- **Extension:** research one Red Summer city from a reputable
  museum or university source (adult-selected) and write three
  sentences on what happened there and what it shows about the war's
  aftermath.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "The U.S. joined the League" — it never
did; the votes are the evidence; (2) "The Espionage Act was
uncontroversial" — Debs's imprisonment and the unanimous *Schenck*
decision show a contested, then judicially settled, repression;
(3) "The war's deadliest toll was battle" — disease and influenza
exceeded it. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`, Lesson 5.
