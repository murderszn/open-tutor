# Lesson 3 — The United States as an Overseas Power: Philippines, Panama, the Caribbean, and China

**Goal:** You will explain how the United States governed its new
possessions and projected power between 1899 and 1914, and judge
whether "empire" is the right word for what it built.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
timeline space. **Adult note:** this lesson covers colonial rule and
intervention. Sources are documentary and textual; period cartoons
contain ethnic stereotypes — frame them as evidence of prejudice,
never as neutral humor.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 2, state the Treaty of
Paris's terms in one sentence. (If you can't: re-read Lesson 2, Part 1
— Cuba relinquished, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines ceded,
$20 million paid. Today's lesson asks what the U.S. *did* with those
places.)

## Part 1 — Governing an empire while denying the word

After 1898 the United States ruled different places in different ways,
and the differences matter. Puerto Rico and Guam became unincorporated
territories — under U.S. sovereignty but, the Supreme Court ruled in
the Insular Cases (1901), not automatically on the path to statehood,
and their residents not automatically full constitutional citizens.
The Philippines became a colony in all but name, governed first by the
military, then by a civilian commission under William Howard Taft, with
independence promised only vaguely and delivered in 1946. Hawaii, whose
queen had been overthrown in 1893 with American help, was annexed in
1898 and became a territory.

Cuba was the special case. The Teller Amendment had promised no
annexation, so the U.S. withdrew its army in 1902 — but only after
Cuba accepted the Platt Amendment (March 2, 1901) as part of its
constitution. The Platt Amendment limited Cuba's right to make treaties
or borrow money and gave the United States the right to intervene to
preserve Cuban independence and order — plus a naval base at
Guantánamo Bay. Cuba was independent on paper and constrained in
practice: a protectorate, not a colony, and not fully sovereign
either. The U.S. used the intervention right in 1906 and again in
1912.

The pattern to notice: the United States built an empire of *varied*
legal forms — territory, colony, protectorate — precisely so it could
claim it was not an empire at all. Historians still argue about the
word. What is not arguable is the control: the U.S. decided who
governed these places, what their economies served, and when their
soldiers could be used.

### Worked example 1 — Is a protectorate an empire?

*Question:* A student argues, "Cuba was independent after 1902, so
American empire ended at Cuba's borders." Evaluate that claim.

*Model reasoning:* The claim confuses a legal label with actual
control. Cuba had its own president and flag after 1902 — that is real
and should not be dismissed. But the Platt Amendment let the United
States veto Cuba's treaties and debts and intervene militarily, and
the U.S. did intervene. A good answer holds both facts: Cuba was more
independent than the Philippines, and less independent than a country
that could make its own treaties freely. "Empire" is a judgment word;
the evidence that matters is the *mechanism* of control — here, the
Platt Amendment's text and the interventions that followed.

### Worked example 2 — Reading a policy's name against its effect

*Question:* The Roosevelt Corollary (1904) said the U.S. would act as
an "international police power" in the Western Hemisphere when
countries failed to pay debts or keep order. Supporters called it
protection; critics called it domination. Which reading fits the
evidence better?

*Model reasoning:* Start with what happened, not what it was called.
Under the Corollary, the U.S. took over the Dominican Republic's
customs houses in 1905, intervened in Haiti in 1915, and in Nicaragua
in 1912 — and the interventions served American creditors and
strategic interests as well as (sometimes) local stability. A policy
that repeatedly produces long occupations and control of another
country's finances fits "domination" better than "protection," even
if individual interventions also stopped a particular crisis. The
method: judge a policy by its repeated outcomes, not by its stated
motive.

## Part 2 — Panama: a canal, a revolution, and a zone

The second great project of the era was a canal across Panama. The
French had failed in the 1880s; the U.S. wanted the route for naval
mobility (the Spanish-American War had shown how slow it was to move
ships between oceans) and for trade. Panama was then a province of
Colombia, which rejected the U.S. treaty offer in 1903. President
Theodore Roosevelt then supported a Panamanian independence movement:
U.S. warships blocked Colombian troops, Panama declared independence in
November 1903, and two weeks later the new Panamanian government
signed the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty (November 18, 1903), giving the
United States a canal zone "in perpetuity" and control over it. The
canal opened August 15, 1914.

The canal was an engineering triumph and a political scandal at once.
It cut weeks off ocean voyages and made the U.S. Navy a two-ocean
force. It was also built with a workforce of mostly Caribbean laborers
working in harsh, disease-ridden conditions in the early years, and it
rested on a treaty signed by a French engineer (Philippe Bunau-Varilla)
acting for Panama before Panama had a functioning government of its
own. When you evaluate the canal, keep both halves of that sentence.

## Part 3 — China: empire without colonies

The Open Door policy was the American answer to a different problem.
In the late 1890s European powers were carving China into spheres of
influence — zones where one power controlled trade. Secretary of State
John Hay's Open Door Notes (September 1899 and July 1900) asked the
powers to keep Chinese trade open to all nations and to preserve
China's territorial integrity. The notes were not a treaty and no
power formally signed them, but none rejected them outright either,
and the policy became the stated basis of U.S. China policy.

When the Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901) — a Chinese anti-foreign uprising
— threatened foreigners in Beijing, the U.S. joined an eight-nation
force to suppress it, then used its share of the indemnity to fund
Chinese students studying in America (the Boxer Indemnity
Scholarships). The Open Door shows the third form of American power in
this era: not annexation, not protectorate, but a claim to equal
commercial access enforced by the threat of joint action. Some
historians call this an "informal empire" — control through trade,
finance, and the threat of force rather than flags.

By 1914 the United States was, by any honest measure, a world power
with colonies, protectorates, a canal zone, and a declared right to
police a hemisphere. The debate from Lesson 2 was not settled; it was
outlived by the facts on the ground.

## Guided practice

1. Classify each place by its form of U.S. control in 1905 —
   territory, colony, protectorate, or canal zone — and give one
   mechanism of control for each: Puerto Rico, the Philippines, Cuba,
   the Panama Canal Zone.
2. Worked-example style: A student says, "The Open Door policy
   protected China." Rewrite that claim to distinguish the policy's
   stated aim from its effect, using the Boxer Rebellion evidence.
3. Explain why the U.S. could claim Cuba was independent while still
   intervening there. Name the exact mechanism (Platt Amendment) and
   one intervention it enabled.
4. Compare the Panama Canal case with the Philippines case: in each,
   who decided, who benefited, and who paid the costs? Two sentences
   per case.

## Independent practice

5. A map of 1914 shows U.S. flags over Puerto Rico, Guam, the
   Philippines, Hawaii, and the Canal Zone. A student captions it
   "America's empire." Another captions it "America's
   responsibilities." Which caption is more defensible, and what
   evidence would settle the argument? (Your answer should discuss
   mechanisms of control, not just labels.)
6. Error analysis: "The Roosevelt Corollary was part of the Monroe
   Doctrine from 1823." Identify the error (date and relationship)
   and correct it: the Corollary was Roosevelt's 1904 *extension* of
   Monroe's 1823 doctrine.
7. Sequence these and explain the link between two neighbors: Platt
   Amendment (1901); Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty (1903); Roosevelt
   Corollary (1904); canal opens (1914).
8. Explain-your-reasoning: Was the Panama Canal worth its political
   cost? Give one argument that it was and one that it was not, then
   state your judgment with a reason.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "All colonies were the same" — the lesson's
four forms exist precisely to break that habit; (2) "The Open Door was
a treaty China signed" — it was unilateral notes no power formally
accepted; (3) "The canal was purely American engineering" — the
workforce was largely Caribbean, and the treaty politics were
contested. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md`, Lesson 3.
