# Lesson 4 — Labor: Unions, Strikes, and Working Conditions

**Goal:** You will describe industrial working conditions with verified
figures, compare the Knights of Labor and the AFL, and explain what
happened at Haymarket (1886), Homestead (1892), and Pullman (1894) —
and why each outcome mattered for labor's future.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Adult note:** strikes involved deaths; keep the account factual,
not sensational.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** In one sentence, why did factories
*need* so many workers — and who filled those jobs? (If you can't:
Unit 01, Lesson 4 + Unit 02, Lesson 1 — industrial expansion created
the demand; immigrants and rural migrants supplied it.)

## Part 1 — What industrial work was like

The numbers first — all verified at authoring, all rounded:

- **Hours:** a 60-hour week (10 hours a day, 6 days) was standard in
  manufacturing around 1890; 12-hour days were common in steel.
- **Wages:** pay varied wildly by skill, but unskilled factory hands
  earned on the order of $1–1.50 a day — enough to survive in a
  tenement, not enough to save.
- **Danger:** industrial accidents killed and maimed thousands yearly;
  mines, railroads, and steel mills were the deadliest workplaces.
  There was no workers' compensation in most states until the 1910s —
  an injured worker's family simply lost its income.
- **Child labor:** about **1.7 million children aged 10–15** were
  recorded as working in the 1900 census — in mills, mines, factories,
  and street trades. (The census counted formal employment; the real
  number, including informal work, was higher.)

Add the legal context: **yellow-dog contracts** (sign-or-don't-work
agreements not to join a union), **blacklists** of organizers, and
courts that treated strikes as conspiracies. Workers who organized
risked not just their jobs but their names.

This is the structural fact the whole lesson turns on: workers had
*numbers* — millions of them — but employers had the law, the courts,
the police, and, when needed, private armies. Every labor strategy in
Part 2 is an attempt to solve that asymmetry.

## Part 2 — Two unions, two theories

**The Knights of Labor** (founded 1869 as a secret society, opened to
the public in the 1880s) welcomed *almost everyone*: skilled and
unskilled, Black workers, women, immigrants. Its theory: all producers
share an interest against concentrated capital, so organize *broadly*.
At its 1886 peak it claimed over 700,000 members — then collapsed
after Haymarket (Part 3), as employers and the press tied it to
radicalism.

**The American Federation of Labor (AFL)** (founded 1886, led by
Samuel Gompers) took the opposite bet: organize *skilled craft
workers* — carpenters, cigar makers, iron molders — whose skills gave
them bargaining leverage. Its theory: win concrete gains (wages,
hours, conditions) for members through strikes and contracts; avoid
broad political crusades. It excluded most unskilled workers, Black
workers, and women by practice if not always by rule — a narrower,
more durable, and more exclusionary model. The AFL survived and grew;
the Knights did not.

Neither theory was obviously right. The Knights' inclusiveness was
morally broader but organizationally fragile; the AFL's craft focus
won real contracts but abandoned the workers who needed unions most.
When you evaluate labor's strategies, keep both the *wins* and the
*exclusions* in view.

## Part 3 — Three strikes that defined the era

**Haymarket, Chicago — May 4, 1886.** A rally for the eight-hour
workday, peaceful at first. As police moved to disperse the crowd,
someone threw a bomb: **7 police officers and several civilians died**
in the blast and the gunfire that followed. Eight labor activists were
tried; **four were executed** despite thin evidence tying them to the
bomb. The effect: public opinion turned against labor radicalism, and
the Knights of Labor — already strained — collapsed. Lesson: *violence
by an unknown actor can destroy a movement that didn't commit it.*

**Homestead, Pennsylvania — July 1892.** Carnegie Steel cut wages at
its Homestead works and locked out the Amalgamated Association of Iron
and Steel Workers. The company brought in 300 armed **Pinkerton**
agents by barge; on July 6 a pitched battle killed workers and
Pinkertons alike. The state militia then occupied the town, and the
strike collapsed after four months. The union was crushed, and steel —
the era's core industry — stayed effectively non-union for decades.
Lesson: *when employers could hire private armies and call the
militia, strikes faced two enemies.*

**Pullman, Chicago — May–July 1894.** The Pullman Palace Car Company
cut wages but **did not cut rents** in its company town, where workers
had to live. The American Railway Union, led by **Eugene V. Debs**,
refused to handle Pullman cars — a nationwide boycott that stopped
rail traffic across the country. President **Grover Cleveland** sent
federal troops, over the Illinois governor's objection, on the grounds
that the strike obstructed the mail and interstate commerce. A federal
court issued an **injunction** against the union — the first use of the
injunction to break a strike — and Debs went to jail for contempt.
Lesson: *the federal government could now break a strike directly, and
the courts gave it the tool.*

The pattern across all three: labor lost each confrontation in the
short run — but each defeat taught the movement something (organize
the unskilled, expect the injunction, build political power), and the
publicity pushed reformers toward the workplace-safety and hours
legislation of Lesson 5.

### Worked example 1 — Comparing union theories

*Question:* A steelworker in 1890 is unskilled, Black, and wants to
join a union. Which organization would take him, and what does that
tell you about each union's theory?

*Model reasoning:* The **Knights of Labor** would take him — its
theory was broad organization of all producers regardless of skill,
race, or (formally) sex. The **AFL** in practice would not: its craft
unions organized skilled workers and largely excluded Black workers.
What this tells you: the Knights optimized for *solidarity* (everyone
in) at the cost of bargaining leverage; the AFL optimized for
*leverage* (skilled workers employers couldn't easily replace) at the
cost of solidarity. The question tests whether you connect membership
rules to strategy, not just memorize names.

### Worked example 2 — Why the outcome mattered, not just what happened

*Question:* At Homestead the company won and the union was crushed.
Why does the *outcome* matter more than the battle's details for
understanding the next 40 years?

*Model reasoning:* The details (Pinkertons, July 6, the militia) are
the story; the outcome is the *structure*. Because the union was
crushed in steel — the economy's core industry — employers across
heavy industry learned that lockouts plus private force plus state
troops worked, and workers learned that craft organization alone
couldn't hold an industry. The result: steel stayed non-union into
the 1930s, and labor's next breakthrough required a different model
(the industrial unionism of the 1930s CIO — beyond this unit, but the
*reason* for it starts here). Always ask of a strike: *what did each
side learn?*

## Part 4 — The Triangle fire: when conditions became a scandal

On **Saturday, March 25, 1911**, fire broke out on the 8th–10th floors
of the Asch Building in Manhattan, in the Triangle Shirtwaist factory.
**146 garment workers died** — 123 women and girls and 23 men, most of
them young Italian and Jewish immigrants aged 14 to 23. The doors to
stairwells and exits were **locked** (to stop theft and unauthorized
breaks); there were **no sprinklers**; the fire escape collapsed;
firefighters' ladders reached only the sixth floor. The tragedy was
over in about 30 minutes.

The aftermath is the lesson's bridge to Week 10: public outrage —
including a silent funeral march of hundreds of thousands — drove New
York to pass roughly **30 workplace-safety laws**, created a factory
investigating commission (where a young **Frances Perkins** did the
fieldwork that would later make her the first woman in a presidential
cabinet), and swelled the International Ladies' Garment Workers'
Union. Conditions alone had not produced reform for decades;
*conditions plus publicity plus organization* did. Remember that
formula for Lesson 5.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Define in one sentence each: strike, lockout, boycott, injunction,
   yellow-dog contract. (Hint: each definition should name *who acts*
   and *against whom*.)
2. Compare the Knights and the AFL on three axes: who could join,
   what they wanted, what happened to them.
3. For each strike (Haymarket, Homestead, Pullman), state in two
   sentences: what triggered it and what the outcome taught labor.
4. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "Strikes failed, so unions were
   useless." Identify the flaw (short-run defeats vs. long-run
   lessons; the AFL *did* win contracts; defeats drove reform) and
   rewrite the claim accurately.

## Independent practice

5. Write a 5-sentence paragraph: why did employers usually win
   strikes in this era? Cite at least three advantages (law, force,
   money, public opinion) with one example each.
6. A 1900 census table shows 1.7 million working children aged
   10–15. Write 4 sentences on what that number *proves* (child
   labor was widespread) and what it *doesn't prove* (exact total —
   informal work was uncounted; conditions — the number alone doesn't
   describe them).
7. Timeline task: order Haymarket (1886), AFL founding (1886),
   Homestead (1892), Pullman (1894), Triangle fire (1911), and add
   one sentence to each on its significance.

## Applied task

Choose one strike (Haymarket, Homestead, or Pullman) and write two
150-word newspaper reports of the same day's events — one from a
pro-labor paper, one from a pro-business paper — both using only facts
from this lesson. Then add a 4-sentence "editor's note": where do the
accounts agree, where do they differ, and which differences come from
*perspective* rather than *facts*? (Both reports must be labeled as
classroom exercises.)

## Exit check

In two sentences: name one way the Knights of Labor and the AFL
differed, and one strike outcome that weakened labor in the short run.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** vocabulary cards (injunction,
  lockout, boycott); the adult reads the strike summaries aloud
  before Q3.
- **Alternate response mode:** the timeline (Q7) may be built with
  cards on a table; the exit check may be oral.
- **Extension:** research Eugene V. Debs after Pullman (his 1894–95
  imprisonment, his turn to socialism, his presidential campaigns)
  and write a paragraph on how defeat radicalized one labor leader.
