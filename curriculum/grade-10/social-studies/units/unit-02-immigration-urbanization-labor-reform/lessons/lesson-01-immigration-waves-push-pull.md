# Lesson 1 — Why People Came: Immigration Waves, 1865–1920

**Goal:** You will explain why roughly 25 million people immigrated to
the United States between 1870 and 1920, distinguish *push* from *pull*
factors, and describe the shift from "old" to "new" immigration — and
why the arrival experience differed by coast.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
timeline space. **Adult note:** no graphic content; preview the
steerage discussion for younger siblings if the lesson is read aloud.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Unit 01, name one thing
industrialization changed about American work. (If you can't: re-read
Unit 01, Lesson 4's opening — factories needed workers, and the people
who filled those jobs are this unit's subject.)

## Part 1 — A wave bigger than any before it

Between 1870 and 1920, roughly **25 million immigrants** arrived in the
United States. That number is a rounded historical estimate, not a
census count, but its shape is certain: the wave was far larger than
anything before it, and most of its passengers settled in cities and
industrial towns rather than on farms. Two forces met to create it.

First, **America was hiring.** Railroads, steel mills, coal mines,
textile mills, and meatpacking plants needed millions of hands. A
factory in Pittsburgh or a mine in Pennsylvania could pay wages that —
even at pennies by today's standards — beat what a landless farm
laborer in Sicily or a Jewish craftsman facing pogroms in the Russian
Empire could expect at home.

Second, **life at home was pushing people out.** Historians sort the
causes into two buckets, and keeping them straight matters:

- **Push factors** — conditions that drive people *away* from home:
  crop failure and famine, land shortage (too many sons for too few
  farms), religious persecution, political repression, and military
  conscription.
- **Pull factors** — conditions that attract people *to* a
  destination: jobs, higher wages, cheap land (early in the period),
  family already in America, religious freedom, and political stability.

A single immigrant's decision usually combined both: a Sicilian farmer
pushed by land hunger *and* pulled by a cousin's letter from a Chicago
packinghouse job; a Russian Jew pushed by pogroms *and* pulled by the
promise that America had no state church.

## Part 2 — "Old" and "new" immigration

Historians divide the era's immigration into two rough waves. The labels
are historian shorthand, not insults — but they were used in the period
itself by nativists who preferred the first group, which is why you
should always ask *who* is using the label and *why*.

**Old immigration (before ~1880):** mostly Northern and Western Europe
— Ireland, Germany, Britain, Scandinavia. Many were Protestant or
Catholic, spoke languages closer to English, and arrived when America
still had cheap western land to offer. Irish immigrants fled the potato
famine of the 1840s; German immigrants included both farmers and
political refugees from the failed revolutions of 1848.

**New immigration (after ~1880):** mostly Southern and Eastern Europe
— Italy, Austria-Hungary, the Russian Empire — plus Asia (China, Japan,
Korea, the Philippines). Most were Catholic, Orthodox, or Jewish, spoke
languages far from English, and arrived *after* the best farmland was
taken. They settled overwhelmingly in industrial cities: New York,
Chicago, Pittsburgh, and the mill towns of New England and the
Mid-Atlantic. The shift matters because it changed what Americans
argued about: not *whether* immigrants would come, but *which*
immigrants were "desirable" — the argument that produced the exclusion
laws in Lesson 3.

### Worked example 1 — Push, pull, or both?

*Question:* A letter from 1903, written by an Italian farm laborer in
Naples to his brother in New York, says: "The landlord took half the
crop again, and the agent says the steel mill in Pittsburgh pays $1.50
a day. I am coming in the spring." Classify each reason.

*Model reasoning:* Two reasons are visible. "The landlord took half the
crop again" is a **push factor**: it describes a condition at home
(sharecropping-style exploitation) that makes staying worse. "The steel
mill pays $1.50 a day" is a **pull factor**: it describes an attraction
at the destination. The decision is *both* — most migration decisions
are. A good answer never forces a single label when the evidence shows
two. (Note the wage claim is from a letter, not a verified pay record:
letters were often optimistic. The lesson's point is the reasoning, not
the exact wage.)

### Worked example 2 — Reading a shift, not just a label

*Question:* In 1870, most immigrants came from Ireland and Germany. In
1907 — the peak year of the era — the largest groups came from Italy,
Austria-Hungary, and Russia. Did immigration *increase*, or did its
*composition* change?

*Model reasoning:* Both, but the question is testing whether you
separate the two claims. Volume rose: the era's total (~25 million,
1870–1920) dwarfs earlier decades. Composition *also* changed: the
leading origin countries shifted from northwestern to southern and
eastern Europe. Conflating the two is a common error — "new
immigration" means the second fact, not merely "more people."

## Part 3 — Two doors: Ellis Island and Angel Island

Almost every immigrant passed through a federal inspection station.
The experience depended on which ocean they crossed.

**Ellis Island (New York Harbor).** The federal station opened
January 1, 1892, and operated until 1954. More than **12 million**
immigrants were processed there, most of them European, most of them
between 1892 and 1924. The standard process was fast — a medical
inspection (including the famous chalk-mark eye check) and a legal
inspection of documents and funds — and most people passed in hours.
About 2% were excluded, usually for illness or likely dependence on
public support. First- and second-class passengers were inspected aboard
ship; **steerage** (third-class) passengers went through Ellis Island
itself — the system was class-filtered from the start.

**Angel Island (San Francisco Bay).** Opened in January 1910 and
operating until 1940, Angel Island processed **300,000+** immigrants —
far fewer than Ellis Island, but under a different logic. Because the
Chinese Exclusion Act (Lesson 3) barred Chinese laborers, Chinese
arrivals faced interrogations designed to *find a reason to exclude*:
detailed questions about family villages, house layouts, and
relatives, with answers compared against files. Detainees waited weeks
or months in barracks and carved poems into the walls — poems that
survive today as primary sources. Japanese, Korean, and Filipino
arrivals faced similar, if less absolute, scrutiny. Same country, same
era, opposite doors: the difference was the law, not the ocean.

**The key insight:** immigration policy is never just about numbers.
It is about *who* the nation wants, *who* it fears, and *what*
inspection is for — welcoming the healthy and willing, or excluding
the unwanted. Keep both doors in mind through Lessons 2–3.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Classify each as push, pull, or both, and name the evidence: (a) a
   Russian Jew fleeing a pogrom in 1905; (b) a German farmer in 1875
   reading a railroad pamphlet advertising $2.50/acre Nebraska land;
   (c) a Chinese laborer in 1873 recruited by a railroad agent.
2. Why did "new" immigrants settle in cities more than on farms? Give
   two reasons, one about America and one about the immigrants
   themselves.
3. Compare Ellis and Angel Island in three sentences: one on scale,
   one on process, one on the law behind the difference.
4. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "Immigrants came for jobs, so the
   economy alone explains immigration." Identify what this claim
   leaves out (push factors, persecution, family networks) and rewrite
   it as a two-factor explanation.

## Independent practice

5. Write a 5-sentence paragraph: why did immigration *surge* after
   1880? Cite at least one push factor, one pull factor, and one
   change in *where* immigrants came from.
6. A nativist pamphlet calls new immigrants "unassimilable." Using this
   lesson, write a 3-sentence reply that challenges the claim with
   evidence (jobs, cities, the inspection system) without denying that
   adaptation was hard.
7. Timeline task: place these in order and add one cause to each —
   1882, 1892, 1907, 1910. (Check: 1882 Exclusion Act; 1892 Ellis
   Island opens; 1907 peak immigration year, ~1.3 million arrivals;
   1910 Angel Island opens.)

## Applied task

Write a one-page "arrival interview" for a fictional immigrant family
in 1905 — clearly labeled as fiction — in which an Ellis Island
inspector asks, and the family answers, questions about origin,
occupation, destination, and funds. Then add a 3-sentence note: which
parts of your scene are documented fact (inspection questions, steerage
processing) and which are invented (the family's names and answers).

## Exit check

In two sentences: what is the difference between a push factor and a
pull factor, and why do most migration decisions involve both?
