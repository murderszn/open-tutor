# Lesson 2 — The New Industrial City

**Goal:** You will explain why American cities grew so fast between 1870
and 1920, describe tenement life and urban infrastructure honestly
(without romanticizing or horrifying it), and read an urbanization
dataset — computing change and explaining what the numbers show and
what they hide.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, the generated
image in `../assets/tenement-street-illustration.png`, a notebook.
**Adult note:** tenement conditions include disease and child labor;
keep the discussion factual and age-appropriate.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** In one sentence, where did most "new"
immigrants settle — farms or cities? (If you can't: Lesson 1, Part 2 —
cities, because the industrial jobs were there.)

## Part 1 — Why cities exploded

Three forces fed urban growth, and they reinforced each other:

1. **Jobs.** Factories, mills, mines, docks, and rail yards clustered in
   cities. A steel town or a garment district could employ tens of
   thousands within walking distance of a train station.
2. **Transport.** Streetcars, elevated trains, and subways (Boston's
   opened 1897; New York's 1904) let workers live farther from the
   factory — and let the city sprawl outward. But the poorest workers
   still walked: carfare cost money, and tenements clustered near work.
3. **Migration.** Immigrants arrived at ports that *were* cities — New
   York, Boston, Philadelphia, Baltimore — and many stayed. Rural
   Americans also moved: about 11 million people migrated from farms
   to cities between 1870 and 1920. Add natural increase (city families
   were large), and the result was growth no city government had
   planned for.

The census tells the story in one number. The share of Americans living
in urban places (the Census definition: towns of 2,500 people or more)
rose from **25.7% in 1870** to **39.7% in 1900** to **51.2% in 1920** —
the first census in which a majority of Americans lived in urban
places. Read that last figure carefully: "urban" here includes every
town of 2,500+, not just big cities. The milestone is real, but the
definition is generous — a good reminder that every statistic carries
its definition with it.

Now, the documentary facts behind the scene:

- **Tenements** were narrow, deep apartment buildings, often with four
  families per floor sharing hall toilets and a single water tap. The
  classic "dumbbell" tenement (named for its floor plan) had air shafts
  so narrow that interior rooms got almost no light or air. Disease
  spread fast: tuberculosis, cholera, and typhoid were tenement
  realities before clean-water and sanitation reforms.
- **But tenements were also communities.** Pushcart markets, mutual-aid
  societies, synagogues and churches, settlement houses (like Jane
  Addams's Hull-House, Chicago, founded 1889), and newspapers in a
  dozen languages made immigrant neighborhoods functioning societies,
  not just slums. Jacob Riis's *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) —
  photographs and text — exposed tenement conditions to middle-class
  readers; it is powerful evidence, but remember Riis was a *reformer
  with an argument*, not a neutral camera.
- **Infrastructure lagged, then caught up.** Cities built waterworks,
  sewers, parks, and elevated trains, but usually after the need was
  desperate. The pattern — growth first, services later — is the
  through-line of this lesson: private profit built the factories and
  tenements; public investment arrived only when reformers forced it.

### Worked example 1 — Computing change (and stating the definition)

*Question:* The urban share rose from 25.7% (1870) to 51.2% (1920). By
how many percentage points did it rise, and what does that number mean?

*Model reasoning:* 51.2 − 25.7 = **25.5 percentage points**. (Say
"percentage points," not "percent" — a 25.5-point rise on a 25.7 base
would be roughly a doubling, i.e., about a 99% *relative* increase, but
the clean statement is the point change.) Interpretation: in 1870 about
1 in 4 Americans lived in an urban place; by 1920 it was just over 1
in 2. Caveat to state with the answer: "urban" means 2,500+ people, so
the figure includes small towns — the big-city share alone was lower.
A number without its definition is a trap; a number *with* its
definition is evidence.

### Worked example 2 — Observation vs. inference

*Question:* From the image, list one observation and one inference, and
explain why they differ.

*Model reasoning:* **Observation:** "Laundry lines cross the street
between tall brick buildings." This is directly visible. **Inference:**
"Families did laundry at home and dried it outdoors because apartments
had no private drying space." This is a reasonable conclusion *drawn
from* the observation plus knowledge of tenement apartments — but it
could be wrong in a detail (a laundry business might own some lines).
Labeling the two keeps you honest: the image shows the lines; the
*explanation* is your reasoning, and it needs a second source before
it becomes a claim. (And remember: this image is an AI reconstruction,
so even its "observations" are observations of an illustration, not of
history itself.)

## Part 3 — Contrasting accounts of city life

Two kinds of voices describe the same city, and they disagree for
reasons you can name:

- **The reformer's report** (Riis, settlement-house workers, sanitary
  inspectors): the city as a *problem* — disease, crowding, vice,
  child labor. Its purpose was to move middle-class readers and
  legislators to act. Its blind spot: it often treated immigrants as
  victims to be rescued rather than as agents building their own
  institutions.
- **The immigrant's recollection** (letters, memoirs, oral histories):
  the city as *opportunity and community* — work, wages, schools,
  churches, newspapers, the chance to bring family over. Its blind spot:
  memory smooths hardship, and successful immigrants are more likely to
  leave memoirs than those who failed and returned.

The historian's job is not to pick a winner. It is to ask, for each
account: *who made it, for whom, and what would they gain by this
version?* — the source habit from Unit 01, applied to a new subject.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Using the census figures, compute the urban share's change from
   1900 (39.7%) to 1920 (51.2%) in percentage points. State the
   definition of "urban" with your answer.
2. From the image's text alternative, name three features that show
   *density* (many people, close buildings, shared spaces) and one
   that shows *work* happening in the street itself.
3. Why did infrastructure lag behind growth? Give one reason about
   money (who profited, who paid) and one about government (who had
   to be persuaded).
4. **Error analysis.** A classmate writes: "The 1920 census proves most
   Americans lived in big cities." Identify the error (the 51.2%
   figure counts towns of 2,500+, not just big cities) and rewrite the
   sentence accurately.

## Independent practice

5. Write a 6-sentence paragraph contrasting a reformer's and an
   immigrant's account of the *same* tenement building: what would
   each notice, and why?
6. Explain in 4 sentences why settlement houses (Hull-House, 1889)
   were both a *service* and a *reform strategy*: what did they do
   for residents, and what did they teach reformers?
7. Map task: on a blank U.S. outline, mark New York, Chicago,
   Pittsburgh, and San Francisco, and write one sentence for each on
   why it grew in this era (port/immigration, rail hub, steel, Pacific
   gateway). Do not invent population figures — use the lesson's
   percentages and qualitative reasons only.

## Applied task

Write a 300-word "city inspector's report" (labeled as a classroom
exercise, not a real document) on one tenement building: describe the
building from the image's features, list two health hazards with
reasons, and propose two reforms — one the landlord could do, one the
city could do. End with a 2-sentence limitation note: what can an
illustration *not* tell an inspector?

## Exit check

In two sentences: name one cause of urban growth and one consequence
that reformers later tried to fix.
