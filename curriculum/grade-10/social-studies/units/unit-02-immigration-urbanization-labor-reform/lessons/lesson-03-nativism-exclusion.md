# Lesson 3 — Nativism and Exclusion

**Goal:** You will explain why anti-immigrant movements grew in the
1880s–1910s, describe the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) as a policy
mechanism, and evaluate exclusion's intended and unintended outcomes.

**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Adult note:** this lesson covers racist law and violence. Preview
the political-cartoon discussion: period cartoons contain ethnic
stereotypes and are studied as *evidence of prejudice*, never
reproduced for humor.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** In one sentence, what was different
about the Angel Island inspection process compared with Ellis Island?
(If you can't: Lesson 1, Part 3 — Angel Island interrogations were
designed to find reasons to *exclude*; the difference was the law.)

## Part 1 — Why nativism grew

**Nativism** — hostility toward immigrants and preference for the
native-born — was not new in the 1880s. What was new was its power: in
this era it moved from pamphlets and riots into *federal law*. Four
causes combined:

1. **Economic competition.** During the hard times of the 1870s,
   European immigrants and native-born workers blamed Chinese laborers
   for falling wages on the West Coast. The charge was that Chinese
   workers — who had built much of the Central Pacific Railroad —
   would work for less and undercut everyone else. Whether the wage
   claim was true mattered less than who believed it: labor unions,
   the era's most organized workers' voice, made Chinese exclusion a
   demand.
2. **Racial ideology.** The era's "science" ranked peoples in
   hierarchies, and politicians spoke openly of "desirable" and
   "undesirable" races. The 1910 federal *Dictionary of Races or
   Peoples* — an official government publication — described whole
   nationalities in crude stereotypes. Exclusion was defended as
   racial self-protection, and its supporters said so plainly.
3. **Cultural anxiety.** The "new" immigrants were Catholic, Orthodox,
   or Jewish in a country whose public culture was Protestant; they
   spoke other languages and built their own institutions. Nativists
   called them "unassimilable" — a claim Lesson 1 already gave you
   the evidence to challenge.
4. **Political opportunity.** Both parties courted the labor vote, and
   western states demanded exclusion as the price of their support.
   Exclusion was popular politics: it let politicians *do something
   visible* about economic anxiety without confronting the
   industrialists who set wages.

Notice the interaction (D2.His.14.9-12): no single cause produced the
law. Economic fear supplied the motive, racial ideology supplied the
justification, and party competition supplied the mechanism.

## Part 2 — The Chinese Exclusion Act as a policy mechanism

On **May 6, 1882**, President Chester A. Arthur signed the Chinese
Exclusion Act — officially "An act to execute certain treaty
stipulations relating to Chinese." Its mechanism was simple and, for
American law, unprecedented:

- It **barred Chinese laborers from immigrating for ten years**.
- It **prohibited Chinese residents from becoming naturalized U.S.
  citizens** — no matter how long they had lived and worked in the
  country.
- It required Chinese laborers already in the U.S. to carry
  certificates of residence — papers proving their right to be here.

This was the **first federal law to bar immigration by nationality**.
Congress extended it for ten more years in 1892 (the Geary Act), made
it effectively permanent in 1902, and kept it on the books until the
**Magnuson Act of December 17, 1943** repealed it — 61 years later,
during World War II, when China was a U.S. ally against Japan.

Read the mechanism, not just the date: the Act did three things at
once. It **closed a door** (no new laborers), it **created a status**
(non-citizen residents who could never naturalize), and it **built an
enforcement system** (certificates, inspections, interrogations) that
Angel Island would later embody. The Chinese population in the U.S.
fell from a peak of about 107,000 in 1880 to about 77,500 in 1940 —
exclusion, working through demography, achieved its demographic aim.

But the logic did not stop with one group. The same decades produced
the **literacy test** campaign (a reading test designed to filter
"southern and eastern" Europeans; passed by Congress, vetoed twice,
finally enacted over President Wilson's veto in 1917), the
**Gentlemen's Agreement of 1907–08** (Japan voluntarily restricted
emigration to avoid a formal exclusion law), and — just past this
unit's edge — the **Emergency Quota Act of 1921** and the
**Immigration Act of 1924**, which capped immigration by national
origin and cut Southern and Eastern European immigration sharply.
Exclusion was a *system* being built, not a single law.

### Worked example 1 — Reading a law's mechanism

*Question:* What three things did the Chinese Exclusion Act do, and
which one affected people *already* in the United States?

*Model reasoning:* (1) Barred Chinese laborers' immigration for ten
years — affects future arrivals. (2) Barred Chinese residents from
naturalization — affects people already here: they could live and work
in America but never become citizens, vote, or gain full legal
standing. (3) Required certificates of residence — also affects people
already here: it turned lawful residents into a population that had to
carry papers. The point of the question: laws act on different
populations at once, and "immigration law" can punish people who
immigrated *legally* years earlier.

### Worked example 2 — Intended vs. unintended outcomes

*Question:* The Act's supporters wanted to protect white workers'
wages. Name one *intended* outcome and one *unintended* outcome, and
explain the difference.

*Model reasoning:* **Intended:** fewer Chinese laborers competing for
jobs on the West Coast — and demographically it worked (the Chinese
population declined for decades). **Unintended:** the enforcement
machinery — interrogations, detentions, certificate checks — created a
model of federal immigration policing that would later be turned on
other groups, and it pushed Chinese immigrants into segregated urban
enclaves (Chinatowns) where community institutions hardened *against*
assimilation — the opposite of what nativists claimed to want. The
difference: intended outcomes are the goals the lawmakers stated;
unintended outcomes are the consequences their mechanism produced
anyway. D2.Civ.13.9-12 asks you to evaluate *both*.

## Part 3 — The cartoon as evidence

Nativist political cartoons of the 1870s–1890s show Uncle Sam or
Columbia turning away Chinese figures, or depict Chinese immigrants in
grotesque stereotype. When you study one (adult-selected, e.g., via
the Library of Congress or the Thinkport inquiry kit in the resource
pack):

1. **Name what you see** — the figures, labels, and symbols.
2. **Name the argument** — what is the cartoonist *for*? (Usually:
   exclusion, framed as protection.)
3. **Name the audience** — who was meant to laugh or nod? (White
   workers and voters, especially in the West.)
4. **Name the harm** — the cartoon is evidence *of* the prejudice,
   and studying it does not endorse it. Ask: what does this source
   *reveal* about the era's racial thinking, and what does it *hide*
   (e.g., the Chinese workers who built the railroad the cartoon's
   readers rode)?

A cartoon is a primary source with a purpose. Treat it the way Unit 01
treated conflicting Reconstruction accounts: creator, audience,
purpose — then evidence.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. List the four causes of nativism's rise and, in one sentence each,
   explain how each one *interacted* with at least one other.
2. Explain in 3 sentences why the Exclusion Act was "unprecedented"
   (first federal nationality bar) — what had immigration policy
   looked like *before* 1882? (Hint: mostly open, with states and
   then the federal station at Castle Garden handling arrivals, not
   barring nationalities.)
3. A nativist argues: "Exclusion protected American workers." Using
   Part 2, write a 3-sentence response that grants one fact the
   nativist gets right and challenges the claim with an unintended
   outcome.
4. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "The Exclusion Act only affected
   Chinese immigrants." Identify the flaw: who else did the
   *precedent* and the *enforcement system* affect? (Later groups
   under the quota logic; anyone subject to the inspection-and-papers
   machinery.)

## Independent practice

5. Write a 5-sentence paragraph: how did the Angel Island experience
   (Lesson 1) follow *logically* from the Exclusion Act? Connect the
   law's mechanism to the interrogation room.
6. Compare the literacy-test campaign and the Exclusion Act: what was
   each *designed* to filter, and how did the mechanism differ
   (nationality bar vs. skills test)? 4 sentences.
7. Source task: examine one nativist cartoon (adult-selected) and
   complete the four steps in Part 3 in writing.

## Applied task

Draft a 250-word "policy memo" (classroom exercise) from a fictional
1902 congressman to a colleague, arguing *for* making exclusion
permanent — using the era's actual arguments (wages, race, politics).
Then add a 4-sentence "historian's note" underneath: which of the
memo's claims are factual, which are ideological, and what evidence
from this lesson contradicts the memo? (The exercise is perspective
practice, not endorsement: you are mapping the argument to
understand it, then answering it with evidence.)

## Exit check

In two sentences: what made the Chinese Exclusion Act a *new kind* of
federal law, and how long did it stay on the books?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "exclusion was about economics, not racism."
Counter with the *Dictionary of Races* and the naturalization ban: a
purely economic policy does not need to declare a people permanently
ineligible for citizenship. Both motives operated; the lesson's four
causes keep them distinct. Solutions: `answer-key.md`, Lesson 3.
