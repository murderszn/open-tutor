# Unit 02 — Answer Key

**For the guiding adult only.** Every item solved independently;
solutions reconciled against the lesson explanations before delivery.
Open-response items show acceptable reasoning and common errors —
accept any answer that gets the mechanism right in the learner's own
words.

## Lesson 1

**Guided practice**

1. (a) **Push:** fleeing a pogrom (violence/persecution at home).
   (b) **Pull:** the railroad pamphlet's advertised cheap land
   (attraction at destination). (c) **Both:** recruited by an agent
   (pull: the job offer) *and* the laborer's home conditions that
   made recruitment attractive — accept "pull" as the primary label
   if the learner notes the recruitment mechanism; the full answer
   notes both sides.
2. Two reasons: (America) the best farmland was already taken and
   industrial jobs were in cities; (immigrants) most "new"
   immigrants arrived without farm capital and with urban work
   experience or family already in cities.
3. Scale: Ellis Island 12M+ (1892–1954) vs. Angel Island 300,000+
   (1910–1940). Process: Ellis Island hours-long medical/legal
   inspection, ~2% excluded; Angel Island weeks-to-months
   interrogations aimed at exclusion. Law: the Chinese Exclusion
   Act (and its extensions) made Chinese arrivals excludable by
   nationality.
4. Flaw: the claim ignores push factors (persecution, famine, land
   hunger), family networks, and the inspection/exclusion system.
   Rewrite example: "Immigrants came because home conditions pushed
   them out and American jobs, family, and freedoms pulled them in —
   and the law welcomed some and excluded others."

**Independent practice**

5. Acceptable paragraph hits: post-1880 surge from Southern/Eastern
   Europe (and Asia); push (land shortage, persecution); pull
   (industrial jobs, family networks); origin shift from Ireland/
   Germany to Italy/Austria-Hungary/Russia.
6. Acceptable reply: cites jobs taken, cities built, inspection
   passed, institutions founded — and concedes adaptation was hard
   (language, poverty, prejudice) without accepting
   "unassimilable."
7. Order: 1882 (Exclusion Act — first nationality bar), 1892 (Ellis
   Island opens), 1907 (peak immigration year, ~1.3M arrivals,
   rounded), 1910 (Angel Island opens).

**Applied task:** Accept if the interview uses documented inspection
elements (origin, occupation, destination, funds, medical check) and
the note correctly separates documented fact from invented names/
answers.

**Exit check:** Push = drives people away from home; pull = attracts
them to a destination; most decisions combine both (e.g., land
hunger + a factory job).

## Lesson 2

**Guided practice**

1. 51.2 − 39.7 = **11.5 percentage points**. With the answer, the
   learner must state: "urban" = places of 2,500+ people.
2. Density features (any three): tall buildings close together;
   fire escapes on every face; laundry lines crossing the street;
   crowded street/pushcarts. Work in the street: pushcart vendors,
   horse-drawn wagon, porters carrying baskets.
3. Money: factories and tenements were privately profitable while
   sewers/water were public costs nobody wanted to pay. Government:
   city machines and legislatures had to be persuaded by reformers
   (Riis, settlement houses, commissions) — services followed
   scandal, not growth.
4. Error: 51.2% counts *all* places of 2,500+, not big cities alone.
   Rewrite: "In 1920, for the first time, just over half of Americans
   lived in urban places (towns of 2,500 or more)."

**Independent practice**

5. Acceptable contrast: the reformer notices crowding, disease, and
   danger (purpose: move legislators); the immigrant notices wages,
   community, and a first affordable home (purpose: memory of
   survival and belonging). Each account's blind spot named.
6. Service: childcare, classes, clinics, legal help for residents.
   Strategy: settlement workers *learned* conditions firsthand and
   carried documented evidence to legislators — service as research.
7. New York (port + immigration + garment/finance), Chicago (rail
   hub + meatpacking), Pittsburgh (steel), San Francisco (Pacific
   gateway + Angel Island). No invented population figures; the
   lesson's percentages and qualitative reasons only.

**Applied task:** Accept if the report names two hazards with
reasons (e.g., dark airless rooms → tuberculosis; shared hall toilets
→ cholera/typhoid) and two reforms split landlord/city, plus a
limitation note (illustration shows typical features, not a real
building's measurements or conditions).

**Exit check:** One cause (jobs/transport/migration) + one
consequence reformers fixed (disease/sanitation, crowding, child
labor, unsafe workplaces).

## Lesson 3

**Guided practice**

1. Four causes with interactions: economic competition ↔ labor
   unions' demands; racial ideology ↔ political justification;
   cultural anxiety ↔ "unassimilable" rhetoric; political opportunity
   ↔ parties courting the labor vote. Any coherent interaction
   sentence per cause is acceptable.
2. "Unprecedented": first federal law barring immigration by
   nationality. Before 1882, immigration was largely open — states,
   then Castle Garden (1855) and the federal stations, *processed*
   arrivals rather than barring nationalities.
3. Acceptable: grants that Chinese laborers did compete for some
   West Coast jobs; challenges with an unintended outcome (the
   enforcement machinery, segregated enclaves, or the demographic
   decline that outlasted the wage question).
4. Flaw: the Act created a *precedent* (nationality bars) and an
   *enforcement system* (certificates, interrogations) later applied
   to others — literacy tests, quotas, and the Angel Island regime
   all descend from it.

**Independent practice**

5. Acceptable: the Act required proof of lawful status and barred
   laborers; Angel Island's interrogations were the *verification
   machinery* — detailed family/village questions to catch
   fraudulent "paper" relationships, with detention while answers
   were checked.
6. Literacy test: designed to filter Southern/Eastern Europeans by
   *reading ability* (a proxy for origin); Exclusion Act: barred by
   *nationality* directly. Mechanisms differ: test vs. category bar.
7. Cartoon analysis: accept if all four steps are completed —
   description, argument, audience, and what the cartoon reveals
   about prejudice (and what it hides, e.g., railroad labor).

**Applied task:** Accept the memo if it uses era arguments (wages,
race, politics) and the historian's note separates factual claims
(wage competition existed in some markets) from ideological ones
(racial hierarchy) and cites a contradicting fact (e.g., Chinese
labor built the Central Pacific; exclusion's demographic effect).

**Exit check:** New kind: first federal nationality bar (and
citizenship bar for residents). Duration: 1882 → repealed 1943
(61 years).

## Lesson 4

**Guided practice**

1. Strike: workers refuse work to pressure employers. Lockout:
   employers refuse workers entry to pressure them. Boycott:
   workers/refusers won't handle an employer's goods. Injunction:
   a court order (used post-Pullman to break strikes). Yellow-dog
   contract: employment conditioned on not joining a union. Each
   must name actor and target.
2. Knights: open to skilled/unskilled, Black workers, women;
   broad-producer theory; peaked 1886, collapsed after Haymarket.
   AFL: skilled craft workers (Gompers, 1886); concrete gains via
   contracts; survived and grew, but exclusionary in practice.
3. Haymarket: trigger — eight-hour-day rally, May 4, 1886; lesson —
   violence by an unknown actor can destroy a movement. Homestead:
   trigger — wage cuts + lockout, July 1892; lesson — private force
   + militia beat a craft union in steel. Pullman: trigger — wage
   cuts without rent cuts + boycott, 1894; lesson — the federal
   government and the injunction could break a national strike.
4. Flaw: "unions were useless" ignores AFL contracts, the lessons
   each defeat taught, and reform that followed defeats (safety
   laws). Rewrite: "Strikes usually lost in the short run, but they
   built the organizations and the public pressure that produced
   later reforms."

**Independent practice**

5. Three employer advantages with examples: law (injunctions,
   yellow-dog contracts); force (Pinkertons, militia, federal
   troops); money (employers could outlast workers' savings);
   public opinion (Haymarket backlash). Any three with examples.
6. Proves: child labor was widespread (1.7M counted, ages 10–15,
   1900). Doesn't prove: the true total (informal work uncounted)
   or conditions (the number alone says nothing about hours or
   danger).
7. Order: Haymarket (1886), AFL founding (1886), Homestead (1892),
   Pullman (1894), Triangle fire (1911) — each with a significance
   sentence (see lesson).

**Applied task:** Accept both reports if they use only lesson facts
and the editor's note correctly separates shared facts (dates,
deaths, boycott) from perspective-driven differences (motives,
blame, "order" vs. "justice" framing).

**Exit check:** One Knights/AFL difference (membership, strategy, or
fate) + one weakening outcome (Haymarket backlash, Homestead's
crushed union, Pullman's injunction/Debs jailed).

## Lesson 5

**Guided practice**

1. Tarbell → Standard Oil → antitrust climate/breakup (1911).
   Steffens → city machines → municipal reform pressure. Sinclair →
   meatpacking → Meat Inspection Act + Pure Food and Drug Act
   (1906). Each with the exposure → pressure → law mechanism.
2. Courts (Happersett, 1875), politics (parties feared the women's
   vote; Southern states feared Black women's votes), movement
   divisions (state vs. federal strategy; militancy vs. respectability).
3. Initiative: citizens propose laws. Referendum: citizens vote on
   laws. Recall: citizens remove officials. Each names the actor.
4. Flaw: state-level barriers (literacy tests, poll taxes, violence)
   kept many Southern Black women from voting, and citizenship bars
   excluded others. Rewrite: "The 19th Amendment barred denial of
   voting rights on account of sex, but state and citizenship
   barriers still kept many women from the polls."

**Independent practice**

5. Acceptable: Sinclair's exposé hit readers' stomachs (food), not
   their politics (labor); the public demanded food safety, and
   Congress answered the demand it heard. The aim/result gap.
6. Any reform evaluated with intended outcomes *and* unintended
   ones (e.g., food laws: safer food *and* the labor conditions
   Sinclair targeted left largely unchanged).
7. Any group with a specific mechanism: Black women (literacy
   tests/poll taxes after 1920); immigrants (Americanization
   programs, exclusion); Native Americans (citizenship bars).

**Applied task:** Accept if both sides argue from lesson evidence
and each side concedes one point before closing.

**Exit check:** One muckraker + target + result, and one limit
(exclusion, control, incomplete coverage).

## Week 8 formative quiz (20 pts)

1. Push: conditions driving people from home (1 pt). Pull:
   conditions attracting people to a destination (1 pt).
2. Any two: origins (NW Europe → S/E Europe + Asia), timing
   (pre-1880 vs. post-1880), settlement (farms vs. cities).
3. Ellis Island: 12M+, 1892–1954. Angel Island: 300,000+,
   1910–1940. Legal difference: the Exclusion Act made Chinese
   arrivals excludable by nationality, so Angel Island
   interrogations aimed at exclusion. (1 pt each element.)
4. (a) 51.2 − 25.7 = **25.5 percentage points** (2 pts). (b)
   Places of 2,500+ (1 pt). (c) It does not prove most Americans
   lived in *big cities* (1 pt).
5. Barred laborers' immigration / barred naturalization /
   certificates of residence, each with the affected population
   (1 pt mechanism + population per item, 4 pts total with one
   point for naming all three).
6. Reformer: strength = documentation of conditions (1 pt), blind
   spot = treating immigrants as victims, not agents (1 pt).
   Immigrant: strength = lived experience/community (1 pt), blind
   spot = memory smoothing hardship (1 pt).

## Culminating assessment (50 pts)

**Part I (20 pts, 4 each)**

1. Push/pull defined + one documented example each (e.g., push:
   pogroms/famine; pull: steel-mill wages/family ticket).
2. Ellis Island 1892–1954, 12M+; Angel Island 1910–1940, 300,000+;
   law: the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882).
3. Any two strikes with trigger + outcome each (Haymarket: bomb
   after eight-hour rally, 4 executed, Knights collapse;
   Homestead: wage cuts/lockout, Pinkerton battle, militia, union
   crushed; Pullman: wage cuts without rent cuts, boycott, troops,
   injunction, Debs jailed).
4. March 25, 1911; 146 dead; any two safety failures: locked exits,
   no sprinklers, collapsed fire escape, ladders reaching only the
   sixth floor.
5. August 18, 1920 (Tennessee, 36th state); one group + reason
   (Southern Black women: literacy tests/poll taxes/violence;
   Native American/immigrant women: citizenship bars).

**Part II (15 pts)**

6. Source A: labor organizer, audience = workers/public, purpose =
   mobilize support for the strike. Source B: company management,
   audience = public/officials, purpose = defend the company and
   justify intervention. (3 pts for the three elements done well
   across both, plus 2 for precision — accept reasonable variants.)
7. Shared fact: wages were cut (both admit it). Difference: whether
   the town/system was fair — a *values* difference (liberty vs.
   property/order), not a disputed number. (5 pts.)
8. Either verdict accepted if argued with one source quote and one
   Lesson 4 fact (e.g., company-town rent trap; or: workers were
   free to leave, and the boycott disrupted others' livelihoods).
   Score the argument (5 pts), not the side.

**Part III — essay (15 pts)**

- Thesis (3): clear, answers both halves of the question.
- Evidence (5): 4+ specific facts from 3+ lessons.
- Both halves (3): who paid the price *and* who defined reform.
- Counterclaim (2): stated and answered.
- Closing question (2): a genuine question for further inquiry,
  not a policy slogan.
- Total: 35+ with no zero part → proceed to Unit 03.
