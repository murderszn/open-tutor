# Unit 02 — Immigration, Urbanization, Labor, and Reform

Grade 10 · Social Studies · Weeks 7–10 · issue #49, section U02 (draft for review).

## Essential question

*Millions of people came to an industrializing America between 1865 and
1920, built its cities, and fought over its future — who paid the price
of that growth, and who got to decide what "reform" meant?*

## What this unit teaches

Between 1865 and 1920 roughly **25 million immigrants** arrived in the
United States — the largest immigration wave in the country's history to
that point. Most came because industrial work was hiring and because
life at home had become untenable; they settled overwhelmingly in
cities, and those cities grew faster than anyone was prepared for. By
the **1920 census**, a majority of Americans (51.2%) lived in urban
places for the first time in the country's history.

The learner studies this transformation from four angles. **Weeks 7–8:**
who came, why, and what the new industrial city was actually like —
tenements, pushcarts, streetcars, and the density that made cities both
opportunity engines and public-health hazards. **Week 8's second half:**
who resisted immigration and how nativism became federal policy, from the
**Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)** — the first federal law to bar
immigration by nationality — to literacy tests and the quota logic that
would follow. **Week 9:** how industrial labor was organized and
contested — unions (the Knights of Labor, the AFL), the strikes that
defined the era (Haymarket 1886, Homestead 1892, Pullman 1894), and the
working conditions that made labor conflict inevitable. **Week 10:** the
Progressive answer — muckraking journalism, workplace-safety and food
legislation after the **Triangle Shirtwaist fire (March 25, 1911; 146
dead)**, and the long campaign for women's suffrage that won the **19th
Amendment (ratified August 18, 1920)** — alongside the unit's recurring
caution that Progressive reformers also sought *control*, not just
justice.

A guiding adult should preview all primary sources and topics for mature
themes — poverty, industrial accidents, racial and ethnic violence, and
child labor are central and are handled through age-appropriate,
adult-supervised historical inquiry. Period political cartoons contain
ethnic stereotypes; the adult frames them as evidence of the era's
prejudices, never as neutral humor.

writing/deliberation, and review.

- A working timeline of 1865–1900 (railroad expansion, industrialization,

## Measurable objectives

By the end of this unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain immigration, 1865–1920:** distinguish *push* from *pull*
   factors; describe the shift from "old" to "new" immigration (origins,
   destinations, timing); and contrast the Ellis Island and Angel Island
   experiences using quantitative and documentary evidence.
2. **Analyze the industrial city:** explain why cities grew (jobs,
   transport, immigration); describe tenement life and urban
   infrastructure; and read an urbanization dataset — computing change
   across censuses and explaining what the numbers show and what they
   leave out.
3. **Analyze nativism and exclusion:** explain the economic, racial, and
   political causes of anti-immigrant movements; describe the Chinese
   Exclusion Act (1882) as a policy mechanism; and evaluate exclusion's
   intended and unintended outcomes (D2.Civ.13.9-12).
4. **Analyze industrial labor conflict:** describe working conditions
   (hours, wages, danger, child labor) with verified figures; compare the
   Knights of Labor and the AFL; explain what happened at Haymarket
   (1886), Homestead (1892), and Pullman (1894) and why each outcome
   mattered.
5. **Evaluate Progressive reform:** connect muckraking journalism to
   specific legislation; explain the suffrage movement's strategies and
   the 19th Amendment's ratification; and argue, with evidence, whether
   Progressive reform was primarily about justice or about control.

## Vocabulary

**Push factor / pull factor** — conditions that drive people from home /
conditions that attract them to a destination. **Old immigration** — the
pre-1880s wave, mostly Northern and Western Europe (Ireland, Germany,
Britain). **New immigration** — the post-1880s wave, mostly Southern and
Eastern Europe (Italy, Russia, Austria-Hungary) plus Asia. **Steerage** —
the cheapest, most crowded passenger quarters on immigrant ships.
**Ellis Island** — the federal immigration station in New York Harbor
(opened January 1, 1892; more than 12 million processed through 1954).
**Angel Island** — the Pacific immigration station in San Francisco Bay
(1910–1940; 300,000+ processed, mostly Asian, with long detentions).
**Tenement** — a crowded, low-cost apartment building housing multiple
poor families, often with poor light, air, and sanitation. **Urbanization**
— the growth of cities and the share of people living in them.
**Nativism** — hostility toward immigrants, favoring native-born
Americans. **Chinese Exclusion Act (1882)** — the first federal law
barring immigration by nationality; barred Chinese laborers for ten
years, later extended and made permanent. **Knights of Labor** — an 1869
labor organization that welcomed many worker types, including Black
workers and women. **American Federation of Labor (AFL)** — the 1886
federation of craft unions led by Samuel Gompers, focused on skilled
workers' wages and conditions. **Strike / lockout / boycott** — workers
refusing work / employers refusing workers entry / refusing to handle an
employer's goods. **Scab (strikebreaker)** — a worker hired to replace
strikers. **Injunction** — a court order; used after Pullman to break
strikes. **Muckraker** — a journalist exposing corruption and abuse
(e.g., Ida Tarbell, Upton Sinclair, Lincoln Steffens). **Progressivism**
— the reform movement (~1890–1920) seeking to fix industrial-era
problems through government action, journalism, and organized pressure.
**Suffrage** — the right to vote. **19th Amendment** — ratified August
18, 1920: voting rights may not be denied on account of sex.
**Initiative / referendum / recall** — Progressive democratic tools:
citizens propose laws / vote on laws / remove officials. **Trust** — a
business combination controlling an industry's prices and output.
**Child labor** — paid work by children, common in mills, mines, and
factories before regulation.

### Key figures used in this unit (all verified 2026-10-21)

- **~25 million immigrants arrived 1870–1920** — the rounded standard
  historical figure for the era's immigration wave (a majority settled
  in cities). Treated as a rounded figure, not a census count.
- **Ellis Island:** federal station opened January 1, 1892; more than
  12 million immigrants processed through its 1954 closing; peak
  operations 1892–1924.
- **Angel Island:** 1910–1940; 300,000+ immigrants processed, mostly
  Asian (~175,000 Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Filipino); detentions of
  weeks to months under the Exclusion laws.
- **Chinese Exclusion Act:** signed May 6, 1882 (President Chester A.
  Arthur); barred Chinese laborers for 10 years; extended 1892 (Geary
  Act) and 1902; repealed by the Magnuson Act, December 17, 1943.
- **Urban share of U.S. population:** 1870: 25.7%; 1900: 39.7%; 1920:
  **51.2%** — first census with an urban majority (Census definition:
  places of 2,500+).
- **Haymarket:** May 4, 1886, Chicago — eight-hour-day rally; a bomb
  killed police and civilians; 7 police officers died; 8 labor
  activists tried, 4 executed.
- **Homestead:** July 1892, Carnegie Steel, Homestead PA — wage cuts,
  lockout, Pinkerton battle (July 6), state militia, union crushed.
- **Pullman:** May–July 1894 — wage cuts without rent cuts in the
  company town; American Railway Union boycott led by Eugene V. Debs;
  federal troops under President Cleveland; Debs jailed; first use of
  a federal injunction to break a strike.
- **Triangle Shirtwaist fire:** March 25, 1911 — 146 garment workers
  killed (123 women and girls, 23 men), mostly young Italian and
  Jewish immigrants; locked exits, no sprinklers; ~30 workplace-safety
  laws followed in New York.
- **19th Amendment:** passed Congress May–June 1919; Tennessee the
  36th state to ratify, **August 18, 1920**; certified August 26,
  1920.
- **The Jungle:** Upton Sinclair's 1906 novel on Chicago meatpacking;
  helped prompt the **Meat Inspection Act and Pure Food and Drug
  Act (1906)**.
