# Unit 02 — Teacher Guide

For the guiding adult. Student materials never link here directly in a
way that exposes answers; the answer key (`answer-key.md`) is a
separate file.

## Unit at a glance

| Week | Focus | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | Immigration waves, push/pull, data reading | Confusing "more immigrants" with "new immigration" (composition vs. volume); forgetting the Census definition of "urban" |
| 8 | The industrial city, nativism, exclusion | Treating tenements as only slums (or only communities); treating exclusion as purely economic, not racial |
| 9 | Labor: unions, strikes, conditions | "Strikes failed, so unions were useless" (short-run vs. long-run); passive-victim framing of workers |
| 10 | Reform, muckraking, suffrage | "Progressives were all good" or "all hypocrites" (the mixed-evidence move); the 19th Amendment as universal enfranchisement |

## Lesson-by-lesson guidance

### Lesson 1 — Why people came

- **Core move:** push/pull as a *pair*, not a single label. Every
  migration decision in the lesson has both; insist learners name both
  before classifying.
- **The old/new distinction** is historian shorthand — and was a
  nativist weapon. If the learner uses "new immigrant" as a slur,
  stop and ask: *who benefits from that label?* (Lesson 3 preview.)
- **Angel Island vs. Ellis Island** is the unit's first
  on-paper-vs.-in-practice moment in a new register: same federal
  system, different law, different door. Revisit it in Lesson 3.
- **Timeline task (Q7):** 1882 Exclusion Act; 1892 Ellis Island
  opens; 1907 peak year (~1.3 million arrivals, rounded); 1910 Angel
  Island opens. If the learner lists dates without causes, prompt:
  "What law, station, or flow does each date *do*?"

### Lesson 2 — The new industrial city

- **Image study:** the illustration is AI-generated — say so *before*
  the learner analyzes it, and repeat the limit in the applied task.
  The text alternative carries all question-relevant information; a
  learner who cannot see the image loses nothing.
- **Data discipline:** the 51.2% figure is the unit's best trap. If
  the learner writes "most Americans lived in big cities," send them
  back to the definition (2,500+) and the rewrite in guided practice
  Q4.
- **Riis:** powerful evidence, partial witness. The adult should
  pre-select any Riis excerpt and frame it: reformer, audience
  (middle-class readers), purpose (move legislators).

### Lesson 3 — Nativism and exclusion

- **Emotional weight:** racist law and violence are central. The
  adult previews cartoons (stereotypes) and frames them as evidence
  of prejudice, never as humor. The perspective memo (applied task)
  is *argument-mapping*, not endorsement — the historian's note
  underneath is mandatory, not optional.
- **The mechanism reading** (three things the Act did) is the
  lesson's core skill: laws act on different populations at once.
  If the learner can only say "it banned Chinese immigration,"
  re-read Part 2 together.
- **Bridge:** the Magnuson repeal extension (1943, wartime alliance)
  is the best "foreign policy reshapes domestic law" example in the
  unit — use it if the learner asks *why then?*

### Lesson 4 — Labor

- **Core move:** short-run defeat vs. long-run lesson. Every strike
  in Part 3 teaches something different (Haymarket: violence's
  blowback; Homestead: private force + militia; Pullman: the
  injunction). Quiz the *lessons*, not just the dates.
- **Knights vs. AFL:** the membership-rules question (Q: who gets
  in?) is the fastest diagnostic of understanding. If the learner
  can't answer it, re-teach Part 2 before the strike details.
- **Triangle fire:** keep the account factual and brief; the
  lesson's point is the *formula* (conditions + publicity +
  organization), which Lesson 5 reuses for muckraking.

### Lesson 5 — Reform

- **The deliberation** (applied task) only works if both sides
  concede a point. Enforce the concession rule — it is the
  assessment essay's counterclaim in rehearsal.
- **Suffrage limits:** if the learner writes "the 19th Amendment
  gave women the vote," stop and re-teach Part 3's final paragraph
  (Southern Black women, citizenship bars). The assessment's Q5
  tests exactly this.
- **The aim/result gap** (Sinclair) is the unit's transferable
  concept: judge reforms by outcomes, not intentions. It recurs in
  the project rubric's "promise vs. evidence" structure.

## Project guidance

- **Investigation A:** the hardest part is finding a *real*
  individual story. If the learner can't document one, pivot to the
  documented-institutions fallback in `project.md` — and record the
  gap honestly rather than inventing a biography. The adult checks
  every source citation before the Session 3 presentation.
- **Investigation B:** the grade must follow from *both* evidences.
  A report card that cites only success (or only failure) earns at
  most Adequate on the justification row.
- **Presentation:** the adult scores live with the rubrics; the
  dossier itself stays in private storage, never in the repository.

## Quiz and assessment guidance

- **Week 8 quiz (20 pts):** 14+ with no zeros on Q4/Q5 → proceed.
  Misses on Q4 (data definition) mean re-teaching Lesson 2's worked
  example 1; misses on Q5 (Act mechanisms) mean re-teaching Lesson
  3's Part 2 before Week 9.
- **Culminating assessment (50 pts):** 35+ with no zero part →
  proceed to Unit 03. Part II, Q8 accepts *either* verdict if argued
  with evidence — score the argument, not the side. Part III's
  closing question must be a genuine question, not a policy slogan;
  dock 2 points if it is a position statement in question marks'
  clothing.
- **Reteach map:** immigration/data misses → Lesson 1–2 + Set A;
  exclusion misses → Lesson 3 + Set B; labor misses → Lesson 4 +
  Set C; reform/suffrage misses → Lesson 5 + Set D.

## Sensitive-content notes

- Racial violence, industrial deaths, and child labor appear
  factually, without graphic detail. The adult previews every
  primary source and cartoon.
- Period cartoons and the *Dictionary of Races or Peoples* contain
  ethnic stereotypes; they are studied as evidence of the era's
  prejudices.
- The applied tasks that ask learners to *argue a historical
  position* (nativist memo, pro-business newspaper) always pair the
  exercise with a historian's note or editor's note answering the
  argument with evidence.
