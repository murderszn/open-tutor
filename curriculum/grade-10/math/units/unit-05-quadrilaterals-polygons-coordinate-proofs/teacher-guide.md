# Teacher Guide — Unit 05: Quadrilaterals, Polygons, and Coordinate Proofs

For the guiding adult. Student materials never contain answer keys; all
solutions are in `answer-key.md`. This guide covers pacing, per-lesson
teaching moves, the misconception table, project and assessment
administration, and reteach triggers.

## Unit at a glance

- **Time:** 20 sessions × ~50 minutes (course Weeks 19–22). Do not
  compress the triangulation lab (S2), the symmetry lab (S7), or the
  two project studios (S14–S15) — the angle-sum meaning, the
  symmetry facts, and the modeling habits are built there, not in
  the lessons.
- **Non-negotiables:** (1) every classification cites a computation
  (slopes, distances, or midpoints), never a picture guess; (2) the
  trapezoid definition in use — exactly one pair of parallel sides
  — is stated out loud before Lesson 03 and again in Session 13;
  (3) every section-formula answer is checked by distances before
  the learner moves on; (4) every shoelace computation starts from
  a labeled sketch with vertices in order.
- **What counts as done:** the project rubric (≥16/24, with
  classification and measurement each ≥3) *and* the unit assessment
  (≥35/50) — see the readiness bands below. The Week-22 quiz is
  formative; scoring below 20 postpones the assessment.

## Per-lesson teaching notes

**Lesson 01 (S1).** The triangulation is the lesson, not a preface —
spend the time on it. Learners who memorize (n − 2)·180° without
drawing the triangles will use n·180° within a week. The exterior
argument (n·180° minus the interior sum) is the first proof in this
unit that works by counting rather than by congruence; let the
learner do the subtraction themselves. The concave-polygon extension
is for strong learners only — do not let it slow the class.

**Lesson 02 (S3).** One diagonal proves three theorems; run the
proofs slowly and in order, because Lesson 04's converses and the
assessment's two-column proof reuse exactly this argument. Keep a
lopsided parallelogram on the board all week — long, thin, diagonals
visibly unequal and visibly not perpendicular — and ask for the
measurement before any claim about diagonals. The rectangle proof in
Worked example 3 is the template for "congruence first, then angle
arithmetic"; the learner will need it again for the rhombus diagonal
proofs in the extension.

**Lesson 03 (S6).** The definition discussion is the lesson: spend
five honest minutes on the exclusive/inclusive trapezoid difference
and the distinct-pairs kite rule, because a learner who meets another
source later will otherwise think one of you is wrong. The hierarchy
is inclusive everywhere else — a square is a rectangle — and that
surprises learners; the family-tree diagram and the nested list say
the same thing two ways for a reason. The symmetry table is worth
memorizing as a table, not as seven separate facts.

**Lesson 04 (S8).** Grade the test chain before grading the answer:
a correct classification with no written slopes earns partial credit
at most. The midpoint test is the fastest parallelogram test and the
one learners forget; drill it with two quick examples before the
lab. The fourth-vertex problem (Worked example 3) is the same
midpoint idea turned around — teach it as "the diagonals share a
midpoint" rather than as a new formula.

**Lesson 05 (S11).** The section formula's weights are backwards
from what learners expect (B's coordinates get weight m, not n) —
derive it once from "m/(m + n) of the way" and then always check by
distances. The shoelace needs a labeled sketch first; a learner who
lists vertices from memory will list them out of order. The
courtyard worked example is the project in miniature — teach it
fully, because the studios then run themselves.

## Misconception table

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Interior sum is n·180° | Triangulation makes n − 2 triangles, not n. Draw it. |
| Exterior angles add to 180° (or depend on n) | One lap around any convex polygon is 360°, always. |
| A parallelogram's diagonals are congruent / perpendicular | Only for rectangles / rhombuses. Bisecting is not the same as either. |
| "Trapezoid" means whatever my source says, and other sources are wrong | It is a definition choice. Name the convention; check the source's. |
| A rhombus is a kite (or a square is not a rectangle) | Inclusive definitions nest figures; the kite rule needs *distinct* pairs. |
| One matching slope pair proves a parallelogram | Both pairs of opposite sides must be parallel; run the full chain. |
| Section-formula weights go with the nearer endpoint | The weight m belongs to B, the far end for AP:PB = m:n. Check by distances. |
| Shoelace works in any vertex order | Vertices must go around the figure; a bowtie order cancels to zero. |

## Project and assessment administration

- **Project studios (S14–S15):** circulate with the rubric in hand;
  the commonest stall is the patio fit check (step 3) — remind the
  learner the plot's right boundary is the slanted side BC, not the
  line x = 10. The cost table's sensitivity question has no single
  right answer; accept any defended choice (usually the walkway,
  because its length carries a square root, or the turf, because its
  area is the largest number).
- **Gallery (S17):** each report gets one number checked and one
  design choice questioned. A critique the learner cannot answer
  goes in the report as an open question — that is a complete
  response, not a failure.
- **Quiz (S18):** 30 points, formative. Below 20: reteach the
  weakest lesson (usually Lesson 04's test chain or Lesson 05's
  section formula) and re-quiz with the practice-set items before
  the assessment.
- **Assessment (S19):** 50 points. Part C is a two-column proof;
  accept a correct paragraph proof at full credit if every statement
  carries a reason.

## Readiness bands and reteach triggers

- **Quiz ≥ 25/30 and assessment ≥ 40/50:** ready for Unit 06
  (circles). Move on; revisit quadrilateral classification in the
  Week 22 review only.
- **Quiz 20–24 or assessment 35–39:** proceed to Unit 06 but
  schedule two retrieval sessions on coordinate classification and
  the section formula in the first week of Unit 06.
- **Quiz < 20 or assessment < 35:** do not proceed. Reteach the
  weakest strand (angle sums → Lesson 01 + Set A; parallelogram
  proofs → Lesson 02 + Set B; coordinate tests → Lesson 04 + Set D;
  section/shoelace → Lesson 05 + Set E), then re-assess with the
  parallel items in the practice sets.
- **Project < 16/24:** revise the two weakest deliverables before
  the unit closes; the classification and measurement criteria must
  each reach 3.

## Extension solutions (for the adult)

- **Trapezoid midsegment proof (Lesson 03):** draw diagonal AC in
  trapezoid ABCD (AB ∥ CD). The midsegment's endpoints are the leg
  midpoints M (on AD) and N (on BC). In △ACD, the segment from M to
  the midpoint of AC is a triangle midsegment, parallel to CD and
  half its length; likewise in △ABC for the other half. Adding:
  MN = (AB + CD)/2, parallel to both bases.
- **Kite area derivation (Lesson 03):** the diagonals split the kite
  into four right triangles. If the symmetry diagonal is d₁ and the
  other is d₂, the four triangles have legs (d₁/2 ± a, d₂/2) for the
  two halves of d₁; their areas sum to (1/2)·d₁·d₂.
- **Shoelace proof, triangle at the origin (Lesson 05):** triangle
  (0, 0), (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂) has area (1/2)|x₁y₂ − x₂y₁| — half
  the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two vectors. Any
  triangle translates to the origin without changing area, and the
  general shoelace is the sum of such triangles with signs.
- **Varignon's theorem (Lesson 04):** joining the midpoints of any
  quadrilateral's sides in order always gives a parallelogram: each
  side of the inner quadrilateral is a triangle midsegment, hence
  parallel to a diagonal of the outer quadrilateral, and the two
  pairs are parallel to the two diagonals.
