# Project — Indirect-Measurement Investigation: How Tall Is It, and How Sure Are You?

**Sessions:** S13 (fieldwork and scale model) and S14 (report).
Adult supervision required for all outdoor work.

## The task

Choose a tall object you cannot measure directly — a tree, a
flagpole, a building, a light pole. (Do not choose anything near
traffic, power lines, or a restricted area, and never climb to
measure it.) Determine its height by **two** independent
similarity methods (shadow and mirror, or shadow and a scale
photograph with a known reference object in the frame), build a
scale drawing or scale model of the object and its measuring
triangles, and write a report that defends your height and states
honestly how accurate it is.

## Part 1 — Fieldwork and scale model (S13)

### Materials

Measuring tape or metre stick; a straight stick or dowel of known
length; a small flat mirror (for the mirror method); a camera or
phone (for the photograph method); notebook; grid paper; ruler;
protractor. GeoGebra optional.

### Steps

1. **Choose and sketch.** Sketch the object, the ground, and
   where you will stand. Record the date, time, weather, and
   ground condition (flat? sloped?).
2. **Method 1 — shadow (if sunny).** Measure your stick's height
   and its shadow length, then the object's shadow length — all
   within a few minutes of each other, so the sun angle does not
   change. Record every measurement with its unit and your
   measuring precision (e.g., "to the nearest centimetre").
3. **Method 2 — mirror (any weather) or photograph.** For the
   mirror: measure your eye height (to your eyes, not the top of
   your head), your distance to the mirror, and the mirror's
   distance to the object's base. For the photograph: photograph
   the object with a reference object of known height standing
   beside it, in the same plane as the object's base; measure
   both heights on the photograph with a ruler (the photograph
   is a dilation of the scene only if both objects are at the
   same distance from the camera — state whether that held).
4. **Compute twice.** For each method, draw the two similar
   triangles, label all four quantities, write the proportion,
   and solve. Show the unrounded computation and then the
   sensibly rounded report.
5. **Compare.** Do the two methods agree? Compute the difference
   and the percentage difference between them. If they disagree
   by more than about 10%, re-measure before concluding.
6. **Scale model.** Build a scale drawing (on grid paper) or a
   physical scale model of the object and its shadow/mirror
   triangles at a stated scale (for example 1:100). Label the
   scale, the real heights, and the model heights, and show one
   scale conversion.

### Simulation / observation alternative

If outdoor work is not possible, use the photographed version
entirely: the adult supplies two photographs of the same object
taken from the same spot — one with a reference object of known
height — or a video of a shadow measurement with stated
measurements. The report must still draw both triangles, write
both proportions, and discuss accuracy. A learner who cannot go
outside loses no marks for using the alternative; the
mathematics and the accuracy discussion are the assessed work.

## Part 2 — Report (S14)

Write a report of 2–4 pages (or the adult's equivalent format)
containing:

1. **The object and the setup** — what you measured, where, when,
   and under what ground and weather conditions.
2. **Both methods, fully worked** — a diagram of each pair of
   similar triangles, the AA justification for similarity (which
   angle pairs match, and why), the proportion, and the solved
   height.
3. **The comparison** — the two heights, their difference, and
   your judgment of which method you trust more, with reasons
   tied to your actual measurements (not generic claims).
4. **The accuracy discussion** — every source of error you can
   name (measuring precision, ground slope, sun movement between
   measurements, eye-height estimation, photograph perspective),
   the direction each error pushes the answer (too high or too
   low, if you can tell), and your final reported height with
   its honest precision ("about X m", with a sentence defending
   the number of figures).
5. **The scale model** — the stated scale, one worked conversion,
   and a photograph or sketch of the model with its labels.
6. **Reflection** — one paragraph: what would you change if you
   repeated the investigation, and what does this investigation
   show about when a similarity proportion can be trusted?

## Deliverables

- Field notes (dated measurements with units and precisions).
- Two worked proportions with triangle diagrams.
- A scale drawing or model with a stated scale.
- The written report (Parts 1–6 above).

## Rubric

| Criterion | Full marks (4) | Partial (2–3) | Missing / weak (0–1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Correct proportions | Both proportions set up with matching parts; solved correctly | One proportion correct, or a matching error corrected in the report | Proportions mismatched or unsolved |
| Similarity justification | AA stated for each method with the matching angle pairs and reasons | AA stated generically without matching pairs | No justification, or "they look similar" |
| Scale model | Stated scale; model matches it; one conversion shown | Scale stated but model inconsistent, or conversion missing | No model, or no stated scale |
| Comparison | Both heights compared numerically; disagreement investigated or explained | Heights stated without comparison | Only one method, or no comparison |
| Accuracy | Error sources named with directions; final height reported to honest precision | Error sources listed without directions or precision discussion | No accuracy discussion, or calculator-string precision |
| Report quality | Diagrams labeled; units throughout; reflection answers both questions | Diagrams or units partly missing | Unlabeled, unitless, or no reflection |

**Scoring:** 20–24: exceeding; 14–19: meeting; 8–13: approaching;
below 8: rework with adult guidance (re-measure one method and
rewrite the accuracy section).

## Safety and boundaries

- Adult-supervised outdoor work only. Stay away from traffic,
  water, and power lines. Never look toward the sun; measure
  shadows on the ground.
- Do not trespass to reach an object or its shadow; choose an
  object you can measure from public or home ground.
- No photographs of people other than the learner's own
  measuring setup, and no photographs are submitted to the
  repository — the report describes the model, it does not
  publish photos.
