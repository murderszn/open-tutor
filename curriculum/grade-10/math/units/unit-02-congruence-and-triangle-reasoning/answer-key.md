## Lesson 02

**Guided practice.**
1. 90° clockwise: (*x*, *y*) → (*y*, −*x*). *A′*(1, −1), *B′*(1, −4),
   *C′*(3, −4). *AB* = 3; *A′B′* = 3. ✓

3. Rotation of 2·90° = 180° about the origin (the mirrors' intersection).
4. Reflection reverses (clockwise → counterclockwise); rotation
   preserves (stays counterclockwise). Final: **counterclockwise**.

**Independent practice.**
5. (a) 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°. (b) 6 lines of symmetry.
   (c) The learner is right if counting *all* symmetries: 6 rotations
   + 6 reflections = 12 (the dihedral group). Clarify the two counts.
6. Each reflection reverses orientation; two reversals restore it, so
   the composition is orientation-*preserving* — a translation or
   rotation, never a reflection. The learner's "each step is a
   reflection" logic ignores what composition *does*.
7. (a) No line of symmetry. (b) 180° rotational symmetry about its
   center. (c) A rectangle's right angles let midline folds match
   corners; the general parallelogram's non-right angles break every
   fold, but the 180° turn still swaps each vertex with its opposite.

**Exit check.**
1. Center *O*, angle θ, direction; *P′* on circle(*O*, *OP*) with
   ∠*POP′* = θ in the given direction.
2. A translation of 8 cm, perpendicular to the mirrors, from the first
   toward the second.
3. Reversing (odd number of opposite motions).

   (c) *GH* (between ∠*G* and ∠*H*).

   reflexive; (5) △*APM* ≅ △*BPM* — SAS; (6) *PA* = *PB* — CPCTC.

**Independent practice.**
4. (a) Midpoint ((−3+5)/2, 1) = (1, 1); *AB* horizontal, so the
   bisector is the vertical line ***x* = 1**. (b) *PA* =
   √[(1+3)² + (7−1)²] = √52; *PB* = √[(5−1)² + (7−1)²] = √52. ✓
   (c) Yes — (1, 7) satisfies *x* = 1. The theorem predicted it:
   equidistant ⇒ on the bisector.
5. (a) Bisector of *AB*: *A*(0,0), *B*(8,0) → *x* = 4; tower at
   (4, 3). (b) distance to each town: √(4² + 3²) = 5. ✓
   (c) Highway *y* = 0: exactly one solution, (4, 0). Infinitely many
   iff the highway *is* the bisector line; none iff the highway is
   parallel to (but distinct from) the bisector.
6. Paragraph (accept equivalent): "Let *M* be the midpoint of *AB*.
   Then *AM* = *BM*; *PM* is common; *PA* = *PB* is given — so
   △*PAM* ≅ △*PBM* by SSS. By CPCTC, ∠*PMA* ≅ ∠*PMB*; they form a

7. SAS would require the *included* angle ∠*APM* ≅ ∠*BPM* — but that
   congruence is exactly what the proof is trying to establish (via
   the right angles). Using it as a reason is circular. SSS uses only
   the three known side equalities.

**Exit check.**
1. Reasons for Worked-example-3 steps 1–6: definition of
   perpendicular bisector; definition of midpoint; definition of
   perpendicular; reflexive; SAS; CPCTC.
2. The construction's arcs mark points *P*, *Q* each equidistant from
   *A* and *B* (equal radii); by the theorem both lie on the
   perpendicular bisector, so line *PQ* is the bisector.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice.**
