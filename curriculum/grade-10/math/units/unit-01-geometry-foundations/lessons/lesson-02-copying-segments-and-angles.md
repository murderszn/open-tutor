# Lesson 02 — Copying What You Cannot Measure: Segments and Angles

**Unit 01 · Week 3, Session 3 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can copy any segment and any angle using only
a compass and straightedge, and I can explain why each step of the
construction is legitimate.

**Materials:** compass, straightedge (unmarked ruler), plain paper,
pencil, ruler with millimeter markings, this lesson.

**Readiness check (5 min):** In your notebook, write the definition of a
circle from Lesson 01. Then answer: *if a compass's hinge never slips,
what single fact about distances does every mark the compass makes
guarantee?* (Every point the pencil touches is the same distance from the
needle point — the radius. That is the whole power of the compass.)

---

## Part 1 — The tools and their rules (explicit teaching, 10 min)

A classical **construction** uses exactly two tools, and each tool is
allowed exactly one move:

- **Compass:** given a center point and a radius, draw the circle (or an
  arc of it) — the set of all points at that distance from the center.
  The compass is a distance-copying machine: set it to span a segment,
  and it carries that exact distance anywhere on the page.
- **Straightedge:** given two points, draw the unique line through them.
  It is *unmarked* — no measuring, no numbers. (This is the postulate
  from Lesson 01 doing physical work: two points determine a line.)

Why the restriction? Because the point is not the picture — it is the
*reasoning*. A measured copy might be accurate, but a constructed copy is
*guaranteed* by the definitions. Every construction in this unit ends
with the same question: "which definition, postulate, or theorem makes
each step legal?" That question is the bridge from drawing to proof.

## Part 2 — Copying a segment (explicit teaching, 10 min)

Given segment *AB*, construct segment *CD* with the same length:

1. Draw a working line (any line) with the straightedge; choose a
   starting point *C* on it.
2. Set the compass to span segment *AB* (needle on *A*, pencil on *B*).
3. Without changing the compass width, place the needle on *C* and draw
   an arc crossing the working line; call the crossing point *D*.
4. Segment *CD* is the copy: the compass carried distance *AB* to
   distance *CD*, so *CD* = *AB* by the definition of a circle (every
   point of the arc is the same distance from *C* as *B* was from *A*).

## Part 3 — Copying an angle (explicit teaching, 10 min)

Given angle *ABC* (vertex *B*), construct an equal angle with vertex at a
new point *P*:

1. Draw a working ray starting at *P* (this will be one side of the new
   angle).
2. With the compass centered at *B*, draw an arc crossing both sides of
   angle *ABC*; label the crossings *X* (on ray *BA*) and *Y* (on ray
   *BC*).
3. With the *same* compass width, center at *P* and draw an arc crossing
   the working ray; label the crossing *Q*.
4. Set the compass to span the distance *XY*; center at *Q* and draw an
   arc crossing the arc from step 3; label the crossing *R*.
5. Draw ray *PR*. Angle *QPR* is congruent to angle *ABC*.

*Why it works (honest version).* Steps 2–4 build two triangles — *BXY*
and *PQR* — whose three sides match pairwise (the radii are equal by
construction, and *XY* = *QR* by step 4). Matching sides force matching
angles, so the angles at *B* and *P* are equal. The full justification
needs the SSS triangle-congruence criterion, which we **prove in Unit 02**.
For now, record it as an *accepted reason*: "copy-angle construction
(justified by SSS, Unit 02)." A geometer always knows which reasons are
proved and which are still on loan.

## Worked example 1 — Copying a segment, with the reasoning attached

*Problem.* Segment *AB* measures 5.3 cm. Copy it onto a working line and
verify the copy.

*Solution.* Follow Part 2's steps: working line, point *C*, compass set
to *AB*, arc crossing at *D*. Verification: measure *CD* with the
millimeter ruler — it should read 53 mm. If it reads 52 or 54 mm, the
compass slipped or the needle wandered; the *method* is exact, the *hand*
is not. Record the copy as 53 mm (nearest millimeter — see the N-Q.3 note
below), not 5.300 cm: the ruler cannot support that precision, and writing
it would be false precision.

## Worked example 2 — Where a copy can fail

*Problem.* A learner copies angle *ABC* but the new angle is visibly
larger. Name two specific steps where the error could have entered, and
for each, state how to check.

*Solution.* (1) Step 3's arc may have used a *different* compass width
than step 2's arc — check by re-setting the compass to the original arc's
radius and seeing whether the pencil retraces it. (2) Step 4 may have
measured the wrong chord (e.g., the distance from *X* to the vertex
instead of *X* to *Y*) — check by labeling all three points before
measuring. **The lesson:** constructions fail at the *transfer* steps,
where a distance moves from one place to another. Check transfers first.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Copy a 4.0 cm segment drawn by the adult. Measure the copy to the
   nearest millimeter and report both numbers. (Set the compass once;
   never re-set between steps 2 and 3.)
2. Copy an acute angle of about 50°. Label every arc-crossing point with
   a letter as you go. (Labeling is not decoration — the "why it works"
   argument needs the points named.)
3. Copy an obtuse angle of about 120°. What changes in the procedure?
   (Nothing changes — the steps do not depend on the angle's size. Noting
   what *doesn't* change is part of understanding.)
4. **Explain your reasoning:** In step 3 of the segment copy, why must the
   compass width stay exactly as set in step 2? State the definition that
   makes this matter. (The compass draws points at a fixed distance from
   the needle — the circle definition. Changing the width changes the
   distance being copied.)
5. A classmate's copied segment is 2 mm short. List the two most likely
   causes in order. (Needle not exactly on the endpoint when setting or
   when marking; pencil width at the mark.)

## Independent practice

1. Draw three segments of different lengths (roughly 3 cm, 6 cm, 9 cm).
   Copy all three onto one working line end to end. Measure the total and
   compare with the sum of the three originals; report the discrepancy to
   the nearest millimeter.
2. Copy an angle, then copy *the copy*. Measure all three with a
   protractor to the nearest degree. What does the comparison tell you
   about error accumulation?
3. Construct a segment twice as long as a given 3.5 cm segment using only
   the copy procedure (no ruler numbers). Describe the steps in writing.
4. **Error analysis:** A learner's copied angle is correct, but their
   written steps say "draw an arc" four times without saying where each
   arc is centered or what radius it uses. Rewrite the steps so another
   person could follow them blind. (Precision of language, Lesson 01's
   habit, applied to procedures.)
5. Measure a copied segment as 47 mm. The original was drawn as 4.7 cm.
   Is this consistent? Explain using the N-Q.3 idea of appropriate
   precision. (Yes — 47 mm and 4.7 cm name the same length at the ruler's
   precision; writing 4.70 cm would claim more than the tool supports.)
6. Draw a segment, copy it, then bisect your *written* description of the
   procedure into "setup steps" and "transfer steps." Which kind fails
   more often, and why?

## Application

A carpenter needs to cut a board to exactly the length of an existing
shelf bracket, but the tape measure is missing. Explain in a short
paragraph how the segment-copy idea solves this with a compass-like tool
(a pair of dividers) and a straight piece of lumber — and identify which
step of the mathematical construction the physical divider performs.
(Transfer of distance without numbers; the divider is the compass, the
lumber edge is the working line.)

## Exit check

Without looking: (1) list the one legal move of each tool; (2) perform a
segment copy on a fresh segment drawn by the adult while narrating each
step aloud; (3) state in one sentence why the copy is guaranteed equal.
The adult checks the narration against Part 2 before Session 4.

---

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** Number each construction step on a
  separate line and read the list aloud before touching the tools.
  Keep a "transfer checklist" card: *set — carry — mark — check*.
- **Alternate response mode:** Constructions may be performed in GeoGebra
  Geometry (circle tool = compass, line tool = straightedge); the written
  step list and the "why it works" sentence are still required on paper.
- **Extension:** Copy a segment, then use only the copy procedure to
  construct a segment *three times* as long as the original. Prove in
  writing that your result is correct, citing the circle definition at
  each transfer.

## Teacher note

Watch for the "re-set compass" habit: learners instinctively adjust the
compass between steps, destroying the distance transfer. Make them
announce "compass locked" before each transfer step. Also watch pencil
width — a thick pencil point can add a full millimeter of error; a sharp
pencil is a precision instrument here. The N-Q.3 thread (appropriate
precision, false precision) starts in this lesson and recurs in Lesson 03
and the project. See `teacher-guide.md` (Lesson 02) and `answer-key.md`
(Practice Set B).
