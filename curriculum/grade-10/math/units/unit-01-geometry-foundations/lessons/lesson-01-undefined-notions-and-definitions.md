# Lesson 01 — From Undefined Notions to Precise Definitions

**Unit 01 · Week 3, Session 1 · ~50 minutes**

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can state precise definitions of segment,
angle, parallel line, perpendicular line, and circle starting from the
undefined notions of point, line, and distance — and I can read and write
geometry notation and diagram markings correctly.

**Materials:** this lesson, notebook with a *definitions* section, pencil,
ruler.

**Readiness check (5 min):** Draw what you think each of these is: a point,
a line, a line segment, an angle. Then answer in one sentence: *what could
go wrong if two people use the word "line" to mean different things?*
(Answers vary; the point is that proofs collapse when words are fuzzy —
precision is what makes agreement possible.)

---

## Part 1 — What we do not define (explicit teaching, 10 min)

Every logical system starts somewhere. In geometry we accept a few ideas
**without definition** — the *undefined notions* — and build everything
else from them:

- **Point** — a location with no size, no length, no width. We mark it
  with a dot and name it with a capital letter: point *A*.
- **Line** — perfectly straight, with no thickness, extending forever in
  both directions. We name a line with a lowercase script letter (line
  *l*) or with any two points on it (line *AB*).
- **Plane** — perfectly flat, with no thickness, extending forever in
  every direction. Think of a tabletop that never ends.
- **Distance** — a number that measures how far apart two points are.

We do not define these because any "definition" would just use other words
that also need defining — an infinite regress. Instead we describe how
they behave (for example: *through any two points there is exactly one
line*) and define everything else in terms of them. A **defined term** is
a new word explained using only undefined notions and previously defined
terms. A **postulate** (also called an axiom) is a statement we accept as
true without proof, because proofs have to start somewhere. A **theorem**
is a statement we prove from definitions and postulates.

## Part 2 — Building the basic objects (explicit teaching, 15 min)

Here are the unit's core defined terms. Copy each into your notebook's
*definitions* section exactly as written, then close the lesson and say
each one back in your own words.

- **Line segment** (or **segment**) *AB*: points *A* and *B* (the
  *endpoints*) together with all points *between* them on line *AB*.
  Notation: write "segment *AB*". Its length is the distance *AB*.
- **Ray** *AB*: the endpoint *A* together with all points of line *AB*
  that lie on the same side of *A* as *B*. The endpoint is always named
  first — ray *AB* and ray *BA* are different rays.
- **Angle**: two rays with a common endpoint. The common endpoint is the
  **vertex**; the rays are the **sides**. Notation: angle *ABC* means the
  angle with vertex *B* and sides ray *BA* and ray *BC*. We measure angles
  in **degrees**: an **acute** angle measures less than 90°, a **right**
  angle measures exactly 90°, an **obtuse** angle measures between 90°
  and 180°, and a **straight** angle measures 180° (its sides form a
  line). Write "the measure of angle *ABC* is 40°" as m∠*ABC* = 40°.
- **Parallel lines**: two lines in the *same plane* that never intersect,
  no matter how far extended. Notation: line *AB* ∥ line *CD*. (Lines in
  different planes that never meet are called **skew** lines — you will
  meet them in 3-D work, not in this unit's proofs.)
- **Perpendicular lines**: two lines that intersect to form right angles.
  Notation: line *AB* ⊥ line *CD*.
- **Circle**: all points in a plane at a fixed distance from a fixed
  point. The fixed point is the **center**; the fixed distance is the
  **radius**. So "circle with center *O* and radius *r*" is a complete
  definition built only from *point*, *plane*, and *distance*.

**Reading diagrams.** Geometers mark diagrams so a reader can trust them:

- Small tick marks across segments: the marked segments are congruent
  (same length). One tick vs. two ticks distinguishes two different
  lengths.
- Small arcs inside angles: the marked angles are congruent.
- A small square at a vertex: the angle is a right angle.
- Arrowheads on lines: the lines are parallel.

Marks are promises. In a proof you may only use what is *given* in words
or *marked* on the diagram — never what merely "looks true."

## Worked example 1 — Naming rays precisely

*Problem.* Name the ray with endpoint *T* that passes through *S*. Then
explain why "ray *ST*" would be wrong.

*Solution.* The endpoint comes first, so the ray is ray *TS*. Ray *ST*
has endpoint *S* and goes through *T* — it points the opposite direction.
The check: cover the first letter of your answer and ask, "does the ray
start here?" If the first letter is not the endpoint, the name is wrong.
**Common error:** treating ray *TS* and ray *ST* as the same because they
"involve the same letters." Order carries the meaning.

## Worked example 2 — Testing a definition

*Problem.* A classmate proposes: "A *midpoint* is a point in the middle
of a segment." Improve this into a real definition using only the words
*point*, *segment*, and *distance*, and explain what was wrong with the
original.

*Solution.* Try: "The *midpoint* of segment *AB* is the point *M* on
segment *AB* such that the distance *AM* equals the distance *MB*."
Why this is better: "in the middle" is vague — middle of what, measured
how? The improved version gives a test you can actually check (measure
the two distances and compare). A definition must let you decide, for any
candidate, whether it fits or not.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work these with the adult nearby. Hints are in parentheses — try without
them first.

1. Points *P*, *Q*, *R* are collinear with *Q* between *P* and *R*.
   Name the two rays with endpoint *Q* determined by these points.
   (The endpoint is named first; the rays point opposite ways.)
2. A diagram shows segment *AB* with one tick mark and segment *CD* with
   one tick mark. What may you conclude? What may you *not* conclude
   about their positions? (Marks promise congruence, not location.)
3. Write a definition of *perpendicular bisector* using only words defined
   in this lesson. (It is a line that is perpendicular to a segment and
   passes through the segment's midpoint — but *midpoint* needs Worked
   example 2's definition first. Build in order.)
4. True or false, with a one-sentence reason: "If two lines never meet,
   they are parallel." (False — they might be skew; "in the same plane"
   is doing real work in the definition.)
5. In the diagram described below, list everything you are *entitled* to
   use in a proof: lines *l* ∥ *m* are marked with arrowheads; they are
   cut by line *t*; angle 1 (upper left at the top intersection) has one
   arc mark and angle 5 (upper left at the bottom intersection) has one
   arc mark. (Parallel markings, and the two marked angles congruent —
   nothing else, even if it looks true.)
6. **Explain your reasoning:** Why does geometry bother with undefined
   notions at all? Why not just define *point* as "a tiny dot"?
   (A dot has size; a point does not. Every definition would smuggle in
   another undefined word — "tiny" compared to what? Accept the starting
   point honestly instead of pretending.)

## Independent practice

1. Draw and label: line *AB*, segment *CD*, ray *EF*, angle *GHI* with
   vertex *H*, and a circle with center *O* and radius 3 cm. Measure the
   radius to check it.
2. Classify each angle as acute, right, obtuse, or straight: 35°, 90°,
   117°, 180°, 89.5°.
3. A diagram marks ∠*ABC* and ∠*DEF* each with two arc marks, and marks
   a square at the vertex of ∠*GHI*. State two conclusions you are
   entitled to.
4. Write definitions (in the style of this lesson) for: (a) *angle
   bisector*, (b) *straight angle*. Use only undefined notions and terms
   already defined.
5. Points *A*, *B*, *C* are the vertices of a triangle. Name all six rays
   determined by pairs of these points (each pair gives two rays).
6. **Error analysis:** A learner writes "parallel lines are lines that
   look like train tracks." Give two specific ways this "definition"
   fails the test from Worked example 2. (What counts as "looking like"?
   Train tracks converge in the distance in a photo; also, the rails are
   segments, not infinite lines.)
7. Draw two lines that appear parallel but are *not* marked parallel.
   Explain in writing why a proof may not use their parallelism.
8. m∠*ABC* = 2x + 10 and the angle is a right angle. Solve for x and
   state the check. (Algebra warm-up: 2x + 10 = 90 → x = 40; check:
   2(40) + 10 = 90.)

## Application

Look around one room. Find and sketch two examples each of (a) segments,
(b) right angles, (c) something *approximately* parallel, (d) something
approximately perpendicular. Label each sketch with correct notation and
add one sentence per sketch explaining which part of the definition your
real-world object only *approximates* (real objects have thickness;
nothing physical is infinite). This is the beginning of the N-Q.3 habit:
the mathematics is exact; measurements and drawings are not.

## Exit check

On a fresh page, without looking: (1) write the definitions of *segment*,
*angle*, and *circle* from the undefined notions; (2) draw ray *PQ* and
ray *QP* and state how they differ; (3) explain what a single tick mark
on two segments promises. The adult checks all three against the lesson
before Session 2.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** Keep a running glossary page: term on the
  left, definition in the learner's own words on the right, diagram in the
  margin. Read definitions aloud before writing them — hearing "the
  endpoint is always named first" cements the convention.
- **Alternate response mode:** Any drawing task may be done in GeoGebra
  Geometry instead of on paper; notation and definitions are still written
  by hand. Oral definitions are accepted if the adult scribes them
  verbatim and the learner confirms each word.
- **Extension:** Research how Euclid (c. 300 BCE) defined *point* ("that
  which has no part") and *line* ("breadthless length") in Book I of the
  *Elements*. Write a paragraph comparing his approach with this lesson's
  undefined notions. (Bring it to Session 8's reading session.)

## Teacher note

The most durable misconception here is **notation order** (ray *TS* vs.
ray *ST*; angle *ABC* vs. angle *CBA* — the vertex must be the middle
letter). Drill it every session until it is automatic; it will matter in
every congruence statement this year. The second is treating diagrams as
evidence: establish now, firmly, that only *marked* or *given* facts may
be used. See `teacher-guide.md` (Lesson 01) and solutions in
`answer-key.md` (Practice Sets A).
