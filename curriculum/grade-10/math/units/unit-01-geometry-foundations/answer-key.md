## Practice Set A — solutions

**A1.** (a) line *AB*: straight mark with arrows both ends, labeled *A*,
*B*. (b) segment *CD*: mark with endpoints *C*, *D*, no arrows.
(c) ray *EF*: endpoint dot at *E*, arrow through *F*. (d) ray *FE*:
endpoint dot at *F*, arrow through *E*. Difference: opposite endpoints
and opposite directions — order carries the meaning.

**A2.** Sample: "Ray *AB* is point *A* together with all points of line
*AB* lying on the same side of *A* as *B*." Test: for ray *FE* the
definition with first letter *F* gives endpoint *F* — correct; ray *FE*
≠ ray *EF* because the defined endpoint differs. Accept any definition
equivalent to this.

**A3.** Entitled: (i) *l* ∥ *m* (arrowhead markings = given); (ii) ∠2 ≅
∠6 (arc markings = given). NOT entitled (example): that ∠2 ≅ ∠4, or that
any angle measures a specific number of degrees, or that *t* ⊥ *l* —
unmarked, so unusable.

**A4.** 12° acute; 90° right; 145° obtuse; 180° straight; 91° obtuse.

**A5.** (a) segment *XZ*. (b) ray *YX* and ray *YZ*. (c) No: same
endpoint *Y* but opposite directions — *X* and *Z* lie on opposite sides
of *Y*, so the rays contain different points.

**A6.** Sample: "A circle is the set of all points in a plane at a fixed
distance from a fixed point." "Round shape" fails because it gives no
test: "round" is undefined, and there is no center or distance to check a
candidate point against.

**A7.** Two errors: (1) the vertex must be the middle letter, so an angle
at *B* must be named ∠*XBY*; (2) ∠*BCA* names the angle at *C* (middle
letter *C*), not an angle at *B* at all.

**A8.** Sample: in chess, "checkmate" is a defined term (king in check,
no legal move escapes); "square" is an undefined primitive (a location
on the board, never defined further). The game needs primitives so
definitions bottom out instead of regressing forever. Accept any
coherent game/system example with both kinds identified.

## Practice Set B — solutions

**B1.** Sample: original 52 mm, copy 52 mm, difference 0 mm. (Adult:
any difference ≤ 1 mm is proficient; the report format — two numbers and
the difference — is required.)

**B2.** Sample: original 65°, copy 65°. Labels required on all
arc-crossing points.

**B3.** Sample steps: 1. Draw a working line; mark *C*. 2. Copy segment
1 onto the line (compass set once): mark *D*. 3. Without erasing, set
the compass to segment 2, center at *D*, mark *E* on the same line on
the far side. 4. Segment *CE* is the sum: *CD* = seg1 and *DE* = seg2 by
the circle definition, and lengths add along a line.

**B4.** Check in this order: (1) compass width changed between setting
and marking (slipped hinge or deliberate re-set); (2) needle/pencil not
placed exactly on the endpoints when setting or marking; (3) ruler
misread (not starting at zero, parallax).

**B5.** The copy needs a determined direction: the working line plus
point *C* lets the straightedge postulate (two points determine a unique
line) fix where *D* goes. Without it, "distance *AB* from *C*" is an
entire circle of candidate points, not a segment.

**B6.** Sample: 65°, 65°, 66° — total drift 1°, within the protractor's
±1° precision. Conclusion: no evidence of method error; the drift is
instrument-level.

**B7.** Sample: "The arcs build triangles *BXY* and *PQR* with *BX* =
*PY* and *BY* = *PQ* (equal radii) and *XY* = *QR* (transferred chord).
Three pairs of equal sides make the triangles SSS-congruent, so their
corresponding angles at *B* and *P* are equal. The SSS criterion is
proved in Unit 02 — cited here on loan."

**B8.** Violates the circle definition: the compass must hold a fixed
distance; re-adjusting changes the distance, so *CD* is no longer
guaranteed equal to *AB*. The "copy" is a segment of unknown length —
the transfer step is broken.

## Practice Set C — solutions

**C1.** Sample: 55° and 55° (nearest degree).

**C2.** Sample: halves 40 mm and 40 mm; crossing angle 90°.

**C3.** Sample: "*P* lies on the same arc as *A* and *B*, so *PA* = *PB*
(radii of one circle); hence *P* is equidistant from *A* and *B* and lies
on segment *AB*'s perpendicular bisector by the equidistance theorem —
so the bisector passes through *P*."

**C4.** Sample: the construction's justification *is* the check — the
angle-copy procedure (SSS, on loan) guarantees the corresponding angles
are congruent, and equal corresponding angles make the lines parallel by
the stated converse. A physical cross-check: construct perpendiculars
from two well-separated points of the new line to *l* and compare the
two perpendicular segments with the compass — equal spacing is
consistent with (but does not prove) parallelism.

**C5.** Sample: 60° and 60°.

**C6.** Sample: sides 56, 56, 57, 56 mm; range 1 mm. (Adult: range ≤
2 mm is proficient at this tool precision.)

**C7.** 6 cm claims nearest-centimeter precision; 6.3 cm claims
nearest-millimeter — honest for a 63 mm reading; 6.30 cm claims
nearest-tenth-millimeter — false precision from a millimeter ruler.
Honest report: 6.3 cm.

**C8.** Correction: six chords each equal to the radius give six central
angles of 360° ÷ 6 = 60°, and 6 × 60° = 360° closes the circle exactly —
the method is exact. The culprit is compass-width drift between marks (a
transfer error). The preventive habit: lock the hinge once and mark all
six points without re-setting.

## Practice Set D — solutions

**D1.** Full two-column proof (from memory, then checked):

| # | Statement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ∠1 and ∠2 form a linear pair | Definition of linear pair |
| 2 | m∠1 + m∠2 = 180° | Linear Pair Postulate |
| 3 | ∠2 and ∠3 form a linear pair | Definition of linear pair |
| 4 | m∠2 + m∠3 = 180° | Linear Pair Postulate |
| 5 | m∠1 + m∠2 = m∠2 + m∠3 | Substitution (Transitive Property of Equality) |
| 6 | m∠1 = m∠3 | Subtraction Property of Equality |
| 7 | ∠1 ≅ ∠3 | Definition of congruent angles |

Paragraph version must contain all seven reasons, rearranged.

**D2.** Vertical ⇒ set equal: 2x + 18 = 6x − 42 → 60 = 4x → x = 15.
m∠1 = 2(15) + 18 = 48°; m∠3 = 6(15) − 42 = 48°. Check: 48 = 48 ✓.

**D3.** Linear pair ⇒ sum to 180: 3x + 4 + 2x + 36 = 180 → 5x + 40 =
180 → 5x = 140 → x = 28. m∠*ABC* = 3(28) + 4 = 88°; bisector halves it:
m∠*ABE* = 44°. Check: m∠*CBD* = 2(28) + 36 = 92°; 88 + 92 = 180 ✓.

**D4.** Label: top intersection ∠1 (UL), ∠2 (UR), ∠3 (LL), ∠4 (LR);
bottom ∠5 (UL), ∠6 (UR), ∠7 (LL), ∠8 (LR). Prove ∠1 ≅ ∠8:

| # | Statement | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *l* ∥ *m* cut by *t* | Given |
| 2 | ∠1 ≅ ∠5 | Corresponding Angles Postulate (∠1, ∠5 corresponding) |
| 3 | ∠5 ≅ ∠8 | Vertical Angles Theorem (∠5, ∠8 vertical) |
| 4 | ∠1 ≅ ∠8 | Transitive Property of Congruence |

**D5.** ∠1 = 127° (given). ∠3 = 127° (vertical with ∠1); ∠5 = 127°
(corresponding with ∠1); ∠7 = 127° (vertical with ∠5, or corresponding
with ∠3). Linear pairs: ∠2 = 53°, ∠4 = 53°, ∠6 = 53°, ∠8 = 53° (each
180 − 127 by the Linear Pair Postulate). Check: same-side interior
pairs sum to 180 (127 + 53 ✓).

**D6.** Alternate interior ⇒ equal: 5y − 10 = 3y + 26 → 2y = 36 → y =
18. Angles: 5(18) − 10 = 80°; 3(18) + 26 = 80°. Check: 80 = 80 ✓.
Same-side interior adjacent: 180 − 80 = 100°. Check: 80 + 100 = 180 ✓.

**D7.** Corrected step: "∠*A* and ∠*B* are alternate interior angles
formed by parallel lines *l* and *m* cut by transversal *t*; therefore
∠*A* ≅ ∠*B* by the Alternate Interior Angles Theorem." Original's
flaw: "looks like 45°" is not on the allowed-reasons list
(given / definition / postulate / algebra / proved theorem) —
appearance is never a reason.

**D8.** The Same-Side Interior proof uses the Corresponding Angles
Postulate (its ∠4 ≅ ∠8 step), so deriving the postulate from the
theorem assumes the postulate to prove it — circular. A postulate is a
starting point by definition; it cannot be proved from its own
consequences.

## Formative quiz — solutions

**1.** Sample: "Segment *AB* is points *A* and *B* (the endpoints)
together with all points between them on line *AB*." (2 pts: endpoints
+ betweenness + line.)

**2.** Ray *ST*. Ray *TS* has endpoint *T*, not *S* — it starts at the
wrong point and extends the opposite direction. (2 pts)

**3.** (a) *l* ∥ *m* (arrowhead markings); ∠2 ≅ ∠6 (arc markings).
(b) Any one unmarked claim, e.g., that ∠2 ≅ ∠4, or a specific degree
measure, or that *t* ⊥ *l*. (3 pts)

**4–6.** Performance: scored on labeled points, visible arcs, and
reported numbers. Sample: (4) 52 mm / 52 mm / 0 mm; (5) 60° / 60°;
(6) *P*, *Q* labeled; halves 35 mm / 35 mm; crossing 90°. (3 + 3 + 4 pts)

**7.** Sample: "*P* and *Q* are each equidistant from *A* and *B*
(equal-radius arcs), so the equidistance theorem puts both on *AB*'s
perpendicular bisector; two points determine a line, so line *PQ* is
that bisector." (3 pts: equidistance + theorem + two-points postulate.)

**8.** 6.3 cm is honest (63 mm at millimeter precision). 6 cm merely
under-reports (nearest centimeter — vague, not false). 6.30 cm is
dishonest: it claims tenth-millimeter precision the ruler cannot
support. (2 pts)

**9.** Most likely: the compass width changed between the original arc
(step 2) and the transfer arc (step 3). Check: re-set the compass to the
original arc's radius and see whether the pencil retraces that arc.
(2 pts)

**10.** Sample: "A postulate is accepted without proof — a starting
point (e.g., the Corresponding Angles Postulate, or 'two points
determine a line'). A theorem is proved from accepted statements
(e.g., the Vertical Angles Theorem)." (2 pts)

## Unit assessment — solutions and scoring notes

**Part I.**
1. Sample: "An angle is two rays with a common endpoint (the vertex)."
   (2 pts)
2. (a) *AB*, *BC*, *AC*. (b) ray *BA* and ray *BC*. (3 pts)
3. (a) ∠2 ≅ ∠8, by the Corresponding Angles Postulate (parallel given
   by markings). (b) Corresponding angles. (c) Any unmarked claim, e.g.,
   a degree measure or congruence of an unmarked pair. (3 pts)
4. (a) False — a ray has exactly one endpoint and extends infinitely in
   one direction. (b) True — perpendicular lines meet at right angles by
   definition, and each adjacent pair forms a linear pair (180°), so all
   four are 90°. (2 pts)

**Part II.**
5. Sample verification: halves 30 mm / 30 mm; crossing 90°. Score the
   labeled points, visible arcs, and the three reported numbers. (5 pts)
6. Sample justification: "Marking the radius six times around the
   circle makes six central angles of 360° ÷ 6 = 60°; taking every other
   vertex triples each step to 120°, and three 120° steps close the
   circle with three equal chords — an equilateral triangle." (5 pts:
   construction 3, justification 2.)
7. Sample: "The compass draws all points at one fixed distance from its
   needle (circle definition). Changing the width changes that distance,
   so the transferred chord would not equal the original — the copy
   would be unguaranteed." (3 pts)

**Part III.**
8. Full two-column table as in Practice D1 (labels adapted to *AEC* /
   *BED*): 8 pts — 1 per correct statement–reason row, deduct 1 per
   missing/wrong reason; a proof citing "looks equal" earns 0 for that
   row.
9. Paragraph proof must cite the Corresponding Angles Postulate and the
   Vertical Angles Theorem by name with every reason present: 6 pts —
   deduct 1 per dropped reason; "rearranged, not reduced" enforced.

**Part IV.**
10. Vertical ⇒ equal: 4x − 7 = 2x + 13 → 2x = 20 → x = 10. Both 33°.
    Check: 4(10) − 7 = 33 ✓. (3 pts)
11. Pair ⇒ 180: 6x + 8 + 4x + 12 = 180 → 10x + 20 = 180 → x = 16.
    m∠*P* = 104°; halves 52° each. Check: m∠*Q* = 76°; 104 + 76 = 180
    ✓. (4 pts)
12. (a) Alternate interior ⇒ equal: 7y + 4 = 3y + 52 → 4y = 48 → y =
    12; both 88°. (b) Same-side interior adjacent: 180 − 88 = 92°.
    Checks: 3(12) + 52 = 88 ✓; 88 + 92 = 180 ✓. (4 pts)

**Part V.**
13. (a) The protractor reads to the nearest degree, so "45.00°" claims
    hundredth-degree precision — false precision. (b) Honest report:
    "about 45°" (or 45°). (c) The protractor can verify the *drawing*
    to ±1° but cannot verify the construction's *exactness* — that comes
    from the definitions and theorems, not from measurement. (4 pts)
