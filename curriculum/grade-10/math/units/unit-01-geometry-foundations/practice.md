# Practice Sets A–D — Unit 01

Worked solutions for every item are in `answer-key.md` (adults only).
Classify each angle pair *before* writing any equation.

## Set A — Definitions, notation, diagrams (Week 3, Session 2; supports Lesson 01)

1. Draw: (a) line *AB*, (b) segment *CD*, (c) ray *EF*, (d) ray *FE*.
   Explain in one sentence how (c) and (d) differ.
2. Write a definition of *ray* using only the undefined notions and
   previously defined terms. Then test it: does your definition correctly
   exclude ray *FE* when you meant ray *EF*?
3. A diagram shows: line *l* with a single arrowhead, line *m* with a
   single arrowhead, intersecting line *t*; ∠1 at the top intersection
   marked with one arc, ∠5 at the bottom intersection marked with one
   arc. List every fact you are entitled to use in a proof — and one
   fact you are *not* entitled to, though it looks true.
4. Classify as acute, right, obtuse, or straight: 12°, 90°, 145°, 180°,
   91°.
5. Points *X*, *Y*, *Z* are collinear, with *Y* between *X* and *Z*.
   (a) Name the segment with endpoints *X* and *Z*. (b) Name two rays
   with endpoint *Y*. (c) Is ray *YX* the same as ray *YZ*? Why not?
6. Define *circle* from the undefined notions (no peeking). Then explain
   why the phrase "round shape" would fail as a definition.
7. **Error analysis.** A learner labels an angle with vertex *B* as
   "∠*BCA*." Explain the two things wrong with this label. (The vertex
   must be the middle letter — the angle at *B* cannot be named with *C*
   in the middle; and *A* is not on either side of the angle at *B* as
   described.)
8. **Explain your reasoning.** Give an example of a *defined term* and an
   *undefined notion* from outside mathematics (e.g., from a game rule
   book), and explain why the game needs both kinds.

## Set B — Copying segments and angles (Week 3, Session 4; supports Lesson 02)

1. The adult draws a segment of undisclosed length. Copy it onto a
   working line. Measure both to the nearest millimeter and report the
   two numbers and their difference.
2. Copy an angle of about 65° (drawn by the adult). Label every
   arc-crossing point. Measure the copy with a protractor to the nearest
   degree and report both measures.
3. Using only the copy procedure, construct a segment equal in length to
   the *sum* of two given segments (drawn by the adult). Write the steps
   as a numbered list another learner could follow.
4. A copied segment measures 61 mm; the original was 60 mm. List the
   three most likely sources of the 1 mm error, in the order you would
   check them.
5. **Explain your reasoning.** Why does the segment-copy procedure begin
   by drawing a working line? What goes wrong if you skip it? (The copy
   needs a line to live on — the straightedge's postulate gives a unique
   line through *C* and *D*; without it the transferred distance has no
   determined direction.)
6. Copy the *same* angle twice in a row (copy of a copy). Measure all
   three angles to the nearest degree. Compute the total drift and state
   whether it is within protractor precision.
7. Write the "why it works" for the angle copy in three sentences,
   naming the two triangles and the reason their matching sides force
   matching angles — and stating honestly which criterion is still on
   loan from Unit 02.
8. A learner sets the compass to the segment, then *re-adjusts* it "to
   fit the page better" before marking *D*. Explain precisely which
   definition is violated and what the consequence is. (The circle
   definition: the compass no longer draws points at the original
   distance, so *CD* ≠ *AB* — the transfer is broken.)

## Set C — Bisectors, perpendiculars, parallels, polygons, precision (Week 4, Session 7; supports Lesson 03)

1. Bisect a 110° angle (drawn by the adult). Measure each half to the
   nearest degree and report.
2. Construct the perpendicular bisector of an 8 cm segment. Verify by
   (a) measuring both halves to the nearest millimeter, (b) checking the
   crossing angle with a protractor.
3. From a point *P* above a line, construct the perpendicular to the
   line. Write the one-sentence equidistance justification for why your
   line passes through *P*.
4. Through a point *Q* not on line *l*, construct the parallel to *l*.
   Describe how you would check the result *without* measuring angles.
   (Copy a segment along the transversal between the lines at two
   places — equal corresponding segments suggest equal spacing; the
   honest check is the construction's own justification, since
   measurement cannot prove parallelism.)
5. Inscribe a regular hexagon in a circle of radius 4.5 cm. Measure two
   adjacent central angles and report each to the nearest degree.
6. Inscribe a square in a circle of radius 4 cm using the double-diameter
   method. Measure all four sides to the nearest millimeter; report the
   range (max − min).
7. **Precision (N-Q.3).** Three learners report the same constructed
   segment as 5 cm, 5.0 cm, and 5.00 cm, all measured with a standard
   millimeter ruler. Which reports are honest, and what does each one
   *claim* about the measurement? (5 cm claims nearest centimeter; 5.0
   cm claims nearest millimeter — honest if the ruler reads 50 mm; 5.00
   cm claims nearest tenth of a millimeter — false precision from this
   tool.)
8. **Error analysis.** A learner's inscribed "hexagon" has a visible gap
   between the sixth mark and the first. They insist the method is
   broken. Write the correction: the 60° arithmetic, the actual culprit,
   and the one habit that prevents it. (6 × 60° = 360° closes exactly;
   the compass width drifted — a transfer error; lock the hinge once and
   mark all six points without re-setting.)

## Set D — Proofs: vertical angles, linear pairs, transversals (Week 5, Session 12; supports Lessons 04–05)

*Write the pair-type classification before every equation.*

1. Complete and then rewrite as a paragraph: the two-column proof that
   vertical angles are congruent (Lesson 04, Part 2). Close the lesson
   first; open it only to check.
2. m∠1 = (2x + 18)° is vertical to m∠3 = (6x − 42)°. Find x and both
   measures, with the check.
3. ∠*ABC* and ∠*CBD* form a linear pair. m∠*ABC* = (3x + 4)°,
   m∠*CBD* = (2x + 36)°. Ray *BE* bisects ∠*ABC*. Find m∠*ABE*, with
   the check.
4. Prove in two-column form: *alternate exterior angles formed by two
   parallel lines cut by a transversal are congruent.* (Chain the
   Corresponding Angles Postulate with the Vertical Angles Theorem.)
5. Lines *l* ∥ *m* cut by transversal *t*; the upper-left angle at the
   top intersection is 127°. Give all eight measures, each with its
   reason (corresponding / vertical / linear pair / alternate interior).
6. Alternate interior angles measure (5y − 10)° and (3y + 26)°. Find y,
   both angles, and the same-side interior angle adjacent to the first.
   Show both checks.
7. **Error analysis.** A proof states: "∠A ≅ ∠B because both look like
   45° in the diagram." Rewrite the step correctly for the case where
   ∠A and ∠B are alternate interior angles of parallel lines cut by a
   transversal — and explain what was wrong with the original in terms of
   the allowed-reasons list.
8. **Explain your reasoning.** A classmate claims the Same-Side Interior
   Angles Theorem makes the Corresponding Angles Postulate unnecessary:
   "just prove corresponding angles from same-side interior instead."
   Explain the circularity. (The same-side interior proof *uses* the
   postulate; you cannot prove a starting point from its own
   consequence.)
