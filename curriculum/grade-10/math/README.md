## What this track covers

A full-year (about 36 weeks) grade-10 math course following the Geometry
pathway: precise geometric language and the habit of proof; rigid motions —
translations, reflections, rotations — as the definition of congruence;
triangle-congruence criteria justified by rigid motions; similarity through
dilations and proportional reasoning; right-triangle trigonometry, the laws of
sines and cosines, and applied triangle solving; quadrilateral and polygon
properties proved synthetically and on the coordinate plane; circles — chords,
tangents, inscribed and central angles, and equations; area, surface area,
volume, and geometric modeling of real objects; and geometric probability with
the addition and multiplication rules. Sessions are about 50 minutes, five per
week, blending paper-and-pencil construction and proof, dynamic-geometry
software (GeoGebra) and Desmos for transformations and coordinate work,
real-measurement modeling, and independent written practice that the adult
reviews the same day.

## Track objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the year the learner will be able to:

1. State precise definitions of angle, circle, perpendicular line, parallel
   line, and line segment from undefined notions of point, line, and
   distance, and distinguish defined terms from undefined notions.
2. Perform translations, reflections, and rotations; distinguish
   distance-and-angle-preserving transformations from others; identify lines
   of symmetry and rotational symmetry in rectangles, parallelograms,
   trapezoids, and regular polygons.
3. Define congruence through rigid motions; use two-column or paragraph proofs
   to justify the ASA, AAS, SSS, and SAS triangle-congruence criteria with
   rigid-motion reasoning; determine whether two figures are congruent.
4. Prove theorems about lines and angles — vertical angles, angles formed by
   a transversal, perpendicular segments — and about triangles: the interior
   angle sum, base angles of isosceles triangles, the midsegment theorem, and
   concurrency of medians.
5. Prove and apply parallelogram theorems (opposite sides and angles
   congruent, diagonals bisecting each other, rectangles' diagonals
   congruent) and use them in synthetic and coordinate proofs.
6. Define similarity through rigid motions followed by dilations; use the AA
   criterion and the triangle-proportionality theorem to prove relationships
   and solve problems involving corresponding angles and proportional sides.
7. Explain sine, cosine, and tangent as ratios determined by similar right
   triangles; relate the sine and cosine of complementary angles.
8. Use trigonometric ratios, the Pythagorean Theorem, and angles of
   elevation and depression to solve applied right-triangle problems; derive
   and apply the laws of sines and cosines to solve general triangles; use
   the area formula (1/2)ab·sin C.
9. Describe relationships among chords, tangents, inscribed and central
   angles, and radii; justify that all circles are similar; construct
   inscribed and circumscribed circles of a triangle and a tangent from an
   exterior point; perform formal constructions (copying segments and
   angles, bisectors, perpendiculars, inscribed regular polygons) and use
   them as proof tools.
10. Derive and use the standard equation of a circle (completing the square
    to find centers and radii); derive the parabola equation from its
    focus/directrix definition; use coordinates to prove geometric facts —
    slope criteria for parallel and perpendicular lines, midpoint-based
    classifications, and perimeter and area on the coordinate plane.
11. Explain and use formulas for circumference, circle area, and the volumes
    of cylinders, pyramids, cones, and spheres; identify 2-D cross-sections
    of 3-D solids and solids generated by rotations; apply geometric methods
    to design problems.
12. Use geometric shapes, measures, and units to model physical objects;
    apply area- and volume-based density; define quantities for descriptive
    modeling; and choose accuracy appropriate to measurement limits.
13. Compute geometric probabilities from length, area, and volume models;
    distinguish independent from dependent events; apply the addition and
    multiplication rules for probability to geometric and other contexts.

## For the guiding adult

Grade-10 math turns grade-9's symbolic fluency — solving linear and quadratic
equations, factoring, function notation, coordinate graphing — into geometric
reasoning: conjecturing, justifying, and proving. Learners enter expected to
solve equations and factor quadratics, graph on the coordinate plane, use the
Pythagorean Theorem from grade 8, and write a clear worked solution with a
justification at each step (the grade-9 track's 14 objectives, currently in
unmerged draft PR #106). The diagnostic weeks verify these entry skills and
the track re-teaches insecure skills in use before assuming them.

Geometry proof is the track's central habit: every unit requires written
justification, counterexamples for false claims, and proof written in
two-column or paragraph form. Dynamic geometry (GeoGebra) supports
conjecturing; paper proof establishes results. Calculator work (trig ratios,
square roots) never substitutes for exact reasoning where the objective asks
for it. A compass, straightedge, protractor, and graph paper are the core
physical tools; a dedicated geometry kit with replacement pencils and
erasers lasts the year.
