# Lesson 01 — What Makes a Sentence?

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find the first word, the capital letter,
and the ending mark that make a sentence."
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** 6 sentence strips and 4
not-sentence strips (adult-made, large print), a pointer.
**Standards:** RF.1.1, RF.1.1.a.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Lay out two strips: "The cat sat." and "cat sat". Read each aloud exactly
as written (for the second, read it flat: "cat sat"). "Which one sounds
finished? Which one sounds like it fell apart?" Note what the learner
notices — that is your starting map.

## Explicit teaching

A **sentence** is a group of words that says a whole, finished thought. In
print, three marks show you where a sentence starts and stops:

1. The **first word** — reading starts at the first word on the left.
2. A **capital letter** — the first word of a sentence starts with a big
   (capital) letter, like **T** in "The". A capital is the tall, special
   version of the letter.
3. An **ending mark** — the sentence stops with one of three marks:
   a **period** (**.**) for telling, a **question mark** (**?**) for
   asking, an **exclamation point** (**!**) for strong feeling.

Say it as a check the learner can repeat: *"First word, capital letter,
ending mark — that's a sentence!"*

### Modeled example 1 — a sentence passes the test

*Adult points under each word of "The dog ran." as she reads:*
"First word: **The** — it starts with a capital T. *(points to the T)*
Last mark: a **period**. First word, capital letter, ending mark — this is
a sentence." *Adult covers the period with her thumb:* "If I hide the
period, the sentence lost its ending mark. It looks unfinished. The period
finishes the job."

### Modeled example 2 — a not-sentence fails the test

*Adult shows "ran the dog":* "First word: **ran** — no capital letter.
Last mark: none at all. The words are also in a mixed-up order, so the
thought is not finished. First word — no. Capital letter — no. Ending
mark — no. This is **not** a sentence. To fix it I would write 'The dog
ran.' — capital T, period at the end."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Show "The sun is hot." "Point to the first word." (Prompt: "reading
   starts at the left.")
2. "What letter does the first word start with — big or small?" (Big —
   capital T.)
3. "Point to the ending mark. Which one is it?" (Period — a telling
   sentence.)
4. Show "Can you hop?" "Read it with me, then name the ending mark."
   (Question mark — an asking sentence.)
5. Show "Look at the fox!" "What feeling does this ending mark show?"
   (Exclamation point — strong feeling, excitement.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Sort 4 strips into SENTENCE and NOT A SENTENCE piles ("I see Sam."
   / "the pig" / "We can run!" / "bus red"). Any correct sorting counts;
   the learner names one reason per strip.
2. Point to the first word of "I see Sam."
3. Find the capital letter in "We can run!" (W).
4. Name the ending mark in "We can run!" (!).
5. Fix "the pig": rewrite or dictate the fixed sentence ("The pig.")
   with capital T and a period. Adult scribes if the learner dictates.

## Application

"Be the sentence doctor." The learner gets two "sick" strips ("sam ran"
and "the bus is red") and fixes each: adds the capital letter and the
ending mark, then reads the healed sentence aloud with expression that
matches the mark (excited voice for **!**, asking voice for **?**).

## Exit check

1. "Point to the first word." (of "The cat sat." — "The")
2. "Show me the capital letter." (T)
3. "What is the ending mark, and what is it for?" (Period — telling.)
4. "Is 'hop to the top' a sentence? How do you know?" (No — no capital,
   no ending mark, thought unfinished.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** teach *first/last* with the learner's body first
  ("point to the first person in our line — that's you"), then the strip.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner may answer every check by pointing
  or gesturing — no speech required; the adult voices what the point means.
- **Seated-table variant:** all sorting at the table with strips in two
  labeled trays.
- **Floor-play variant:** "sentence road" — the learner walks three floor
  cards (FIRST WORD → CAPITAL → ENDING MARK) and taps each while chanting
  the check.
- **Extension:** find a sentence in a picture book; name its ending mark
  and read it with matching expression.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"Every line is a sentence":* children count lines, not sentences. Show
  a two-sentence line ("The cat sat. The dog ran.") — two capitals, two
  periods, two sentences on one line.
- *Capital = any big letter:* children may point to a tall lowercase
  letter (like t or h). Contrast side by side: "t and T are partners —
  the capital is the one that starts the sentence."
- *The period is "a dot":* name all three marks every time until the
  names stick; the chant helps.
