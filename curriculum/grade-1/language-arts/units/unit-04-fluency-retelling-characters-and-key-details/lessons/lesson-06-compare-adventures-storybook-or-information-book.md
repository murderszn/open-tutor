# Lesson 06 — Compare Adventures; Storybook or Information Book

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare what two characters do
in their adventures — what is the same and what is different —
and I can tell a storybook apart from an information book."
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** "Milo and the Red Boat"
and "Ana and the Lost Mitten" (unit README); two-column chart
(Same / Different).
**Standards:** RL.1.9; RL.1.5.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Adult: "Milo loses his boat's balance — it tips over. Ana loses
her mitten. What is the same about those two problems?" If the
learner says "both lose something" (or points to both), they are
ready. If not, re-retell both middles first.

## Explicit teaching

**Compare** means looking at two things side by side: what is the
**same**, and what is **different**. Characters' adventures can
be compared the same way:

- **Same:** Milo and Ana both lose something they care about.
  Both feel sad. Both get help and go home happy.
- **Different:** Milo loses a boat at a pond in the wind; Ana
  loses a mitten in the snow. Pip the dog helps Milo; a little
  cat helps Ana (by sitting on the mitten!). Milo's problem is
  fixed by a rescue; Ana's is fixed by looking carefully.

And a **storybook** is different from an **information book**:

- A storybook tells a made-up story with characters, a problem,
  and an ending: "Milo and the Red Boat."
- An information book gives real facts about a topic: it might
  have headings, photographs, and facts like "A pond is a small
  body of water." It does not have Milo or Pip or a problem to
  solve.

Our three unit stories are all storybooks. If a book teaches you
facts about ponds with no story characters, it is an information
book.

### Modeled example 1 — Same and different: Milo and Ana

*Adult fills the chart aloud:* "Same: both lose something; both
feel sad; both end happy. Different: Milo is at a pond, Ana is in
the snow. Milo's helper is a dog; Ana's helper is a cat. Milo's
boat tips over in the wind; Ana's mitten hides under a cat."
"Same AND different — a good comparison has both."

### Modeled example 2 — Storybook or information book?

*Adult holds up two imaginary books:* "Book A: 'Milo and the Red
Boat' — characters, a problem, an ending. Storybook! Book B:
'All About Ponds' — facts about water, ducks, and mud, with
headings. Information book! How do I know? Book B has no
characters with a problem — it teaches facts." Learner repeats
the test: "Characters and a problem? Storybook. Facts and
headings? Information book."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "What is the same about Milo's and Ana's feelings?" (*Both
   feel sad, then happy*)
2. "What is different about where their stories happen?" (*Pond
   vs. snow*)
3. "Who helps Milo? Who helps Ana?" (*Pip the dog; the little
   cat*)
4. "Is 'The Slow Race' a storybook or an information book? How
   do you know?" (*Storybook — Hare and Turtle, a race, a lesson*)
5. Learner invents one information-book title about ponds and
   one fact it might teach ("Ponds have ducks" — any true,
   simple fact).

## Independent practice (4–6 short tasks; adult checks same day)

1. Learner completes the Same/Different chart for Milo and Ana
   with one more item in each column (dictated or drawn).
2. Learner sorts three titles into Storybook / Information Book:
   "Ana and the Lost Mitten" (storybook), "All About Snow"
   (information), "The Slow Race" (storybook).
3. Learner answers: "What is the same about how Milo's and
   Ana's stories end?" (*Both go home happy*)
4. Learner draws one picture for a storybook and one for an
   information book about the same topic (pond), and tells how
   they differ.

## Application

**Adventure compare poster:** the learner makes a poster with
two columns (Milo / Ana), three rows (Problem, Helper, Ending),
and one "Same" star at the bottom. The adult scribes dictated
words; the learner reads the poster back.

## Exit check

Learner names one sameness and one difference between Milo's and
Ana's adventures, and classifies "Milo and the Red Boat" as a
storybook with a reason ("it has characters and a problem").
Success: 3/3.

## Supports

- **Seated-table variant:** Same/Different cards sorted into
  two piles on the chart.
- **Floor-play variant:** learner stands on "Same" or
  "Different" floor cards as the adult reads a comparison
  sentence.
- **Language access:** adult re-reads both middles before the
  comparison; learner answers by pointing to story sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner draws the same/different
  items instead of writing them.
- **Extension:** learner compares Hare and Turtle within "The
  Slow Race" (fast vs. slow; naps vs. keeps walking) and states
  which way of racing the lesson praises.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- **Only same or only different:** a comparison needs both.
  Prompt the missing side: "You found a sameness — now find a
  difference."
- **"Real" vs. "made up" confusion:** a storybook can feel real
  (ponds are real!) and still be a storybook. The test is
  characters + problem vs. facts + headings, not real vs. fake.
- **Helper confusion:** the cat does not hand Ana the mitten —
  it sits on it. Credit the exact help: "The cat shows Ana where
  it is by sitting on it."
