# Lesson 02 — Ask and Answer Key-Detail Questions

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can ask and answer who, what, where,
when, and how questions about the important parts — the key
details — of a story."
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** "Milo and the Red Boat"
(unit README), five question cards (Who? What? Where? When? How?).
**Standards:** RL.1.1; SL.1.2.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Adult asks about breakfast or the walk to school: "Who went with
you? Where did you go?" If the learner answers a who and a where
question about real life, they can do it about a story. If not,
practice two real-life questions first.

## Explicit teaching

A **key detail** is an important part of a story — not every
little thing, but the parts you need to understand what happened.
Questions find key details. Five question words do most of the work:

- **Who?** — the characters (people or animals in the story).
- **What?** — what happened, or what something is.
- **Where?** — the place.
- **When?** — the time (today, one cold day, at night).
- **How?** — the way it happened, or how someone felt.

A good question can be answered from the story — not from a guess.
"Who has the red boat?" The story says: Milo. That is a key-detail
answer. "What color is Milo's coat?" The story never says — so
that is not a story question at all.

### Modeled example 1 — Who and where in the Milo story

*Adult reads:* "Milo and Pip go to the pond." *Adult asks:* "Who
goes to the pond?" *Adult thinks aloud:* "The story says Milo and
Pip. So: Milo and his dog Pip." *Then:* "Where do they go? The
story says: to the pond." Point back to the sentence for each
answer — answers live in the story.

### Modeled example 2 — A question you can answer, and one you cannot

*Adult asks:* "What happens to the boat?" *Thinks aloud:* "The
wind blows hard. The boat tips over! Splash! So the boat tips
over." *Then:* "What does Milo eat for lunch? Hmm — the story
never says. That is not a key-detail question, because the story
cannot answer it. Good questions have story answers."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Who is Milo's dog?" (*Pip*)
2. "Where does Milo put the boat?" (*In the water, at the pond*)
3. "What does the wind do?" (*Blows hard; the boat tips over*)
4. "How does Milo feel when the boat tips over?" (*Sad*)
5. Learner asks the adult one question about the story, using a
   question card (Who/What/Where/When/How). Adult answers from
   the story.

## Independent practice (4–6 short tasks; adult checks same day)

1. Learner answers: "How does Pip help Milo?" (*Pip jumps in,
   gets the boat, brings it back*)
2. Learner answers: "How does the story end?" (*They go home
   happy*)
3. Learner draws Pip bringing the boat back, and dictates or
   writes one sentence telling what Pip did (adult scribes if
   the learner only dictates).
4. Learner asks two questions of their own about the Milo story;
   the adult answers, and together they check each answer in
   the story text.

## Application

**Question-card game:** the adult shuffles the five question
cards. The learner draws a card and must ask a story question
starting with that word; the adult answers from the story. Then
they switch. Three rounds each.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Who tips the boat over — Milo or the wind?" and
"Where does the story happen?" Success: learner answers "the
wind" and "at the pond" (or "in the water at the pond"), pointing
to or quoting the story sentence for at least one answer.

## Supports

- **Seated-table variant:** question cards laid in a row; learner
  picks a card and points to the story sentence that answers it.
- **Floor-play variant:** learner hops to the Who, What, or Where
  card taped on the floor before answering.
- **Language access:** adult re-reads the key sentence before
  each question; the learner answers by pointing first, then
  speaking.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner answers by drawing or
  pointing at the picture (Session D illustration) instead of
  speaking a full sentence.
- **Extension:** learner asks a "why" question ("Why is Milo
  sad?") and answers it with a because-sentence from the story.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- **Answering from the picture alone:** pictures help, but key
  details must also be in the words. Ask: "Show me the sentence
  that says that."
- **Every detail is key:** "Milo's boat is red" is key (the story
  keeps naming it); the color of the sky is not in this story at
  all. Teach learners to check: does the story need this detail
  to make sense?
- **Guessing the adult's question:** if the learner answers a
  different question than the one asked, repeat the question word
  slowly: "I asked WHO — a person or animal."
