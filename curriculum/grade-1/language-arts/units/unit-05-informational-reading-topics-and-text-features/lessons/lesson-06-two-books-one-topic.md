# Lesson 06 — Two Books, One Topic

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare two books about
beavers and tell one way they are the same and one way they are
different."
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** [Unit Text A](../README.md#unit-text-a--beavers-build-a-home)
and [Unit Text B](../README.md#unit-text-b--a-beaver-family),
two-column chart (SAME | DIFFERENT) on paper.
**Standards:** RI.1.9; RI.1.2; RI.1.7.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Adult: "Tell me one thing Text A taught you." (Any key detail.)
"Now I will read a second book about the same animal. Listen
for something it teaches that the first book did not." If the
learner recalls one Text A detail, the ear is ready.

## Explicit teaching

Two books can share one **topic** and still teach different
things. Text A is about beavers and how they build. Text B is
about beavers and their families. Same topic — different focus.

To **compare** means to find what is the **same** and what is
**different**. Use both books' words and pictures:

- **Same:** both books are about beavers. Both tell us the
  lodge is the beaver's home of sticks and mud. Both have a
  table of contents and a glossary.
- **Different:** Text A teaches building (dams, lodges, food
  storage). Text B teaches family life (kits born, growing up,
  tail-slap warnings). Text B has a fact Text A never gives: a
  beaver can slap its tail as a warning. Text A has a fact Text
  B never gives: beaver teeth keep growing.

Compare means more than "both are about beavers." Find one real
same and one real different, and say where each came from.

### Modeled example 1 — Same: the lodge

*Adult:* "Text A says: 'A beaver builds a lodge in the pond.'
Text B says: 'The family keeps the kit warm and safe inside the
lodge.' Same fact in both books: the lodge is the beaver's home.
When both books agree, that fact is extra sure."

### Modeled example 2 — Different: teeth vs. tail slap

*Adult:* "Text A teaches: beaver teeth keep growing, so beavers
gnaw wood. Text B never mentions teeth. Text B teaches: a beaver
slaps its flat tail as a warning. Text A never mentions the
slap. Different books, same animal, different lessons. That is
why readers read more than one book about a topic!"

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "What is the topic of both books?" (Beavers.)
2. "Name one fact in BOTH books." (Lodge is the home of sticks
   and mud; kits/babies live there; deep water keeps beavers
   safe.)
3. "Name one fact ONLY in Text A." (Teeth keep growing; dams
   of sticks, mud, stones; food stored under water.)
4. "Name one fact ONLY in Text B." (Kits are born in spring;
   kits stay two years; tail slap warns of danger.)
5. "Which book would you open to learn what a kit is? How do
   you know?" (Either — both glossaries define *kit*. Text B's
   glossary and its 'Kits Are Born' section.)

## Independent practice (4–6 short tasks; adult checks same day)

1. Fill the chart: one SAME row (both books: lodge home) and
   one DIFFERENT row (A: teeth; B: tail slap) — dictation or
   drawing accepted.
2. Retell Text B in two sentences: kits are born in spring and
   stay two years; the tail slap warns the family.
3. Answer: "Do both books have a glossary?" (Yes.)
4. Draw one thing you learned ONLY from Text B (a kit, or a
   tail slapping water) and label it with adult help.
5. Say which book you liked more and one reason why (any honest
   reason counts — this practices opinion language, not a
   right answer).

## Application

**Two-book quiz show:** the adult reads six facts; the learner
says "A," "B," or "Both": "Beaver teeth keep growing." (A.)
"Kits stay two years." (B.) "The lodge is made of sticks and
mud." (Both.) "A tail slap warns of danger." (B.) "Beavers eat
bark." (A.) "The lodge door is under the water." (A — Text B
never says it; accept "Both" only if the learner defends it
from a picture, then check the texts together.)

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Tell me one way Text A and Text B are the same
and one way they are different." Success: one true likeness
(topic, lodge, glossary, or shared fact) and one true difference
(A-only or B-only fact).

## Supports

- **Seated-table variant:** fact cards sorted into three piles:
  A only / B only / Both.
- **Floor-play variant:** two hoops (A, B) overlapping in the
  middle (Both); learner places fact cards in the right zone.
- **Language access:** the adult rereads the key sentence for
  each fact before the learner sorts it.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner points to the book or
  draws the fact instead of naming it.
- **Extension:** the learner writes (or dictates) one question
  that NEITHER book answers (for example, "How long can a
  beaver stay under water?") — and the adult notes it as a
  research question for the project, answered from the
  resource pack, not invented.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- **"Same" = same topic only:** push past "both about
  beavers" to a shared fact (the lodge) — that is the skill.
- **Guessing A/B from memory:** always check the texts. Model
  flipping back: "Let's look — does Text B say anything about
  teeth? No. So that fact is A only."
- **Pictures count as evidence:** Text B has no picture in
  this unit, so comparison here rests on words. In the project,
  the learner's own picture page will let pictures carry facts
  again — keep the distinction explicit.
