# Lesson 02 — Headings and the Table of Contents

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can use headings and the table of
contents to find a fact fast, without reading the whole book."
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** [Unit Text A](../README.md#unit-text-a--beavers-build-a-home)
with its table of contents, sticky notes or small paper flags.
**Standards:** RI.1.5; RI.1.1.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Adult shows the table of contents: "This list tells the name of
each part and the page it starts on. Point to a part name." If
the learner can point to any part name, the eyes are ready. If
not, read the five part names aloud together first.

## Explicit teaching

An information book is built for finding. You do not have to read
it from start to finish.

A **heading** is a title over one part. It tells what that part
is about. Read the heading first, and you know whether that part
has your fact.

A **table of contents** is a list at the front of the book. It
tells the name of every part and the page where it starts. To find
a fact fast: (1) ask your question, (2) find the part name that
matches, (3) go to that page.

An **icon** is a small picture that stands for something — like
a magnifying glass for "search" or a book for "read." Icons help
you find things without reading words.

### Modeled example 1 — Find where beavers live

*Adult:* "Question: Where does a beaver live? Which part name
matches? 'A Lodge in the Pond' — a lodge is a home! Go to page
3. Read: 'A beaver builds a lodge in the pond.' Found it — fast.
No need to read pages 1, 2, and 4."

### Modeled example 2 — Find what beavers eat

*Adult:* "Question: What do beavers eat? Table of contents…
'Beaver Food' — page 4. That part must tell about food. Page 4
says: 'Beavers eat bark, twigs, and leaves.' The heading told us
where to look before we read a single fact."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "I want a fact about beaver teeth. Which part do I open?"
   ("Beaver Builders," page 1.)
2. "I want a fact about the dam. Which heading matches?" ("A Dam
   Across the Stream.")
3. Adult covers the headings: "Without headings, how would you
   find the food fact?" (You would have to read everything —
   headings save time.)
4. "What does the table of contents tell you that a heading
   alone does not?" (The page number, and all the parts at once.)
5. "Find the glossary in the table of contents. What page is
   it?" (Page 5.)

## Independent practice (4–6 short tasks; adult checks same day)

1. Flag the "A Lodge in the Pond" heading with a sticky note.
2. Answer without reading the whole book: "Where is the door
   of the lodge?" (Under the water — found on page 3.)
3. Answer: "What page tells about beaver food?" (Page 4.)
4. Draw a tiny table of contents for a 3-part book about cats
   (adult scribes part names the learner dictates: for example,
   "Cat Bodies," "Cat Food," "Cat Babies").
5. Point to one icon in a household book or on a device screen
   and say what it stands for (adult confirms).

## Application

**Fact race (no rushing, just finding):** the adult asks three
questions, one at a time. The learner uses only the table of
contents and headings to open the right part, then answers:
"What are beaver teeth like?" (Big, orange, keep growing —
page 1.) "What does a dam do?" (Slows the water, makes a pond
— page 2.) "What keeps beaver food fresh in winter?" (Cold
pond water, like a refrigerator — page 4.)

## Exit check

Adult asks: "I want to know what a beaver lodge is made of.
Show me how you find it — no reading the whole book." Success:
learner names the "A Lodge in the Pond" heading (or page 3 in
the contents) and answers "sticks and mud."

## Supports

- **Seated-table variant:** parts printed on separate pages the
  learner can flip to; flags mark each heading.
- **Floor-play variant:** part names on floor cards; learner
  hops to the card that answers each question.
- **Language access:** the adult reads headings aloud while the
  learner points; the skill is matching, not decoding headings.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner points to the heading or
  contents line instead of saying the page number.
- **Extension:** the learner writes (or dictates) one new
  question and the part that would answer it.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- **Reading everything anyway:** praise finding first. Say:
  "A reader who finds fast is a smart reader, not a lazy one."
- **Heading = topic:** the topic is the whole book (beavers);
  a heading is one part's topic. Ask: "Is the whole book about
  dams, or just this part?"
- **Page-number mix-ups:** contents page numbers count the
  book's pages, not the lesson's pages. Keep the printed text
  in order so the numbers work.
