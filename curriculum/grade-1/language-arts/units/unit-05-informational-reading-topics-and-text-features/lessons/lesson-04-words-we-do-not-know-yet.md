# Lesson 04 — Words We Do Not Know Yet

**Goal (learner hears):** "When I meet a hard word, I can use
the sentence around it, a word part I know, or the glossary to
figure it out."
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** [Unit Text A](../README.md#unit-text-a--beavers-build-a-home)
and its glossary, word cards: *gnaw, lodge, dam, kit, bark,
pond, rebuild, lodges, looking*.
**Standards:** RI.1.4; L.1.4.a; L.1.4.b; L.1.4.c.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Adult: "What does *happy* mean?" (Learner answers.) "What does
*unhappy* mean?" If the learner can use *un-* to get "not
happy," the word-part ear is ready. If not, play with *un-* on
two known words (*tie/untie, lock/unlock*) first.

## Explicit teaching

Information books use hard words. A good reader does not skip
them — a good reader has three tools:

1. **Sentence clues.** The words around the hard word often
   explain it. "The beaver gnaws wood with its teeth. Gnaw means
   to bite and chew again and again." The next sentence gave
   the meaning away!
2. **Word parts (affixes).** A small part added to a word
   changes its meaning. *Re-* means "again": *rebuild* = build
   again. *-s* means "more than one": *lodges* = more than one
   lodge. *-ing* means "happening now": *looking* = look
   happening now.
3. **The glossary.** The glossary is a mini-dictionary at the
   back of the book. It lists hard words in ABC order and tells
   what each one means. If the sentence does not explain a
   word, the glossary will.

Also: a **root word** is the main word before parts are added.
In *looking*, the root is *look*. In *lodges*, the root is
*lodge* (drop the e, add -s — a Unit 03 rule!).

### Modeled example 1 — Sentence clue for *gnaw*

*Adult:* "'A beaver gnaws wood with its teeth.' Do we know
*gnaw* yet? Read on: 'Gnaw means to bite and chew again and
again.' The sentence itself told us! And it fits: teeth + bite
and chew = gnaw. Check: does 'bite and chew again and again'
make sense in the first sentence? 'A beaver bites and chews wood
again and again with its teeth.' Yes!"

### Modeled example 2 — Affix clue for *rebuild*

*Adult:* "The dam broke. The beaver must *rebuild* it. *Re-*
means again, and *build* is a word we know. Rebuild = build
again. So the beaver builds the dam again. Now *lodges*: root
*lodge* + *-s* = more than one lodge. Word parts are clues,
just like sentence clues."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "The book says: 'Bark is the rough skin of a tree.' What is
   bark?" (The rough outer skin of a tree — sentence clue.)
2. "Find *dam* in the glossary. What does it say?" (A wall
   built across a stream to slow the water.)
3. "What does *reheat* mean? Use *re-*." (Heat again.)
4. "Root word game: in *swimming*, what is the root?" (*Swim*.)
   "In *kits*, what is the root?" (*Kit*.)
5. "The sentence says: 'The pond is deep. Deep water keeps the
   beaver safe.' What does *safe* mean here?" (Protected from
   animals that hunt it — sentence clue plus earlier facts.)

## Independent practice (4–6 short tasks; adult checks same day)

1. Look up *pond* in the glossary and say its meaning (a small
   body of still water).
2. Use a sentence clue: "Beavers store food. They push branches
   down into the mud." What does *store* mean here? (Keep/save
   for later.)
3. Use a word part: what does *regrow* mean? (Grow again.)
4. Sort the cards into "root" and "root + part": *look, looks,
   lodge, lodges, build, rebuild* (roots: look, lodge, build;
   with parts: looks, lodges, rebuild).
5. Ask the adult one question about a word's meaning
   (RI.1.4 — asking is the skill, not just answering).

## Application

**Word detective case:** the adult reads a new sentence: "In
winter, the beaver family huddles in the lodge." The learner
uses the three tools in order: (1) sentence clue — family
together inside, winter, warm? (2) word parts — none familiar;
(3) glossary — *huddle* is not in our glossary, so the adult
supplies: "to crowd close together for warmth." The learner
repeats the meaning in their own words and the adult notes:
sometimes even detectives ask a person — asking counts.

## Exit check

Adult shows the word *gnaw* in a sentence: "The beaver gnaws
the branch." Learner explains the meaning (bite and chew again
and again) and names the tool used (sentence clue — or glossary).
Success: correct meaning plus a named tool.

## Supports

- **Seated-table variant:** word cards in two piles (roots /
  with parts); learner sorts by sliding cards.
- **Floor-play variant:** root words on floor cards; learner
  jumps on the root, then on the added part.
- **Language access:** act out *gnaw* (pretend chewing),
  *store* (putting branches in a pile), *huddle* (crowding
  close) before defining.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner points to the glossary
  entry or draws the meaning instead of saying a definition.
- **Extension:** the learner finds one more affix in Text A or
  B (*-s* in *sticks*, *-ed* in *stored* — Unit 03 endings!)
  and explains it.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- **Guessing from the first letter:** "It starts with g, so it
  must be…" — stop and use a tool instead. Tools beat guesses.
- **Glossary as punishment:** the glossary is a tool, not a
  test of memory. Praise every glossary use, even mid-sentence.
- **One tool only:** some words need two tools (sentence +
  glossary). The application case models asking a person as a
  valid last step — RI.1.4 includes asking questions, not only
  answering them.
