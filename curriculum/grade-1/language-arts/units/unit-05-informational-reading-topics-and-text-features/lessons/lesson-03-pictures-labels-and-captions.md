# Lesson 03 — Pictures, Labels, and Captions

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell what a picture shows that
the words do not, and use labels and captions to learn facts."
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** the generated
illustration [`assets/beaver-dam-scene.png`](../assets/beaver-dam-scene.png),
printed or on screen; blank labels (small cards) and a marker.
**Standards:** RI.1.6; RI.1.7.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Adult shows the illustration: "Tell me one thing you see." Any
naming counts (beaver, dam, duck, trees). If the learner names
nothing, the adult points and names three things first, then asks
again.

## Explicit teaching

In an information book, pictures teach too — and sometimes a
picture teaches something the words never say.

A **label** is a word that names a part of a picture. Labels have
lines that point, or they sit right next to the part they name.

A **caption** is a sentence under or beside a picture. It tells
what the picture shows, or one fact about it.

Look at our picture (AI-generated illustration; caption: "A
beaver stands on its dam of sticks, mud, and stones, holding a
stick. Behind it, its stick lodge rises from the pond. A duck
swims nearby."):

- The words in Text A say a dam is made of sticks, mud, and
  stones. The picture shows you what that pile looks like.
- The words say a lodge is shaped like a dome. The picture shows
  the dome shape.
- The picture shows something the words never say: a yellow duck
  swims in the pond. That fact comes from the picture only!

*Alt text for the illustration:* "A cartoon beaver holding a
stick stands on a dam of sticks, mud, and stones in a pond. Behind
it is a dome-shaped lodge made of sticks. A yellow duck swims in
the pond, with cattails and trees nearby."

*Text-only alternative (same information, no picture needed):*
"The beaver stands on a dam made of sticks, mud, and stones. It
holds a stick. Behind the beaver is a dome-shaped lodge made of
sticks. A yellow duck swims in the pond. There are cattails and
trees at the pond's edge."

### Modeled example 1 — Picture-only information

*Adult:* "Do the words in Text A tell us a duck swims in the
pond? No. So where did we learn it? From the picture. Pictures
can teach facts the words leave out. That is RI.1.6: telling
picture information apart from word information."

### Modeled example 2 — Labels name parts

*Adult places blank labels near the picture:* "This part is the
**dam**. This part is the **lodge**. This is the beaver's flat
**tail**. A label names a part so you can find it and talk
about it. Now you try: which label goes on the pile of sticks
the beaver stands on?" (Dam.)

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Point to the lodge. What shape is it?" (Dome-shaped, like
   a round hill.)
2. "What does the beaver hold?" (A stick.) "Did the words say
   that, or the picture?" (The picture.)
3. Adult writes labels *dam, lodge, tail, duck*: "Put each
   label on the right part." (Dam = stick pile; lodge = dome
   behind; tail = flat brown shape behind the beaver; duck =
   yellow bird in the water.)
4. "Read this caption with me: 'A beaver stands on its dam.'
   What does the caption tell us that the picture alone does
   not?" (That the pile is a dam — the picture shows a pile;
   the caption names it.)
5. "Name one fact from the picture and one fact from the
   words." (Picture: a duck swims here / cattails grow at the
   edge. Words: the lodge door is under the water / beavers
   eat bark.)

## Independent practice (4–6 short tasks; adult checks same day)

1. Draw the beaver pond from memory, then add two labels
   (*dam*, *lodge*) with lines pointing to the right parts
   (adult scribes the label words if needed).
2. Write or dictate one caption for the drawing: "A beaver
   stands on its dam."
3. Answer: "What does the picture show that the words do not?"
   (The duck; the cattails; what the dam looks like.)
4. Answer: "What do the words tell that the picture does not?"
   (The lodge door is under the water; beavers store food for
   winter; teeth keep growing.)
5. Point to the beaver's tail in the picture and say what it is
   for (swimming rudder, balance, warning slap — any one
   counts; adult supplies "tail" vocabulary).

## Application

**Picture detective:** the adult covers the caption and asks:
"Without reading, what three things can you learn from this
picture alone?" (Accept any three true picture facts: beaver
stands on a stick dam; lodge is a dome of sticks; duck swims in
the pond; cattails and trees grow at the edge; beaver holds a
stick.) Then the adult reads the caption aloud: "What did the
caption add?" (It names the dam and the lodge.)

## Exit check

Adult shows the illustration: "Tell me one thing you learned
from the picture only, and one thing you learned from the words
only." Success: one true picture-only fact (duck, cattails,
dam's look) and one true word-only fact (underwater door, stored
winter food, ever-growing teeth).

## Supports

- **Seated-table variant:** labels on sticky notes the learner
  sticks onto a printed picture.
- **Floor-play variant:** big print of the picture on the floor;
  learner steps on the part the adult names.
- **Language access:** the adult names each part first; the
  learner repeats and points.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner points or draws instead
  of writing labels; the adult writes the words.
- **Extension:** the learner writes a second caption: "The duck
  swims in the beaver's pond." and explains why a caption helps
  a reader who cannot yet read the whole book.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- **"The picture is just decoration":** in information books
  pictures carry facts. The duck question proves it — ask it
  every time a new picture appears.
- **Caption vs. label:** a label is one naming word; a caption
  is a sentence about the picture. If the learner mixes them,
  hold up one finger for a label ("dam") and a whole hand for a
  caption sentence.
- **Stylized illustration limits:** this picture is an
  AI-generated illustration, not a photograph. The lodge's
  opening is drawn at the water's edge; the text teaches that
  real lodge entrances are under the water. Say so plainly —
  pictures can simplify, and words can correct them.
