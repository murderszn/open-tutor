# Lesson 04 — Carrying Messages with Sound

**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** 2 paper cups, ~2 meters of string
or yarn (adult handles the string), 2 paper clips, the generated image
[assets/cup-string-telephone.png](../assets/cup-string-telephone.png)
(printed or on screen), journal, crayons

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will solve a real problem: your
voice is too quiet to reach across the room. You will build a telephone
from cups and string and send a message through it.

**Readiness check:** "Stand at one end of the room and whisper my name.
Did I hear it?" (Adult does not react.) "The problem: whispers don't
travel far. A **message** is words you send to someone; to
**communicate** is to get the message to them. Today we build a helper."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

People long ago had the same problem: voices fade with distance. Their
solution was a **device** — a tool built to solve a problem — that carries
sound farther than a voice alone.

**Worked example 1 — study the picture first:**

> Adult (shows the generated image): "Look at these two children. Each
> holds a paper cup, and one long string connects the cups. The string is
> pulled tight — not saggy. See the little wiggle lines along the string?
> Those are our vibration lines from Lesson 02: the sound's shaking
> **travels along the string** from the talking cup to the listening cup.
> Point to the part that carries the sound." *(Learner points to the
> string.)*

**Text-only alternative:** "Two children sit facing each other, each
holding a paper cup. A tight string connects the two cups, and small
wiggle lines run along the string from the talking child's cup to the
listening child's cup. A drum and a bell sit nearby on the rug."

**Worked example 2 — build and send:**

> Adult (threads the string through each cup bottom, knots a paper clip
> inside each cup so the string can't pull out, and hands the cups to the
> learner and a partner — or the adult takes the other end): "I talk
> *into* my cup — normal voice, not a shout. You hold your cup to your
> ear. Keep the string tight!" *(Adult: "Can you hear me?")* Learner
> nods. "Now swap — you talk, I listen. The message traveled along the
> string. The cup caught my voice's shaking, the string carried it, your
> cup gave it back as sound."

**String safety (adult states, learner repeats):** "String stays in our
hands. String never goes around a neck — not mine, not yours, not a toy's."

Key idea to say plainly: **A device can carry sound farther than a voice
alone — the string carries the message from cup to cup.**

## Guided practice — message relay

The learner and the adult (or a partner) stand ~2 meters apart and pass
**three messages** through the telephone ("My favorite color is blue,"
"Knock knock," a number). For each, the receiver repeats what they heard.
Adult scribes: message 1 ✓, message 2 ✓, message 3 ✓ (or ✗ and retry with
a tighter string — tightening is a *fix*, celebrated as engineering).

## Independent practice

Learner tries **one change** and reports the result: "What happens if the
string goes saggy?" (Message gets fuzzy or vanishes — the learner
discovers that a tight string carries sound better.) Or: "Whisper into
the cup vs. talk normally — which arrives clearer?"

## Application

"Your friend is across the playground and you have no cups. What sound
device could you use *right now* to send a message?" (Accept: cupped
hands around the mouth and shouting, clapping a code, banging two sticks.
The point: a device is anything built or used on purpose to carry the
message — this previews Lesson 05's design task.)

## Exit check

Learner answers: "What three parts does our telephone have, and what does
the string do?" (Two cups + string; the string carries the sound/message
from one cup to the other.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** label the three parts with picture cards (cup,
  string, ear) the learner sequences.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner gives a thumbs-up/thumbs-down for
  "message arrived" and points to the string when asked what carried it.
- **If string work is hard:** the adult holds both cups' string ends taut
  while the learner only talks and listens.

## Extension

"Longer string challenge": add another meter of string. Does the message
still arrive? The learner tests and records the longest distance that
still works — this data feeds the Lesson 05 comparison.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- *The cups do the magic; the string doesn't matter.* The saggy-string
  test exists to kill this: loose string → fuzzy message. The string is
  the carrier.
- *Shouting into the cup is required.* Model normal voice; shouting
  distorts the message *and* hurts ears (safety tie-in).
- *It works like a real phone.* Keep it concrete: "Our telephone carries
  shaking along a string. Real phones use electricity — that's a grown-up
  topic for later." (Assessment boundary: no technological details of how
  devices work.)
