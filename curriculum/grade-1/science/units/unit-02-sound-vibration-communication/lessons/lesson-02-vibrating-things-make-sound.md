# Lesson 02 — Vibrating Things Make Sound

**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** a shoebox or oatmeal container,
3 rubber bands (thin, medium, thick), a drum or upside-down pot with a
wooden spoon (adult strikes), journal, crayons

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will discover the secret behind
every sound: something is shaking back and forth very fast. That fast
shaking is called **vibrating**.

**Readiness check:** "Say the word *vibrate* with me. Now shake your hand
back and forth as fast as you can. That fast shaking is what we are
hunting today."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Every sound starts the same way: something **vibrates** — shakes back and
forth very, very fast. The shaking is so fast you sometimes cannot see
each shake; you just see a blur.

**Worked example 1 — the rubber band box:**

> Adult (stretches a rubber band over the open shoebox and plucks it):
> "Watch closely while I twang it. *Twang!* Did you see it? The band
> blurred — it shook back and forth super fast. That shaking is the
> **vibration**. And at the *same moment* the shaking started, the sound
> started. When the shaking stopped, the sound stopped. The shaking *made*
> the sound."

Adult repeats, and the learner watches for the blur. Then the learner
plucks the band and says: "I see it shaking. I hear the sound."

**Worked example 2 — the drum (sound you can feel):**

> Adult (strikes the drum once, then rests the learner's hand lightly on
> the drum skin — adult guides the hand, no hard striking while the hand
> is there): "Feel that? The drum skin is shaking under your hand — buzzing
> fast. You *felt* the vibration, and you *heard* the boom. Same
> vibration, two senses found it. Now I hold the drum skin still with my
> hand and strike again — the sound is tiny. **Stop the shaking, stop the
> sound.**"

Key idea to say plainly: **Vibrating things make sound. No vibration, no
sound.**

**Seeing is believing:** the adult draws a wavy line in the journal next
to the word *vibrate* — "wiggle lines mean something is shaking fast." This
symbol returns in every lesson.

## Guided practice — three bands, three tests

The learner tests the three rubber bands over the box, one at a time, and
for each answers (adult scribes):

1. "Did you **see** it shake?" (yes — the blur)
2. "Did you **hear** a sound at the same time?" (yes)
3. "Was the sound high or low?" (thin band → higher; thick band → lower)

## Independent practice

Learner finds **two more vibrating sound-makers** in the room (humming
with lips closed while touching the throat to feel the buzz; plucking a
stretched hair tie — adult supervises; tapping a metal spoon and watching
it shiver). For each, the learner draws the object with wiggle lines and
tells the adult: "It vibrates, so it makes sound."

## Application

"Your friend says the TV makes sound by magic. What would you show them to
prove the sound comes from vibration?" (Learner should propose feeling
the speaker buzz or watching something shake while the sound plays. Accept
any plan that connects a shake to the sound.)

## Exit check

Learner points to the rubber band and says the rule in their own words:
**"It shakes fast, so it makes sound."** Adult checks that both the shake
and the sound are mentioned.

## Supports

- **Language access:** the wiggle-line symbol stands in for the word
  *vibrate* everywhere; the learner can answer by drawing wiggle lines.
- **Alternate response mode:** yes/no + pointing ("Did the thin band
  shake? Did you hear it?") with adult scribing.
- **If the blur is hard to see:** pluck a long, loose band — the swing is
  slow enough to watch, then tighten it and show the same motion getting
  faster until it blurs.

## Extension

The learner tests what happens when the band is stretched tighter or
looser: "Tighter makes the shaking faster and the sound higher." (Names
the pattern; the unit never asks *why* frequency works — the assessment
boundary stays at observation.)

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- *The sound is inside the object, waiting to come out.* Counter with the
  still-drum strike: no shaking → almost no sound. The sound is *made* by
  the shaking, not stored.
- *Only instruments vibrate.* The throat-hum and table-tap show everyday
  vibrations; voices vibrate in the throat.
- *Seeing the shake is the same as hearing.* Keep the two linked but
  distinct: "Your eyes found the shake; your ears found the sound. They
  happen together."
