# Lesson 03 — Sound Can Make Things Move

**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** drum (or pot + wooden spoon,
adult strikes), **dry rice opened and handled by the adult only** (small
bowl; rice stays on the drum head, never near the mouth), journal,
crayons, the Lesson 02 rubber-band box for the closing demo

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will see something amazing: a
sound is strong enough to make tiny things jump. Louder sounds make them
jump higher.

**Readiness check:** "Remember yesterday's rule?" (Learner: "Shaking
makes sound.") "Today we flip it around. Can a *sound* make something
*shake*? Let's test."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Yesterday: shaking → sound. Today: sound → shaking. The vibration does
not stop at the thing that made it — it **travels** to other things and
can move them.

**Worked example 1 — rice on the drum:**

> Adult (sprinkles a few grains of rice on the drum head): "The rice is
> sitting still. Watch it while I strike the drum softly." *(Soft tap —
> the rice trembles slightly.)* "It wiggled! Now a **loud** strike."
> *(Loud strike — grains hop.)* "The rice jumped! The sound's shaking
> traveled through the air and the drum skin and pushed the rice."

Adult repeats soft/loud twice more so the learner sees the pattern, then
says the rule plainly.

**Worked example 2 — comparing amounts of movement:**

> Adult: "Scientists don't just say 'it moved' — they **compare**. Soft
> strike: the rice *trembled* (adult draws a tiny wiggle in the journal).
> Loud strike: the rice *hopped* (adult draws a big jump arrow). So:
> **louder sound → more movement.** We tested it three times and got the
> same answer each time — that makes our evidence stronger."

Key idea to say plainly: **Sound can make things move, and louder sounds
move things more.**

**Safety note (adult states, learner repeats):** "Rice is for the drum,
not for mouths. I keep it on the drum." The adult opens the container,
sprinkles, and reseals it. If any grain leaves the drum, the adult picks
it up — the learner does not put anything small near their mouth.

## Guided practice — soft vs. loud trials

The learner directs the adult's strikes ("soft!" / "loud!") while watching
the rice, then makes a **tally**: for 3 soft strikes and 3 loud strikes,
the learner marks whether the rice *trembled* or *hopped*. Adult scribes
the tally chart:

| Strike | Rice did |
|---|---|
| soft 1, 2, 3 | trembled / trembled / trembled |
| loud 1, 2, 3 | hopped / hopped / hopped |

Learner states the conclusion: "Loud strikes made the rice hop."

## Independent practice

Learner tests **one new sound-maker** for the same effect: the adult holds
a piece of paper near (not touching) the learner's humming mouth — "Can
you make the paper wiggle with a loud hum? A soft hum?" The learner tries
both and reports which moved the paper more. (Alternate if humming is
hard: clap near a dangling strip of paper.)

## Application

"A baby is sleeping in the next room. You drop a pan with a huge CLANG.
What might happen to the baby — and what does that tell you about sound?"
(Learner should connect: the loud sound could startle/wake the baby —
sound's shaking reached across the room and moved something. Accept any
answer that links loud sound traveling to an effect at a distance.)

## Exit check

Learner answers two questions: "What did the rice do on a loud strike?"
(hopped/jumped) and "What does a louder sound do?" (moves things more).

## Supports

- **Language access:** tremble = tiny wiggle-line drawing; hop = big jump
  arrow. The learner can answer entirely in drawings.
- **Alternate response mode:** the adult strikes; the learner points to
  the "tremble card" or "hop card" after each strike.
- **If rice feels unsafe for this learner:** use small torn paper bits on
  the drum instead — same effect, no choking-size pieces.

## Extension

The learner predicts *before* each strike: "I predict a loud strike will
make the rice hop." After three correct predictions in a row, the adult
names what the learner did: "You used evidence from our tests to predict
— that is what scientists do."

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- *The drum moved the rice, not the sound.* Gentle redirect: "What if I
  press the drum skin down hard with my hand and tap the side — no boom,
  no hop? The *sound* (the shaking) is what reached the rice." Keep it at
  observation level; no wave vocabulary.
- *Bigger rice movement means a "better" sound.* Louder is not better —
  it is just more movement. (This protects ears later: loud is powerful,
  and powerful needs care.)
- *One trial proves it.* The tally chart exists to show the pattern across
  repeated trials; praise "we tested it three times."
