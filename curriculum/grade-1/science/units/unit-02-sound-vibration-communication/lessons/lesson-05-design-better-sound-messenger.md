# Lesson 05 — Design a Better Sound Messenger

**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** journal and crayons (for the
design sketch), cup-and-string telephone from Lesson 04, plus loose parts:
extra cups, string, a bell, the sealed shaker, two sticks to tap

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you are an engineer. You will name
a communication problem, **sketch** your own device to solve it, and test
it to see if it works.

**Readiness check:** "What problem did our telephone solve yesterday?"
(Learner: "My voice couldn't reach across the room." Ready — the problem
comes before the device, always.)

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Engineers solve problems in order: **problem → plan (sketch) → build →
test → compare → improve.** Yesterday we built someone else's design. Today
you design your own.

**Worked example 1 — naming the problem and sketching:**

> Adult: "Our problem: I need to send the message *'come here'* from the
> kitchen to the bedroom — too far to whisper, and I don't want to shout.
> First I **sketch** — a quick drawing of my idea — before touching any
> materials." *(Adult draws: a bell on the kitchen table with an arrow to
> the bedroom, labeled by the adult: "ring bell = come here.")* "My plan:
> one ring means 'come here.' The bell's **shape** — small and easy to
> shake — helps it do its job: make a sound I can hear from far away."

**Worked example 2 — testing and comparing like an engineer:**

> Adult (tests the bell from the next room — learner listens): "Test 1:
> did the message arrive? Yes — I heard it. Now test 2: the shaker."
> *(Shakes it.)* "Also arrived. Now I **compare**: the bell is louder, so
> it works from farther away; the shaker is softer, better for close up.
> **The bell wins for this problem** because our problem is *distance*."
> (Adult scribes a two-row compare chart: bell ✓ far / shaker ~ near.)

Key idea to say plainly: **Engineers sketch first, then build, then test
two ideas and pick the one that solves the problem best.**

**Ear safety (adult states):** "Engineers protect their ears. We test with
*one* ring or *one* shake, never right next to someone's ear."

## Guided practice — your sketch

The learner picks a communication problem from two choices (adult reads
both):

1. "Tell someone across the room it is cleanup time — without shouting."
2. "Send a secret 'yes / no' message to a friend during quiet reading time."

The learner **sketches** their device idea in the journal (drawing +
adult-scribed labels). The adult asks the engineering question: "How does
the **shape** of your device help it do its job?" (K-2-ETS1-2 — e.g., "the
cup is open like a funnel to catch my voice," "the bell is small so I can
shake it fast").

## Independent practice

Learner **builds** the sketched device from the loose parts and runs
**one test**: does the message arrive? Adult scribes the result as
**yes / not yet** plus one observed detail ("the bell was heard from the
hallway").

## Application

"Your first design didn't work — the message didn't arrive. What is the
*engineer* thing to do: quit, or change one thing and test again?" (Change
one thing and retest. The adult celebrates a failed test as data: "Now we
know that design needs a louder sound.")

## Exit check

Learner shows their sketch and says: "My problem was ___. My device is
___. My test showed ___." All three blanks filled — oral is fine.

## Supports

- **Language access:** a four-picture engineering strip (problem →
  sketch → build → test) the learner points through while explaining.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner draws only; the adult scribes
  every label and the yes/not-yet result.
- **If building frustrates:** the adult builds *exactly* what the sketch
  shows while the learner directs ("put the string here") — the design
  stays the learner's.

## Extension

"Two-design showdown": the learner builds a *second* device for the same
problem and runs the K-2-ETS1-3 comparison: which solved the problem
better, and what is the evidence? (Stronger learners can test at two
distances and tally yes/not-yet per distance — this is the project's core
and can start here.)

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- *The fanciest device wins.* Redirect to the problem: "Fancy doesn't
  matter — *did the message arrive?* The winner is the one that solves the
  problem."
- *Sketching is just drawing time.* The sketch is the plan: build must
  follow the sketch (or the learner must say what changed and why).
- *One test is enough.* Engineers retest; the project requires two designs
  compared, and Session 14's redesign exists for exactly this.
