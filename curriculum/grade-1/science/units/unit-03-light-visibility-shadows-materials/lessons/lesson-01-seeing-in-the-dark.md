# Lesson 01 — Seeing in the Dark

**Student goal:** I can show that we see something only when light shines
on it.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- The dark box (shoebox with lid, small peep hole, adult-built) with 3
  familiar objects inside: a toy animal, a crayon, a small ball
- One flashlight (adult holds it or the learner holds it, pointed into the box)
- Science journal, crayons

**Safety note for the adult:** the flashlight beam points into the box or
at the wall — never at anyone's eyes. The learner never looks at the sun
in this unit; the dark box is the indoor, safe version of "a completely
dark room." If the learner is uneasy in darkness, keep the room lights
dimmed rather than off and the box lid ajar.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Close your eyes. What can you see?" (Nothing, or darkness.) "Open them.
What can you see now?" (The room, the adult, the table.) "What changed?
Light reached your eyes. Today we test a big idea: can we see something
in the dark with no light at all?" If the learner can follow "close/open
your eyes" and name one thing they see, begin.

## Teaching

**Dark** means no light is reaching our eyes from that place. **Light**
is what lets us see things. To **illuminate** something means to shine
light on it.

Here is the rule we test today:

> **We see an object only when light shines on it and then reaches our
> eyes.**

A **light source** is something that makes its own light: the sun, a lamp,
a flashlight, a candle flame (adult-only). Most things are NOT light
sources. A toy, a crayon, a ball — they do not make light. We see them
only when light from a source shines on them and bounces to our eyes.

That is why a cave explorer carries a flashlight, and why a bedroom looks
different with the lamp on and the lamp off. The toys do not change. The
light changes.

## Worked example 1 — the toy animal in the dark box (modeled)

The adult puts the toy animal in the dark box, closes the lid, and asks
the learner to peep through the hole with the room lights dimmed.

> "What do you see in the box?" (Nothing — darkness.)
>
> "Is the toy gone? No. It is still in there. We just cannot see it,
> because no light is shining on it."
>
> Now the adult shines the flashlight into the box. "What do you see
> NOW?" (The toy animal!) "The toy did not move. The light changed.
> Light on the toy → we see the toy. No light on the toy → we do not
> see it."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we did not say "it is dark so the toy
disappeared." The toy stayed. Seeing needs light on the object first.

## Worked example 2 — the crayon, predicted first (modeled)

Before opening the box again, the adult asks: "I will put a crayon in the
box and close the lid. Will you see it?"

> "No — because no light will shine on it." (If the learner says yes,
> that is fine: we test it.)
>
> The adult closes the lid. The learner peeps: darkness. The adult shines
> the flashlight in: the crayon appears. "You predicted it. No light, no
> seeing. Light, seeing. That is the rule."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a good scientist predicts BEFORE looking,
then checks. The prediction is not graded — the checking is the science.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Ball round: the adult puts the ball in the box, lid closed. "Do you
   see the ball?" *(No — darkness.)* Shine the light in. "Now?" *(Yes.)*
   "What changed — the ball or the light?" *(The light.)*
2. Two-object round: toy animal AND crayon in the box. Light off: "How
   many things do you see?" *(Zero.)* Light on: "How many now?" *(Two.)*
   "Did I add toys while the lid was closed? No. The light let you see
   what was already there."*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "I think the
   toys hide when it is dark, and come back when the light is on."
   Ask: "Is that right? Or do the toys stay, and only the light
   changes?" *(Expected: the toys stay; only the light changes. Hiding
   would mean the toys move — they do not.)*
4. Light-source sort: hold up the flashlight (on) and the ball. "Which
   one MAKES its own light?" *(The flashlight.)* "Which one needs light
   shone on it?" *(The ball.)*
5. Room round: "Name one light source in this room." *(A lamp, a window
   with sun, a screen — accept any true source.)* "Name one thing that
   is not a light source." *(Table, book, toy — anything that only
   reflects light.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: we see something
   only when ___." *(Light shines on it.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws two boxes in the journal: the dark box with the lid
closed (drawn dark, nothing visible) and the dark box with the flashlight
on (the toy visible). The adult labels each drawing with the dictated
words ("no light — cannot see"; "light — can see"). The adult sits nearby
and observes: does the second drawing show the object that the first
drawing hides? When finished, the adult asks, "Why can you see the toy in
the second picture?" and scribes the answer for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner points to the "cannot see" card
and the "can see" card in the right order while the adult acts out
lid-closed / flashlight-on, or sorts two picture cards (dark box / lit box).

## Application

"Tonight, notice one moment when a light turns on or off at home — a
lamp, a hallway light. What could you see after the light came on that
you could not see before?" (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports
back at the next session.) The learner practices the rule in a real setting.

## Exit check

The adult puts a new object (the spoon) in the dark box, lid closed, and
asks: "Can you see it? Why not?" Then shines the light in: "Can you see
it now? Why?" The learner should answer both "why" questions with the
light rule (words, pointing, or mime). The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "Is the sun a light source?"
(Any correct yes counts.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "dark" and "light" by experience first, word
  second. Dim the room before saying "dark." Accept home-language words
  alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, mime (covering eyes for "cannot
  see"), sorting cards, drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** sit on the floor with the box between adult and
  learner; the learner holds the flashlight and controls the light.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the box
  and journal.

## Extension

"Do you think a cat can see in the dark? What about an owl?" (Preview of
observation: some animals see better in dim light than people do, but
even they need some light — total darkness means no seeing for anyone.
Accept any sensible answer; the unit returns to this in the review week.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "things disappear in the dark" — the
two-object round shows the objects were there all along; (2) "our eyes
make the light" — the room round shows light comes from sources, and
closing your eyes in a lit room still blocks seeing; (3) "the moon is a
light source like the sun" — at grade 1, accept "the moon shines at
night" and note for the adult only that moonlight is reflected sunlight;
the unit does not test this.

the expected responses for the error-analysis round and exit check.
