# Lesson 02 — What Materials Do to Light

**Student goal:** I can test materials in a light beam and name what each
one does: lets light through, lets some through, blocks it, or bounces it.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**

## Materials

- One flashlight, a white wall or large white paper screen
- Four framed panels, each in a cardboard frame the adult made: clear
  plastic (cling wrap stretched flat), wax paper, cardboard, and a small
  hand mirror taped to a stand
- The generated illustration
  [`assets/light-beam-materials.png`](../assets/light-beam-materials.png)
  (printed or on a screen) plus its text alternative in
  [`assets/README.md`](../assets/README.md#text-only-alternative)
- Science journal, crayons

**Safety note for the adult:** the flashlight beam points at the panels
and the wall — never at eyes. The mirror is fixed to its stand; the
learner never waves it around. This lesson tests one material at a time:
the picture shows all three panels side by side only so we can compare
their results, not as a setup to copy.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Yesterday we learned: we see something only when light shines on it.
Today: what happens when light hits different stuff? Point to the window
(or a lamp). Does light come through it? What about this book — does
light come through a book?" (Window: yes; book: no.) If the learner can
answer one "does light come through it" question, begin.

## Teaching

A **beam** is a straight stream of light, like from a flashlight. When a
beam hits a material, four things can happen. Each has a word:

1. **Transparent** — lets almost all the light through. You can see
   clearly through it. Example: clear plastic, a clean window.
2. **Translucent** — lets some light through, but blurry. You cannot see
   clearly through it. Example: wax paper, frosty glass.
3. **Opaque** — lets no light through. It blocks the light and makes a
   **shadow** — a dark shape on the wall behind it. Example: cardboard,
   a book, your hand.
4. **Reflective** — bounces light back, like a mirror. The light does not
   go through; it turns around and goes somewhere else.

The test is always the same, so it is fair: same flashlight, same
distance, same white wall. Only the material changes. That is a **fair
test**.

## Worked example 1 — clear plastic, step by step (modeled)

The adult holds the clear-plastic frame between the flashlight and the
wall.

> "Flashlight on. Look at the wall. What do you see? A bright round
> spot — almost as bright as with nothing in the way. Did the light go
> through? Yes, almost all of it. So clear plastic is **transparent**."
>
> "Can you see the wall through it? Yes, clearly. Bright spot +
> see-through = transparent."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we judge by the wall, not by looking at the
plastic. The wall tells us how much light got through.

## Worked example 2 — cardboard, and a wrong-guess check (modeled)

The adult holds the cardboard frame in the beam.

> "Flashlight on. Look at the wall. What do you see? A dark shape — a
> shadow shaped like the cardboard. Did any light get through? No. So
> cardboard is **opaque**: it blocks light and makes a shadow."
>
> "Now — would cardboard be transparent, like the plastic? No! If it
> were transparent, the wall would show a bright spot. It shows a dark
> shadow instead. The wall result decides the word."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the word matches the result. Bright spot =
transparent. Dark shadow = opaque. We do not guess from how the material
looks; we test it.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Wax-paper round: the adult holds wax paper in the beam. "What do you
   see on the wall — a bright spot, a dim glow, or a dark shadow?"
   *(A dim glow.)* "Does some light get through? Yes, but blurry. So wax
   paper is…?" *(Translucent.)*
2. Mirror round: the adult holds the mirror in the beam, angled so the
   bounced spot lands on the table or a side wall. "Did the light go
   through the mirror? No. Where did it go? It bounced!" *(Reflective.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "Wax paper is
   opaque, because you cannot see clearly through it." Ask: "Is that
   right?" *(Expected: no. Opaque means NO light gets through and a dark
   shadow appears. Wax paper lets a dim glow through, so it is
   translucent — "cannot see clearly" is not the same as "no light.")*
4. Picture round: show the generated illustration. "Find the clear
   plastic panel. What is on the wall behind it?" *(A bright spot.)*
   "Find the wax paper. What is behind it?" *(A dim glow.)* "Find the
   cardboard. What is behind it?" *(A dark shadow.)* (Learners who cannot
   use the picture use the text alternative — same questions, same answers.)
5. Sorting round: the adult names four new items — a window, a towel, a
   sheet of paper, a spoon. "Which word for each: transparent,
   translucent, opaque, reflective? You may say 'not sure' and we test
   it later." *(Window → transparent; towel → opaque; paper → translucent
   or opaque depending on thickness — accept either with the wall test
   as the decider; spoon → reflective.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: opaque means light
   ___." *(Cannot get through; it is blocked and makes a shadow.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws the four beam-test results in the journal: four small
wall squares labeled (by the adult, from dictation) "clear plastic,"
"wax paper," "cardboard," "mirror," each with the matching wall result
drawn inside (bright spot / dim glow / dark shadow / bounced spot with
an arrow). The adult sits nearby and observes: does each wall result
match its material? When finished, the adult asks, "Which material let
the most light through?" and scribes the answer for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner points to the four framed panels
in order of "most light through" to "least light through," or sorts four
result cards (bright spot / dim glow / dark shadow / bounced light) under
the matching panel.

## Application

"Find one transparent thing and one opaque thing at home. Tomorrow, tell
me what they are and how you knew — did light go through, or did it make
a shadow?" (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back at the next
session.) The learner practices the beam-test idea without a flashlight,
using window light.

## Exit check

The adult holds up each panel in turn and asks: "What word? What will
the wall show?" The learner should name the word and the wall result for
at least three of the four panels (words, pointing, or mime). The adult
records: secure / developing / not yet. A second probe: "Which material
makes a shadow?" (Any opaque item counts.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach each word by result first, word second.
  Show the bright spot, then say "transparent." Accept home-language
  words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, sorting result cards, drawing
  wall results.
- **Floor-play variant:** panels stand on the floor in a row; the learner
  walks the flashlight along and "reads" each wall result.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with a small
  white paper screen taped to a book.

## Extension

"Can you find a material that is BOTH reflective and something else — a
shiny spoon is reflective, but is it also opaque?" (Yes: it blocks light
AND bounces it. Words can overlap; the wall test plus the bounce test
together decide. Accept any sensible answer.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "translucent means you cannot see
through it at all" — the dim glow proves some light passes; (2) "a
shadow is a thing, like a dark object" — a shadow is blocked light, not a
thing; it vanishes when the light moves; (3) "reflective things let light
through" — the mirror round shows light bouncing, not passing.
**Safety:** re-check the mirror is fixed before the mirror round.
