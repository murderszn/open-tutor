# Lesson 03 — Shadows: Shape and Size

**Student goal:** I can explain what a shadow needs, what shape it shows,
and how it grows and shrinks.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- One flashlight, a white wall or large white paper screen
- Paper shapes on craft sticks: a star, a tree, an animal (shadow puppets)
- A ruler or paper strip; the learner measures in "hand-widths" (hands
  laid side by side) — no formal units at this grade
- Science journal, crayons

**Safety note for the adult:** the flashlight beam points at the wall —
never at eyes. Outdoor shadow work is optional: the learner's own shadow
on the ground, with the adult, never looking at the sun. The indoor
version below teaches everything the outdoor version does.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Yesterday: cardboard in the beam made what on the wall?" (A dark shadow.)
"What two things did the shadow need?" (Light and something blocking it.)
If the learner can name the shadow and one of its two needs, begin.

## Teaching

A **shadow** is the dark shape where an object blocks light. A shadow
needs exactly two things:

1. A **light source** (flashlight, lamp, sun).
2. An **object** in the light's way (opaque — it blocks the light).

No light, no shadow. No object, no shadow. The shadow **shows the
object's shape**: a star shape makes a star shadow; a tree shape makes a
tree shadow.

And shadows change size. When the object moves **toward the light**, its
shadow **grows bigger**. When the object moves **away from the light**
(toward the wall), its shadow **shrinks smaller**. The object did not
change size. The distance changed — and the shadow followed.

## Worked example 1 — the star shadow, step by step (modeled)

The adult holds the star puppet between the flashlight and the wall.

> "Flashlight on. What do you see on the wall? A star! A dark star
> shape. What made it? The star puppet blocked the light. Light +
> object = shadow, and the shadow shows the star's shape."
>
> "Now I move the star closer to the flashlight. Watch the wall. The
> shadow… grows! Bigger! The star did not grow. It moved closer to the
> light, so its shadow grew."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we changed ONE thing — the distance — and
watched the shadow. That is how we know distance caused the size change.

## Worked example 2 — the tree shadow, and a wrong-rule check (modeled)

The adult holds the tree puppet close to the wall, then moves it toward
the flashlight.

> "Tree near the wall: small shadow. Tree near the light: big shadow.
> Closer to light = bigger shadow. Farther from light = smaller shadow."
>
> "Now — someone says: 'Shadows grow when the object grows.' Is that
> right here? No! Our tree never changed size. Only its distance changed.
> Distance, not object size, made this shadow grow."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a rule must match what we actually changed.
We changed distance, so distance is the cause in this test.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Animal round: the adult holds the animal puppet mid-way. "What shape
   is the shadow?" *(The animal's shape.)* "What two things made it?"
   *(Flashlight light + the puppet blocking it.)*
2. Near/far round: the learner moves the star puppet while the adult
   holds the flashlight still. "Move it close to the light. Bigger or
   smaller?" *(Bigger.)* "Move it close to the wall. Now?" *(Smaller.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "If I turn the
   flashlight off, the shadow will stay on the wall, because shadows are
   dark paint." Ask: "Is that right?" *(Expected: no. Turn the light off
   and the shadow vanishes — a shadow is blocked light, not paint. Test
   it: lights off, wall empty.)*
4. Prediction round: the adult holds the tree puppet very close to the
   flashlight. "Before I turn it on — big shadow or small shadow?"
   *(Big.)* Turn it on and check. "You predicted from the rule: close to
   light = big shadow."
5. Two-shadow round: two puppets at different distances, one flashlight.
   "Which shadow is bigger? Why?" *(The puppet closer to the light — its
   shadow is bigger.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: a shadow needs light
   and ___." *(An object blocking the light.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws two wall squares in the journal: the star puppet close
to the light (big star shadow) and the star puppet close to the wall
(small star shadow), with arrows showing "close to light" and "close to
wall" (adult labels from dictation). The adult sits nearby and observes:
is the close-to-light shadow drawn bigger? When finished, the adult asks,
"What made the shadow bigger?" and scribes the answer for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner holds the puppet at two distances
while the adult traces or photographs nothing — the learner simply points
to the bigger shadow and then to the puppet position that made it, or
sorts two shadow cards (big / small) under "near light" / "near wall" labels.

## Application

"Find a shadow at home — from a lamp and a toy, or your own shadow
outside with a grown-up (never look at the sun). Is it big or small? Can
you make it bigger by moving closer to the light?" (In a one-on-one
setting, the learner reports back at the next session.)

## Exit check

The adult holds the animal puppet at an untested distance and asks:
"Big shadow or small shadow — and how do you know?" Then: "What two
things does a shadow need?" The learner should answer the size question
with the distance rule and name both shadow needs (words, pointing, or
mime). The adult records: secure / developing / not yet. A second probe:
"What shape will this puppet's shadow be?" (Its own shape counts.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "bigger/smaller" with hands held wide/narrow
  before saying the words. Accept home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, moving the puppet, sorting
  shadow cards, drawing.
- **Floor-play variant:** the learner walks the puppet toward and away
  from the flashlight on the floor while the adult narrates the wall.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with a small
  white paper screen.

## Extension

"Why is your shadow outside longer in the morning than at noon?" (The
sun is lower in the morning, so the light comes at a slant and stretches
the shadow. Accept any sensible answer; the unit does not test sun
angles — this previews Unit 06's sun observations.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "shadows are things that stay" — the
lights-off test kills this; (2) "bigger object = bigger shadow, always" —
distance matters as much as size; at grade 1, hold object size constant
and vary distance only; (3) "a shadow needs darkness" — reversed: a
shadow needs light plus a blocker. **Safety:** re-check the beam never
points at eyes during the near/far round.
