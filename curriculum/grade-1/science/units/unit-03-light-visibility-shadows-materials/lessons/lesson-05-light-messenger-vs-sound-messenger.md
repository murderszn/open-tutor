# Lesson 05 — Light Messenger vs. Sound Messenger

**Student goal:** I can test two messengers the same way and say which
works better, where, and why.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 13 of 16**

## Materials

- The flashlight messenger (built in Session 12) and its written code list
- The Unit 02 sound device: cup-string telephone (rebuilt if not kept) or
  the tap-code setup
- A comparison table in the journal (adult-drawn): rows = light messenger
  / sound messenger; columns = "message got through?" / "where it fails"
- A noisy condition (adult claps or plays music softly) and a bright
  condition (room lights fully on) for fair comparison

**Safety note for the adult:** flashlight beams point at the wall — never
at eyes. The string telephone's string is adult-handled and never goes
around a neck (Unit 02 rule). Keep the noise condition gentle — no
shouting, no banging near ears.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"We built two messengers: light flashes and a sound telephone. Which one
do you think works better? Why?" (Any reasoned guess counts — "light,
because it is fast," "sound, because you can hear it in the dark.")
"Today we do not guess. We TEST both the same way and compare." If the
learner can name both devices, begin.

## Teaching

To **compare** is to tell how two things are the same and different. A
fair comparison tests both devices on the SAME job: the same message,
the same distance, the same room. Only one thing changes at a time — the
device.

We test three conditions:

1. **Quiet room, lights dim** — the easy condition. Both should work.
2. **Noisy room** — clapping/music while sending. Which messenger still
   gets through?
3. **Bright room, lights fully on** — which messenger still gets through?

Then we judge: a messenger is "better" only for a condition. Light wins
where it is quiet and visible; sound wins where it is bright or the
receiver is not looking at the wall. Neither wins everywhere. That is
what **compare strengths and weaknesses** means.

## Worked example 1 — the easy condition, both devices (modeled)

The adult sends "come here" (2 flashes) across the room; the learner
receives. Then the adult sends "come here" through the cup telephone;
the learner receives.

> "Light message: got through? Yes. Sound message: got through? Yes.
> In the easy condition, both work. Same message, same distance — both
> delivered. That is our baseline: the devices are both real messengers."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we tested both before judging. A comparison
without a baseline is just an opinion.

## Worked example 2 — the noisy room, and a wrong-conclusion check (modeled)

The adult claps steadily (gentle) while sending. Light first: 2 flashes
on the wall — the learner reads "come here." Sound second: the cup
telephone message, drowned by clapping — the learner cannot tell the
message.

> "Light: got through, even with noise. Sound: did NOT get through in
> noise. So in a noisy room, the light messenger is better."
>
> "Now — someone says: 'Light is better, period. Sound is useless.' Is
> that right? No! We only tested noise. What about a bright room, or a
> receiver who is not looking at the wall? A fair conclusion names the
> condition: light is better IN NOISE. We still have to test brightness."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a comparison result always carries its
condition. "Better" without "where" is an overclaim — the exact error
this lesson trains against.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Bright-room round: room lights fully on; send "yes" by light (flashes
   still visible on the wall? usually yes but weaker) and by sound.
   Record both results in the table. *(Expected: sound clearly through;
   light through but harder to see — discuss honestly.)*
2. Not-looking round: the receiver faces away from the wall; send by
   light (missed — the receiver never saw it) and by sound (heard).
   *(Expected: sound wins when the receiver is not looking.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "The light
   messenger won in noise, so it is the best messenger for every place."
   Ask: "Is that right?" *(Expected: no. It lost (or struggled) when the
   receiver was not looking and in bright light. Best depends on the
   condition.)*
4. Table round: the learner fills the comparison table with the adult:
   light — wins in noise, struggles when receiver is not looking; sound
   — wins when receiver is not looking, struggles in noise. "Which
   messenger for a noisy playground? Which for a dark bedroom where
   someone is facing away?" *(Playground: light, if watched; bedroom
   facing away: sound.)*
5. Improvement round: "How could we make the light messenger better for
   a receiver who is not looking?" *(Add a sound signal too — a flash
   AND a tap; or a bigger wall. Any sensible improvement counts.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: the light messenger
   is better when ___." *(It is noisy and the receiver is watching the
   wall.)*

## Independent practice

The learner completes the comparison table in the journal (adult scribes
words; learner draws tick/cross marks): 2 devices × 3 conditions, with
one "winner" star per condition. The adult sits nearby and observes:
does each mark match what the tests actually showed? When finished, the
adult asks, "Which messenger would you pick for a noisy room, and why?"
and scribes the answer for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner sorts condition cards (noisy /
bright / not-looking) under "light wins" or "sound wins" labels, or points
to the winning device for each condition.

## Application

"At home, test one condition with a grown-up: send a flashlight message
in a bright room and in a dim room. Which room was easier? Tell me
tomorrow." (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back at the next
session.)

## Exit check

The adult names a condition ("a noisy room," "a receiver facing away")
and asks: "Which messenger wins here, and why?" The learner should answer
both conditions correctly with a test-based reason (words, pointing, or
mime). The adult records: secure / developing / not yet. A second probe:
"Is one messenger best everywhere?" (No — it depends on the condition.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "compare" with two real objects first (two
  cups: same/different). Condition cards carry pictures (ear with noise
  lines, sun for bright, turned head for not-looking). Accept home-language
  words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to the winning device, sorting
  condition cards, drawing ticks/crosses.
- **Floor-play variant:** devices tested across the floor; the learner
  crawls to the "winner" card for each condition.
- **Seated-table variant:** test across the table at short distance;
  the table serves as the comparison record.

## Extension

"Could we build ONE messenger that uses light AND sound, so it wins in
both conditions?" (Yes — a flash plus a tap, or a flashlight taped to a
bell. This is real engineering: combine strengths. The learner may sketch
such a combined device as a bonus; it is not tested.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "the winner of one test wins everywhere"
— the core error; the condition table exists to prevent it; (2) "sound
failed in noise, so sound is bad" — sound failed in ONE condition, not
all; (3) "brighter flash = better message" — brightness does not change
meaning; the count does. **Safety:** keep noise gentle; re-check beam
discipline and string handling.
