# Lesson 01 — Questions Scientists Ask

**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
an apple, 5 smooth pebbles (or buttons)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn what makes a question
a *scientist's question* — one you can explore by looking, touching, or
testing.

**Readiness check:** Adult holds up the apple. "Tell me one thing you
notice about this apple." Any observation ("it's red," "it has a leaf")
shows the learner is ready. If the learner shrugs, model one observation
together first.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Scientists are question-askers. But not every question is one a scientist
can *explore* — find the answer to by looking, touching, or testing. A
question you can explore is called **testable**.

**Worked example 1 — explored out loud, slowly:**

> Adult: "Here is a question: *What does the apple's skin feel like?*
> Can I explore that? Let me think. I could **touch** it with my finger.
> Touching is one way to explore. So — yes! That is a testable question.
> Now watch: I touch it. My observation: the skin feels smooth and a
> little cool. I explored my question and got an answer."

Adult touches the apple and narrates the answer, then writes or draws it
in the journal so the learner sees recording modeled.

**Worked example 2 — a question we cannot explore:**

> Adult: "Here is another question: *Is the apple happy?* Hmm. Can I
> explore that by looking, touching, or testing? Feelings like 'happy'
> are not something my senses can find in an apple. I cannot test it —
> so it is **not testable**. That does not make it a bad question! It
> goes on my *wonder list* — questions I keep wondering about. But a
> scientist's *explorable* question is one I can look at, touch, or test."

Adult draws a two-column page in the journal: a magnifying-glass column
("I can explore this") and a cloud column ("I wonder about this"), and
places each example question in its column.

Key idea to say plainly: **A testable question is one you can explore by
looking, touching, or testing.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each question aloud; the learner points to the magnifying-glass
column or the cloud column, then says *how* they would explore it (or why
they cannot).

1. "Which pebble is the heaviest?" (testable — hold one in each hand, compare)
2. "Do the pebbles like being in a pile?" (not testable — feelings are not observable)
3. "What sound does a pebble make when I drop it in the cup of water?" (testable — drop and listen)
4. "How many pebbles fit in my hand?" (testable — count, test by trying)

For each, the adult asks: "How could you explore it — look, touch, or
test?" and accepts any reasonable method. For #2, confirm: "Right — we
can't observe a feeling, so it goes on the wonder list."

## Independent practice

Learner holds the apple and thinks of **two questions** about it. The
adult scribes them into the journal's two columns (the learner points to
which column). Then the learner picks one testable question and explores
it right away (touch, look, or test), and the adult scribes the answer
beside the question.

## Application

Start the "I notice / I wonder" journal page: the learner walks to a
window (or looks around the room) and the adult scribes one "I notice"
observation and one "I wonder" question. Explain that scientists keep both
lists every day.

## Exit check

Adult reads two new questions; learner sorts each (point to the column)
and names the sense or test they would use:

- "What color is the apple's leaf?" (testable — look)
- "Does the apple dream at night?" (not testable — wonder list)

## Supports

- **Language access:** the adult may rephrase "testable" as "a question my
  hands, eyes, or ears can answer." Sorting is by pointing — no reading
  or writing required.
- **Alternate response mode:** thumbs up/down for testable/not-testable,
  then the adult scribes.
- **Attention:** keep the sort game brisk; stand and point for each card.

## Extension

The learner invents a testable question about the pebbles, predicts the
answer before testing ("I think the big one is heaviest"), then tests and
compares prediction to result.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- *Not-testable = bad question.* Correct explicitly: wonder-list questions
  are welcome; they just need different tools than a first grader has.
  Never let a learner feel punished for asking.
- *Touching anything counts as testing.* Distinguish exploring (using
  senses/tools on purpose) from random handling; tie the test to the
  question asked.
- *One look is enough.* Model looking again and touching to check —
  scientists confirm.
