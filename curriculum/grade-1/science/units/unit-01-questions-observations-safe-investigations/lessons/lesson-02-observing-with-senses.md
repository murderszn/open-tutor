# Lesson 02 — Observing with the Senses

**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** apple, pebbles, paper cup of
water, journal, crayons, one opaque bag with a mystery object (a spoon,
a pinecone, or a sponge)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will observe like a scientist —
using your senses to notice details — and describe at least four things
you observe about one object.

**Readiness check:** "Show me with your body: which part do you see
with?" (Learner points to eyes.) "Which part do you hear with?" Continue
for nose, hands, mouth. All five identified — ready.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

To **observe** means to notice carefully. Scientists use five senses:
**sight, hearing, touch, smell,** and **taste**. In science we mostly use
the first four — **taste is only for food, and only when a grown-up says
it is safe.** (This previews Lesson 05's safety rule.)

**Worked example 1 — one object, two senses:**

> Adult (holding the apple): "I will observe this apple with my **eyes**
> first. I notice: it is red, it is round, and it has one green leaf on
> top. Now I observe with my **hands** — touch. I notice: the skin feels
> smooth, and it feels a little cool. Same apple, different observations!
> Each sense tells me something new."

Adult scribes each observation as a short phrase or quick drawing in the
journal, labeling which sense found it.

**Worked example 2 — a sense you might forget:**

> Adult (dropping one pebble into the cup of water): "Listen. *Plip!* My
> **ears** observed a sound — a small splashy plip. Now I use my eyes: I
> see tiny circles — ripples — moving on the water. Hearing found
> something my eyes could not, and my eyes found something my ears could
> not."

Key idea to say plainly: **Each sense can notice different things, so
scientists use more than one.**

**Smell note:** the adult models smelling the apple from a short distance
("It smells sweet and fresh") and states the rule: *we smell by wafting —
we never put our nose in the thing.*

## Guided practice — the mystery bag

The adult holds the opaque bag closed. The learner may **not** look
inside yet.

1. **Touch:** learner reaches in, feels, describes 2 things ("bumpy,"
   "hard"). Adult scribes.
2. **Shake gently near the ear:** learner describes 1 sound ("it rattles
   / it's quiet").
3. Learner **guesses** what it is, then looks to check. Adult asks: "Were
   your touch observations right? What did your eyes add?"

## Independent practice

Learner chooses the apple or a pebble and makes **four observations**
using at least two senses. The adult scribes words; the learner draws one
observation. Sentence starter (adult reads): "I observe with my ___ that
___."

## Application

"Which sense would you use to find out if the seedling's soil is dry?"
(Learner should pick touch — feel the soil — with the adult confirming
that looking can help too, but touching answers it. Accept look + touch
as a strong answer; the point is choosing a sense on purpose.)

## Exit check

Learner names **two senses** and gives **one observation from each**
about any object in the room. Oral or pointing is fine.

## Supports

- **Language access:** name each sense with a gesture (point to eyes,
  cup a hand to ear, wiggle fingers, tap nose). Let the learner answer
  with the gesture + one word.
- **Alternate response mode:** observation cards — learner points to
  "smooth / bumpy / loud / quiet / sweet-smell" picture cards the adult
  prepares.
- **Sensory sensitivity:** if touch or smell is uncomfortable, the learner
  may observe with sight and hearing only; note it in the teacher guide.

## Extension

The learner observes the same object with a "scientist's second look":
find **one new detail** the first look missed (a tiny brown dot on the
apple, a stripe on a pebble). Discuss why scientists look more than once.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- *Opinions are observations.* "It is pretty" is a feeling, not an
  observation. Redirect: "What do your eyes notice that makes you say
  pretty?" (color, shape).
- *Tasting to explore.* Reinforce every session: nothing goes in the
  mouth during science unless the grown-up names it as safe food.
- *One sense is enough.* Keep asking, "What would your ears/hands add?"
  until multi-sense observing becomes the habit.
