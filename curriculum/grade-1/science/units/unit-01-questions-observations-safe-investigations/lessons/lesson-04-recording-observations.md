# Lesson 04 — Recording What We Observe

**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Materials:** journal, pencil/crayons, printed
or on-screen copy of the observation image below, 5 pebbles

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will record observations three
ways — with words, drawings, and tallies — so you can remember and share
what you found.

**Readiness check:** Learner gives two observations of the apple from
memory ("red," "smooth"). The adult asks: "If I asked you tomorrow what
you observed, would you remember? How could we *save* it?" Any idea
about writing/drawing shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

To **record** means to save what you observed. Scientists record so they
do not forget, and so they can share findings. Three ways to record:

- **Words** — short phrases ("red apple," "smooth skin")
- **Drawings** — quick sketches that show shape and detail
- **Tallies** — marks for counting: one mark per thing (⁄ ⁄ ⁄)

**Worked example 1 — recording from a picture:**

Study this scientist's observation table together:

*Caption: An AI-generated illustration of a scientist's observation table.
Use it to practice recording observations — it is a practice picture, not
a photograph of a real experiment.*

> Adult (modeling, pointing at the picture): "I will record **five**
> observations. One — words: 'blue hand lens.' Two — words: 'yellow
> flashlight.' Three — drawing: I sketch the red apple quickly. Four —
> drawing: I sketch the seedling's three leaves. Five — tally: I see gray
> pebbles — one, two, three, four, five — so I make five tally marks:
> ⁄ ⁄ ⁄ ⁄ ⁄. Words, drawings, and tallies — all three, on one journal
> page."

**Text-only alternative** (same information, no picture needed): *The table
holds: a blue hand lens; an open notebook with a pencil; a potted green
seedling with three leaves; a yellow flashlight; a wooden ruler; five
smooth gray pebbles; one red apple with a green leaf; one paper cup of
blue water.*

**Worked example 2 — why tallies beat memory:**

> Adult: "Yesterday I saw pebbles and thought 'there were some.' Today I
> look at my tally — ⁄ ⁄ ⁄ ⁄ ⁄ — and I know exactly: **five**. Recording
> turns 'some' into a number I can trust and share."

Key idea to say plainly: **Record right away — memory fades, but your
journal keeps the observation.**

## Guided practice — record from the picture

The learner studies the observation image and records **five observations**
in the journal, using at least two of the three ways (words, drawings,
tallies). The adult scribes words on request; drawings and tallies are
the learner's own marks. Prompt: "Find something blue. Find something you
could measure. Find the living thing."

## Independent practice

Learner picks a real object (apple, pebble, seedling) and fills one dated
journal page: the date (adult writes), **4–6 recorded items** in any mix
of words/drawings/tallies, and one "I wonder" question at the bottom.

## Application

"Your friend wants to know how many pebbles are on the table but cannot
see the picture. What would you send them — your memory, or your tally?
Why?" (Strong answer: the tally, because it is exact and the friend can
count it too — recorded evidence can be shared.)

## Exit check

Learner shows the journal page and **tells one observation** from it
("I recorded that the apple is red — here is my drawing"). The adult
checks: is there a record the learner can point to and explain?

## Supports

- **Language access:** the adult reads the text-only alternative aloud
  while the learner follows the picture with a finger.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner points to objects in the picture
  while the adult scribes the words; learner makes only the tally marks.
- **Vision:** use the text-only alternative as the primary source; the
  learner records from the read-aloud list.

## Extension

The learner records the *same* five pebbles twice — once with tallies,
once with a drawing — and decides which record answers "how many?" faster
and which answers "what do they look like?" better. Discuss: different
records are good at different jobs.

## Teacher note — misconceptions

- *Drawing must be pretty.* Correct: a scientist's drawing is quick and
  shows the important parts. Praise labeled, informative sketches over
  decorative ones.
- *Words are the only "real" recording.* Affirm drawings and tallies as
  real scientific records; many scientists sketch first.
- *Recording can wait.* Build the habit now: observe → record immediately.
