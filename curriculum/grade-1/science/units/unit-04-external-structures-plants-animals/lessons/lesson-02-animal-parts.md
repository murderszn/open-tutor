# Lesson 02 — Animal Parts You Can See

**Student goal:** I can name the outside parts of an animal — legs and
feet, ears, eyes, beak, fins, tail, wings — and say what each part does.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**

## Materials

- Toy animals or animal picture cards (duck, fish, rabbit, bird, turtle,
  cat or dog — at least three different animals)
- The generated illustration
  [`assets/plant-animal-parts.png`](../assets/plant-animal-parts.png)
  plus its text alternative in
  [`assets/README.md`](../assets/README.md#text-only-alternative)
- Science journal, crayons

**Safety note for the adult:** this lesson uses toys and pictures, never
a live animal the learner must hold. Wild animals are observed from a
distance only. A family pet may be observed gently if the adult judges
it calm and safe — never held up, poked, or chased for a lesson.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Last week we learned plant parts. Plants stay in one place — but
animals move! What parts does an animal need to move, to find food, and
to stay safe? Show me with your own body: point to your eyes, your ears,
your legs." If the learner can point to eyes, ears, or legs, begin.

## Teaching

Animals have outside parts too, and every part has a job. Different
animals have different parts, because they live in different places and
eat different foods. The jobs are the same kinds of jobs everywhere:
**see, hear, move, grasp, eat and drink, stay safe.**

- **Eyes** — for **seeing**. Eyes help an animal find food, find its
  way, and spot danger.
- **Ears** — for **hearing**. Ears help an animal hear food, friends,
  and danger coming. (Some animals' ears are easy to see, like a
  rabbit's long ears. A duck's ears are hidden under its feathers —
  they are still there, just hard to see. That is an important lesson:
  "I cannot see it" does not mean "it is not there.")
- **Legs and feet** — for **moving**. Legs walk, run, jump, and climb.
  Feet are shaped for the place: a duck's **webbed feet** are like
  paddles for swimming; a rabbit's long back feet are for hopping.
- **Beak (or mouth)** — for **getting food**. A bird's beak is shaped
  for its food: a duck's flat beak scoops, a hummingbird's long thin
  beak reaches into flowers. Other animals use mouths, teeth, or paws
  to grasp food — the job is the same, the tool is different.
- **Fins** — flat parts on a fish that help it **swim and steer** in
  water, the way a rudder steers a boat.
- **Tail** — the part at the **back** of an animal. Tails help with
  balance (a cat on a fence), steering (a fish), swimming (a duck's
  short tail), or holding on (a monkey's tail — enrichment only).
- **Wings** — the parts a bird or insect uses to **fly**.

Rule to say aloud: "Same job, different tool." Every animal needs to
move, eat, and stay safe — but a fish swims with fins where a duck
paddles with webbed feet, and a bird flies with wings where a rabbit
hops with legs.

## Worked example 1 — the duck, part by part (modeled)

The adult shows the duck in the generated illustration (or a duck toy)
and points slowly.

> "This is a duck. Find its **beak** — the flat yellow-orange part on
> its face. Its job is to scoop food from water. Find its **eyes** —
> two small dark curves. They help the duck see food and danger. Find
> its **wing** — the folded shape on its side. Wings help birds fly,
> and they also cover and warm the duck's body. Find its **tail** —
> the short pointy feathers at the back. Find its **feet** — orange,
> flat, and **webbed**, with skin between the toes, like paddles. Webbed
> feet help the duck swim."
>
> "Now you try: point to the beak… the feet… the wing… the tail. Say
> the job with each one."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we match part to place the same way as
with plants — beak on the face, feet at the bottom, tail at the back —
and then we ask, "What does this part help the duck do?"

## Worked example 2 — the fish, and a wrong-guess check (modeled)

Show the fish in the pond at the bottom of the illustration.

> "This fish lives in water. It has **fins** — the flat orange parts on
> its side and back — and a **tail** fin at the back. Fins help it swim
> and steer. It has an **eye** to see. It does **not** have legs, wings,
> or a beak — it does not need them in water."
>
> "Now a trick question. Someone says: 'The fish should walk to its
> food with legs, like the duck.' Is that right? No! Legs would not
> help in water — fins do the moving job in water, the way legs do the
> moving job on land. The **job** (moving) is the same; the **tool**
> (fins vs. legs) fits the place."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** do not judge an animal by another animal's
parts. Ask what the animal needs where it lives, then look for the part
that does that job.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Toy round: the adult holds up a toy animal. "Name two parts you can
   see, and one job for each." Repeat with a second animal.
2. Ear round: "Which animals here have ears you can see?" (Rabbit, cat,
   dog — long or pointy ears.) "The duck's ears are hidden under
   feathers. Can the duck still hear?" (Yes — hidden does not mean
   missing.)
3. Moving round: "Show me how each animal moves: duck (paddle with
   webbed feet / swim), fish (wiggle fins and tail), rabbit (hop with
   back legs), bird (flap wings)." The learner mimes or points.
4. Picture round: show the illustration. "Find the duck's beak. Find
   the fish's tail fin. Find the duck's webbed feet." (Learners who
   cannot use the picture use the text alternative — same questions,
   same answers.)
5. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "A fish uses
   its fins to walk on land." Ask: "Is that right?" (Expected: no.
   Fins are for swimming and steering in water; on land a fish cannot
   walk. Fins do the moving job only in water.)
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: webbed feet help a
   duck ___." (Swim — they work like paddles.)

## Independent practice

The learner draws one animal from the lesson (duck or fish) in the
journal and draws arrows (or the adult draws arrows from dictation) to
three parts, with the adult writing each part's job beside it from
dictation. The adult observes: are the three parts real outside parts
of that animal, and is each job right? When finished, the adult asks,
"Which part helps your animal move?" Full solutions are in the
[answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Application

"Our duck wants lunch in the pond, and our rabbit wants lunch on the
grass. Which animal needs webbed feet, and which needs hopping legs?
Why?" The learner answers: the duck needs webbed feet for swimming; the
rabbit needs hopping legs for moving on land. If the learner swaps them,
the adult re-runs the moving round: "Try swimming with hopping legs…
try hopping with paddles. Which tool fits which place?"

## Exit check

The adult holds up two toy animals (or two picture cards) in mixed
order and asks for one part and one job for each. Four correct
part-and-job pairs across the two animals counts as secure for today;
fewer is re-taught in Practice Session D.

## Supports, alternate response, extension

- **Language access:** the adult says the part word, the learner repeats
  or points; a home-language word for each part is welcome alongside the
  English word.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, miming the movement, or drawing
  all count; no writing is required.
- **Seated-table variant:** toys and picture cards on the table; the
  learner points and sorts.
- **Floor-play variant:** the learner becomes each animal in turn —
  waddle and paddle like a duck, wiggle like a fish, hop like a rabbit —
  and names the part doing the work.
- **Extension:** "A turtle has a hard shell. What job does a shell do?
  What human thing copies a shell?" (Protects the turtle; a helmet or a
  hard case copies a shell — the framework's own example.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "every animal has the same parts" or "hidden
means missing" (duck ears). Re-teach with "same job, different tool"
and the hidden-ears example. Also watch for "fish walk with fins" —
re-teach place-fit: fins work in water. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md). Answer-key reference:
[answer key, Lesson 02](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
