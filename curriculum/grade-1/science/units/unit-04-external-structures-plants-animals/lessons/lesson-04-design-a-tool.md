# Lesson 04 — Copying Nature: Design a Tool

**Student goal:** I can state a human problem, name the plant or animal
part my tool copies, and sketch my tool before I build it.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 11 of 16**

## Materials

- The [project](../project.md) problem cards (adult-made, three
  choices — see below)
- Science journal for the sketch, pencils, crayons
- The nature-tool examples: a kitchen spoon, tweezers, a small cup

**Safety note for the adult:** sketching only today — no cutting, no
tape, no building until Session 12, when the adult supervises tools.
Scissors, when used, are adult-handled or blunt child scissors with the
adult beside the learner throughout.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Name one animal part and the human thing that copies it." If the
learner offers any pair (shell → helmet, webbed feet → swim fins,
beak → scoop, roots → anchor), begin. If not, re-run the Lesson 02
extension question once: "A turtle has a hard shell. What human thing
copies a shell?" (A helmet.) Then begin.

## Teaching

People copy nature all the time — that is called **mimicking**, or
copying nature. A helmet copies a shell. Swim fins copy webbed feet. A
scoop copies a duck's beak. The copy works because the **shape** does
the job: a shell is hard and curved, so it protects; webbed feet are
wide and flat, so they push water; a beak is a scoop shape, so it
scoops.

Designing has three steps, in this order — never build first:

1. **Define the problem** — say it in one sentence: "I need to…"
   (This is K-2-ETS1-1: define a simple problem people want to change.)
2. **Pick the nature part** — name the plant or animal part that
   already solves this kind of problem, and say what it does.
3. **Sketch before building** — draw the tool, label the part you are
   copying (adult scribes the label), and point to where the copied
   shape is in your drawing. (This is K-2-ETS1-2: the sketch shows how
   the shape helps it work.)

Today the learner picks **one** of three problems. The adult reads all
three aloud, and the learner chooses:

- **Problem A — Seed pickup:** "I need to pick up tiny seeds from a
  table without them rolling away and without dirty fingers."
  Nature part: a bird's beak (or tweezers-shape) — it pinches and
  grips small things.
- **Problem B — Pond scoop:** "I need to scoop leaves and bits out of
  a bowl of water without scooping all the water too."
  Nature part: a duck's beak — flat and wide, with edges that let
  water out and keep the bits in.
- **Problem C — Sign anchor:** "I need to hold a paper sign upright in
  the wind so it does not blow over."
  Nature part: plant roots — they spread wide underground and hold
  the plant steady.

Whichever problem the learner picks, the sketch must show: the tool,
the copied nature shape (circled or pointed to), and the problem it
solves. The adult writes the learner's one-sentence problem at the top
of the journal page, from dictation.

## Worked example 1 — Problem B, sketched step by step (modeled)

The adult sketches a duck-beak scoop on the journal page (or on scrap
paper) while talking.

> "Problem: I need to scoop leaves out of water without scooping all
> the water. Nature part: a duck's beak — flat, wide, and it lets water
> drain while keeping food in. My sketch: a flat spoon-shape with
> little holes in the bottom — the holes are the copy. Water drains
> through the holes; leaves stay in the scoop. I circle the holes and
> write (the adult writes): 'copies a duck beak.' Now I know what to
> build, and I have not cut anything yet."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the sketch is a **promise** about the
shape. If the sketch does not show the copied shape, the build will
forget it. Sketch first, build second — always.

## Worked example 2 — a bad sketch, and how to fix it (modeled)

The adult shows a sketch of a plain cup with no labels and says it is
the plan for Problem A (seed pickup).

> "Is this a good plan? It is a cup — but where is the beak shape?
> A cup scoops, it does not pinch tiny seeds. The plan forgot the
> nature part. Fix it: draw two flat pinching arms on the cup, like a
> beak, and label them 'copies a bird beak.' Now the plan tells the
> builder what to build. A sketch without the copied shape is just a
> picture; a sketch **with** the copied shape is a **design**."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a design sketch must answer three
questions: what problem, what nature part, and where is that shape in
my drawing? If any answer is missing, fix the sketch before building.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Problem sentence round: the learner picks a problem (A, B, or C)
   and says it in their own one sentence. The adult writes it down
   exactly. (Any sentence naming the problem counts.)
2. Nature-part round: "Which plant or animal part already solves this?"
   (A → bird beak; B → duck beak; C → roots.) "What does it do?"
   (A: pinches and grips; B: scoops and drains; C: spreads and holds
   steady.)
3. Sketch round: the learner sketches the tool. The adult asks, "Point
   to the part of your drawing that copies nature." If the learner
   cannot point to it, the adult helps them add it before moving on.
4. Label round: the adult writes "copies a ___" beside the circled
   shape, from the learner's dictation.
5. Error-analysis round: the adult shows the plain-cup sketch again.
   "Is this ready to build for Problem A? Why not?" (Expected: no —
   it shows no beak shape, so the builder would not know to make
   pinching arms.)
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: I sketch before I
   build because ___." (So I know what shape to copy / so I do not
   forget the plan.)

## Independent practice

The learner finishes the sketch in the journal: the tool, the circled
copied shape, and the problem sentence at the top (adult-scribed). The
adult observes: does the sketch show a tool for the chosen problem, and
is the copied nature shape visible and labeled? When finished, the
adult asks, "What will you build first tomorrow — and what shape must
it have?" Full solutions are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Application

"Your little brother wants the same tool, but smaller, for his tiny
hands. Does your sketch still work if everything is smaller?" The
learner answers: yes, if the copied shape stays the same — a small
beak-shape still pinches, a small scoop still scoops. (If the learner
says no, the adult re-runs the shape rule: it is the **shape**, not the
size, that does the job.)

## Exit check

The learner shows the sketch and answers three questions: What problem
does it solve? What nature part does it copy? Where is that shape in
the drawing? All three answered (by speaking, pointing, or gesture)
counts as secure for today; the sketch is kept for Session 12's build.

## Supports, alternate response, extension

- **Language access:** problem cards carry a picture as well as words;
  the adult reads every card aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build the sketch from
  cut paper shapes instead of drawing, or dictate the design while the
  adult draws it — the design is the learner's if the choices are theirs.
- **Seated-table variant:** sketch at the table with the spoon,
  tweezers, and cup in view as shape examples.
- **Floor-play variant:** the learner lays out the tool shape on the
  floor with craft sticks and string, then draws it.
- **Extension:** "Pick a second nature part that could solve the same
  problem. Which copy is better, and why?" (Any defensible pair counts;
  the learner will test two versions in Lesson 05.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "copying means making it look like the animal"
(a duck-shaped scoop that does not scoop). Re-teach: we copy the
**working shape**, not the animal's looks — a beak-shape that pinches
or scoops, not a duck face. Also watch for build-first impulse: hold the
line — sketch today, build tomorrow. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md). Answer-key reference:
[answer key, Lesson 04](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
