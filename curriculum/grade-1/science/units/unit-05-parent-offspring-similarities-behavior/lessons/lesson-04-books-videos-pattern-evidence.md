# Lesson 04 — Books and Pictures as Evidence

**Student goal:** I can find a parent-help pattern in a book or picture
the adult reads and shows me, and say it as evidence.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 11 of 16**

## Materials

- One adult-read text or picture set about animal parents (choose one
  from the [resource-pack](../resource-pack.md): for example, the
  National Geographic Kids Adélie Penguin page, read aloud by the
  adult, or the Meerkat page). The adult previews it before the session
  and picks two short passages or picture moments in advance.
- The behavior word cards from Lesson 03 (FEED, WARM, SHELTER, PROTECT,
  GUIDE)
- Science journal, crayons

**Safety note for the adult:** the learner never reads alone for this
lesson and never clicks through a website unsupervised. The adult reads
aloud, pauses, and points. If video is used instead of text, the adult
previews it, mutes comments, and stops after a short segment. If no
screen is available, the printed picture cards from Lesson 03 are the
text — the lesson works fully offline.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Yesterday we named five ways parents help babies. Can you name one?"
(Any of feed, warm, shelter, protect, guide counts.) "Today we do not
guess. We look in a book and find the pattern there. The book is our
evidence." If the learner can name one behavior, begin. If not, lay out
the five word cards and re-name them together first.

## Teaching

In Lesson 03 we watched pictures and named patterns ourselves. Today we
get the patterns from somewhere else: a **text** (words in a book or on
a page) or **media** (pictures or a short video). That is what
scientists do when they cannot watch the animal themselves — a penguin
in Antarctica is far away, so we read what people who watched penguins
wrote down.

The rule for using a book as evidence is simple:

> **Say what the book shows, then say what pattern it proves.**

Not "I think penguins are good parents." Instead: "The book shows both
penguin parents taking turns keeping the egg warm, so keeping warm is
one way parents help babies survive." The first half is what the book
shows. The second half is the pattern. Both halves are needed, or it is
not evidence yet.

How to read a book like a scientist (the adult models each step):

1. **Look and listen first.** The adult reads a short part aloud and
   shows the picture. The learner just watches and listens.
2. **Point to the pattern.** "What did the parent do?" The learner
   points to the picture or says the action.
3. **Name the behavior word.** Feed? Warm? Shelter? Protect? Guide?
4. **Say it as evidence.** "The book shows ___ , so ___ helps babies
   survive."

One book can show more than one pattern. Adélie penguin parents take
turns keeping eggs warm AND bring food after the chicks hatch. That is
two patterns from one text — warming and feeding.

## Worked example 1 — the penguin page (modeled)

The adult reads aloud, slowly, from the Adélie Penguin page (or shows
its picture): both parents care for the eggs; while one stays behind
keeping the eggs warm and safe, the other goes to sea to eat; after the
chicks hatch, the parents take turns caring for them.

> "What did the book show? Both parents take turns. One keeps the egg
> warm and safe while the other finds food. So what patterns do we
> have? Keeping warm — and feeding. Say it as evidence: 'The book
> shows penguin parents taking turns keeping the egg warm, so keeping
> warm is one way parents help babies survive.' And: 'The book shows a
> parent bringing food to the chick, so feeding is one way parents
> help babies survive.' Two patterns, one book."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** "taking turns" matters because it proves
the pattern is shared work, not one parent doing everything once. And
"the book shows" matters because it tells where the evidence came from
— we did not make it up.

## Worked example 2 — the meerkat page, predicted first (modeled)

Before reading, the adult asks: "Meerkats live in a group in the desert.
What do you think the group does for its babies?"

> "Maybe they watch over them, or feed them. Let's check the book."
> The adult reads aloud from the Meerkat page: everyone in the group
> helps gather food, keep watch for predators, and take care of the
> babies; babysitters stay behind to watch newborn pups. "What did the
> book show? The group watches the pups and protects them. Say it as
> evidence: 'The book shows meerkat babysitters staying with the pups
> to protect them, so protecting is one way parents — and helpers —
> help babies survive.'"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** meerkats stretch the pattern in a good
way: sometimes helpers, not only mothers and fathers, protect babies.
The pattern is still "protecting helps babies survive." The unit keeps
the word "parents" for the pattern but notes helpers honestly.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Penguin round 2: the adult re-reads one sentence: "Parents take
   turns caring for their youngsters after they hatch." "What pattern
   is that?" *(Feeding and protecting — accept either with the
   evidence sentence.)* "Say it as evidence." *(Prompt: "The book
   shows…")*
2. Meerkat round 2: "The babysitters stay behind to watch the pups."
   "Which behavior word?" *(Protect.)* "Say it as evidence."
   *("The book shows babysitters staying with the pups, so protecting
   helps babies survive.")*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "The book
   says penguins live in Antarctica, so penguins are good parents."
   Ask: "Is that evidence? Or do we need what the parents DO?"
   *(Expected: we need what the parents do — keeping warm, feeding.
   Where penguins live is a fact, but it is not a behavior pattern.)*
4. Picture round: the adult shows the Lesson 03 duck card without
   naming it. "What does this picture show?" *(A mother duck leading
   ducklings.)* "Which behavior word?" *(Guide.)* "Say it as evidence."
   *("The picture shows a mother duck leading her ducklings, so guiding
   helps babies survive.")*
5. Two-pattern round: "Find two patterns in the penguin reading."
   *(Warming and feeding; or warming and protecting.)* The learner
   points to each part of the text or picture as the adult re-reads it.
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: the book shows ___,
   so ___ helps babies survive." *(Any true behavior pair counts.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws one scene from the book in the journal — for example,
a penguin parent keeping an egg warm — and the adult labels it from
dictation ("penguin keeps the egg warm"). Under the drawing, the
learner points to or dictates the evidence sentence: "The book shows a
penguin keeping its egg warm, so keeping warm helps babies survive."
The adult writes it. The adult sits nearby and observes: does the
drawing show the parent doing something for the baby, not just the
animals standing side by side? When finished, the adult asks, "Where
did we learn that — from the book, or from guessing?" and scribes the
answer for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner sorts the behavior word cards
to match two short sentences the adult reads aloud, instead of drawing,
and points to the matching picture for each.

## Application

"Tonight, ask the adult to read you one short page about any animal
baby. Tell the adult one thing the parent does in that page." (In a
one-on-one setting, the learner reports back at the next session.) The
learner practices finding patterns in texts outside the lesson.

## Exit check

The adult reads one new sentence aloud (from the same pages, a part not
yet used — for example, that meerkat group members gather food and keep
watch) and asks: "What pattern is that? Say it as evidence." The
learner should name the behavior and give the "the book shows… so…"
sentence (words, pointing, or gesture). The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "Why do we say 'the book shows'
instead of just 'I think'?" (Because the book is the evidence — accept
any answer that points to the source.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** the evidence sentence is taught as a chant
  first — "The book shows… so…" — with gestures (point to the book,
  then hold up the behavior card). Accept home-language words alongside
  English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to the picture, sorting word
  cards, drawing the scene, or completing the chant with one word.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay the book pages (printed) on the floor;
  the learner stands on the page that shows each behavior.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  book, cards, and journal.

## Extension

"Could a book ever be wrong? What would you do if two books said
different things about penguins?" (Accept any sensible answer — for
example, check a second book, or ask what the writer actually watched.
The point is that evidence comes from sources, and sources can be
compared; the unit does not test source criticism.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Any fact from the book is a behavior
pattern" — the error-analysis round corrects this: habitat facts are
not behaviors; (2) "Saying 'I think' is evidence" — the evidence
sentence form is the correction; (3) "Only one pattern per book" — the
penguin page alone shows warming, feeding, and protecting.

the expected responses for the error-analysis round and exit check.
