# Lesson 03 — Parents Help Babies Survive

**Student goal:** I can name ways parents help their babies stay alive
and say what each way helps the baby do.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- Behavior picture cards (adult-made or printed): a mother bird feeding
  chicks in a nest; a penguin parent keeping an egg warm; a mother duck
  leading ducklings to water; a mother cat nursing kittens in a safe
  den; a mother elephant walking with her calf in a herd
- Science journal, crayons
- Five word cards, adult-read: FEED, WARM, SHELTER, PROTECT, GUIDE
  (the adult reads them; the learner is not expected to read them)

**Safety note for the adult:** all animals in this lesson are pictures
or text. Wild animals are never touched, chased, or fed by the learner.
If the learner asks about touching a wild baby animal, the answer is
the safe alternative: "We watch from far away, or in pictures. Its
parent takes care of it."

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Think of a baby bird in a nest. It cannot fly yet. How does it get
food?" (Its parent brings food.) "How does it stay warm?" (Its parent
sits with it, or the nest keeps it safe.) If the learner can name one
way a parent helps, begin. If not, show the bird-feeding card and name
it together: "The mother bird brings food. That is one way parents help."

## Teaching

Babies cannot do everything grown animals can do. A chick cannot fly
to find its own food. A kitten cannot keep itself warm alone at first.
A duckling does not know where the water is. So parents do things that
help their babies **survive** — stay alive, grow, and get strong enough
to do things on their own one day.

Scientists notice a **pattern**: the same kinds of help happen again
and again, in many kinds of animals. Here are five:

1. **Feed** — parents bring food, or give milk, to babies that cannot
   find food themselves. A mother bird feeds her chicks. A mother cat
   feeds her kittens with milk.
2. **Keep warm** — parents sit on eggs, or cuddle babies close, so the
   babies stay warm. A penguin parent keeps its egg warm on its feet.
3. **Shelter** — parents build or find a safe home: a nest, a burrow,
   a den. The shelter keeps babies out of wind, rain, and danger.
4. **Protect** — parents stay close, watch for danger, and stand
   between their babies and harm. A mother elephant walks with her
   calf inside the herd, where the grown elephants surround it.
5. **Guide** — parents lead babies and show them what to do. A mother
   duck leads her ducklings to the pond and shows them how to swim and
   find food.

Babies help too: they cheep, cry, or call, and that signal tells the
parent "I am hungry" or "I need you." The pattern goes both ways —
babies signal, parents respond. That is why the same story shows up in
bird nests, duck ponds, penguin colonies, and elephant herds: it is a
pattern, not a one-time event.

## Worked example 1 — the mother bird (modeled)

The adult shows the bird-feeding card.

> "Look at this nest. The chicks have their beaks open wide. They are
> cheeping — that is their signal. What does the mother bird do? She
> brings food and puts it in a chick's beak. So the pattern is: baby
> signals, parent feeds. What does feeding help the chick do? It helps
> the chick grow and stay alive. Without food, a chick cannot grow.
> Feeding is one way parents help babies survive."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we did not say "the mother is nice." We
said what she DOES (brings food) and what it HELPS (the chick grows).
Every behavior answer in this unit needs both parts: the action and
the help.

## Worked example 2 — the penguin parent, predicted first (modeled)

Before showing the penguin card, the adult asks: "A penguin egg sits on
the ice. It is very cold. What could a parent do to help the egg?"

> "Keep it warm! Let's check." The adult shows the penguin card. "Yes —
> the parent keeps the egg warm on its feet, under its feathers, while
> the other parent goes to find food. They take turns. So the pattern
> is: parent keeps the egg warm, and parent brings food. What does
> keeping warm help the egg do? It helps the baby inside stay alive
> until it hatches."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** "taking turns" is itself a pattern — two
parents sharing the job so the egg is never left cold and the chick is
never left hungry for long.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Duck round: mother duck leading ducklings card. "What is the
   mother doing?" *(Leading them to water — guiding.)* "What does
   guiding help the ducklings do?" *(Find water and food; learn to swim.)*
2. Cat round: mother cat with kittens card. "What is the mother
   doing?" *(Feeding them with milk; keeping them in a safe den.)*
   "Which words fit — feed, warm, shelter?" *(All three can fit; accept
   any one with a reason.)*
3. Elephant round: elephant herd card. "Where is the calf?"
   *(Inside the herd, surrounded by grown elephants.)* "What does that
   help the calf do?" *(Stay safe — protection.)*
4. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "The mother
   bird feeds her chicks because she likes singing." Ask: "Is that
   right? Or does she feed them so they grow and survive?" *(Expected:
   so they grow and survive. The cheeping is the baby's signal; the
   feeding is the parent's help.)*
5. Signal round: "A chick cheeps. What is it telling its parent?"
   *(It needs food, warmth, or help — accept any need.)* "What does the
   parent do?" *(Comes, feeds, comforts, or protects it.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: parents help their
   babies survive by ___." *(Feeding, warming, sheltering, protecting,
   or guiding them.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws one behavior in the journal — for example, a mother
bird feeding chicks, or a mother duck leading ducklings — and the adult
labels it from dictation ("mother bird feeds her chicks"). Under the
drawing, the learner points to or dictates the "helps the baby…"
sentence: "Feeding helps the chick grow." The adult writes it. The
adult sits nearby and observes: does the drawing show a parent AND a
baby, with the help happening between them? When finished, the adult
asks, "What would happen if no parent helped?" and scribes the answer
for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner sorts the five behavior cards
under the five word cards (FEED, WARM, SHELTER, PROTECT, GUIDE) instead
of drawing, and points to the "helps" part while the adult names it.

## Application

"Tonight, notice one way a grown-up helps a young one at home — bringing
food, keeping someone warm, showing the way. Tell the adult what you
noticed." (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back at the
next session.) The learner practices spotting the pattern outside the
lesson. The adult keeps this general: no family details are recorded in
the journal.

## Exit check

The adult shows a new behavior card the learner has not sorted yet
(a mother bird sitting on a nest, if available; otherwise the penguin
card with a new question) and asks: "What is the parent doing? What
does it help the baby do?" The learner should name the behavior and
its help (words, pointing, or gesture). The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "Name one more way parents help
babies survive." (Any of feed, warm, shelter, protect, guide counts.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach each behavior word with the picture first,
  word second. Act out "guide" (walk in a line, following) before
  saying the word. Accept home-language words alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to the behavior card, acting
  out the behavior (mime feeding, mime leading), sorting cards.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay the behavior cards on the floor; the
  learner walks to the card that shows "feed," then "protect," and so on.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  cards and journal.

## Extension

"Some animals do not stay with their babies at all — many fish and
frogs leave their eggs behind. Do those babies still need food, warmth,
and safety? How might they get them without a parent?" (Accept any
sensible answer — for example, that the eggs are hidden, or that the
babies must find food at once. The point is that needs stay the same
even when the help looks different; the unit does not test these cases.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Parents help because they are nice" —
redirect to action + help: what the parent does and what it helps the
baby do; (2) "Only mothers help" — penguin parents take turns, and
many fathers and whole herds help; (3) "Babies do nothing" — babies
signal (cheep, cry, call), and the signal starts the pattern;
(4) "Every animal parent does all five things" — different animals
show different parts of the pattern; the pattern is the set of kinds
of help, not a checklist every parent completes.
