# Lesson 05 — Mystery Baby: Use the Evidence

**Student goal:** I can match a mystery baby to its parent using traits
I can see, and explain my match.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 13 of 16**

## Materials

- Mystery cards: 4 baby cards shown WITHOUT their parents at first
  (puppy, kitten, duckling, chick) and 4 parent cards (mother dog, mother
  cat, mother duck, hen) kept face down until the learner guesses
- Cow + calf cards (exit check only)
- The "alike / different" chart from Lessons 01–02
- Science journal, crayons

**Safety note for the adult:** all animals are pictures. The mystery
game never uses a real animal the learner must catch, hold, or guess
about from handling. If the learner wants a harder mystery, add the
cow/calf pair — never a wild animal the learner might try to approach.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Name the unit rule." (Like, but not exactly like.) "Name one way
parents help babies survive." (Any of feed, warm, shelter, protect,
guide.) If the learner can say the rule, begin. If not, re-chant it
together once: "Like — but not exactly like."

## Teaching

Today the babies are a mystery. You see the baby first, and you must
find its parent by evidence — not by guessing, and not by color alone.
A scientist matching a mystery baby asks three questions, in order:

1. **What body parts does this baby have?** (Four legs and fur? Two
   legs and feathers? A flat beak? Webbed feet?)
2. **Which parent has those same parts?** (A baby with four legs and
   fur belongs to a four-legged, furry parent. A baby with a flat beak
   and webbed feet belongs to a duck.)
3. **What is different, and does the difference make sense for a baby?**
   (Smaller? Different color? Fewer feathers? If the differences are
   only size, color, or "not grown yet," the match still holds. If the
   body parts do not match, the match is wrong — no matter how much
   you like the animal.)

Then you explain the match out loud, with both halves: "I matched this
baby to this parent because both have ___, but the baby is ___."
That sentence is the whole unit in one breath: alike, different, and
evidence.

## Worked example 1 — the mystery puppy (modeled)

The adult shows only the puppy card, face up, with the parent cards
face down.

> "Mystery baby! What body parts do you see? Four legs, fur, a tail,
> floppy ears. Which parent could have those parts? A dog has four
> legs, fur, a tail, and floppy ears. A hen does not — a hen has two
> legs and feathers. So I guess the dog. Let's check." The adult turns
> over the mother dog card. "Yes! Now explain it: 'I matched this puppy
> to the mother dog because both have fur and four legs, but the puppy
> is smaller and its spots are in different places.' Alike, different,
> evidence."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we ruled OUT the hen first (wrong body
parts), then confirmed the dog. Ruling out is evidence too.

## Worked example 2 — the mystery duckling, predicted first (modeled)

Before turning over any parent card, the adult asks: "This mystery baby
has a flat beak and webbed feet. Which parent do you predict?"

> "A duck — because ducks have flat beaks and webbed feet. Let's
> check." The adult turns over the mother duck card. "Yes! Now the
> differences: the duckling is small and yellow; the mother is big and
> brown. Explain it: 'I matched this duckling to the mother duck
> because both have a flat beak and webbed feet, but the duckling is
> smaller and yellower.' Like — but not exactly like."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the flat beak and webbed feet are the
matching clues; yellow is a difference, not a clue. If we matched by
color alone, we might have picked the yellow chick — and been wrong,
because a chick has a pointed beak and no webbed feet.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Mystery kitten: show the kitten card only. "What body parts?"
   *(Four legs, fur, tail, ears.)* "Which parent?" *(Mother cat.)*
   Check, then explain with one alike trait and one difference.
2. Mystery chick: show the chick card only. "What body parts?"
   *(Two legs, feathers, a small pointed beak, wings.)* "Which parent?"
   *(Hen.)* Check, then explain.
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "This yellow
   baby must be a duckling, because it is yellow." (Showing the chick
   card.) Ask: "Is that right? Or do we check the beak and feet?"
   *(Expected: check the beak and feet — the chick has a pointed beak
   and no webbed feet, so it is not a duckling. Color alone is not
   evidence.)*
4. Ruling-out round: show the calf card (if available; otherwise the
   puppy card again with a new question). "Could this baby belong to
   the hen? How do you know not?" *(No — four legs and fur do not
   match two legs and feathers.)*
5. Plant mystery round: show the sunflower seedling card only. "Is this
   a baby plant or a grown plant? How do you know?" *(Baby plant — it
   is tiny, has only two small leaves, and no flower yet.)* "Which
   grown plant does it belong to?" *(Sunflower — same leaf shape family
   and stem; the adult confirms with the adult card.)*
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: I matched this baby
   to this parent because both have ___, but the baby is ___."
   *(Any true trait pair counts.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws one mystery pair of their own in the journal: a baby
animal on one side and its parent on the other, with the parent drawn
bigger. The adult labels each drawing from dictation. Under the pair,
the learner points to or dictates the explanation sentence: "I matched
them because both have ___, but the baby is ___." The adult writes it.
The adult sits nearby and observes: does the explanation name a real
shared part, not just a color? When finished, the adult asks, "Could
this baby belong to a different parent? Why not?" and scribes the answer
for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner matches the four mystery baby
cards to the four parent cards on the table instead of drawing, and
points to the shared body part on each pair while the adult names it.

## Application

"Tonight, play the mystery game at home: the adult shows you a baby
animal picture you have not seen in this unit, and you guess its parent
by its body parts." (In a one-on-one setting, the learner reports back
at the next session.) The learner practices evidence matching outside
the lesson.

## Exit check

The adult shows the calf card with the parent cards face down and asks:
"Which parent does this baby belong to? How do you know? What is
different?" The learner should name the
parent, give at least one alike trait as evidence, and name one
difference (words, pointing, or gesture). The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "Is a baby exactly like its
parent?" (Any correct "no — like, but not exactly like" counts.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** the explanation sentence is taught as a chant
  first — "Both have ___, but the baby is ___" — with gestures (point
  to both animals, then crouch small). Accept home-language words
  alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to shared parts, sorting
  cards, drawing, or completing the chant with one word.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay parent cards in a row on the floor; the
  learner carries each mystery baby card to its parent.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  cards and journal.

## Extension

"Imagine you find a photo of a baby animal with no parent in the photo.
What would you look at first to guess its parent, and what would you
look at second?" (Accept any sensible answer — for example, its legs
and feet first, then its beak or ears. The point is that one trait is
rarely enough; scientists gather several traits before they decide.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Matching by color alone" — the
yellow chick vs. yellow duckling round is the correction; (2) "A
difference breaks the match" — size and color differences are
expected for babies; only body-part mismatches break a match;
(3) "Guessing fast is good science" — the three-question order
(parts → parent → differences) is the correction; a checked guess
beats a fast guess.
