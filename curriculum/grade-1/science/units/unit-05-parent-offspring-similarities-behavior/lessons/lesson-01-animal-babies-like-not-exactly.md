# Lesson 01 — Animal Babies: Like, but Not Exactly Like

**Student goal:** I can match animal babies to their parents and say one
thing that is alike and one thing that is different.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- Picture cards: dog mother + puppy, cat mother + kitten, duck mother +
  ducklings, hen + chicks (adult-made drawings or printed pictures; the
  adult checks that each baby clearly belongs to its parent by body
  shape, not by color alone)
- Cow + calf cards (exit check only)
- Science journal, crayons
- The "alike / different" chart: two columns drawn by the adult before
  the session, headed "Alike" and "Different"

**Safety note for the adult:** all animals in this lesson are pictures.
No live animal is handled. If the learner has a pet at home, talk about
it from memory only — the lesson never asks the learner to pick up,
chase, or feed an animal for school.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Show me a baby animal you know." (Any answer counts: puppy, kitten,
baby bird.) "What animal does it grow up to be?" If the learner can name
a baby and its grown-up — even with help — begin. If not, start with the
dog pair on the table and name both together: "This is a mother dog.
This is her puppy. A puppy grows up to be a dog."

## Teaching

A **parent** is a mother or father animal. **Offspring** is the science
word for the young of a living thing — a baby animal is its parent's
offspring. A **trait** is something you can see about a living thing:
its color, its shape, its size, or how many legs it has.

Here is the rule for this whole unit:

> **Young animals are like their parents — but not exactly like them.**

"Like" means alike in some ways. "Not exactly like" means different in
some ways. Both parts matter. A puppy is like its mother: it has fur,
four legs, a tail, and ears on its head. A puppy is not exactly like its
mother: it is much smaller, and its spots or patches may sit in
different places. A duckling is like its mother duck: it has a flat
beak, webbed feet, and feathers. A duckling is not exactly like its
mother: it is small and yellow, while its mother is bigger and brown.

Scientists do not guess a match. They use **evidence**: what they can
see. "Both have a flat beak" is evidence. "I like ducks" is not
evidence. Today we match by evidence, and we always name one alike trait
and one different trait.

## Worked example 1 — the dog pair (modeled)

The adult puts the mother dog card and the puppy card side by side.

> "Look at the mother dog. She has fur, four legs, a tail, and floppy
> ears. Now look at the puppy. What is alike? The puppy also has fur,
> four legs, a tail, and floppy ears. That is our evidence that they
> belong together.
>
> What is different? The puppy is much smaller. And look at the spots:
> the mother has a dark patch over one eye; the puppy has a dark patch
> on its back. Same kind of animal, different spots, different size.
> Like — but not exactly like."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we matched by body parts that both animals
have, not by size (babies are always smaller) and not by "they are both
cute." Size is a difference, not a matching clue.

## Worked example 2 — the duck pair, predicted first (modeled)

Before showing the duckling card, the adult asks: "A mother duck has a
flat beak and webbed feet. What do you think her duckling will have?"

> "A flat beak and webbed feet too — because babies are like their
> parents. Let's check." The adult shows the duckling card. "Yes! Flat
> beak, webbed feet. Now, what is different? The mother is big and
> brown. The ducklings are small and yellow. Their feathers are a
> different color and they are much smaller. Like — but not exactly
> like."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a good scientist predicts BEFORE looking,
then checks. The prediction is not graded — the checking is the science.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Offer choices; never demand a spoken answer.

1. Cat round: cat mother + kitten cards. "What is alike?" *(Fur, four
   legs, tail, ears, whiskers.)* "What is different?" *(The kitten is
   smaller; its stripes may be lighter or in different places.)*
2. Hen round: hen + chicks cards. "What is alike?" *(Feathers, two
   legs, a beak, wings.)* "What is different?" *(Chicks are small and
   fluffy; the hen is bigger with smooth feathers.)*
3. Error-analysis round: the adult says, very seriously: "The puppy is
   exactly like its mother, because they both have fur." Ask: "Is that
   right? Or is something different?" *(Expected: not exactly alike —
   the puppy is smaller, and its spots are in different places. Having
   fur is one alike trait, not the whole story.)*
4. Mix-up round: the adult puts the puppy card next to the hen card.
   "Do these belong together?" *(No.)* "How do you know?" *(The puppy
   has four legs and fur; the hen has two legs and feathers. The body
   parts do not match.)*
5. Chart round: together, put one alike trait ("both have four legs")
   in the "Alike" column and one different trait ("puppy is smaller")
   in the "Different" column for the dog pair. The learner points or
   dictates; the adult writes.
6. If the learner is verbal: "Finish the sentence: a baby animal is
   like its parent, but ___." *(Not exactly like it.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws the dog pair in the journal: a big dog and a small
puppy. The adult labels each drawing from dictation ("big mother dog";
"small puppy"). Under each, the learner points to or dictates one alike
trait and one different trait, and the adult writes them in the two
chart columns. The adult sits nearby and observes: does the drawing
show the size difference? When finished, the adult asks, "Are they
exactly alike?" and scribes the answer for private storage.

*Alternate response mode:* the learner sorts the four animal pairs
(mother + baby together) on the table instead of drawing, and points to
the alike part on each card while the adult names it.

## Application

"Tonight, look at a picture of a baby animal — in a book, or one the
adult shows you. Tell the adult one thing that is alike and one thing
that is different from its parent." (In a one-on-one setting, the
learner reports back at the next session.) The learner practices the
rule outside the lesson.

## Exit check

The adult shows the cow + calf cards and asks: "Do these belong
together? What is alike? What is different?"
The learner should name at least one alike trait and one different
trait (words, pointing, or gesture). The adult records: secure /
developing / not yet. A second probe: "Is a baby exactly like its
parent?" (Any correct "no — like, but not exactly like" counts.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "alike" and "different" by showing first,
  word second. Hold up two matching cards for "alike," then point to
  the size difference for "different." Accept home-language words
  alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to the matching body part,
  sorting cards, drawing, mime (crouching small for "baby," standing
  tall for "adult").
- **Floor-play variant:** spread the cards on the floor; the learner
  walks each baby card to its parent.
- **Seated-table variant:** do the whole lesson at the table with the
  cards and journal.

## Extension

"Two puppies from the same mother can have different spots. Are they
still both puppies? Why?" (Yes — animals of the same kind are
recognizable as similar but can vary in many ways. Accept any sensible
answer; the unit returns to this in Lesson 05.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Babies are exactly like their parents"
— the size and spot differences in both worked examples are the
correction; (2) "Babies are totally different from their parents" —
the shared body parts (legs, beak, fur, feathers) are the correction;
(3) "Matching by color alone" — the duck pair shows color is a
difference, not the match; body shape and parts are the match.
