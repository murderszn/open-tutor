# Lesson 2 — What Is a Right?

**Session 3 · about 18–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can say what a right is and name one right I have.

**Materials:** three printed picture cards or simple drawings: a child raising
a hand, a child with a warm blanket/coat, two children playing together;
crayons.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Yesterday you named your communities. Say one
community and one person in it." (Retrieval from Lesson 1.)

## Explicit teaching

Members of a community get some good things **just because they belong**. A
**right** is something every member gets to have — nobody has to earn it and
nobody can take it away to be mean.

Three rights every child in this classroom has:

1. **The right to be heard.** When it is your turn, people listen to your words.
2. **The right to be safe.** Your body and your feelings are protected here.
3. **The right to be included.** You get to join in — no one is left out on purpose.

Rights are for *everyone*, not just some people. If one person has a right,
every member has it too. That is what makes a right different from a treat or
a prize.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** "Maya raises her hand in circle time. The teacher says, 'Maya,
we are listening.' Maya is using her right to be **heard**. Did Maya have to
win a game to earn that? No — she gets it because she is a member of our
class."

**Example 2.** "At the park, Sam sees a game starting and asks, 'Can I play?'
The children make room. Sam is using his right to be **included**. Notice:
making room did not take anything away from the other children — rights are
not like the last cookie. Everyone can have them at the same time."

## Guided practice (4 tasks)

1. Hold up the raised-hand card. "Is this a right? Which one — heard, safe,
   or included?" *(Prompt: "Look at the hand. What is the child doing?")*
2. Hold up the blanket card. "Is this a right? Which one?"
3. "Tell me a right you have at home." *(Accept: "I get dinner," "I am safe,"
   "people listen to me." Redirect treats: "Ice cream is nice, but is it
   something EVERY member gets?")*
4. True-or-false with thumbs: "A right is something you have to win." *(Thumb
   down — and the adult models why: "You have it because you belong.")*

## Independent practice

The learner draws themselves using one right (e.g., raising a hand, joining a
game, sitting in a safe cozy corner). The adult writes the learner's dictated
label: "my right to ___". **Alternate mode:** the learner points to the
picture card that shows their chosen right and says its name.

## Application

During the next group time, the adult watches for a moment the learner *uses*
a right (raises a hand, joins play) and names it back to them: "You used your
right to be heard."

## Exit check

The learner completes: "A right is something every member gets to ___" and
names one of their own rights. Record secure / developing / emerging.

## Supports

- **Language access:** act out each right before naming it; pair the English
  word with a gesture (ear for heard, arms-around-self for safe,
  arms-welcoming for included) and the home-language word.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to cards; thumbs up/down for true-false.
- **Movement:** "rights freeze dance" — freeze in a pose showing heard, safe,
  or included when the music stops.

## Extension

"Rights reporter": find one right in a picture book at home (someone being
listened to, kept safe, or included) and describe it.

## Teacher note

**Common misconceptions:** (a) "A right means I can do anything I want." —
redirect: rights belong to *everyone*, so my right to be heard does not remove
your right to be heard; that is why we take turns. (b) Confusing rights with
rewards ("I was good, so I get a right") — return to Example 1: membership,
not behavior, is the source. **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md)
§L2.
