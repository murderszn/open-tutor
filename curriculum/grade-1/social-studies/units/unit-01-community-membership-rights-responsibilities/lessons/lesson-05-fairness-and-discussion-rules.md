# Lesson 5 — Fairness and Discussion Rules

**Session 9 · about 18–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can show fairness, name a person in authority I respect, and follow our discussion rules in a group talk.

**Materials:** two cups and a small pitcher of water (or two plates and
crackers) for the fairness demo; the class's agreed discussion-rules poster
(made today if it does not exist yet); picture cards: teacher, principal,
firefighter, crossing guard.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "What are the two jobs of a rule?" (Retrieval
from Lesson 4: safe; learn and play.)

## Explicit teaching

**Fair** means the same rules apply to everyone and everyone gets what they
need. Fair is *not* always "everyone gets the exact same thing." Watch:

*(Adult pours water: one cup nearly full, one cup with a sip.)* "Is this fair?
No — the same rule, 'everyone gets a drink,' was not followed." *(Adult
re-pours: both cups half full.)* "Now? Yes — same rule for everyone."

But sometimes fair looks different: a classmate with a hurt ankle gets the
chair closest to the door — not because the rules changed for them, but
because **everyone gets what they need** is part of being fair too.

**Respect** means treating people and their words as important. We show
respect to **people in authority** — people our community trusts to keep
things safe and fair: our teacher, the principal, firefighters, the crossing
guard. Respect does not mean never asking questions; it means asking kindly
and listening to the answer.

So that every voice can be heard, our class agrees on **discussion rules**:

1. **Listen** while someone speaks (eyes and ears on them).
2. **One speaker at a time** (raise your hand; wait your turn).
3. **Respond to what was said** ("I agree with ___ because ___" / "I think
   differently because ___").

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** "Two children both want the red crayon. The teacher says,
'We will each use it for five minutes — you first, then you.' Is that fair?
Yes: the *same rule* (take turns) applied to *everyone*, and both children get
what they need (a turn with the crayon). Fairness is not 'the loudest child
gets it' — that would break the right to be included."

**Example 2.** "In circle time, Jay says, 'I think our class pet should be a
fish.' Then it is Priya's turn, and she says, 'I agree with Jay because fish
are quiet and we can all watch them.' Priya used discussion rule 3 — she
*responded to what Jay said* instead of starting a whole new topic. That is
how a group thinks together instead of just taking turns making noise."

## Guided practice (5 tasks)

1. Fair-or-not showdown (thumbs): "Everyone gets one cookie." / "Only the
   tallest children get cookies." / "A child with a hurt ankle sits closest
   to the door." *(The third one tests "gets what they need" — pause and let
   them argue it out; that argument IS the lesson.)*
2. Hold up the firefighter card. "This person keeps our neighborhood safe.
   That is a person in **authority**. How do we show them respect?" *(Accept:
   listen, follow safety rules, say thank you.)*
3. "Name one person in authority at school. What is their job for us?"
4. Practice round: the adult poses a tiny real question ("Should we read the
   lion book or the tree book next?" — see the illustration's book!) and the
   group runs a 2-minute discussion using all three discussion rules. The adult
   coaches: "Who is listening? One speaker — whose turn? Priya, can you
   respond to what Jay said?"
5. "What happens to fairness when someone breaks a discussion rule — for
   example, shouts over others?" *(Accept: "Then not everyone is heard — it
   is not fair." — connecting rules back to rights.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws a "fairness picture": two children and something shared
fairly (crayons split, turns on a swing, water cups level). Dictated label:
"fair means ___." **Alternate mode:** arrange real objects (crackers on two
plates) to show fair vs. not fair and explain the difference aloud.

## Application

The discussion-rules poster stays on the wall. For the rest of the unit, every
group talk begins with the class chanting the three rules — and the adult
names it when they are followed: "Rule 2 — one speaker — nice."

## Exit check

The learner (a) states the three discussion rules in any order, (b) gives one
fairness example, (c) names one person in authority and their job. Record
secure / developing / emerging per part.

## Supports

- **Language access:** sentence frames on the poster: "I agree with ___
  because ___." / "I think differently because ___." — frames stay up all
  year.
- **Alternate response mode:** thumbs up/down for fair-or-not; pointing to
  authority cards.
- **Movement:** "discussion-rule statues" — freeze as listener / hand-raiser /
  responder; the class names the rule.

## Extension

"Rule maker": the learner proposes a *new* discussion rule ("we can pass if
we are not ready") and defends it to the adult using the two jobs of rules.
If the class adopts it, the learner's name goes on the poster — first authorship.

## Teacher note

**Common misconceptions:** (a) "Fair means equal amounts of everything" — the
ankle example is the antidote; (b) "Respect means never disagreeing" — model
respectful disagreement all week ("I see it differently, and here is why");
(c) discussion rules as a script rather than a tool — if the talk is lively
*and* everyone is heard, the rules are working even if the chant was skipped.
