# Lesson 2 — When We Cannot Have Everything

**Session 3 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can explain that sometimes there is not enough, and I
can choose fairly.

**Materials:** 3 real apples (or apple picture cards) and 4 chairs or place
mats; 2 choice cards (a sticker sheet, a balloon); one swing picture card
for the turn-taking example.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Point to the *need* mat: which card is a
need?" (Any correct card counts.) Retrieval: "Is a toy robot a need or a
want?" (Want — Lesson 1 retrieval.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

Sometimes there is not enough of something for everyone to have everything
they want. That is called **scarcity**. Scarcity is not about being
unfair, and it is not about someone being greedy. It is about numbers:
three apples, four friends. Four friends, one swing. Two fun things, only
enough tokens for one.

When there is scarcity, we have to make a **choice**: pick one thing when
you cannot have everything. And every choice has a give-up: if you pick
the sticker sheet, you give up the balloon — at least for now. Choosing
does not mean the other thing was bad. It means you picked this one first.

There are fair ways to choose when something is scarce. We can **share**
it — cut the apple so everyone tastes it. We can **take turns** — one
person swings first, then the next. We can **wait** — "not today, maybe
tomorrow." And we can **vote** — remember Unit 04: listen, propose, vote,
do. Fair does not always mean everyone gets the same thing at the same
moment. Fair means everyone is counted.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** "Three apples, four friends on Willow Street. Scarcity?
Yes — there are fewer apples than friends. What can the friends do? They
can **share**: cut one apple into pieces so everyone tastes apple. Or they
can **take turns**: three friends eat an apple today, the fourth friend
gets the first apple tomorrow. Either way, someone waits or shares — that
is the give-up. Nobody gets a whole apple all at once, and that is all
right."

**Example 2.** "Maya has enough tokens for one thing: a sticker sheet or a
balloon. She picks the sticker sheet. What did she give up? The **balloon**
— for now. The sticker sheet is her choice; the balloon is her cost. Next
time she earns tokens, she can choose the balloon. A choice is not forever;
it is for now."

**Example 3.** "Four friends, one swing. If one friend swings all morning,
is that fair? No — the other three never get a turn. A fair choice is
**take turns**: count to twenty, then swap. Everyone swings, nobody swings
all morning. Scarcity made the rule necessary: one swing, four friends, so
turns."

**Example 4.** "Two children want the same book at the same time. There is
one copy. What are the fair choices? **Take turns** — one reads first,
then the other. Or **read together** — share the book, one reads aloud.
Or **vote** on who goes first, and the other goes second. Notice: every
fair choice still has a give-up — someone reads second. Fair does not
remove the give-up; it shares it."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. "Three apples, four friends — is that scarcity? How do you know?"
   *(Hint if stuck: "Count the apples. Count the friends.")*
2. "Maya picks the balloon instead of the sticker sheet. What did she
   give up?"
3. "One swing, four friends. Name one fair way to choose. What does the
   person who waits give up?"
4. "You and a friend both want the red crayon. Name two fair choices —
   share, take turns, or vote — and pick one."

## Independent practice

The learner works through 4 two-want choice cards (sticker or balloon;
storybook or puzzle; apple slices or crackers; swing first or slide
first). For each, the learner points to their choice and says what they
give up. The adult scribes one "I choose ___; I give up ___" sentence per
card, dictated by the learner.
**Text alternative:** the learner acts out the choice (pretends to blow
up a balloon, pretends to read) instead of naming it aloud.

## Application

"Tonight, notice one time you had to choose — at play, at a meal, or with
a game. Tell someone what you chose and what you gave up." The adult notes
whether the learner tries it unprompted the next day.

## Exit check

The learner explains scarcity in their own words ("not enough for
everyone to have everything") and makes one two-want choice, naming the
give-up. The adult records: *secure* (scarcity explained + choice +
give-up), *developing* (choice made, give-up shaky), *emerging* (cannot
name a give-up — reteach with the sticker/balloon pair).

## Supports

- **Language access:** pre-teach *scarcity / choice* with the learner's
  home language alongside English; accept answers in any language.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to choice cards, or acting out
  sharing and turn-taking, instead of explaining aloud.
- **Movement:** hold up three fingers for three apples, four fingers for
  four friends, then wiggle the "missing" finger — the scarcity finger.

## Extension

"Scarcity detective": the learner watches for one scarce thing during the
day (one bathroom, two children; one ball, three players) and reports the
fair choice used — with the question "what was given up?" held open for
Lesson 3's benefit-and-cost boards.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners think scarcity means "someone is being
mean" or "it is not fair." Return to numbers every time: count the things,
count the people. Another common mix-up: thinking a fair choice removes
the give-up — it does not; it shares it. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L2.
