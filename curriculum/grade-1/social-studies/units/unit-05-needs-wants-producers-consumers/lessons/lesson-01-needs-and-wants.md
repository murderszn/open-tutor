# Lesson 1 — Needs and Wants

**Session 1 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can tell a need from a want and say why.

**Materials:** 6 picture cards the adult draws or prints before the session
(water in a cup, an apple, a warm coat, a toy robot, an ice-cream treat, a
new board game); two labeled mats, *need* and *want* (the adult reads the
labels aloud; the learner need not read them).

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Name one thing you used today before you
came to our session." (Any answer counts — the adult is warming up
everyday-object talk.) Retrieval: "Put these in order: waking up, eating
breakfast, coming to class." (Unit 03 timeline-strip retrieval.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

Some things our bodies and lives **must** have. Those are **needs**. Food
is a need — our bodies need food to grow and stay strong. Water is a need
— our bodies need water every day. A safe place to sleep is a need. Clothes
that fit and keep us warm are a need. Without needs, we cannot stay healthy
and safe.

Other things we would **like** but can live without. Those are **wants**.
A toy robot is a want — fun to play with, but you can live without it. An
ice-cream treat is a want — tasty, but it is extra, not the food your body
needs. A new board game is a want — fun, but you can play other games
without it.

Here is the tricky part: a thing can be a need in one form and a want in
another. Shoes are a need — your feet need shoes. But a *second* pair of
sparkly shoes, when the shoes you have still fit, is a want. Food is a
need — but an ice-cream treat is a want, because it is extra food for fun,
not the food your body counts on. Ask: "Must we have it, or would we like
it?" That question sorts almost everything.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** "It is a hot day, and Maya has been running. She is thirsty.
Is a cup of water a need or a want? A **need**. Her body must have water.
'Would we like it?' — yes, but the stronger question is 'must we have
it?' Yes. So water goes on the *need* mat."

**Example 2.** "Maya already has blocks, dolls, and a ball at home. She sees
a toy robot in a shop window. Need or want? A **want**. It would be fun,
but Maya can live without it — she already has toys to play with. Wanting
something a lot does not turn it into a need."

**Example 3.** "Maya's coat is too small — her wrists stick out and she
shivers outside. Is a coat that fits a need or a want? A **need**. Clothes
that fit and keep us warm are needs. But a *second* coat, just because it
is a prettier color, would be a want. Same kind of thing, different
answer — it depends on whether we must have it."

**Example 4.** "For lunch, Maya eats an apple. For dessert, she has an
ice-cream treat. Which is the need? The **apple** — food her body counts
on. The ice-cream treat is a **want** — extra, for fun. Food can be a need
and a treat can be a want, even at the same meal."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. "Is drinking water on a hot day a need or a want? How do you know?"
   *(Hint if stuck: "Must your body have it?")*
2. "Maya has a ball. She wants a second ball, exactly the same. Need or
   want? Why?"
3. "A warm coat that fits, on a cold day — need or want? What question
   did you ask yourself?"
4. "An ice-cream treat after lunch — need or want? Could Maya live
   without it?"

## Independent practice

The learner sorts the 6 picture cards onto the *need* and *want* mats:
water, apple, warm coat → needs; toy robot, ice-cream treat, board game →
wants. Then the learner points to one card on each mat and says why it
belongs there. The adult scribes the learner's two reasons.
**Text alternative:** the learner acts out the card (drinks pretend water,
hugs a pretend robot) instead of naming it aloud.

## Application

"Tonight, notice one need and one want in your day — for example, the
water you drink and a game you play. Tell someone which is which and why."
The adult notes whether the learner tries it unprompted the next day.

## Exit check

The learner sorts 5 of the 6 cards correctly and explains one sort with a
"must have it" or "would like it" reason. The adult records: *secure*
(5–6 correct with a reason), *developing* (4 correct, reason shaky),
*emerging* (0–3 — reteach with the water/robot pair first, then add one
card at a time).

## Supports

- **Language access:** pre-teach *need / want* with the learner's home
  language alongside English; accept answers in any language.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to mats, or sorting real objects
  (a cup, a toy) instead of picture cards.
- **Movement:** stand tall and stretch for a need ("my body must have
  it"), wiggle fingers like wanting hands for a want.

## Extension

"Need-or-want detective": the learner listens during the day for one more
need and one more want, and brings both back next session — with the
question "is it a must-have or a would-like?" held open for Lesson 2's
scarcity game.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners think "I want it a lot" means "I need
it." Return to the sorting question every time: "Must we have it, or
would we like it?" Another common mix-up: every food is a need — use the
apple vs. ice-cream-treat pair to show that treats are wants. **Answer-key
reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L1.
