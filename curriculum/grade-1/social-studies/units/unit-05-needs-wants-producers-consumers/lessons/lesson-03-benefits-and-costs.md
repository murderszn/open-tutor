# Lesson 3 — Benefits and Costs

**Session 5 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can name a good thing and a give-up for a choice I make.

**Materials:** 6 play tokens (counters); two choice boards the adult draws
before the session — Board 1: *spend now (stickers)* vs. *save (puzzle
later)*; Board 2: *share my crayons* vs. *keep my crayons*; a small jar
labeled with a picture of a puzzle (the "save" jar).

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Three apples, four friends — what is the
fair choice we practiced?" (Share, take turns, or wait — any one counts.)
Retrieval: "What does scarcity mean?" (Not enough for everyone to have
everything — Lesson 2 retrieval.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

Every choice has two sides. The **benefit** is a good thing about the
choice — what you gain. The **cost** is what you give up — what you do
not get because you chose this instead. Benefit and cost are a pair: you
cannot see a choice clearly until you look at both sides.

Watch Maya with her tokens. She earned 6 tokens for helping sort apples at
the market stall. She wants stickers today, and she wants a puzzle later.
The stickers cost 3 tokens; the puzzle costs 6 tokens. If she spends 3
tokens on stickers now, she has 3 tokens left — not enough for the puzzle
yet. The benefit of stickers now: fun today. The cost: waiting longer for
the puzzle. If she saves all 6 tokens, the benefit is the puzzle sooner;
the cost is no stickers today. Neither choice is wrong. But they feel
different — and naming both sides is how we choose on purpose instead of
by accident.

The same pair works for sharing. If Maya shares her crayons, the benefit
is a friend who can draw too — and a friend who may share back tomorrow.
The cost is fewer crayons in her own hand right now. If she keeps her
crayons, the benefit is all her colors for her own picture; the cost is a
friend who cannot draw. Again: neither side alone tells the whole story.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** "Maya spends 3 tokens on stickers today. Benefit? **Fun
today** — she can stick and trade stickers right now. Cost? **The puzzle
waits** — she has only 3 tokens left, and the puzzle needs 6, so she must
earn 3 more before she can get it. Benefit: stickers today. Cost: puzzle
later. Both are true at once."

**Example 2.** "Maya saves all 6 tokens for the puzzle. Benefit? **The
puzzle sooner** — she has enough right now. Cost? **No stickers today** —
she walks past the sticker table with empty hands. Saving is not losing
your tokens; it is keeping them for later. But the cost is real: today
feels less fun."

**Example 3.** "Maya shares her crayons with a friend. Benefit? **Her
friend can draw too**, and the picture they make together is bigger and
brighter. Cost? **Fewer crayons in her own hand** — she must wait for the
red crayon when her friend is using it. Sharing's cost is waiting, not
losing: the crayons come back."

**Example 4.** "Maya keeps her crayons for her own picture. Benefit? **All
her colors, right when she wants them** — her picture is exactly as she
planned. Cost? **Her friend cannot draw** — the friend sits with no
crayons, and Maya misses the bigger picture they could have made
together. Keeping is not selfish and sharing is not always better — but
every choice has its cost, and we name it."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. "Maya spends 3 tokens on stickers. What is the benefit? What is the
   cost?" *(Hint if stuck: "Benefit = good thing now. Cost = what waits?")*
2. "Maya saves all 6 tokens for the puzzle. What is the benefit? What is
   the cost?"
3. "Maya shares her crayons. Name one benefit and one cost."
4. "Maya keeps her crayons for her own picture. Name one benefit and one
   cost. Is keeping always wrong? Why not?"

## Independent practice

The learner works two new choice boards, each with a benefit card and a
cost card to place: (a) *eat the apple now* vs. *save the apple for
after play* — benefit of now: tasty now; cost: no apple later; benefit of
later: apple to look forward to; cost: hungry waiting. (b) *play first*
vs. *help sort first, then play* — benefit of play first: fun right
away; cost: sorting still waits; benefit of help first: job done, then
free play; cost: play starts later. The learner places one benefit card
and one cost card per choice and dictates one sentence per board. The
adult scribes.
**Text alternative:** the learner points to the benefit/cost cards and
acts out the feeling (happy face for benefit, waiting arms for cost).

## Application

"Tonight, notice one choice you make — a snack, a game, a turn. Tell
someone one benefit and one cost of it." The adult notes whether the
learner tries it unprompted the next day.

## Exit check

The learner names one benefit and one cost for the spend-or-save choice.
The adult records: *secure* (benefit + cost, both correct), *developing*
(one side only, or benefit/cost swapped), *emerging* (cannot name either
— reteach with the stickers/puzzle board only).

## Supports

- **Language access:** pre-teach *benefit / cost* with the learner's home
  language alongside English; accept answers in any language. The
  sentence frames "The good thing is ___" and "What I give up is ___"
  work in any language the learner uses.
- **Alternate response mode:** placing benefit/cost picture cards on the
  boards instead of speaking sentences.
- **Movement:** thumbs-up for a benefit, open waiting hands for a cost —
  one gesture per side, every time.

## Extension

"Benefit-and-cost detective": the learner watches for one choice an adult
makes during the day (cook now or later, walk or ride) and guesses one
benefit and one cost — with the question "do grown-ups have costs too?"
held open for Lesson 5's earning discussion.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners swap the words — they call the fun part
the cost, or call the give-up the benefit. Anchor with the frames: benefit
= "the good thing is," cost = "what I give up is." Another common mix-up:
thinking saving means losing tokens — stress that saved tokens are kept,
in the jar, for later. **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L3.
