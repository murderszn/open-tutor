# Lesson 3 — What Is a Government?

**Session 5 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can say what a government is and name one thing it does.

**Materials:** 6 "shared things" picture cards (a road, a park, a library, a
fire truck, a school, a streetlight — adult reads labels aloud); one card
showing a single house, for contrast.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Name one person in authority and one helper
from Lessons 1–2." (Any pair counts.) Retrieval: "Who helps lead the town?"
(Mayor Sam, in our fictional Willow Street.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

Some things belong to one person: your shoes, your lunchbox. Some things
belong to **everyone** and are shared: the road, the park, the library, the
school. Shared things need someone to look after them — and that is what a
**government** is.

A **government** is a group of people whose job is to make rules for a town
and to run the things everyone shares. The word sounds big, but the idea is
simple: one person cannot fix every road, but a group of people with jobs —
led by the mayor — can.

What does a government do? Here are four things, all in Willow Street:

1. **It keeps people safe.** It runs the fire department and pays the
   police officers who keep the streets safe.
2. **It takes care of shared places.** It fixes roads, mows the park, and
   puts in streetlights so people can see at night.
3. **It runs the library and the schools.** It keeps the library open and
   the schools staffed, so every child can learn and read.
4. **It makes rules for the whole town.** Rules for the whole town are
   called **laws**: drive on the right side of the road, do not litter in
   the park, dogs on leashes. Laws are town rules everyone follows.

One sentence to keep: **a government makes rules for the town and runs the
things we share.** If the learner remembers only that sentence, the lesson
worked.

A note for the adult: grown-ups choose their town's leaders by voting, the
way our class will vote in Lesson 5 — but grown-up elections and real
candidates are not part of this lesson. If the learner asks, one neutral
sentence is enough: "Grown-ups vote to choose people like the mayor."

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** "A pothole opens in the middle of Willow Street. Who fixes it?
Not one neighbor with a shovel — the hole is in a shared road. The **town
government** sends road workers to fix it, because fixing shared roads is
one of its jobs. That is 'running the things we share.'"

**Example 2.** "Willow Street wants a new swing set in the park. Who decides
and pays? The **town government**: the mayor and the town's workers decide
the park needs it, buy it, and put it in. A park belongs to everyone, so the
group that looks after shared things handles it."

**Example 3.** "Mia's lunchbox breaks. Does the government buy her a new one?
No! A lunchbox belongs to one person, not everyone. The government looks
after shared things — roads, parks, libraries — not one person's things.
Knowing what is shared and what is personal is the key test."

**Example 4.** "At night, the streetlights on Willow Street come on. Who put
them there and keeps them working? The **town government** — streetlights
are a shared thing that keeps everyone safe after dark. Safety + shared
things: two government jobs in one example."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. "In your own words: what is a government?" *(Hint if stuck: "A group of
   people whose job is to...?" Accept any version of the one-sentence
   definition.)*
2. "A road has a big hole. Shared thing or one person's thing? Who fixes
   it?"
3. "A child's shoes wear out. Does the government buy new shoes? Why or why
   not?" *(Accept: "No — shoes belong to one person.")*
4. "Name one thing a government does, from the four in the lesson."

## Independent practice

The learner sorts the 7 cards (6 shared things + 1 house) into "shared —
government looks after it" and "belongs to one person." Then the learner
picks one shared-thing card and finishes the sentence (adult scribes): "The
government ___ the ___." (For example: "The government fixes the road.")
**Text alternative:** the learner points to the shared pile and mimes the
government job (mending, sweeping, shelving) instead of speaking.

## Application

"On your next walk or ride, spot one shared thing the government looks
after — a road, a park, a streetlight, a library. Tell someone what it is
and who looks after it."

## Exit check

The learner says the one-sentence definition (in any words) and names one
thing a government does. The adult records: *secure* (definition + one job),
*developing* (one job only, or definition with heavy prompting), *emerging*
(neither — reteach with the road/pothole example only, then re-ask).

## Supports

- **Language access:** pre-teach *government / shared* with the learner's
  home language alongside English; accept answers in any language.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to shared-thing cards or miming
  the job instead of speaking the sentence.
- **Movement:** arms wide for "everyone shares this," arms hugging self for
  "belongs to one person" — the sort becomes a movement game.

## Extension

"Government spotter": the learner draws one shared thing in their
neighborhood and labels it with 2–3 words ("park," "road," "library") for
the project wall.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners think the government is one person (the
mayor) or that it does everything. Correct both every time: "a *group* of
people" — the mayor leads it, but road workers, librarians, firefighters,
and officers do the jobs. And: "not your lunchbox" — the shared/personal
test fixes the does-everything error. **Answer-key reference:**
