# Lesson 1 — People in Authority

**Session 1 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can name people in charge of places and say one job each
one does.

**Materials:** 5 role cards the adult draws or prints before the session
(teacher at a board, principal at a school door, mayor at town hall steps,
firefighter with a hose, police officer at a crosswalk — the adult reads the
labels aloud; the learner need not read them); a simple three-column chart
labeled with pictures: *person / in charge of / one job*.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "What is one rule we agreed on in our
classroom?" (Any rule counts — the adult is warming up Unit 01's rules
language.) Retrieval: "Who reminds us about our classroom rules?" (Accept:
the teacher.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

Every place has a person in charge. That person has **authority** — that
means other people count on them to make decisions and keep things running.
Being in charge is not about being bossy. It is about having a
**responsibility**: a job someone is counted on to do.

Meet five people in authority in our fictional town, Willow Street:

- The **teacher** is in charge of learning in a classroom. Her job is to
  teach, to keep the class safe, and to help everyone learn.
- The **principal** is in charge of a whole school. His job is to help all
  the teachers, to keep the school safe, and to solve big school problems.
- The **mayor** is chosen to help lead a town or city. Her job is to help
  make town rules and to look after the town's shared things, like parks
  and roads.
- The **firefighter** is in charge of putting out fires and rescuing people.
  His job is to keep people safe from fire and to help in emergencies.
- The **police officer** helps keep people safe and makes sure people follow
  the town's rules. Her job is to help when someone is lost or hurt, and to
  keep the streets safe.

A teacher is in charge of one classroom. A principal is in charge of the
whole school. A mayor helps lead the whole town. Bigger place, bigger job —
but every one of these people is counted on by others.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** "The school bell rings and a new child arrives who does not
know where her classroom is. Who helps her? The **principal**? The teacher
of her class will welcome her — but the principal is in charge of the whole
school, so he makes sure every new child finds a class. The principal's
responsibility is the whole school, not just one room."

**Example 2.** "A tree falls across the road on Willow Street. Who is counted
on to clear it and keep drivers safe? Not the teacher — her job is the
classroom. The **mayor** helps lead the town, so the town's workers clear
the road. The mayor's responsibility is the town's shared things."

**Example 3.** "Smoke comes from a kitchen on Willow Street. Who is counted on
to put out the fire? The **firefighter**. That is exactly his responsibility:
fires and rescues. We do not try to fight a fire ourselves — we tell a
grown-up, and the grown-up gets the firefighters."

**Example 4.** "A child is lost in the park and cannot find her grown-up. Who
helps her? The **police officer**. Her responsibility is keeping people safe
and helping when someone is lost or hurt."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. "Who is in charge of learning in a classroom?" *(Hint if stuck: "She
   reads stories and teaches us letters.")*
2. "Who is in charge of a whole school?" *(Hint: "Bigger than one
   classroom.")*
3. "A fire starts in a wastebasket. Who is counted on to put it out — the
   mayor, the teacher, or the firefighter? Why?"
4. "Someone is lost and needs help finding their grown-up. Who helps — the
   principal or the police officer? Why?"

## Independent practice

The learner lays out the 5 role cards and, for each card the adult points to,
says the role name and one job that person does. The adult scribes the
learner's answers as a row of 5 marks (a check for role + job both right, a
half-mark for role only).
**Text alternative:** the learner points to the card and acts out the job
(holds a pretend hose, waves like a mayor greeting the town) instead of
naming it aloud.

## Application

"Tonight, tell someone: one person in authority you saw today (a teacher, a
principal, or a helper in uniform) and one job they were doing." The adult
notes whether the learner tries it unprompted the next day.

## Exit check

The learner names 3 of the 5 roles and one responsibility each, with cards
face-up as support. The adult records: *secure* (3 roles + jobs),
*developing* (3 roles, jobs shaky), *emerging* (0–2 — reteach with the
classroom and school pair first, then add town roles).

## Supports

- **Language access:** pre-teach *authority / responsibility* with the
  learner's home language alongside English; accept answers in any language.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to cards or acting out the job
  instead of naming aloud.
- **Movement:** stand tall like a mayor waving, mime holding a hose, march
  like an officer on patrol — one motion per role.

## Extension

"Authority detective": the learner listens during the day for someone being
"in charge" of something (a coach, a librarian, a bus driver) and reports one
example the next session — with the question "is that person a leader, or a
helper?" held open for Lesson 2.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners think "in charge" means "can tell everyone
what to do." Return to responsibility every time: the principal cannot make
the class do homework for fun; the principal's job is the school's safety and
learning. Another common mix-up: mayor vs. principal — anchor with place
size: classroom → teacher, school → principal, town → mayor. **Answer-key
reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L1.
