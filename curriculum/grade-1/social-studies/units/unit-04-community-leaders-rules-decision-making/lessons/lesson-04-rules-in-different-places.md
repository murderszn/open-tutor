# Lesson 4 — Rules in Different Places

**Session 7 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can sort rules by where they belong and say why a rule
exists.

**Materials:** 6 rule cards the adult draws or prints (raise your hand to
speak; walk in the hall; stay with your grown-up in a parking lot; do not
litter in the park; return library books on time; drive on the right side
of the road — adult reads labels aloud); three setting mats labeled with
pictures: classroom, school, town.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "What is one rule we agreed on in our
classroom, and why do we have it?" (Any rule + any reason counts — the adult
is warming up Unit 01's rules language.) Retrieval: "What is a rule for a
whole town called?" (A law.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

A **rule** is an agreement about what to do (or not do) in a place. Different
places have different rules, because different places have different jobs:

- **Classroom rules** help one class learn together: raise your hand to
  speak, listen while others talk, take care of shared crayons.
- **School rules** help a whole school stay safe and calm: walk in the hall,
  stay in your line, use a quiet voice near other classes.
- **Town rules — laws** — help a whole town live together: drive on the
  right side of the road, do not litter in the park, dogs on leashes.

Rules exist for reasons, and almost every reason is one of two things:

- **Safety:** the rule keeps bodies safe. We walk in the hall so nobody
  trips and falls. We stay with a grown-up in a parking lot because cars
  cannot see small children well.
- **Fairness:** the rule treats everyone right. We raise a hand so everyone
  gets a turn to speak. We return library books on time so the next reader
  can enjoy them too.

A rule is **fair** when it protects everyone the same way — not when it
helps one person and hurts another. If a rule feels unfair, people may talk
about it respectfully and try to change it. That is part of how communities
improve — and it is exactly what Lesson 5 practices.

What happens when someone breaks a rule? In our classroom, the answer is
never "get in trouble and that's it." The answer is: stop, fix what broke,
say sorry if someone was hurt, and try again. Fixing is more important than
punishing.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** "Rule: walk in the hall. Where does it belong — classroom,
school, or town? **School** — the hall belongs to the whole school, not one
classroom. Why does it exist? **Safety**: running in a hall full of people
means someone trips and falls. Setting + reason, both named."

**Example 2.** "Rule: return library books on time. Where does it belong?
This one is tricky — the library is a town place, but our class borrows
books for the classroom too. Accept **town** (the library serves the whole
town) or **school** (our school library) — what matters is the reason:
**fairness**. If one person keeps a book forever, nobody else can read it.
Fair rules share good things."

**Example 3.** "Rule: do not litter in the park. Where? **Town** — the park
belongs to everyone in town. Why? Both reasons fit: **safety** (broken glass
cuts feet) and **fairness** (everyone deserves a clean park). When both fit,
say so — rules often have two reasons."

**Example 4 (the deliberate error).** "Someone says: 'Rules are just for
stopping fun.' Is that right? No. Think of the hall rule: it stops running
— but it *gives* everyone a safe walk. Rules stop one thing so a better
thing can happen. Ask always: 'what does this rule protect?'"

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. "Rule: raise your hand to speak. Classroom, school, or town? Why does
   it exist — safety or fairness?"
2. "Rule: drive on the right side of the road. Where? Why?" *(Hint if
   stuck: "What would happen if everyone drove on any side?")*
3. "Rule: stay with your grown-up in a parking lot. Where? Why?"
4. "Is this rule fair: 'Only the tallest children may use the swings'?
   Why or why not?" *(Accept: "No — it is not fair; it leaves some children
   out for no safety reason.")*

## Independent practice

The learner sorts the 6 rule cards onto the three setting mats, then picks
one card and finishes the sentence (adult scribes): "The rule ___ exists
because ___." (For example: "The rule walk in the hall exists because
running could hurt someone.")
**Text alternative:** the learner points to the mat and mimes the reason
(holds up a stop hand for safety, holds out sharing hands for fairness)
instead of speaking the sentence.

## Application

"Notice one rule at home or at school today. Ask yourself: where does it
belong, and does it protect safety or fairness? Tell someone your answer."

## Exit check

The learner sorts 3 new rule cards by setting (adult reads them: "listen
while others talk," "dogs on leashes," "use a quiet voice near other
classes") and explains why one of them exists. The adult records: *secure*
(3 settings + 1 reason), *developing* (2 settings + reason attempted),
*emerging* (0–1 — reteach with the hall rule only, then re-sort).

## Supports

- **Language access:** pre-teach *rule / fair / safety* with the learner's
  home language alongside English; accept answers in any language.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to mats or miming the reason
  instead of speaking.
- **Movement:** freeze like a statue for "stop" rules, walk slowly for
  "walk in the hall" — the body remembers the setting.

## Extension

"Rule inventor": the learner invents one fair rule for the reading corner
("one book at a time, so everyone gets a turn") and says whether it protects
safety or fairness — a warm-up for the project decision.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners sort by where they *heard* the rule, not
where it *belongs* ("my teacher said walk in the hall, so it's a classroom
rule"). Correct with the place test: "whose place is the hall — one class,
or the whole school?" Also watch for "fair = I like it": fairness is about
everyone, not one child — the swings example exists to catch this.
