# Lesson 5 — Using a Source to Answer a Question

**Session 11 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can answer a question about the past by pointing to
evidence in a source, and I can say how a child long ago might have seen the
same thing differently from me.

**Materials:** one real source the adult chooses and previews: a printed
photograph of an old street (library book or archive print) OR a real old
object (an oil lamp, a wash basin, a hand tool) OR a short told story from
[practice.md](../practice.md). A question card: "How did people travel on
this street long ago?" (adapt the question to the source chosen). The Willow
Street illustration may be used for *practice wording only* — the adult says
so explicitly if it is used.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Name the three source kinds." (Quick Lesson
4 retrieval — picture cards allowed.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

A source is useful when it answers a **question**. First ask the question.
Then look (or listen) closely. Then answer with **evidence** — something you
can actually see or hear in the source.

The sentence frame is: "The [photograph / object / story] shows
______, so ______." The first blank is the evidence. The second blank is the
answer. If you cannot fill the first blank, you are guessing — go back and
look again.

Example with a photograph of an old street: Question — "How did people
travel on this street long ago?" Look — a horse and a cart are in the
picture, and there is no car. Answer — "The photograph shows a horse pulling
a cart, so people traveled by horse and cart on this street."

Now the second skill: **perspective**. Perspective is how something looks or
feels to a particular person. A child long ago who washed clothes in a basin
every week might think a basin is normal and a washing machine is strange.
You might think a washing machine is normal and a basin is strange. Neither
child is wrong — they just grew up with different normal things. When we
compare a child's day long ago with a child's day today, we are comparing
perspectives.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1 (photograph evidence).** Question: "How did people light this
street long ago?" The adult points at the photo. "I see a tall lamp with a
flame inside, and a person reaching up to it. The photograph shows a lamp
with a flame, so people lit the street with a flame lamp. I did not say
'electric light' — there is no electric light in this picture, so that would
be guessing."

**Example 2 (object evidence).** Question: "How did people wash clothes long
ago?" The adult holds up a wash basin. "I see a basin, soapy water marks, and
a scrub board. The object shows a basin for scrubbing, so people washed
clothes by scrubbing them in a basin. I cannot tell from the basin alone
*whose* clothes these were — that would be guessing."

**Example 3 (story evidence + perspective).** The adult tells: "Nia, a child
in Willow Street long ago, carries water to the basin every morning and
thinks nothing of it — it is just morning." Question: "How would Nia feel
about a washing machine?" Model: "The story shows Nia doing washing by hand
every morning, so a machine that does it alone might feel surprising — or
even a little scary, because it is new. And I might feel the opposite about
a basin: surprising, because it is new to me. Same washing, two
perspectives."

**Example 4 (guessing vs. evidence).** "The photograph shows a horse. Can I
answer 'what was the horse's name?' No — the photograph does not show a
name. Some questions a source cannot answer. Saying 'I cannot tell from this
source' is a good historian answer."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. Using the session's source, the adult asks the question card question.
   "Answer with the frame: 'The ___ shows ___, so ___.'" *(Hint if stuck:
   "Point to the part that answers it first, then say the sentence.")*
2. "What is one question this source *cannot* answer? Why not?"
3. Perspective task: "Nia long ago sees your washing machine. What might she
   think or feel? Why?" *(Accept any feeling with a reason tied to Nia's
   normal — "surprised, because she washes by hand.")*
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "The picture shows a horse, so the horse's
   name was Brownie." "Is that evidence or guessing? Fix the sentence."
   *(Guessing — the picture shows no name.)*

## Independent practice

The learner picks one of two sources the adult offers (a photo or an object),
answers a question about it with the evidence frame, and answers one
perspective question ("What would a child long ago think of this thing in
our room?"). The adult scribes both answers. **Text alternative:** the
learner points to the evidence in the source, then points to a feeling card
(surprised / happy / unsure) for the perspective answer, and the adult
scribes the reason the learner dictates.

## Application

"Ask someone: 'What is something you used long ago that children today do
not use?' Listen for the answer, then tell me one thing they said and whether
it was a photograph, an object, or a story." (The answer is a told story —
the adult checks the learner names it as a story source the next session.)

## Exit check

The learner answers one past-question with evidence from a source and gives
one perspective comparison (long-ago child vs. today child). The adult
records: *secure* (evidence sentence + perspective with a reason),
*developing* (one of the two, or evidence with a prompt), *emerging*
(neither — reteach with the photograph example only).

## Supports

- **Language access:** the evidence frame is posted as pictures (eye → source
  → speech bubble); accept answers in the home language.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to evidence, then to feeling cards,
  instead of full sentences.
- **Movement:** the learner steps to the "evidence" spot to point, then to
  the "answer" spot to speak — the two-step routine makes the frame physical.

## Extension

"Two sources, one question": the adult asks the same question of a photograph
and a told story. The learner says whether the answers agree, and what to do
if they do not (look for a third source, or say "we are not sure yet").

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners answer from what they already know ("cars
exist, so the old street had cars") instead of from the source. Keep
covering the picture and asking, "But what does *this source* show?" The
frame's first blank is the cure. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L5.
