# Lesson 4 — Kinds of Historical Sources

**Session 9 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can name three kinds of historical sources — photograph,
object, and story people tell — and tell them apart.

**Materials:** three real, safe items the adult gathers: a printed photograph
(any old family-free picture, for example a printed photo of an old street
from a library book — never the learner's private photos), a household object
with an old-fashioned form (a candle, a washcloth, a wooden spoon), and a
short story the adult tells aloud (the Willow Street lantern story from
[practice.md](../practice.md) works as the told story). Source-kind cards:
camera picture (photograph), hand holding a thing (object), talking mouth
(story).

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Name one thing that changed between long ago
and today, and one thing that stayed the same." Quick Lesson 3 retrieval.

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

How do we *know* anything about long ago? Nobody alive today was there. We
know because of **sources** — things that give information about the past.

There are three kinds in this unit:

1. A **photograph** is a picture taken with a camera of a real moment. A photo
   shows what the camera saw: the clothes, the street, the lamp. It does not
   tell us what people were thinking.
2. An **object** is a real thing from the past you can look at or hold: a
   lamp, a wash basin, an old spoon. An object shows how something was made
   and used. It does not come with words explaining itself.
3. A **story people tell** is words passed from person to person about the
   past. A grandparent, a neighbor, or an elder storyteller might tell how
   things were. Stories carry feelings and reasons — but a story can change
   a little each time it is told, so we check it against other sources when
   we can.

**Important, say this out loud:** a drawing someone made up — like our
Willow Street picture — is **not** a historical source. It is an
illustration: it helps us practice, but it is not evidence about the real
past. A photograph of a real street *is* evidence. The difference is whether
it shows something real that actually happened, or something imagined to
help us learn.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** The adult holds up the printed photograph. "Is this a
photograph, an object, or a story? It is a picture taken with a camera of a
real moment — so it is a **photograph**. What can it tell us? What people
wore, what the street looked like. What can it not tell us? What those
people were thinking."

**Example 2.** The adult holds up the wooden spoon. "Is this a photograph, an
object, or a story? It is a real thing I can hold — so it is an **object**.
What can it tell us? What it was made of, how big it is, what job it did.
What can it not tell us? Who exactly held it, unless someone tells us."

**Example 3.** The adult tells two sentences of a story aloud. "Did I show you
a picture? No. Did I hand you a thing? No. I told you words — so this is a
**story people tell**. What can it tell us? How people felt, why they did
something. What must we remember? Stories can change a little each telling,
so we check them when we can."

**Example 4 (the illustration test).** The adult shows the Willow Street
illustration. "Is this a photograph? No — nobody photographed Willow Street;
it is a made-up town. Is it an object from the past? No. Is it a story
people tell? No — it is a drawing made to help us practice. So it is none of
the three. It is an **illustration**, and illustrations are practice tools,
not evidence."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. The adult shows a second printed photo. "Photograph, object, or story?
   How do you know?"
2. The adult holds up a candle. "Photograph, object, or story? How do you
   know?"
3. The adult tells one sentence ("Long ago, the lamp-lighter walked the
   street at dusk"). "Photograph, object, or story? How do you know?"
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "This drawing of Willow Street proves
   people used oil lamps." "Is that right? What is wrong with that sentence?
   Fix it." *(A drawing cannot prove a real past fact; it can only help us
   practice.)*

## Independent practice

The learner sorts 6 source cards (2 photographs, 2 objects, 2 story
prompts — the adult reads each aloud) into three piles, then names each pile.
**Text alternative:** the learner points to the camera / hand / talking-mouth
card for each example while the adult sorts.

## Application

"Find one source in this room: a photo on the wall, an old object, or a
story someone could tell about this room. Tell me which kind it is and one
thing it could teach us about the past." The adult notes the kind named.

## Exit check

The learner names all three source kinds (with picture-card prompts if
needed) and sorts 2 new examples correctly. The adult records: *secure*
(three kinds named + 2 sorts), *developing* (two kinds or one sort),
*emerging* (0–1 — reteach with photograph vs. object only).

## Supports

- **Language access:** pre-teach *photograph / object / story* with the
  picture cards and the home language; accept answers in any language.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to kind cards or sorting cards
  instead of naming aloud.
- **Movement:** mime each kind — click a pretend camera, hold up a pretend
  thing, cup hands around the mouth to tell.

## Extension

"Source hunt": the learner walks the room with the adult and collects (by
pointing, not moving) one example of each kind, then explains one thing
each example cannot tell us.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners call every picture a photograph — including
drawings. Keep asking the test question: "Was this taken with a camera of a
real moment, or drawn by someone?" That single question carries the whole
lesson. **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L4.
