# Lesson 2 — Ordering Events on a Timeline

**Session 3 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can put events in order on a timeline, earliest first, and
explain one order with "before" or "after."

**Materials:** a long paper strip (the timeline); 4 picture cards for one
morning: waking up, eating breakfast, coming to class, playing outside
(the adult reads each card aloud); arrow cards for "before" and "after."

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Is breakfast past, present, or future right
now?" (Quick Lesson 1 retrieval — any reasonable answer with reasoning
counts.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

A **timeline** is a line of events in **order** — the order they happened.
The earliest event goes first, on the left. Then the next one. Then the next
one. We read a timeline the way we read a route map: from the start to the
end.

Two helper words tell order. **Before** means earlier than something else.
**After** means later than something else. Breakfast comes *before* class.
Class comes *after* breakfast. Both sentences say the same order — they just
start from different ends.

A timeline does not have to be about a whole lifetime. A morning is enough.
A day is enough. Later, in Unit 07, timelines will hold whole lives — but the
skill is the same: earliest first, then next, then next.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** The adult lays out three cards out of order: class, breakfast,
waking up. "Which happened first? Waking up — you cannot eat breakfast before
you wake up. So waking up goes first, on the left. Next is breakfast. Last is
class. Now read it back: waking up, breakfast, class. Earliest first."

**Example 2.** "Say it with *before*: 'Breakfast comes before class.' Now say
the same order with *after*: 'Class comes after breakfast.' The order did
not change — only the word changed. If someone says 'class comes before
breakfast,' we check: did class really happen earlier? No — so that sentence
is wrong, and we fix it."

**Example 3 (counting positions).** "Point to the first event. Point to the
last event. How many events are on our strip? Three. The middle one is
breakfast — it comes after waking up and before class."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 4 tasks)

1. The adult shuffles the 3 morning cards. "Put them in order, earliest
   first." *(Hint if stuck: "What did you do first of all?")*
2. "Point to the event that comes *after* breakfast."
3. "Say a sentence with *before* about waking up and class."
4. Error-analysis: the adult deliberately places class before breakfast.
   "Is this right? How do you know? Fix it and tell me why."

## Independent practice

The learner orders a new 4-event strip on their own: waking up, getting
dressed, eating breakfast, coming to class — plus one distractor the adult
adds: "playing outside later" (which belongs last, after class, if the
learner's day works that way; the adult accepts any order the learner can
defend with "before/after" reasoning, and scribes the defense). **Text
alternative:** the learner points to cards in order while the adult moves
them, instead of handling the cards.

## Application

"Draw a 3-event strip of what you will do after this lesson: first, next,
last." The adult labels it with the learner's words. This strip stays in
private storage.

## Exit check

The learner orders 3 events earliest to latest and explains one placement
with "before" or "after." The adult records: *secure* (correct order +
explanation), *developing* (correct order, explanation needs a prompt),
*emerging* (cannot order yet — reteach with only 2 events).

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach *before / after* with gestures (point back /
  point forward) and the home language alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing in order, or numbering cards 1–2–3
  with adult help, instead of speaking sentences.
- **Movement:** the learner walks a floor timeline, stepping on each event
  card in order while saying "first… next… last…"

## Extension

"Backward timeline": the adult gives the order last-to-first and the learner
flips it to earliest-first, then checks it by reading it aloud.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners order by size, importance, or liking
("playing outside is my favorite, so it goes first") rather than by time.
Keep asking the time question: "Did that *happen* first, or do you just
*like* it most?" **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md)
§L2.
