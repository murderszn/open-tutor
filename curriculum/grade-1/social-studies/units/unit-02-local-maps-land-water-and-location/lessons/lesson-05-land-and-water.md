# Lesson 5 — Land and Water on Maps and Globes

**Session 9 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can point to land and water on a map and a globe, and name
one landform and one water body I have seen.

**Materials:** a globe or world map (adult-side); blue and green crayons; the
bird's-eye illustration.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Point to something blue in our neighborhood
illustration. Is it land or water?" (Water — the river and the pond.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

Earth has two big parts: **land** — the solid ground we walk on — and
**water** — rivers, lakes, ponds, and oceans. Map-makers made an agreement
long ago so every map is easy to read: **water is blue, land is green** (or
sometimes brown for mountains). Because everyone follows the agreement, you
can read any map in the world: blue means water, green means land.

A **globe** is a ball-shaped model of Earth — the whole planet, round like
the real thing. Spin it slowly. See all the blue? Most of Earth is water. The
green and brown parts are land. The water has names: the huge waters are
**oceans**, the smaller ones are **lakes** and **ponds**, and the long winding
ones are **rivers**. Land has names too: flat land, **hills**, and tall
**mountains**.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** *The adult points to the river in the illustration, then spins
the globe to blue.* "Our illustration's river is blue — water, following the
agreement. Now the globe: all this blue is water too — oceans, much bigger
than our river, but the same blue agreement. Blue always means water, whether
it is a pond or an ocean."

**Example 2.** "Have *you* seen water outside? A puddle after rain, a creek,
a lake on a trip — all water. Have you seen land shaped in a special way? A
hill you climbed, a flat field? Those shapes have names: a hill is land that
goes up; a river is water that flows. When you point to the globe and say
'water,' you are reading the map-maker's agreement like a geographer."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 5 tasks)

1. "Point to water on the globe. Now point to land." *(Blue vs. green/brown.)*
2. "In our illustration, point to two different waters." *(River and pond.)*
3. "Name one water you have seen in real life." *(Any: puddle, creek, lake,
   ocean, bathtub — accept all, then sort: which are outside waters?)*
4. "Name one land shape you have seen." *(Hill, flat field, mountain on a
   trip.)*
5. "If I color a map and make the river green, what is wrong?" *(Breaks the
   agreement — green means land; readers would be confused.)*

## Independent practice

The learner colors a simple outline map (adult draws: one winding river, one
round pond, land around them): all water blue, all land green. Then they label
(or dictate) one water body and one land shape with its name. **Text
alternative:** the learner points to pre-colored blue/green areas and the adult
names them for confirmation.

## Application

"On your walk this week, spot one water and one land shape. Tell the adult:
'The water I saw was ___. The land shape I saw was ___.'"

## Exit check

*The adult shows the globe.* "Point to land. Point to water." Then: "Name one
landform and one water body you have seen." The adult records: *secure*
(points correctly + names one of each from experience), *developing* (points
correctly but cannot name from experience), *emerging* (confuses blue/green —
replay Example 1 with the illustration before the globe).

## Supports

- **Language access:** *land*, *water*, *globe* taught with real touch first
  (soil, water in a cup, a ball) before the map meanings.
- **Alternate response mode:** sorting blue/green cards into "land" and
  "water" piles instead of naming.
- **Movement:** "be the water" — the learner flows like a river (wavy arms)
  vs. "be the land" (stands still like a hill) when the adult calls each.

## Extension

The learner predicts: "If we flew over our neighborhood like a bird, where
would we see blue?" They check on the next walk. (No correction if the
neighborhood has no visible water — "no blue nearby" is a correct geographic
observation.)

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners think blue = "the ocean only" or that
rivers are land because "you can walk next to them." Keep returning to the
agreement: *blue means water, any water.* Do not introduce salt vs. fresh
water — out of band. **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md)
§L5 for acceptable landform/water-body answers and globe-pointing checks.
