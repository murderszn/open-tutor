# Lesson 4 — My Route Map

**Session 7 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can name the landmarks on a route in order, and start a
route map with symbols and a key.

**Materials:** paper, crayons; the adult pre-thinks one real route the learner
knows well (front door to a park, school, store, or bus stop).

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "What is a key on a map?" (It tells what each
symbol means.) "Point to the key on your bedroom key from last time."

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

A **route** is the way you go from one place to another. Every route has a
start, an end, and things you pass on the way. The buildings and places that
help you know where you are — the big tree, the red house, the corner store —
are called **landmarks**. Good travelers remember landmarks: "turn at the big
tree" works even when street names do not.

A route map shows one route from above. It does not show the whole
neighborhood — only the way. It uses symbols for the landmarks and a key that
explains them, and it shows the order: first this, then this, then this. If
someone else can follow your route map and arrive, your map works.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** *The adult draws a simple route: start (house symbol) → big
tree symbol → corner store symbol → park symbol (end), with a key.* "Our
route: home to the park. Landmarks in order: first the big tree, then the
corner store, then the park. I read the key: green triangle = big tree. If I
follow the symbols in order, I arrive. Notice what I did *not* draw: the
houses across the street, the other park. A route map shows only the way."

**Example 2.** "Now the same route, told wrong: 'Go to the park. Pass the
store. Oh wait, first the tree.' The landmarks are right but the *order* is
wrong — a traveler following that would get lost. On a route map, order is
everything: the line of travel connects the landmarks first-to-last."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 5 tasks)

1. "Think of our real route: from our front door to [landmark]. What is the
   *first* landmark we pass?" *(Adult confirms or corrects gently.)*
2. "What is the *last* place — where the route ends?"
3. "Name one landmark in the middle."
4. "Say the whole route in order: first ___, then ___, then ___."
5. "Which of these would you put on a route map: the big tree we pass, or a
   house three streets away we never see?" *(Only what is on the way.)*

## Independent practice

The learner starts their route map: draws the start (home), the end
(landmark), and at least one middle landmark as simple symbols, connected in
order by a line of travel. They begin a key with at least two symbols.
Finished or not, the adult dates it — Session 8's walk will complete it.
**Text alternative:** the learner places pre-drawn symbol cards in order on a
long strip of paper and dictates the key.

## Application

"Tell someone at home your route in order — first, then, last — without
showing the map. If they can picture it, your landmark memory is strong."

## Exit check

"Name the three parts of a route." (Start, landmarks in order, end.) "Show me
the line of travel on your map — which way does the traveler go?" The adult
records: *secure* (3 landmarks in order + line of travel), *developing* (2
landmarks or order shaky), *emerging* (cannot sequence — walk the real route
tomorrow narrating first/then/last before drawing).

## Supports

- **Language access:** *route* and *landmark* taught by walking a mini-route
  inside the home first ("our route: couch to kitchen; landmark: the rug").
- **Alternate response mode:** sequencing picture cards of the landmarks
  instead of drawing.
- **Movement:** "be the traveler" — the learner walks a taped line on the
  floor, stopping at landmark cards in order.

## Extension

The learner draws the *same* route a second way — a different path to the same
landmark (if one exists) — and compares: which route is shorter? Which has
more landmarks? No measuring; compare by landmark count and walking memory.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners draw everything they know (the whole
neighborhood) instead of one route, or draw landmarks in random positions
with no travel line. Keep asking: "Is this on the way? Which comes first?"
The Session 8 walk exists to fix exactly this. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L4 for acceptable landmark counts and key
entries. Never map the learner's home address or full street name — "home"
and "the park" are enough.
