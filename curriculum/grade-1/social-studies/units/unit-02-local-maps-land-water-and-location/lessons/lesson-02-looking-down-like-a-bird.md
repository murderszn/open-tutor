# Lesson 2 — Looking Down Like a Bird

**Session 3 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can explain what a map is, and I can name things I see in
a picture made from above.

**Materials:** the [bird's-eye neighborhood illustration](../assets/neighborhood-from-above.png)
(printed or on screen); 3–4 small objects on a table (for the demo).

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Yesterday we learned location words. Point
to something *above* you and something *below* you." (Quick retrieval.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

Stand up and look at this table. You see the *sides* of things — the side of
the cup, the side of the book. Now imagine you are a bird, flying high above
the table and looking straight down. You would not see sides anymore. You
would see *tops* — the top of the cup looks like a circle, the top of the
book looks like a rectangle. That is called the **bird's-eye view**: what
something looks like from high above.

A **map** is a picture of a place drawn as if seen from above — a bird's-eye
view on paper. Map-makers draw from above because then everything fits: the
whole street, the whole park, the whole neighborhood, all on one page. You
cannot fit a whole neighborhood in a drawing made from the sidewalk.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** *The adult holds a cup at the learner's eye level, then holds
it high above and looks down at it with the learner.* "From the side, the cup
is tall — you see its side. From above, the cup is a circle — you see only
its top. Same cup, two views. A map always uses the from-above view."

**Example 2.** *The adult shows the neighborhood illustration.* "This picture
was made from above, like a bird sees it. Do you see the sides of the houses,
like windows and doors? No — from above you see only the *roofs*. The blue
winding shape is a river seen from above; from the sidewalk it would look
like water flowing past you. The gray curving lines are roads seen from
above. This picture is almost a map — it is missing only the symbols and the
key, which we learn next."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 5 tasks)

1. "Look at our illustration. Point to something blue. What do you think it
   is, seen from above?" *(River or pond — water.)*
2. "Point to the gray curving lines. What are they from above?" *(Roads.)*
3. "Find the houses. How do you know they are houses if you cannot see doors?"
   *(Roofs seen from above; accept "the red/brown squares.")*
4. "Is this picture made from the sidewalk or from above? How do you know?"
   *(From above — we see tops/roofs, no sides.)*
5. "Tell me in your own words: what is a map?" *(Accept "a picture of a place
   from above" or any close wording.)*

## Independent practice

The learner studies the illustration for one minute, then closes their eyes
(or the adult covers it) and names three things they remember seeing from
above. The adult scribes the list. **Text alternative:** use the text-only
description in [assets/README.md](../assets/README.md) — the learner listens
and names three things from the description instead of the picture.

## Application

"Lie on your back under the table (or crouch low) and look up. Then stand on
a chair with the adult holding you safe and look down at the table. Tell the
adult one thing that looks different from above. That is the bird's-eye view
at work."

## Exit check

"Show me with your hands: how does a bird see the table — from the side or
from above?" (Hands flat above, looking down.) "And what is a map?" The adult
records: *secure* (defines a map as an above-view picture + names the view
difference), *developing* (says "a picture of a place" without the above
part), *emerging* (cannot distinguish the views — redo the cup demo).

## Supports

- **Language access:** *bird's-eye view* taught with arms-out "flying" first,
  word second; home-language equivalent welcome.
- **Alternate response mode:** pointing to roofs/roads/river in the
  illustration instead of naming.
- **Movement:** "be the bird" — the learner stands, flaps arms, and the adult
  lifts picture cards to "fly over" them.

## Extension

The learner draws the classroom rug from above (a rectangle with circle
"heads") and from the side (children sitting), then tells the adult which one
is more like a map and why.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners think a map is "a drawing of a place" with
no view specified, and then draw side-view houses on their maps. Keep asking
"would a bird see that?" until the above-view habit sticks. **Answer-key
reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L2 for acceptable map
definitions and illustration-spotting answers.
