# Lesson 3 — Map Symbols and Keys

**Session 5 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can use a map key to tell what each symbol stands for.

**Materials:** paper, crayons; 5 simple symbol cards drawn by the adult before
the session (blue wavy line, green triangle tree, red square house, gray
curving line road, blue circle pond); the bird's-eye illustration for reference.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "What is a map?" (A picture of a place seen
from above.) "Would a bird see the door of a house?" (No — only the roof.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

A map-maker has a problem: a real house is big, but on a map there is only a
tiny space for it. So map-makers do not draw the whole thing. They draw a
**symbol** — a small, simple drawing that *stands for* a real thing. A little
blue wavy line can stand for a whole river. A little green triangle can stand
for a whole tree.

But how does the reader know what each small drawing means? The map-maker
adds a **key**. The key is a box on the map that shows each symbol next to
its meaning: blue wavy line = river, green triangle = tree. You read the key
first, then you can read the whole map. Without a key, the symbols are just a
guessing game.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** *The adult shows the blue wavy line card.* "What could this
stand for on a map? It is blue and wavy — water that moves. On our
illustration, the river looks like this from above. So on a real map, this
symbol could mean *river*. But I am only guessing — a map-maker would put it
in the key: blue wavy line = river. The key turns my guess into knowledge."

**Example 2.** *The adult draws a quick map: three red squares, one green
triangle, a gray curving line, and a key box listing all three.* "Read the
key with me: red square = house, green triangle = tree, gray line = road.
Now read the map: how many houses? Three. Is there a tree next to a road?
Yes — the triangle sits beside the gray line. I did not visit this place; the
key told me everything. That is the power of symbols."

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 5 tasks)

1. *Show the pond card (blue circle).* "What could this stand for? Check the
   color." *(Water that does not move — a pond or lake.)*
2. "If I draw a brown square for a school, where do I write what it means?"
   *(In the key.)*
3. *Cover the key on the adult's quick map.* "Can you still read this map?
   Why not?" *(No — without the key the symbols are guesses.)*
4. "Point to the river in our bird's-eye illustration. If you were the
   map-maker, what symbol would you draw for it?" *(Blue wavy line.)*
5. "Make up a symbol for *your bed* for a bedroom map. Tell me what it is."
   *(Any simple drawing; the learner must state its meaning — that is the key
   habit.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws a 3-symbol key of their own: one symbol each for something
in their bedroom (bed, window, toy box — any three). Each symbol gets a label
or dictated meaning. **Text alternative:** the learner chooses from pre-drawn
symbol cards and dictates what each stands for while the adult writes the key.

## Application

"Find one map in real life this week — a mall directory, a park map, a page
in an atlas, a phone map with the adult. Find its key (it might be called a
*legend*) and tell the adult one symbol and its meaning."

## Exit check

The adult shows the 5 symbol cards in mixed order: "Tell me what each one
stands for." The adult records: *secure* (4–5 correct with key language —
"the blue circle stands for a pond"), *developing* (3 correct, or names things
without "stands for"), *emerging* (0–2 — replay the quick-map demo with the
key covered and uncovered).

## Supports

- **Language access:** *symbol* and *key* taught with the "secret code" game
  (a star drawn on paper = "hug") before the map context.
- **Alternate response mode:** matching cards to meaning cards instead of
  naming aloud.
- **Movement:** floor map — symbols taped to the floor; the learner walks to
  the symbol the adult names and says its meaning.

## Extension

"Map detective": the learner draws a 5-symbol map of an imaginary island and
writes (or dictates) a key, then tests the adult — can the adult read the map
using only the key? Swap roles.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners draw detailed pictures instead of symbols
(a whole house with windows) and skip the key ("everyone knows what it is").
Insist on the rule: *every symbol on the map must appear in the key.* Praise
simple symbols — simple is the skill. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L3 for the five card meanings and
acceptable learner-made symbols.
