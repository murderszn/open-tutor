# Lesson 6 — Places Have Features

**Session 11 · about 15–20 minutes.**

**Learner goal:** I can name one cultural feature and one environmental
feature of my neighborhood, and tell how they are different.

**Materials:** the bird's-eye illustration; paper, crayons.

**Readiness check (1 minute):** "Point to land in our illustration. Point to
water." (Blue/green agreement from Lesson 5.)

## Explicit teaching

*(Adult reads or paraphrases; pause at the bold words.)*

Every place has **features** — the things that make it what it is. Geographers
sort features into two groups. **Cultural features** are things *people made*:
houses, schools, roads, park benches, playgrounds. **Environmental features**
are things people did *not* make: rivers, hills, trees, ponds, rocks. The
test is one question: "Did people make it?" Yes → cultural. No →
environmental.

Why sort them? Because it tells the story of a place: what nature gave it,
and what people built on it. Our neighborhood has both — and now you can read
both on a map.

## Worked examples (adult models the reasoning aloud)

**Example 1.** *The adult points to the school in the illustration.* "The
school with the playground — did people make it? Yes. So it is a *cultural*
feature." *Points to the river.* "The river — did people make it? No, water
flows on its own. So the river is an *environmental* feature. One question,
two groups."

**Example 2.** "Tricky one: the park trees. Did people make trees? No — trees
grow on their own, so trees are environmental. But the *park* — the paths, the
benches, the planted rows — people arranged those, so the park as a *place*
is cultural, even though its trees are environmental. One thing can have both
kinds of features. Geographers love tricky ones; the question still works:
*did people make it?*"

## Guided practice (adult prompts, learner responds; 6 tasks)

1. "In our illustration: the houses — cultural or environmental?" *(Cultural
   — people built them.)*
2. "The pond?" *(Environmental — water on its own.)*
3. "The roads?" *(Cultural — people paved them.)*
4. "The round green trees in the park?" *(Environmental — trees grow.)*
5. "Think of our real street: name one cultural feature." *(Accept any:
   sidewalk, mailbox, school, store.)*
6. "Name one environmental feature on our street." *(Accept any: tree, hill,
   creek, sky — sun/sky count as not-made-by-people.)*

## Independent practice

Two-column sort: the learner draws (or the adult draws and the learner sorts
picture cards into) two columns — "People made it" and "People did not make
it" — with at least two items in each column from the neighborhood. The adult
scribes labels. **Text alternative:** the learner points to illustration
features while the adult sorts them into the two columns aloud for
confirmation.

## Application

"Tonight, look out the window with someone at home. Find one cultural feature
and one environmental feature together. Teach them the test question: 'Did
people make it?'"

## Exit check

"Tell me one cultural feature of our neighborhood and one environmental
feature. How are they different?" (One people made, one they did not.) The
adult records: *secure* (one of each + states the difference), *developing*
(one of each but cannot state the difference), *emerging* (cannot sort —
replay Example 1 with only two clear items: house vs. river).

## Supports

- **Language access:** *cultural* and *environmental* are long words — anchor
  them as "people-made" and "nature-made" first, formal words second.
- **Alternate response mode:** thumbs-up (people made it) / thumbs-down
  (nature made it) for each feature named.
- **Movement:** two floor zones labeled with picture signs; the learner stands
  in the zone matching each feature the adult names.

## Extension

"Feature detective": the learner picks one feature (e.g., the school garden)
and argues *both* sides — what is cultural about it, what is environmental —
then decides which side wins and why. There is no single right answer; the
reasoning is the skill.

## Teacher note

**Common misconception:** learners classify by material ("wood is nature, so
the bench is environmental") instead of by making ("people built the bench,
so it is cultural"). Keep repeating the test question: *did people make it?*
Planted gardens and mowed lawns are the classic edge cases — let the learner
argue them; the discussion matters more than the verdict. **Answer-key
reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L6 for acceptable sorts and
edge-case rulings.
