# Lesson 01 — Subtraction Is a Missing-Addition Problem

**Student goal:** I can solve a subtraction by asking "what goes with the
smaller number to make the bigger number?" — 10 − 8 is really 8 + ? = 10.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- 20 counters in two colors, a ten frame, a cloth
- Number cards 0–20

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"8 + ? = 10." The learner counts up from 8: "9, 10" — two hops, so ? = 2.
Then "5 + ? = 12": count up "6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12" — seven hops, so ? = 7.
Missing-addend counting must be quick — it is the engine of this lesson.
If it is shaky, re-run Unit 02's counting-up game before continuing.

## Teaching

Subtraction has a secret: every take-away problem is hiding an addition
problem. When we see 10 − 8, we can ask a friendlier question instead:
"8 plus what makes 10?" The answer to the addition question IS the answer
to the subtraction.

> "Here are 10 counters. I take 8 away. How many are left? Don't count
> down — flip the question. 8 plus what makes 10? Count up from 8: 9, 10.
> That's 2 hops. So 8 + 2 = 10, and 10 − 8 = **2.**"

**Key vocabulary:** *unknown addend* (the missing number in an addition),
*take away*, *how many are left*. The standard's own example is exactly
this move: subtract 10 − 8 by finding the number that makes 10 when added
to 8.

**Why it works (say it aloud):** addition and subtraction undo each other.
If 8 + 2 = 10, then taking 8 away from 10 must leave 2 — nothing else could
be left, because 8 and 2 are the two parts of 10.

## Worked example 1 — 10 − 8 with counters

The adult builds 8 counters in plain sight and asks the flipped question
first; the remaining 2 counters stay under the cloth until the check.

> "Start at 8. Count up to 10: 9… 10. Two hops. So 8 + 2 = 10, and
> 10 − 8 = **2.** Now check: uncover — 8 visible plus 2 hidden is 10, and
> the hidden pile really has 2."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we did not count the 8 again. Counting up
starts *after* the number we already have.

## Worked example 2 — 12 − 5, counting up a longer hop

> "12 − 5. Flip it: 5 plus what makes 12? Count up from 5: 6, 7, 8, 9, 10,
> 11, 12. Count the hops: seven. So 5 + 7 = 12, and 12 − 5 = **7.**
> Check with counters: 5 and 7 together really make 12."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** longer hops are fine — the method never
changes. Flipping the question turns every subtraction into a counting-up
game the learner already knows.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds.

1. 9 − 6: flip to 6 + ? = 9. Count up: 7, 8, 9 — 3 hops. (Expected: 3.)
2. 11 − 4: flip to 4 + ? = 11. Count up 5…11 — 7 hops. (Expected: 7.)
3. 13 − 9: flip to 9 + ? = 13. Count up 10, 11, 12, 13 — 4 hops.
   (Expected: 4.)
4. 10 − 3: flip to 3 + ? = 10. Count up 4…10 — 7 hops. (Expected: 7.)
5. Error analysis: the adult solves 10 − 7 by counting "7, 8, 9, 10" and
   announces "? = 4 — four numbers!" Ask: "Did I count right?"
   (Expected: no — 7 was already counted; only the new numbers 8, 9, 10
   are hops, so 7 + 3 = 10 and 10 − 7 = 3. Counting the start number is
   the classic off-by-one slip.)
6. The learner models 14 − 6 alone with counters while the adult watches:
   6 + ? = 14, count up 7…14 — 8 hops. (Expected: 8.)

## Independent practice

The learner gets 14 − 6 (again, fresh counters) and 12 − 8. They flip each
to an addition question, count up aloud, and record the difference. The
adult observes silently: do they count up from the subtrahend, without
recounting it?

*Alternate response mode:* point to each hop number on the number line
instead of saying it; adult scribes the equations.

## Application

Sticker book: "Maya's book holds 11 stickers. 4 are already stuck in.
How many empty spaces are left?" The learner flips it: 4 + ? = 11, count
up 5…11 — 7 hops. (Expected: 7 empty spaces; 11 − 4 = 7.)

## Exit check

"15 − 9. Flip it and count up." Expected: "9 + ? = 15 — count up 10, 11,
12, 13, 14, 15, six hops, so 15 − 9 = 6." Record: secure / developing /
not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "what goes with ___ to make ___?" by acting
  it out first, then naming it *unknown addend*. The flipped question can
  be asked in the learner's own words.
- **Alternate response mode:** cover-and-count — build the subtrahend,
  cover it, and count up with fingers showing each hop.
- **Floor-play variant:** a floor number line; the learner stands on the
  subtrahend and hops forward, counting hops, until landing on the
  minuend.

## Extension

15 − 9 by unknown-addend and then check it a second way (count back:
14, 13, 12, 11, 10, 9 — six hops back, same answer 6). Or: invent a
subtraction whose unknown-addend hop count is exactly 5, and prove it
with counters.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) counting the start number as a hop
(off-by-one — always ask "is that number already counted?"); (2) flipping
to the wrong addition (e.g. 12 − 5 flipped as 12 + ? = 5 — remind: the
addition must *make the start number*); (3) counting all the way from one
instead of counting up — re-run the cover game.
