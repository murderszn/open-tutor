# Lesson 03 — Back to Ten

**Student goal:** I can split the number I take away so the first step
lands exactly on ten — 13 − 4 is 13 − 3 − 1 = 10 − 1 = 9.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- Two ten frames, 20 counters in two colors, a number line to 20

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Show 13 on ten frames (a full ten plus 3). "How many loose ones are
there?" (Expected: 3.) "How many must we take away to land exactly on
ten?" (Expected: 3.) Then with 15: "Loose ones? Take away to reach ten?"
(Expected: 5 ones; take 5.) Landing on ten must be instant — it is the
hinge of this strategy.

## Teaching

Ten is the friendliest number there is. When we subtract from a teen
number, we can take away in two steps: first take the loose ones, landing
exactly on ten, then take whatever is left of the subtrahend from ten.

> "13 − 4. Look at the frames: a full ten and 3 loose ones. The 4 we take
> away splits into 3 and 1 — because 3 is how many loose ones there are.
> First step: 13 − 3 = 10. We took 3, but we owe 1 more: 10 − 1 = **9.**
> So 13 − 4 = 9."

**Key vocabulary:** *back to ten* (go to ten first, then finish),
*decompose* (split a number into parts), *loose ones* (the ones beyond
the full ten).

**Why it works (say it aloud):** taking away 4 all at once is the same as
taking away 3 and then 1 — we split the 4, we didn't change it. And
10 − 1 is easy, because tens are easy.

## Worked example 1 — 13 − 4 on ten frames

The adult builds 13 (10 red + 3 blue) and removes the 3 blue ones first,
then 1 red one.

> "Count the loose ones: 3. Split 4 into 3 + 1. Step one: 13 − 3 = 10.
> Step two: 10 − 1 = 9. Write it as a chain: 13 − 4 = 13 − 3 − 1 =
> 10 − 1 = **9.**"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the split is chosen by the loose ones, not
by guessing. 13 has 3 loose ones, so 4 must split as 3 + 1.

## Worked example 2 — 15 − 7, a bigger second step

> "15 − 7. Loose ones: 5. Split 7 into 5 + 2. Step one: 15 − 5 = 10.
> Step two: 10 − 2 = **8.** So 15 − 7 = 8. Check by unknown-addend:
> 7 + 8 = 15. ✓"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the second step can be bigger than 1 — here
it is 2. What matters is that the first step lands exactly on ten.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds.

1. 14 − 5: loose ones 4; split 5 into 4 + 1; 14 − 4 = 10; 10 − 1 = 9.
   (Expected: 9.)
2. 12 − 6: loose ones 2; split 6 into 2 + 4; 12 − 2 = 10; 10 − 4 = 6.
   (Expected: 6.)
3. 16 − 8: loose ones 6; split 8 into 6 + 2; 16 − 6 = 10; 10 − 2 = 8.
   (Expected: 8.)
4. 11 − 4: loose ones 1; split 4 into 1 + 3; 11 − 1 = 10; 10 − 3 = 7.
   (Expected: 7.)
5. Error analysis: the adult solves 13 − 5 as "13 − 3 = 10, then
   10 − 3 = 7" and announces 7. Ask: "Did I split right?" (Expected: no
   — 5 splits into 3 + 2, not 3 + 3, because only 2 of the 5 remain
   after reaching ten. Correct: 10 − 2 = 8.)
6. The learner models 14 − 6 alone: loose ones 4; split 6 into 4 + 2;
   14 − 4 = 10; 10 − 2 = 8. (Expected: 8.)

## Independent practice

The learner gets 15 − 6 and 12 − 4. They build each on ten frames, say
the split and both steps aloud, and record the chain and the answer.
The adult observes silently: does the first step always land on ten?

- 15 − 6: split 6 into 5 + 1; 15 − 5 = 10; 10 − 1 = 9. (Expected: 9.)
- 12 − 4: split 4 into 2 + 2; 12 − 2 = 10; 10 − 2 = 8. (Expected: 8.)

*Alternate response mode:* draw the two ten frames and cross out dots
in two stages instead of moving counters; adult scribes the chain.

## Application

Cracker box: "A box holds 10 crackers and 6 more sit beside it — 16 in
all. You eat 7. First eat the 6 loose ones: 16 − 6 = 10. One more to eat:
10 − 1 = 9 crackers left." The learner acts it out with counters, then
says the chain: 16 − 7 = 16 − 6 − 1 = 9.

## Exit check

"13 − 5. Back to ten." Expected: "Loose ones are 3, so split 5 into
3 + 2. 13 − 3 = 10, 10 − 2 = 8." Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** "back to ten" is acted out before it is named —
  the learner physically removes loose ones first, every time.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the loose ones, then to the ten
  frame, before touching anything; use a number line and jump back to
  ten first.
- **Floor-play variant:** a floor ten frame; the learner steps the loose
  ones off first, then steps the rest off the ten.

## Extension

15 − 8 by back-to-ten (15 − 5 = 10, 10 − 3 = 7) and check it by
unknown-addend (8 + 7 = 15). Or: find a subtraction where back-to-ten
is *harder* than counting up (e.g. 14 − 13) and explain why.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) splitting the subtrahend any old way
instead of by the loose ones — always ask "how many loose ones?" first;
(2) forgetting the second step and stopping at ten; (3) splitting the
minuend instead of the subtrahend — re-anchor: "we split what we take
away."
