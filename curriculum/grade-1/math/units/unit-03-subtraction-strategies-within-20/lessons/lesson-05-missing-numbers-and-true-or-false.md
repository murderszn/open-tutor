# Lesson 05 — Missing Numbers and True or False?

**Student goal:** I can find a missing number in a subtraction equation
and judge whether a subtraction equation is true or false, because the
equal sign means "is the same amount as."
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U03](../README.md) · **Session 13 of 16**

## Materials

- Number cards 0–20, a balance gesture (hands as a seesaw), counters
- True/false equation cards from [practice.md](../practice.md)

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"14 − 5 = 9. True or false?" The learner computes 14 − 5 (back to ten:
14 − 4 = 10, 10 − 1 = 9) and says "true." Then "9 = 14 − 5?" — same
amounts, other way around, still true. If the learner reads `=` as "the
answer goes here," re-run Unit 02's balance demo before continuing.

## Teaching

Missing numbers come in two new places now. A missing *subtrahend*
(14 − ? = 9) asks "how many were taken away?" — count up from the answer
to the start: 9 → 14 is 5 hops, so ? = 5. A missing *minuend*
(? − 6 = 7) asks "what did we start with?" — add the two numbers you can
see: 7 + 6 = 13, so ? = 13. And true/false judging never changes: compute
both sides, then compare. The equal sign means "is the same amount as" —
never "write the answer here."

> "14 − ? = 9. The 9 is what's left. Count up from 9 to 14: 10, 11, 12,
> 13, 14 — five hops. Five were taken away: 14 − 5 = 9. ✓
> ? − 6 = 7. What minus 6 leaves 7? Put the parts back together:
> 7 + 6 = 13. Check: 13 − 6 = 7. ✓"

**Key vocabulary:** *missing subtrahend* (the taken-away number we must
find), *missing minuend* (the start number we must find), *true* (both
sides the same amount), *false* (both sides different amounts).

## Worked example 1 — Missing subtrahend: 14 − ? = 9

> "What's left is 9; we started at 14. Count up from 9: 10 (one), 11
> (two), 12 (three), 13 (four), 14 (five). Five hops — so ? = **5.**
> Check: 14 − 5 = 9. ✓ That's Lesson 01's unknown-addend idea wearing a
> subtraction coat: 9 + 5 = 14."

## Worked example 2 — Missing minuend: ? − 6 = 7

> "We don't know the start. But we know 6 were taken and 7 are left.
> Put them back: 7 + 6 = **13.** Check: 13 − 6 = 7. ✓ If the check fails,
> the missing number is wrong — the check is part of the answer."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** missing-minuend problems are addition in
disguise. When in doubt, rebuild the whole from its parts.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds.

1. 12 − ? = 8: count up from 8 to 12 — 9, 10, 11, 12, four hops.
   (Expected: ? = 4; check 12 − 4 = 8.)
2. ? − 5 = 9: rebuild — 9 + 5 = 14. (Expected: ? = 14; check 14 − 5 = 9.)
3. 6 = _ − 4: rebuild — 6 + 4 = 10. (Expected: _ = 10; check 10 − 4 = 6.)
4. Error analysis: the adult judges 15 − 8 = 6 as true, saying
   "because 8 + 6 = 14." Ask: "Did I check right?" (Expected: no —
   8 + 6 = 14, not 15, so 15 − 8 = 7, and the equation is false. The
   check must rebuild the *start* number, 15.)
5. True or false: 12 − 5 = 7. (Expected: true — 12 − 5 = 7.)
6. True or false: 13 − 4 = 8. (Expected: false — 13 − 4 = 9, and
   9 ≠ 8. Fix it by changing 8 to 9.)

## Independent practice

The learner gets 13 − ? = 6, ? − 4 = 11, and two true/false cards:
14 − 6 = 8 (true) and 11 − 5 = 7 (false — 11 − 5 = 6). They solve each,
write or dictate the check, and for the false card say how to fix it.
The adult observes silently: does the learner compute both sides before
judging, and check every missing number?

- 13 − ? = 6 → ? = 7 (count up 7…13; check 13 − 7 = 6).
- ? − 4 = 11 → ? = 15 (11 + 4 = 15; check 15 − 4 = 11).
- 14 − 6 = 8 → true.
- 11 − 5 = 7 → false (11 − 5 = 6; fix by changing 7 to 6).

*Alternate response mode:* sort cards into "true" and "false" piles by
pointing, and dictate fixes; adult scribes.

## Application

Fix-the-equation game: the adult writes three equations, two true and
one false — 16 − 7 = 9 (true), 12 − 3 = 9 (true), 17 − 8 = 10 (false;
17 − 8 = 9). The learner judges each and repairs the false one by
changing one number (10 → 9). Repairing, not just judging, proves the
learner computed the real value.

## Exit check

"16 − ? = 9, and is 12 − 9 = 3 true or false?" Expected: "? = 7 — count
up 10…16, seven hops, and 16 − 7 = 9 checks. 12 − 9 = 3 is true, because
9 + 3 = 12." Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** "missing" is shown by a covered card before it is
  written as `?` or `_`. "True" and "false" are decided with the balance
  gesture — hands level for equal, tipped for unequal.
- **Alternate response mode:** use counters to rebuild every missing
  number before writing it; judge true/false by building both sides.
- **Support:** keep missing-number problems within 15 until the two
  types (subtrahend vs. minuend) are told apart confidently — the
  question "are we finding what was taken, or what we started with?"
  comes before any computing.

## Extension

Write a missing-minuend problem with a total above 20 using counters
(e.g. ? − 9 = 14 → 23) as enrichment, and explain why the same rebuild
rule works. Or: write a false equation and challenge the adult to fix it
two different ways (change the answer, or change a number on the left).

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) solving missing-minuend by subtracting
(e.g. ? − 6 = 7 answered as 1) — re-anchor: rebuild by adding;
(2) judging true/false from one side only; (3) reading `9 = 14 − 5` as
backwards or wrong because the "answer" is on the left — the balance
gesture fixes this: same amounts, either order.
