# Lesson 02 — When the Ones Make a New Ten

**Student goal:** I can add a one-digit number to a two-digit number
even when the ones reach ten: I trade ten ones for one new ten.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**

## Materials

- Tens rods and ones cubes (at least 10 loose ones per problem)
- A "trade tray" — a small box or circle drawn on paper where ten
  ones become one ten

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"7 ones + 5 ones?" The learner counts or recalls: 12 ones. Then:
"What is 12 ones as tens and ones?" — "1 ten and 2 ones." If 10 ones =
1 ten is shaky, re-run Unit 01's bundling game before continuing.

## Teaching

Yesterday the ones pile never reached ten. Today it does — and that is
good news, not a problem. Ten ones are exactly one ten, so we **trade**
them: the ten ones leave the ones pile and come back as one tens rod.
The tens pile grows by one, and only the leftover ones stay behind.

> "Here is 27: 2 tens and 7 ones. Add 5 ones. First count the ones:
> 7 ones + 5 ones = 12 ones. 12 ones is too many for the ones pile —
> trade 10 of them for 1 ten. Now the tens pile has 2 tens + 1 new ten
> = 3 tens, and the ones pile has 2 ones left. So 27 + 5 = **32**."

**Key vocabulary:** *compose a ten*, *trade ten ones for one ten*,
*leftover ones*. The learner may say "make a new ten."

**The bridge shortcut (say it aloud):** 27 + 5 can also be walked in
two steps: 27 + 3 = 30 (fill up to the next decade), then + 2 = 32.
The 5 splits into 3 + 2 because 27 needs 3 ones to reach 30. Both ways
give 32; the trade explains *why* the bridge works.

## Worked example 1 — 27 + 5, both ways

> "Trade way: 7 ones + 5 ones = 12 ones = 1 ten and 2 ones. 2 tens +
> 1 ten = 3 tens. 3 tens and 2 ones = **32**."
>
> "Bridge way: 27 needs 3 to reach 30, so split 5 into 3 + 2. 27 + 3 =
> 30, then 30 + 2 = **32**. Same answer, two paths."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the answer's ones digit (2) is not 12,
and not 7 + 5 written side by side. Ten of those ones *became* a ten.

## Worked example 2 — 48 + 6

> "48 is 4 tens and 8 ones. Add 6 ones: 8 ones + 6 ones = 14 ones =
> 1 ten and 4 ones. 4 tens + 1 ten = 5 tens. So 48 + 6 = **54**."
>
> "Bridge check: 48 needs 2 to reach 50, so split 6 into 2 + 4.
> 48 + 2 = 50, then 50 + 4 = **54**. Agrees."

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds, trading real ones every time.

1. 36 + 7: 6 ones + 7 ones = 13 ones = 1 ten and 3 ones; 3 tens + 1
   ten = 4 tens. (Expected: 43. Bridge: 36 + 4 = 40, + 3 = 43.)
2. 39 + 4: 9 ones + 4 ones = 13 ones = 1 ten and 3 ones; 3 tens + 1
   ten = 4 tens. (Expected: 43. Bridge: 39 + 1 = 40, + 3 = 43.)
3. 58 + 5: 8 ones + 5 ones = 13 ones = 1 ten and 3 ones; 5 tens + 1
   ten = 6 tens. (Expected: 63.)
4. 29 + 8: 9 ones + 8 ones = 17 ones = 1 ten and 7 ones; 2 tens + 1
   ten = 3 tens. (Expected: 37.)
5. Error analysis: the adult solves 47 + 6 by saying "7 ones + 6
   ones = 13 ones, so keep 4 tens and write 13 ones: 413? No — 43."
   Ask: "What did I forget?" (Expected: the trade. 13 ones = 1 ten
   and 3 ones, so 4 tens + 1 ten = 5 tens, and 47 + 6 = **53**, not
   43. Forgetting the new ten is today's classic slip.)
6. The learner models 35 + 8 alone: 5 ones + 8 ones = 13 ones = 1 ten
   and 3 ones; 3 tens + 1 ten = 4 tens. (Expected: 43.)

## Independent practice

The learner gets 26 + 8 and 55 + 7, with rods and cubes available.
They must show the trade (or the bridge) before writing each answer.

*Alternate response mode:* act the trade with the trade tray — put
ten ones in, take one tens rod out — and say the new tens count;
adult scribes the equations.

## Application

Crayon box: "Sam has 38 crayons. He buys a pack of 6 more. How many
crayons does Sam have now?" The learner builds 38, adds 6 ones:
8 ones + 6 ones = 14 ones = 1 ten and 4 ones; 3 tens + 1 ten = 4 tens.
(Expected: 44 crayons; 38 + 6 = 44. Bridge check: 38 + 2 = 40, + 4 = 44.)

## Exit check

"49 + 5. Show the trade in words." Expected: "9 ones + 5 ones = 14
ones = 1 ten and 4 ones; 4 tens + 1 ten = 5 tens, so 49 + 5 = **54**."
Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "trade" by acting it out first — ten
  ones go into the tray, one tens rod comes out — then name it
  *compose a ten*.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw the trade instead of moving
  cubes: circle ten ones, draw an arrow to one tens rod.
- **Floor-play variant:** a floor trade tray; the learner physically
  carries ten ones to the tray and brings back one tens rod.

## Extension

Invent a problem where the ones add up to exactly 10 (for example
24 + 6 = 30, with 0 leftover ones) and explain why the ones digit
becomes 0. Or: solve 47 + 6 by the trade *and* by the bridge, and say
which path felt easier and why.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) forgetting the composed ten (47 + 6 =
43); (2) writing the full ones total as the ones digit (27 + 5 =
212, or "32 with 12 ones"); (3) splitting the added number at the
wrong place in the bridge (27 + 5 as 27 + 5 = 30 + 5). Insist on the
physical trade before the bridge shortcut. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
