# Lesson 01 — Count On From the Bigger Number

**Student goal:** I can add two numbers by starting at the bigger one and
counting on the smaller one.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- A number line to 20 (drawn on a long strip of paper or printed)
- 15 small counters
- Two number cards (e.g., 7 and 3), or dice

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Point to 7 on the number line. "Start at 7 and count forward three numbers."
If counting forward from a non-starting number is wobbly, spend the lesson
with sums to 10 and re-check on Session 2; note it for re-teaching.

## Teaching

When we add, we don't always have to count everything from one. The first
number is already counted — so we can **start there and count on** the rest.

> "Look: 7 + 3. The 7 is already counted. Put your finger on 7. Now count
> the 3 more: 8, 9, 10. The total is 10. We never had to count 1, 2, 3, 4,
> 5, 6, 7 at all."

Say it slowly and land hard on the hop numbers: "**8, 9, 10.**"

**Key vocabulary:** *count on* (keep counting forward from the first number),
*addend* (each number we add), *total* (the answer to an addition).

## Worked example 1 — 7 + 3 by counting on

The adult places a marker on 7 and shows 3 counters.

> "7 is already counted. I only need to count the 3 more." *(taps each
> counter)* "8 — that's one more. 9 — two more. 10 — three more. The total
> is **10.**"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the 7 means seven things we already have. We
only count what is new. Watch that we don't count the starting number again —
the first new count is 8, not 7.

## Worked example 2 — counting on from the bigger number

The adult writes 3 + 9 and places a marker on 3.

> "Counting on from 3 would be nine long hops: 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11,
> 12. That's a lot of counting." *(moves the marker to 9)* "Start at the
> bigger number, 9, and count on 3: 10, 11, 12. Same total — **12** — with
> much less counting."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we may start at either addend, so choose the
bigger one and count on the smaller one. Fewer hops, fewer chances to lose
count. (Why the totals match comes in Lesson 02 — for now, check it with
counters if the learner asks.)

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. The learner may use the number line, fingers, or
counters; any hop-counting is fine.

1. 6 + 2: start at 6, count on 2 → "7, 8." (Expected: 8.)
2. 8 + 3: start at 8, count on 3 → "9, 10, 11." (Expected: 11.)
3. 4 + 9: which number do we start at? (Expected: 9.) Count on 4 → "10, 11, 12, 13." (Expected: 13.)
4. 5 + 5: start at 5 (either addend — same size), count on 5 → "6, 7, 8, 9, 10." (Expected: 10.)
5. Error analysis: the adult does 9 + 2 but counts "9, 10" and announces
   10. Ask: "Did I count right?" (Expected: no — the 9 was already counted;
   only the 2 new ones count: 10, 11. The total is 11.)
6. The learner picks two number cards, names the bigger one, and counts on.

## Independent practice

The learner gets 5 + 4 and 8 + 3. They choose the bigger addend, count on,
and say or draw the total. The adult observes silently: do they start at the
bigger number? Do they keep the hop count right?

*Alternate response mode:* hop a toy along the number line instead of
pointing; for limited mobility, the learner says "next" while the adult moves
the marker.

## Application

Snack math: the learner has 6 crackers and gets 4 more. "How many now?"
(Count on: 7, 8, 9, 10.) Counting on works anywhere the first group is
already counted — no need to recount the crackers in the bowl.

## Exit check

"9 + 2. Start where?" Expected: "At 9." "Count on 2." Expected: "10, 11 —
11 in all." Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "count on" by doing the hop first, then naming
  it. Accept home-language words for add/more alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** whisper the start number, then tap-count the
  hops on fingers.
- **Floor-play variant:** stand on a floor number line; hop forward the
  smaller addend while the adult counts the hops.

## Extension

"13 + 2" — count on works past 10 too (14, 15). Or: 4 + 12 — start at 12,
count on 4.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) recounting the starting number as a hop —
model "the start is already counted" with a hand over the first group;
(2) always starting at the left addend — praise the bigger-start choice
explicitly; (3) losing track of hop count — keep hops to 3 or fewer at
first, then grow to 4–6.
