# Lesson 03 — Make Ten With Ten Frames

**Student goal:** I can split one addend to make a full ten, then add what's
left — 9 + 4 becomes 10 + 3 = 13.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- Two blank ten frames (drawn or from the project)
- 13 counters in one color, plus 4 in a second color for the moving dot
- The generated illustration
  [`assets/make-ten-ten-frames.png`](../assets/make-ten-ten-frames.png)

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Show a ten frame with 9 dots: "How many to fill it?" (Expected: 1.) Then
with 8 dots: "How many to fill it?" (Expected: 2.) Filling-to-ten must be
quick — it's the engine of this strategy.

## Teaching

Nine is almost ten. If we borrow one counter from the other addend, the
almost-ten becomes a full ten, and full tens are easy to add.

> "9 + 4. Look at the ten frame: nine red dots, one empty box." *(shows the
> illustration)* "The orange arrow shows one blue dot hopping over to fill
> that last box. Now the first frame is a full ten — 10 — and only 3 blue
> dots are left. 9 + 4 turned into **10 + 3 = 13.**"

Study the picture with the learner: count the 9 red dots together, then the
4 blue dots, then count all 13.

**Key vocabulary:** *make a ten* (borrow from one addend to complete a ten),
*ten frame* (2 × 5 box tool). The standard's example is exactly this move:
8 + 6 = 8 + 2 + 4 = 10 + 4 = 14.

## Worked example 1 — 9 + 4 on real ten frames (with the picture)

The adult builds 9 red dots on the learner's ten frame and 4 blue dots on a
second frame, matching the picture.

> "Count the red dots: 9. How many boxes are empty? One. Take one blue dot
> and move it — just like the arrow. Now count: the first frame is a full
> **ten**, and there are **3** blue dots left. 9 + 4 = 10 + 3 = **13.**"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we didn't change the total — we moved one dot,
not removed it. Moving is allowed; vanishing is not. Ten-plus-something is
the easiest addition there is.

## Worked example 2 — 8 + 5, choosing what to borrow

The adult shows 8 dots on a ten frame and 5 counters beside it.

> "How many does 8 need to make ten? Two. Borrow 2 from the 5 — now it's
> 10 + 3 = **13**."

Then the adult tries the other way: "What if we filled the 5 up to ten
instead? 5 needs 5 more, but 8 only has 5 to give… 8 − 5 = 3 left… 10 + 3 =
13. Same answer! But borrowing from the *smaller* pile to fill the
*almost-ten* pile is the smooth move."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** fill the addend that is closest to ten. Two
roads reach the same total, but one has less carrying.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds.

1. 9 + 3: how many to fill the 9? (1.) Move 1 over: 10 + 2 = 12.
2. 8 + 4: borrow how many? (2.) 10 + 2 = 12.
3. 7 + 5: the learner chooses which frame to fill; both give 10 + 2 = 12.
   Praise the "fill the 7" choice as efficient, accept the other.
4. Look at the generated picture: "Cover the arrow. Without moving anything,
   how many dots are there?" (Expected: count 9 + 4 = 13.) "Now use the
   arrow's idea." (Expected: 10 + 3 = 13.)
5. Error analysis: the adult moves 2 dots to fill the 9-frame and announces
   "10 + 2 = 12 for 9 + 4." Ask: "Did I move right?" (Expected: no — 9 only
   needed 1; moving 2 leaves 10 + 2, which counts only 12 dots. Move back
   one: the true total is 13.)
6. The learner models 9 + 6 alone with frames while the adult watches
   (10 + 5 = 15).

## Independent practice

The learner gets 8 + 6 and 9 + 5. They model each on ten frames, say the
make-ten rewrite aloud ("8 + 6 is 10 + 4"), and record the total. The adult
observes silently: do they borrow exactly what fills the frame?

*Alternate response mode:* draw the dots and the moving arrow instead of
moving counters; adult scribes the equations.

## Application

Egg carton math: a 10-egg carton holds 8 eggs; 5 more eggs wait on the
counter. "Move 2 into the carton — full ten! 3 eggs stay out. 8 + 5 = 13."
Real ten-containers (cartons, ten-frames) make the strategy visible at home.

## Exit check

"7 + 4. Make a ten." Expected: "7 needs 3 — borrow 3 from the 4: 10 + 1 =
11." Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "make a ten" by filling the frame first, then
  naming it. "Borrow" may confuse — say "move" or "slide."
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the empty boxes and the donor dots
  before touching anything.
- **Floor-play variant:** a floor ten-frame (tape boxes on the floor); the
  learner steps one beanbag into the empty box.

## Extension

8 + 7 by make-ten (10 + 5 = 15), or try 9 + 8 two ways. Or: invent a sum
where make-ten is harder than counting on (e.g., 4 + 5) and explain why.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) moving too many dots — always ask "how many
empty boxes?" first; (2) thinking the total changed — recount all dots after
moving; (3) applying make-ten to doubles — gently suggest doubles (Lesson
04) when both addends match.

expected responses for the guided error-analysis round and exit check.
