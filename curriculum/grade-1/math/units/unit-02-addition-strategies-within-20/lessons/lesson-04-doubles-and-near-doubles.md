# Lesson 04 — Doubles and Near-Doubles

**Student goal:** I can use a known double to solve a near-double:
6 + 7 is 6 + 6 + 1 = 13.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 11 of 16**

## Materials

- Mirror or folded paper (to "double" a group visually)
- 20 counters
- Doubles cards: 5+5, 6+6, 7+7, 8+8, 9+9, 10+10 (print or write)

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Quick-fire the doubles the learner already owns (usually 5 + 5 = 10,
10 + 10 = 20 from Unit 01 games, plus whatever stuck): show each card, the
learner answers. Any double not yet instant becomes today's first build.

## Teaching

Doubles are the facts your brain keeps for free. And a **near-double** is
just a double with one extra — if you know the double, you're one step from
the answer.

> "6 + 7. Hmm — but I know 6 + 6 = 12 cold. 7 is one more than 6, so 6 + 7
> is one more than 6 + 6: 12 + 1 = **13.** I didn't count at all — I used
> what I knew."

The standard names this exactly: 6 + 7 = 6 + 6 + 1 = 12 + 1 = 13 — creating
an easier known sum.

**Key vocabulary:** *doubles* (same addend twice: 7 + 7), *near-double*
(one addend is one more than the other: 6 + 7).

## Worked example 1 — 8 + 9 from the double 8 + 8

The adult builds 8 counters in each of two rows.

> "8 + 8 = 16 — say it with me. Now I add one more counter to the second
> row: 8 + 9. Nothing else changed, so the total is one more than 16:
> **17.**"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** adding one counter to a known double adds
exactly one to the total. One change in, one change out.

## Worked example 2 — 5 + 6 two ways (near-double both directions)

The adult writes 5 + 6.

> "Way one: I know 5 + 5 = 10. 6 is one more than 5, so 5 + 6 = 10 + 1 =
> **11.**" *(turns it around)* "Way two: 6 + 6 = 12, but I only have 5 —
> one less — so 6 + 6 − 1… wait, we don't subtract yet." *(smiles)* "So way
> one is the keeper: anchor on the smaller double. 5 + 5 = 10, one more
> makes **11.**"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** anchor on the double you know best — usually
the smaller one — then add the extra one. Notice how turn-around thinking
(Lesson 02) helped us pick the friendly anchor.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds.

1. 7 + 7 = ? (Expected: 14 — the double itself.)
2. 7 + 8: which double helps? (Expected: 7 + 7 = 14, one more → 15.)
3. 9 + 9 = ? then 9 + 10: (Expected: 18, then 19 — one more.)
4. 4 + 5: anchor on 4 + 4 = 8 → 9.
5. Error analysis: the adult solves 6 + 7 as "6 + 6 = 12, and 7 is one more
   than 6, so… 14." Ask: "Did I finish right?" (Expected: no — one more
   than 12 is 13, not 14. You added two by accident.)
6. The learner builds 8 + 9 with counters, states the double, and lands on
   17.

## Independent practice

The learner gets 6 + 6, 6 + 7, and 5 + 6. They state the anchor double for
each and the final total. The adult observes silently: do they name the
double before adding the one?

*Alternate response mode:* mirror the smaller group to "see" the double,
then add the extra counter beside it.

## Application

Staircase math: a staircase has pairs of steps — 7 steps up to a landing,
then 8 more. "You know 7 + 7 = 14, so 7 + 8 = 15 steps." Near-doubles hide
in stairs, egg pairs, and shoe pairs.

## Exit check

"8 + 7. Use a double." Expected: "7 + 7 = 14, and 8 is one more than 7, so
one more: 15." (Anchor on the smaller double — the unit does not use
subtraction yet.) Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** "double" first means "two of the same" with objects;
  the word follows the doing.
- **Alternate response mode:** tap the double rhythmically (ta-ta, ta-ta)
  then one extra tap.
- **Floor-play variant:** two rows of beanbags; the learner hops the double,
  then one extra hop.

## Extension

Near-doubles with a gap of 2 (6 + 8 = 6 + 6 + 2)? Only if the learner asks —
this previews "creating easier sums" beyond the grade-1 core.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) adding the "one more" twice — slow down and
physically add a single counter; (2) anchoring on the larger double and then
being stuck without subtraction — redirect to the smaller anchor;
(3) calling any close pair a double (5 + 7 is not a near-double) — check:
"are the addends the same, or one apart?"
