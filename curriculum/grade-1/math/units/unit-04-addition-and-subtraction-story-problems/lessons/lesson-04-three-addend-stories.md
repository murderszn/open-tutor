# Lesson 04 — Three-Addend Stories

**Student goal:** I can solve a story with three groups by adding two
groups first — especially a pair that makes ten.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 11 of 16**

## Materials

- 20 counters in three colors (or two colors plus a ten frame), a
  number line to 20

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"6 + 4." The learner: 10. "10 + 5." The learner: 15.
"2 + 6 + 4, the easy way." The learner groups 6 + 4 = 10, then
10 + 2 = 12. Grouping to ten from Unit 02 is the engine of this
lesson; if grouping is shaky, re-run the Unit 02 make-ten game before
continuing.

## Teaching

Some stories have three groups, not two. The rule from Unit 02 still
works: you may add groups in any order, so pick the order that makes
the arithmetic easy. Usually that means finding the pair that makes
ten first, then adding the third group to the ten.

> "Listen: 'At the pond there are 6 ducks, 5 frogs, and 4 turtles. How
> many animals are there in all?' Three groups: 6, 5, 4. Adding left
> to right gives 6 + 5 = 11, then 11 + 4 = 15 — correct, but clumsy.
> Group smarter: 6 + 4 = 10, then 10 + 5 = **15.** The ten does the
> heavy lifting."

**Key vocabulary:** *three addends* (three numbers being added),
*group* (add two numbers first, then add the third), *in any order*
(changing the order does not change the total).

**Why grouping is legal (say it aloud):** the animals do not care
which two you count first. 6 ducks + 4 turtles is 10 animals no matter
when you count them, and 10 + 5 frogs is 15. Order changes the work,
never the answer.

## Worked example 1 — 6 + 5 + 4 = 15, grouping 6 + 4

> "6 ducks, 5 frogs, 4 turtles. Group the ducks and turtles: 6 + 4 =
> 10. Add the frogs: 10 + 5 = **15.** Check left to right: 6 + 5 = 11,
> 11 + 4 = 15. Same answer — but the ten-first route is the one to
> keep."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we chose 6 + 4 because it makes ten, not
because it came first in the story. The story's order is not the
solving order.

## Worked example 2 — 3 + 7 + 8 = 18, grouping 3 + 7

> "A bead string has 3 red beads, 7 blue beads, and 8 yellow beads.
> How many beads in all? Group red and blue: 3 + 7 = 10. Add yellow:
> 10 + 8 = **18.** Check another way: 7 + 8 = 15, then 15 + 3 = 18.
> Same answer three ways — grouping is a choice, not a rule with one
> right answer."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** any grouping that is computed correctly
gives 18. "Best" grouping means easiest, and easiest usually means
"makes ten."

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. For each, name the pair grouped first.

1. "A fruit bowl has 5 apples, 5 oranges, and 6 bananas. How many
   pieces of fruit in all?" (Expected: 5 + 5 = 10, + 6 = 16.)
2. "On a shelf: 2 red books, 9 blue books, 8 green books. How many
   books in all?" (Expected: 2 + 8 = 10, + 9 = 19.)
3. "A garden has 4 roses, 7 tulips, and 3 daisies. How many flowers
   in all?" (Expected: 7 + 3 = 10, + 4 = 14.)
4. "A snack tray has 9 crackers, 1 cheese cube, and 7 grapes. How
   many snacks in all?" (Expected: 9 + 1 = 10, + 7 = 17.)
5. Error analysis: the adult solves 6 + 5 + 4 as 6 + 5 = 11, then
   11 + 4 = 14. Ask: "Check the last step." (Expected: 11 + 4 = 15,
   not 14 — a counting slip, not a grouping error. Grouping 6 + 4 = 10
   first avoids the slip: 10 + 5 = 15.)
6. The learner models "8 stickers, 2 stamps, and 9 erasers. How many
   things in all?" alone with counters while the adult watches.
   (Expected: 8 + 2 = 10, + 9 = 19.)

## Independent practice

The learner gets two fresh stories, groups a ten pair first, and
writes both steps. The adult observes silently: do they look for the
ten pair before adding left to right?

1. "A party table has 4 sandwiches, 8 apples, and 6 juice boxes. How
   many things are on the table in all?"
   (Expected: 4 + 6 = 10, + 8 = 18.)
2. "A toy box has 2 cars, 6 balls, and 10 blocks. How many toys in
   all?" (Expected: 2 + 6 = 8, + 10 = 18 — or 10 + 6 = 16, + 2 = 18;
   either grouping is correct.)

*Alternate response mode:* circle the ten pair on paper before
writing the equation; adult scribes the steps.

## Application

Field-trip bags: "Each bag has 5 granola bars, 5 bananas, and 9
crackers. How many snacks are in one bag?" The learner groups, writes
both steps, and checks left to right. (Expected: 5 + 5 = 10, + 9 = 19;
check: 5 + 5 = 10, 10 + 9 = 19 — same.)

## Exit check

"7 + 6 + 3. Group the ten pair first and tell me both steps."
Expected: "7 + 3 = 10, then 10 + 6 = 16." Record: secure / developing /
not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** "three groups" is acted out with three piles
  before any numbers are written; the learner physically pushes two
  piles together for the first step.
- **Alternate response mode:** three ten frames — fill two, then
  combine; or a number line with two hops marked.
- **Floor-play variant:** three baskets; the learner carries two
  baskets together to make the ten pile, then counts on the third.

## Extension

A three-addend story where no pair makes ten: "5 + 6 + 7" — group
doubles-ish instead (6 + 6 = 12 is not available; group 5 + 7 = 12,
then + 6 = 18) and explain why "closest to ten" is still a good
choice. Or: a four-group story as enrichment ("2 + 3 + 8 + 7 = 20")
with two ten pairs.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) always adding left to right even when
a ten pair is visible — praise grouping, never require one order;
(2) forgetting the third group after making ten ("6 + 4 = 10, done"
— always ask "how many groups have we counted?"); (3) grouping two
numbers but adding the third to the wrong partial sum — write both
steps, every time.
