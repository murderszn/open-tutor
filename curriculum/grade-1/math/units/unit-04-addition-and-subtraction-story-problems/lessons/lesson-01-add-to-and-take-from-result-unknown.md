# Lesson 01 — Stories That Add To and Take From

**Student goal:** I can act out a story with counters, say what happened,
and write the equation that matches it.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- 20 counters in two colors, a ten frame, a number line to 20
- Story cards from [practice.md](../practice.md#session-a-act-it-out-stories)

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"9 + 4, by make-ten." The learner: 9 + 1 = 10, then + 3 = 13.
"14 − 6, by back-to-ten." The learner: 14 − 4 = 10, then − 2 = 8.
If either strategy is shaky, the story work waits — re-run the Unit 02
or Unit 03 game for that strategy first. This unit adds no new
computation; it only asks the old strategies new questions.

## Teaching

A story problem is a little play in three parts: a **start**, a
**change**, and a **result**. In an *add-to* story, something joins and
the pile grows. In a *take-from* story, something leaves and the pile
shrinks. The trick is to act the story out *before* writing any
numbers — counters first, equation second.

> "Listen: 'Mia has 7 red apples. Her friend gives her 5 green apples.
> How many apples does Mia have now?' First we act it. Put down 7 red
> counters — that's the start. Now 5 green counters join them — that's
> the change. Count them all: 12. The pile grew, so the equation is
> 7 + 5 = **12.**"

**Key vocabulary:** *add to* (something joins), *take from* (something
leaves), *start* (what we begin with), *change* (what joins or leaves),
*result* (what we end with), *in all*, *left*.

**Why acting first matters (say it aloud):** the numbers in a story
are not an instruction to add or subtract. The *action* decides. "Gives
her 5" grows the pile; "flies away" shrinks it. If you skip the acting,
you are guessing — and guessing gets stories wrong even when the
arithmetic is perfect.

## Worked example 1 — Add-to, result unknown: 7 + 5 = 12

> "Mia has 7 red apples. Her friend gives her 5 green apples. How many
> apples does Mia have now? Act it: 7 red counters, then 5 green
> counters join. Count: 12. Equation: 7 + 5 = **12.** Check by
> make-ten: 7 + 3 = 10, then + 2 = 12. Same answer two ways."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the question asks for the result — "how
many now?" — after something joined, so we add. The green counters are
a different color so the two parts stay visible inside the total.

## Worked example 2 — Take-from, result unknown: 14 − 6 = 8

> "There are 14 birds at the feeder. 6 fly away. How many birds are
> left? Act it: 14 counters, then take 6 away. Count what stays: 8.
> Equation: 14 − 6 = **8.** Check by back-to-ten: 14 − 4 = 10, then
> − 2 = 8. Same answer two ways."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** "fly away" shrinks the group, so we
subtract — even though the story's question ("how many are left?")
sounds like the add-to question ("how many now?"). The action, not the
question words, picks the operation.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. Act each story out before writing the equation.

1. "Sam has 9 pencils. His teacher gives him 4 more. How many pencils
   does Sam have now?" Act: 9, then 4 join. (Expected: 9 + 4 = 13.)
2. "There are 12 cookies on a plate. Mia eats 5. How many cookies are
   left?" Act: 12, take 5 away. (Expected: 12 − 5 = 7.)
3. "Ana has 8 stickers. She buys 8 more. How many stickers in all?"
   Act: 8 and 8. (Expected: 8 + 8 = 16, a double.)
4. "There are 17 marbles in a bag. 9 roll out. How many marbles are
   left?" Act: 17, take 9 away. (Expected: 17 − 9 = 8.)
5. Error analysis: the adult acts out "6 frogs, then 9 more hop over"
   but writes 6 + 9 = 14. Ask: "Does the equation match the acting?"
   (Expected: no — the acting shows 6 and 9 together, and 6 + 9 = 15
   by make-ten: 6 + 4 = 10, + 5 = 15. The adult added right but
   computed wrong; check every equation a second way.)
6. The learner models "11 ducks on a pond. 4 swim away. How many are
   left?" alone with counters while the adult watches.
   (Expected: 11 − 4 = 7.)

## Independent practice

The learner gets two fresh stories, acts each out, and writes the
equation. The adult observes silently: do they act before they compute?

1. "Tom has 6 toy cars. He gets 7 more for his birthday. How many toy
   cars does Tom have now?" (Expected: 6 + 7 = 13.)
2. "There are 15 grapes in a bowl. Ben eats 8. How many grapes are
   left?" (Expected: 15 − 8 = 7.)

*Alternate response mode:* draw the start group and the change group
instead of moving counters; adult scribes the equations.

## Application

Library story: "There are 9 books on a shelf. The teacher adds 6 more
books. How many books are on the shelf now?" The learner acts it out,
writes the equation, and checks by a second strategy.
(Expected: 9 + 6 = 15; check by make-ten: 9 + 1 = 10, + 5 = 15.)

## Exit check

"8 ducks are on a pond. 5 more ducks join them. How many ducks are
there now? Act it, then tell me the equation." Expected: "8 and 5
together — 8 + 5 = 13." Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "joins" and "leaves" by acting them out
  first, then naming them *add to* and *take from*. "In all" and
  "left" are question words, not operation rules.
- **Alternate response mode:** a floor number line — the learner
  stands on the start number and hops forward (add-to) or backward
  (take-from), counting hops.
- **Floor-play variant:** two baskets, one for the start group and one
  for the change group; the learner physically moves counters between
  them as the story is read.

## Extension

A two-step story: "There are 9 apples. Mia picks 5 more, then eats 3.
How many apples are left?" (Expected: 9 + 5 = 14, then 14 − 3 = 11.)
Or: retell Worked example 2 with the numbers swapped — "6 birds, then
14 join" — and explain why that is a different story even though it
uses the same numbers.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) computing before acting — always ask
"show me the story first"; (2) treating "how many are left" as
subtraction even when nothing left (take-from always has something
leaving — check the action); (3) adding when the story says "more"
without checking whether more joined or more is being compared
(compare stories arrive in Lesson 03).
