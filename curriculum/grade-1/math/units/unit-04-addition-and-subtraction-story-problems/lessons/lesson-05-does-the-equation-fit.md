# Lesson 05 — Does This Equation Fit the Story?

**Student goal:** I can tell whether an equation matches a story, tell
whether an equation is true or false, and write a story of my own for
an equation.
**Time:** 20–25 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 13 of 16**

## Materials

- 20 counters in two colors, a ten frame, a number line to 20
- Equation cards from [practice.md](../practice.md#session-g-equation-match-game)

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"9 + 4." The learner: 13. "9 − 4." The learner: 5.
Same numbers, different stories — today's whole lesson. If the
learner hesitates, re-run Lesson 01's act-it-out routine once: the
action, not the numbers, picks the operation.

## Teaching

So far the learner wrote equations for stories. Today the direction
reverses: the equation is given, and the learner judges it. Two
separate questions, asked in order: **(1) Does this equation fit the
story?** — does its action match what happened? **(2) Is this equation
true or false?** — do both sides show the same amount? An equation can
fail either question on its own, and the fixes are different: a
wrong-operation equation needs a new operation; a false equation needs
a corrected number.

> "Story: 'There are 9 fish. 4 swim away. How many fish are left?'
> Equation offered: 9 + 4 = 13. Question 1: does it fit? The fish
> swam away — take-from — but the equation adds. It does not fit,
> even though 9 + 4 really is 13. The fitting equation is 9 − 4 = 5.
> Question 2, on the fitting equation: is 9 − 4 = 5 true? Yes — both
> sides are 5."

**Key vocabulary:** *matches* (the equation tells the same story),
*does not fit*, *true* (both sides are the same amount), *false*
(the sides differ), *the same amount as* (the meaning of the equal
sign, from Units 02–03).

**Why the two questions must stay separate (say it aloud):** "9 + 4
= 13" is true arithmetic and a wrong story model at the same time.
Learners who only check the arithmetic will accept it. Learners who
only check the action may accept "9 − 4 = 6" because the operation
looks right. Both checks, every time.

## Worked example 1 — Wrong operation, true arithmetic: 9 + 4 = 13

> "Story: '9 fish, 4 swim away.' Offered: 9 + 4 = 13. Act it: 9
> counters, take 4 away — 5 stay. The offered equation adds, so it
> does not fit the story, even though 9 + 4 = 13 is true on its own.
> Fitting equation: 9 − 4 = **5.** Verdict: does not fit; replace the
> operation."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** fit is about the story's action.
"Swim away" is take-from, full stop. A true equation that tells the
wrong story is still the wrong answer to the story.

## Worked example 2 — True or false: 7 + 6 = 13, and 12 − 5 = 8

> "No story this time — just the equal sign. Is 7 + 6 = 13 true?
> Compute the left: 7 + 3 = 10, + 3 = 13. Both sides are 13 — true.
> Is 12 − 5 = 8 true? Compute the left: 12 − 2 = 10, − 3 = 7. The
> left side is 7, the right side is 8 — not the same amount, so
> false. The true equation is 12 − 5 = **7.**"

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the equal sign is a balance, not a
"write the answer here" sign. Both sides get computed, then compared.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds. For each, ask both questions in order.

1. Story: "There are 10 pencils. The teacher gives Mia 3 more."
   Offered: 10 − 3 = 7. (Expected: does not fit — the teacher gives,
   so add-to; fitting equation 10 + 3 = 13. The offered equation is
   also true arithmetic on its own, which is exactly why the fit
   check comes first.)
2. True or false: 8 + 7 = 15; 16 − 9 = 6.
   (Expected: 8 + 7 = 15 is true — 8 + 2 = 10, + 5 = 15. 16 − 9 = 6
   is false — 16 − 9 = 7 by back-to-ten: 16 − 6 = 10, − 3 = 7.)
3. Missing-number match: story "Some cats are on a porch. 5 more
   cats come. Now there are 14 cats." Offered equation: 9 + 5 = 14.
   (Expected: fits — the start was 9, and 9 + 5 = 14 is true, so the
   story's missing start is 9.)
4. Write a story for 11 − 3 = 8. (Expected: any take-from story with
   start 11, change 3, result 8, e.g. "There were 11 cookies. Mia ate
   3. Now there are 8 left." — action, numbers, and result must all
   match.)
5. Error analysis: the adult announces "6 + 6 = 11 is true because
   doubles are easy." Ask: "Check the double." (Expected: false —
   6 + 6 = 12. Easy to say is not the same as checked.)
6. The learner judges 14 − 7 = 7 (true — 14 − 4 = 10, − 3 = 7) and
   writes a story for 5 + 9 = 14 alone while the adult watches.
   (Expected: true; story e.g. "There are 5 red balloons and 9 blue
   balloons. How many balloons in all? 14.")

## Independent practice

The learner gets two fresh judgments. The adult observes silently: do
they ask the fit question before the true/false question?

1. Story: "There are 13 birds. 6 fly away." Which equation fits:
   13 − 6 = 7 or 13 + 6 = 19? (Expected: 13 − 6 = 7 fits; 13 + 6 = 19
   is true arithmetic that tells the wrong story.)
2. True or false: 9 + 8 = 17; 15 − 6 = 10.
   (Expected: 9 + 8 = 17 is true — 9 + 1 = 10, + 7 = 17. 15 − 6 = 10
   is false — 15 − 6 = 9 by back-to-ten: 15 − 5 = 10, − 1 = 9.)

*Alternate response mode:* thumbs up/thumbs down for fit, then a
balance drawing (two pans) for true/false; adult scribes the verdicts.

## Application

Write a story for 12 + 5 = 17. The learner writes two or three
sentences, draws or builds the two groups, and reads the story back
while the adult checks: does the action add, and do the numbers match?
(Expected: any add-to or put-together story with parts 12 and 5 and
total 17, e.g. "There are 12 crayons in a box. Mia adds 5 more. How
many crayons are there now? 17.")

## Exit check

"Is 11 − 4 = 8 true or false? Why?" Expected: "False. 11 − 4 = 7 —
11 − 1 = 10, − 3 = 7 — so the sides are 7 and 8, not the same amount."
Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** "fit" is taught by acting: if the acted story
  and the equation show different actions, they do not fit — no
  vocabulary quiz needed.
- **Alternate response mode:** green card for "fits/true," red card
  for "does not fit/false," held up before any explanation is asked
  for.
- **Floor-play variant:** a balance scale made of a ruler on a block
  — counters on both sides must level before the equation is called
  true.

## Extension

A two-error equation: story "14 stickers, 5 given away" with offered
equation 14 + 5 = 18 — both wrong operation AND false arithmetic
(14 + 5 = 19). The learner names both errors. Or: write a story for
an equation with a missing number, e.g. "? + 6 = 15," and solve it.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) accepting true arithmetic as a fitting
equation — always act the story first; (2) calling a fitting equation
true without computing both sides; (3) in write-a-story tasks, changing
the numbers mid-story (e.g. writing "12 + 5" but describing 12 and 6)
— read the story back against the equation, number by number.
