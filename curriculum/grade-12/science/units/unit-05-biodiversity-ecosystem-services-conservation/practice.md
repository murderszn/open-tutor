## Practice Set A — What biodiversity measures (Lesson 01)

All counts are fictional practice data.

1. Compute the Shannon index H′ and evenness J for a fictional
   sample with counts 50, 30, 10, 10 (total N = 100, richness
   S = 4). Show each p·ln p term.
2. Explain in two sentences why a nature reserve could lose
   diversity even while its species richness stays the same.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says "Meadow B (Lesson 01,
   Example 1) has H′ = 0.46, so it has almost no biodiversity."
   What is wrong with that sentence? Correct it.
4. Two fictional plots: Plot X has richness 8 and H′ = 2.00;
   Plot Y has richness 12 and H′ = 1.90. Which plot is more
   diverse by the Shannon index, and why does the richness
   comparison point the other way?
5. Two fictional bird surveys: Site 1 was watched for 1 hour and
   found 6 species; Site 2 was watched for 5 hours and found 11
   species. Can you conclude Site 2 is richer? Explain in two
   sentences.
6. From the unit image (`assets/meadow-pond-biodiversity.png`):
   (a) name the three habitat zones; (b) list the organism types
   you can name (count grasses and background trees as one type
   each); (c) state one thing the picture cannot tell you about
   the diversity of the place it shows.
7. Compute H′ and J for a fictional 3-species sample with counts
   60, 30, 10 (N = 100). Compare with the perfectly even
   3-species case (H′ = ln 3 = 1.10) and say what the gap means.

## Practice Set B — How biodiversity changes (Lesson 02)

All datasets are fictional practice data.

1. A fictional forest fragment of 25 km² follows S = 5 · A^0.20.
   Predict S. If logging halves the fragment to 12.5 km²,
   predict the new S and state the loss.
2. In two sentences, explain why "extinction is natural, so
   current extinctions do not matter" is a bad argument. Use the
   background-rate comparison.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says "wolves returned to
   Yellowstone and the willows came back, so wolves caused the
   willow recovery." What is missing from that sentence? Correct
   it.
4. A fictional reserve follows S = 6 · A^0.30. Compute S for
   A = 10 km² and for A = 100 km². What does the change from 10
   to 100 tell you about how richness grows with area?
5. In Paine's starfish-removal experiment the pool's species
   count fell from 15 to 8 as mussels took over. Which changed —
   richness, evenness, or both? Explain in two sentences.
6. Give one piece of evidence for life changing Earth and one
   piece for Earth changing life, and in one sentence say why
   the pair together (and neither alone) makes a coevolution
   argument.
7. A habitat shrinks from 100 km² to 25 km² and follows
   S = 10 · A^0.25. Predict S before and after. (Fourth roots:
   100^0.25 ≈ 3.16; 25^0.25 ≈ 2.24.)
8. If z = 0.25, by what factor does richness multiply when area
   increases 16-fold? Show the calculation in one line.

## Practice Set C — Ecosystem services (Lesson 03)

All money figures are fictional practice data.

1. Classify each service into one MEA category (provisioning,
   regulating, cultural, supporting): (a) honey harvested from
   hives; (b) bees pollinating an orchard; (c) views from a
   hiking trail; (d) soil forming under a forest floor; (e)
   timber cut for building; (f) a wetland slowing a flood. For
   two of your answers, name the category test you used.
2. A fictional coastal mangrove forest of 60 hectares protects a
   town from storm surge, avoiding $3,000,000 per year in damage,
   and supports a fishery worth $500,000 per year. Compute the
   total annual value and the per-hectare value. State two
   assumptions the estimate depends on.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says "the wetland in Lesson 03 is
   worth $680,000 a year, so draining it would cost the town
   $680,000 a year." What is wrong with that sentence? Correct it.
4. A fictional 25-hectare woodland filters water at a replacement
   cost of $8,000 per hectare per year. What is its annual
   filtration value, and what does the number leave out?
5. Classify each service from Lesson 03, guided practice 1:
   (a) a forest's timber harvest; (b) the same forest slowing a
   flood; (c) a child's first birdwatching walk in that forest;
   (d) soil forming under the forest floor.
6. For the pollination in Lesson 03, Example 2: name one group
   that depends on the pollinators and one person or body that
   decides whether they survive, and write one sentence on why
   the two might disagree.
7. A learner values a farm as soil formation $50,000 per year
   plus crops $200,000 per year, and reports a total of $250,000.
   The crops depend on that soil. What is wrong with the total?
   Correct it.

## Practice Set D — Threats to biodiversity (Lesson 04)

1. Explain in three sentences why "threatened" does not mean
   "already extinct," using the IPBES figure of around 1 million
   threatened species.
2. A fictional river's fish populations fell by half after a dam
   was built, while the river's water quality stayed clean and
   fishing stayed light. Rank the five IPBES drivers for this
   river with one evidence phrase each, and name the driver you
   rank first.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says "the Red List says 28% of
   species are threatened, so 28% of all species on Earth are
   threatened." What is wrong with that sentence? Correct it.
4. Place each species in its IUCN Red List category from the
   description: (a) a frog assessed as facing a very high risk
   of extinction in the wild; (b) a bird assessed as facing a
   high risk; (c) a fish facing an extremely high risk; (d) a
   plant with too little data to judge.
5. A news headline says "73% of wildlife wiped out since 1970."
   Write the two-sentence correction.
6. A fictional island's main threat is rats eating seabird eggs.
   Which IPBES driver is that, and which two drivers is it not?
7. The Red List reports more than 49,500 threatened species out
   of more than 175,900 assessed (checked 2026-11-13). What
   percentage is that, roughly, and what is the one most
   important caveat about applying that percentage to all life on
   Earth?

## Practice Set E — Conservation design (Lesson 05)

1. A fictional reserve's design scores 9 of 15 on the Lesson 05
   matrix. The adult asks for one refinement. Write the
   refinement, the evaluation finding it answers, and how you
   would test it in the spreadsheet model.
2. Explain in three sentences why ex situ conservation alone
   (seed banks, captive breeding, botanical gardens) cannot meet
   Target 3 of the Kunming–Montreal framework.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says "30 by 30 means protecting
   30% of every country." What is wrong with that sentence?
   Correct it.
4. A fictional town can protect 30% of its green space by 2030
   or protect 15% managed twice as well. Which better serves
   Target 3's words "effectively conserved and managed"?
   Explain in two sentences.
5. Score a fourth option for Miller's Marsh — D. Do nothing —
   on the Lesson 05, Example 1 matrix (biodiversity gain 1,
   cost 3, feasibility 3, community support 1; biodiversity gain
   is weighted ×2). What is its total, and why does a high score
   on cost not save it?
6. Match each tool to the driver it answers best: protected area,
   corridor, captive breeding, fishing quota. Drivers: land-use
   change, fragmentation, extinction risk for one species,
   overexploitation.
7. Score two fictional options on the Lesson 05 matrix
   (biodiversity gain ×2; cost, feasibility, community support
   ×1; 3 = best, and for cost 3 = cheapest): (a) protect a
   5-hectare pond — biodiversity 3, cost 1, feasibility 2,
   support 3; (b) run a school awareness campaign — biodiversity
   1, cost 3, feasibility 3, support 2. Compute both totals and
   say which ranks higher and what the totals hide.
