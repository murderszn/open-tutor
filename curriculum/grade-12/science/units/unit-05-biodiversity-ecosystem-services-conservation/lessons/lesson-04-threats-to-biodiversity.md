# Lesson 04 — Threats to Biodiversity

**Unit 05 · Session S12 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** HS-LS2-6 (evaluate claims, evidence, and reasoning
that stable conditions maintain consistent numbers and types of
organisms, but changing conditions may result in a new ecosystem
— planning reference only; no claim of state adoption)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can name the five IPBES drivers of
biodiversity loss, read the Living Planet Index and the IUCN Red
List without overstating what their numbers mean, and rank threats
for a named place with evidence.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, calculator.

(Adult: question 2 is the key one — if the learner's three

## Explicit teaching

**Five drivers, one pattern.** The IPBES Global Assessment
(2019) names five direct drivers of biodiversity change over the
past 50 years, in rough order of impact on land:

1. **Changes in land and sea use** — forests cleared for farms,
   wetlands drained, coasts built over, seafloors trawled. The
   largest driver on land, and the one Lesson 02's species–area
   relationship predicts: less area, fewer species.
2. **Direct exploitation of organisms** — hunting, fishing, and
   harvesting faster than populations replace themselves.
3. **Climate change** — warming, shifting rainfall, and ocean
   acidification moving the conditions species are adapted to.
4. **Pollution** — nutrients, pesticides, plastics, and other
   chemicals in air, water, and soil.
5. **Invasion of alien species** — species moved by people to
   places they did not evolve in, where they can outcompete,
   eat, or infect native species.

The drivers interact: a forest fragmented by roads (driver 1) is
easier for an invasive plant to enter (driver 5), and a warming
climate (driver 3) lets that plant spread further. Behind the
five direct drivers sit indirect ones — population growth,
consumption, trade, technology, and governance — which the unit
does not ask the learner to rank, only to name when a ranking
needs context.

**Two headline numbers, read carefully.** Two figures dominate
public discussion, and both are routinely overstated. Read them
exactly:

- *IPBES (2019): around 1 million of an estimated 8 million
  animal and plant species are threatened with extinction, many
  within decades.* That is about 25% of species in the plant and
  animal groups assessed — including around 40% of amphibians,
  almost a third of reef-building corals, more than a third of
  marine mammals, and 10% of insects. "Threatened" means at risk
  by the IUCN's criteria, not already gone: most of the million
  are still alive, which is exactly why action can still matter.
- *WWF / ZSL Living Planet Report 2024: the average size of
  monitored wildlife populations fell 73% between 1970 and
  2020.* The index tracks about 35,000 populations of 5,495
  species of amphibians, birds, fish, mammals, and reptiles.
  Read the words: an *average of population-size trends*. It does
  not mean 73% of animals died, 73% of species vanished, or that
  every population fell — freshwater populations fell 85% on
  average, terrestrial 69%, marine 56%. The index is a signal of
  ecosystem stress, measured honestly; it is not a body count.

**The IUCN Red List is a risk scale, not a death list.** The
International Union for Conservation of Nature's Red List,
established in 1964, is the world's most comprehensive source on
species' extinction risk. It sorts assessed species into nine
categories: **Not Evaluated, Data Deficient, Least Concern, Near
Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered,
Extinct in the Wild, Extinct**. The three "threatened" categories
are Vulnerable, Endangered, and Critically Endangered — rising
risk, in that order. Checked 2026-11-13, the Red List reports
more than 49,500 species threatened (28% of assessed species)
out of more than 175,900 species assessed; by group, 41% of
amphibians, 26% of mammals, 34% of conifers, 11% of birds, 38%
of sharks and rays, 44% of reef corals, 28% of selected
crustaceans, 21% of reptiles, and 71% of cycads are threatened.
Two cautions: **Data Deficient** means "we do not know enough to
judge," not "safe" — a Data Deficient species may be in grave
danger. And the assessed set is not a random sample of life:
groups people study get assessed; most insects and fungi have
never been evaluated at all.

**Stable conditions, new ecosystems.** HS-LS2-6 states the
principle behind this lesson: complex interactions in ecosystems
maintain relatively consistent numbers and types of organisms in
stable conditions, but changing conditions may result in a new
ecosystem. A coral reef that loses its corals to warming does not
become an empty reef — it becomes an algae-dominated reef, a
different ecosystem with different species, different services,
and different value to people. "Loss" in this unit usually means
a change of state, not a blank. That is why the project asks for
a design that keeps a system in its current state, not a design
that merely counts species.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Read the 73% without overstating it.**

*Given:* The Living Planet Index fell 73% on average between
1970 and 2020 across about 35,000 monitored populations.

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — say what the number is: the average
change in population *size* across monitored populations. Step 2
— translate with a clearly labeled illustration: *if* one
population of 1,000 animals followed the average exactly, it
would hold about 1,000 × (1 − 0.73) = **270 animals** in 2020.
Step 3 — say what it is not: not 73% of species extinct (the
index tracks populations of living species), not 73% of all
individual animals gone (unmonitored populations, plants,
invertebrates, and fungi are mostly outside the index), and not
a uniform fall (freshwater −85%, marine −56%). Step 4 — the
honest sentence: "Monitored vertebrate populations shrank
sharply on average between 1970 and 2020, most steeply in
freshwater." *Common error:* "73% of wildlife is gone." That
sentence is wrong in three ways — it confuses populations with
species, an average trend with a total, and a monitored sample
with all wildlife.

**Example 2 — Rank the drivers for a fictional place.**

*Given (fictional place):* "Miller's Marsh," a 60-hectare
wetland beside a growing town. Over 20 years: a third of the
marsh was drained for housing; the town's storm drains now carry
road runoff into the marsh; a non-native reed has spread across
the drained edge; fishing in the marsh's creek is light and
regulated; the climate record shows warmer summers but no
drying trend yet.

*Reasoning.* Rank by evidence, strongest first: (1) **land-use
change** — a third of the habitat is gone, and Lesson 02's
species–area logic says richness falls with area; (2)
**pollution** — road runoff is a present, measured input;
(3) **invasive species** — the reed is spreading, though it
follows the drainage rather than causing it; (4) **climate
change** — warmer summers are recorded but no drying trend
links them to marsh loss yet; (5) **direct exploitation** —
fishing is light and regulated, with no evidence of decline.
*Check:* the ranking is a judgment tied to *this* place's
evidence, not a global ranking — globally, land-use change is
also first, but the other four would order differently
elsewhere. A ranking without place evidence is a guess.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult; hints are given, answers are in the
adult's answer key.

1. Place each species in its Red List category from the
   description: (a) a frog assessed as facing a very high risk of
   extinction in the wild; (b) a bird assessed as facing a high
   risk; (c) a fish facing an extremely high risk; (d) a plant
   with too little data to judge. *Hint: the risk words are
   "high," "very high," "extremely high" — in that order,
   Vulnerable, Endangered, Critically Endangered.*
2. A news headline says "73% of wildlife wiped out since 1970."
   Write the two-sentence correction, using Example 1.
3. A fictional island's main threat is rats eating seabird eggs.
   Which driver is that, and which is it not? *Hint: rats were
   brought by people — but they are not pollution, and they are
   not harvest.*

## Independent practice

Do these alone, then check with the adult. (Full items are in
Practice Set D.)

1. Explain in three sentences why "threatened" does not mean
   "already extinct," using the IPBES 1-million figure.
2. A fictional river's fish populations fell by half after a dam
   was built, while the river's water quality stayed clean and
   fishing stayed light. Rank the five drivers for this river
   with one evidence phrase each, and name the driver you rank
   first.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says "the Red List says 28% of
   species are threatened, so 28% of all species on Earth are
   threatened." What is wrong with that sentence? Correct it.

## Application

Pick a real place you know — a park, a river, a woodlot, a coast
you have visited. In your notebook, list the five drivers and
write one sentence per driver: either evidence that it acts on
your place, or the words "no evidence seen." Do not enter private
land or disturb anything; use what you can observe from public
areas and what you already know. Bring the list to the project
planning session (S14) — it is the first draft of the project's
threat ranking.

Answer in the notebook:

1. Name the five IPBES drivers in any order, one line each.
2. The Red List reports more than 49,500 threatened species out

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the five drivers are kept visible
  as a numbered list during the ranking tasks; the Red List
  categories are introduced with their risk words ("high," "very
  high," "extremely high") as memory hooks.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may rank the drivers
  by ordering five labeled cards instead of writing the ranking.
- **Extension:** choose one threatened species from the IUCN Red
  List website (adult previews) and write four sentences: its
  category, its main driver, one conservation action listed for
  it, and one question the listing does not answer. (The Red List
  is free and needs no account; record the access date.)

## Teacher note

This is the lesson where numbers get weaponized, so slow down on
Example 1: learners who can recite "73%" but cannot say what it
is an average *of* will misuse it in every later argument,
including the project's. The Data Deficient caution is worth a
full minute — bright learners often read "not enough data" as
"probably fine," and in conservation it usually means the
opposite risk: unstudied species are often the ones in trouble.
If the ranking discussion drifts into global generalities, pull
it back to the fictional place: a ranking is only as good as its
place evidence.
