# Lesson 03 — Ecosystem Services

**Unit 05 · Session S11 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** HS-LS2-7 strand (solutions reduce human impacts on
the environment and biodiversity); track objective 9 — planning
reference only; no claim of state adoption

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can sort ecosystem services into the
four Millennium Ecosystem Assessment categories, estimate the
value of one service from stated data, and say who depends on
that service and who decides its fate.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, calculator.

## Explicit teaching

**An ecosystem service is a benefit people get from nature.**
A wetland that cleans water, a forest that steadies a hillside,
bees that pollinate an orchard, a park where a family walks on a
Sunday — all are services, whether or not anyone pays for them.
The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MEA, 2005), a four-year
study by more than 1,300 scientists for the United Nations,
sorted services into four categories. Learn the four; every
classification task in this unit uses them:

- **Provisioning services** — products taken from ecosystems:
  food, fresh water, timber, fibre, fuel, medicinal plants.
  The test: you harvest or extract something.
- **Regulating services** — benefits from ecosystems keeping
  conditions steady: water purification, flood control, climate
  regulation, pollination, pest control, disease regulation,
  erosion control. The test: the ecosystem does work that would
  otherwise need engineering or medicine.
- **Cultural services** — non-material benefits: recreation,
  beauty, spiritual value, education, a sense of place. The test:
  the benefit is in experience, meaning, or learning.
- **Supporting services** — the processes underneath all the
  others: photosynthesis, nutrient cycling, soil formation, the
  water cycle. The test: without it, the other three stop. A
  common error is to count a supporting service *and* the
  service it supports as two separate values — that double-counts
  the same benefit.

**Valuation is an estimate with named assumptions, not a price
tag on nature.** Economists value services in several ways. This
lesson uses the simplest, **replacement cost**: what would it cost
to replace the service with engineering? If a wetland filters the
water a town drinks, the replacement cost is the price of the
treatment plant that would do the same job. A second way is
**avoided damage**: what harm would happen without the service —
flood damage a floodplain prevents, hospital visits a clean-air
forest avoids. A third is **market value**: the price of the extra
crop that pollinators make possible. Every method needs stated
assumptions: the area, the rate, the price, the time period, and
what is left out. A valuation that hides its assumptions is not
an estimate; it is an advertisement. And some values — a species'
existence, a sacred grove — resist money entirely; a good
valuation says so instead of forcing a number.

**Who depends, and who decides.** Services have beneficiaries
and decision-makers, and they are often different people. A
downstream town depends on an upstream forest's flood control,
but the forest's owner decides whether to log it. Pollinators
serve every farm in a valley, but no single farmer owns them.
Indigenous and local communities often depend most directly on
ecosystem services and hold knowledge about them that no
dataset replaces — the IPBES assessment (2019) was the first of
its kind to include Indigenous and local knowledge as evidence.
When this unit asks "who decides," it is asking about power,
not just ecology: a conservation design that ignores who
benefits and who pays will fail even if its biology is right.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Value a wetland's water filtration (replacement cost).**

*Given (fictional practice data):* A 40-hectare wetland filters
water for a nearby town. A treatment plant doing the same job
would cost $12,000 per hectare of wetland replaced, per year.
The wetland also stores floodwater, avoiding an estimated
$200,000 per year in flood damage.

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — filtration: 40 ha × $12,000/ha/year =
**$480,000 per year**. Step 2 — flood storage: **$200,000 per
year** (an avoided-damage estimate; the lesson states it as an
estimate, not a measurement). Step 3 — total: $480,000 +
$200,000 = **$680,000 per year**, or $680,000 ÷ 40 =
**$17,000 per hectare per year**. Step 4 — name the
assumptions: the $12,000 figure assumes the plant matches the
wetland's filtration exactly; the $200,000 assumes one flood
pattern; neither counts the wetland's wildlife, recreation, or
carbon storage — so $680,000 is a *partial* value, a floor, not
a total. *Check:* compare with the alternative — converting the
wetland to farmland is stated to net $900 per hectare per year
(fictional). The service value is roughly 19 times the farming
return, which is the kind of comparison a planner needs — but
only if the assumptions travel with the number.

**Example 2 — Value pollination (market value).**

*Given (fictional practice data):* A 120-hectare orchard yields
30 tonnes per hectare with pollinators and 18 tonnes per hectare
without them. Fruit sells for $400 per tonne (fictional price).

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — the pollination gain: 30 − 18 = **12
tonnes per hectare**. Step 2 — across the orchard:
12 × 120 = **1,440 tonnes**. Step 3 — market value:
1,440 × $400 = **$576,000 per year**. Step 4 — name what is
left out: the price is fictional and changes yearly; the
calculation assumes the yield gap is all pollination (weather,
soil, and pests also move yields); it values the crop, not the
pollinators' other work. *Common error:* valuing the whole
30 tonnes per hectare as "the pollinators' contribution" — the
trees, soil, water, and farmer do most of that work; only the
12-tonne difference belongs to pollination.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult; hints are given, answers are in the
adult's answer key.

1. Classify each service into one MEA category, and state the
   test you used: (a) a forest's timber harvest; (b) the same
   forest slowing a flood; (c) a child's first birdwatching walk
   in that forest; (d) soil forming under the forest floor.
   *Hint: harvest = take a product; flood-slowing = keeps
   conditions steady; the walk = experience; soil formation =
   underneath everything else.*
2. A fictional 25-hectare woodland filters water at a
   replacement cost of $8,000 per hectare per year. What is the
   annual filtration value? *Hint: one multiplication; then say
   what the number leaves out.*
3. Who depends on the pollination in Example 2, and who decides
   whether the pollinators survive? Name one dependent group and
   one decision-maker, and one sentence on why they might
   disagree.

## Independent practice

Do these alone, then check with the adult. (Full items are in

1. Classify six services (given in Practice Set C) and name the

2. A fictional coastal mangrove forest of 60 hectares protects a

   and supports a fishery worth $500,000 per year. Compute the
   total annual value and the per-hectare value. State two

3. Error analysis: a classmate says "the wetland is worth
   $680,000 a year, so draining it would cost the town
   $680,000 a year." What is wrong with that sentence? Correct it.

## Application

Choose one service from your own neighborhood — shade from
street trees, a park's recreation, a stream's water, bees on a
garden. In your notebook, write: the service, its MEA category
with the test, one group that depends on it, one person or body
that decides its fate, and one sentence on what a valuation of
it would have to assume. Do not contact anyone or enter private
land; this is observation and reasoning only.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the four categories are introduced
  with a one-line test each; the adult keeps the category list
  visible during classification tasks.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort service cards
  (written by the adult from the guided-practice list) into four
  piles instead of writing the classification.
- **Extension:** research (adult previews) one real valuation
  study — for example, the city that protects its watershed
  instead of building a filtration plant — and write three
  sentences: what service was valued, by what method, and what
  the study left out. (No real figure is required to be
  memorized; the method and the caveat are the point.)

## Teacher note

The double-counting trap is the lesson's core discipline: learners
love to add supporting services to the total, which inflates the
number and teaches bad accounting. Insist that every valuation
ends with "and this leaves out…" — the sentence that makes an
estimate honest. The "who decides" discussion can run long; keep
it to one dependent group and one decision-maker per service, and
save the full stakeholder analysis for the project.
