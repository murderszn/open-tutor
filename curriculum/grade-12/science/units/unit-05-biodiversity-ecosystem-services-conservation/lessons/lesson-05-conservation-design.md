# Lesson 05 — Conservation Design

**Unit 05 · Session S16 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** HS-LS2-7 (design, evaluate, and refine a solution
for reducing human impacts on the environment and biodiversity),
HS-LS4-6 (create or revise a simulation to test a solution to
mitigate adverse impacts on biodiversity), HS-ESS3-3 (computational
simulation relating resource management, population sustainability,
and biodiversity — planning references only; no claim of state
adoption)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can compare conservation strategies,
score options against explicit criteria, and run the
design–evaluate–refine cycle on a solution for a real place.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, calculator or spreadsheet.

## Explicit teaching

**Conservation is a menu, not a single tool.** Different threats
need different responses, and the first skill is matching the
tool to the driver:

- **Protected areas** — land or sea set aside and managed for
  nature (national parks, reserves, marine protected areas).
  Strongest against land-use change; weakest when the threat
  comes from outside the boundary, like climate change or
  upstream pollution.
- **Corridors and connectivity** — strips or stepping stones of
  habitat linking protected areas, so individuals (and genes)
  can move. The answer to fragmentation: Lesson 02's fragments
  regain some of what a single block would hold.
- **Restoration** — rebuilding habitat that was damaged: replanting
  native vegetation, re-wetting a drained marsh, removing a
  small dam. Slower than protection and never a perfect replica,
  but it can return services and species.
- **Ex situ conservation** — keeping species alive away from their
  habitat: seed banks, captive breeding, botanical gardens. A
  last resort or a bridge, not a substitute for habitat: a species
  kept only in a zoo is not a functioning part of an ecosystem.
- **Sustainable use** — harvesting at rates populations can
  replace: quotas, seasons, certified timber and fisheries. The
  answer to overexploitation when a total ban would fail or be
  unjust.
- **Other effective area-based conservation measures (OECMs)** —
  areas outside formal protected areas that still conserve
  biodiversity in practice, such as Indigenous and community
  conserved territories or a watershed managed for clean water.
  The Kunming–Montreal framework counts them toward its targets.

**The global target: 30 by 30.** The Kunming–Montreal Global
Biodiversity Framework, adopted by parties to the Convention on
Biological Diversity on 19 December 2022, sets 4 goals for 2050
and 23 targets for 2030. Target 3 — "30 by 30" — commits
countries to conserve at least 30% of terrestrial, inland-water,
and coastal and marine areas by 2030 through protected areas,
OECMs, and, where applicable, Indigenous and traditional
territories. Target 2 commits to restoring at least 30% of
degraded ecosystems by 2030. At adoption, about 17% of land and
10% of ocean were protected — so the target means roughly
doubling land protection and tripling ocean protection in eight
years. The framework also requires that conservation be
effectively managed and equitably governed, with respect for the
rights of Indigenous Peoples and local communities. A target is
not a result: 30% of an area on paper, badly managed, protects
less than 10% managed well.

**Judge options by criteria, not by enthusiasm.** A design is
evaluated against **criteria** (what counts as good: biodiversity
gain, cost, feasibility, community support) under **constraints**
(what cannot change: budget, law, land ownership, safety). This
lesson scores each option 1–3 on each criterion, multiplies by a
stated weight when one criterion matters more, and totals the
score — a decision matrix. The matrix does not choose for you:
it makes the tradeoff visible so the choice can be argued about.
A 1–3 score is a judgment, not a measurement; the adult should
ask "what evidence is that 3 based on?" for every high score.

**Design, evaluate, refine — then repeat.** HS-LS2-7 names a
cycle, not a single act: **design** a solution from the evidence,
**evaluate** it against criteria and the first results,
**refine** it in response to what the evaluation found, and run
the cycle again. A refinement is evidence-based when it answers
a specific finding — "the corridor is too narrow for the target
species, so widen it at the road crossing" — and it is decoration
when it only adds features. HS-LS4-6 adds a tool to the cycle: a
simulation — in this unit, the project's spreadsheet model — that
lets the learner test a refined design *before* spending money or
moving earth: change the corridor width in the model, watch the
predicted richness change, and only then recommend the change.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Score three options for Miller's Marsh.**

*Given:* Miller's Marsh (Lesson 04) lost a third of its area to
housing; road runoff pollutes it; a non-native reed spreads
along the drained edge. Three options, scored 1–3
(3 = best) on four criteria. Weights: biodiversity gain ×2,
cost ×1 (3 = cheapest), feasibility ×1, community support ×1.

| Option | Biodiversity gain (×2) | Cost (×1) | Feasibility (×1) | Community support (×1) | Total (max 15) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Buy and re-wet 10 ha of drained edge | 3 → 6 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 11 |
| B. Build a runoff treatment strip | 2 → 4 | 2 | 3 | 3 | 12 |
| C. Remove the invasive reed by hand, yearly | 1 → 2 | 3 | 3 | 2 | 10 |

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — read the totals: B scores highest (12),
A next (11), C last (10). Step 2 — ask what the totals hide:
A is the only option that restores *area*, which Lesson 02 says
is the strongest lever on richness — but it is also the most
expensive and needs a willing seller, which is why its cost and
feasibility scores are low. B treats the second-ranked driver
(pollution) with the best feasibility and support. C is cheap
and easy but fights a symptom: the reed will return while the
drained edge stays drained. Step 3 — the recommendation: start
with B now, pursue A as the longer-term goal, and use C as
maintenance inside A, not as a standalone plan. *Check:* the
matrix did not "pick" B; it showed that B wins on feasibility
and support while A wins on biodiversity. The judgment — start
with B, pursue A — comes from the ranking plus the matrix, and
it says so.

**Example 2 — Refine a design with a simulation.**

*Given:* The learner's first design for a fictional woodland
corridor is a 10-metre-wide planted strip linking two woods,
predicted by the project's spreadsheet model (S = cA^z, with
the strip's effective area) to add 2 species to the smaller
wood. Field notes show deer browse the strip's seedlings.

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — evaluate: the design meets the
connectivity criterion in the model but fails in the field —
seedlings do not survive to become corridor. Step 2 — refine:
widen the strip to 20 metres at the road crossing (where
browsing is heaviest) and protect seedlings with guards for two
years; re-run the model with the larger effective area and check
the predicted gain. Step 3 — name the evidence for the change:
the browsing observation, not a preference for wider strips.
*Common error:* refining by adding features ("also add a pond
and a bird hide") without a finding that calls for them. Every
refinement in the project must name the evaluation finding it
answers.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult; hints are given, answers are in the
adult's answer key.

1. Match each tool to the driver it answers best: protected area,
   corridor, captive breeding, fishing quota. Drivers: land-use
   change, fragmentation, extinction risk for one species,
   overexploitation. *Hint: a quota limits a take; a corridor
   fixes a split, not a loss.*
2. A fictional town can protect 30% of its green space by 2030
   or 15% managed twice as well. Which better serves Target 3's
   words "effectively conserved and managed"? *Hint: read the
   target's words — "effectively" is doing work.*
3. Score a fourth option for Miller's Marsh — D. Do nothing —
   on the Example 1 matrix (biodiversity gain 1, cost 3,
   feasibility 3, community support 1). What is its total, and
   why does a high score on cost not save it?

## Independent practice

Do these alone, then check with the adult. (Full items are in
Practice Set E.)

1. A fictional reserve's design scores 9 of 15 on the Example 1
   matrix. The adult asks for one refinement. Write the
   refinement, the evaluation finding it answers, and how you
   would test it in the spreadsheet model.
2. Explain in three sentences why ex situ conservation alone
   cannot meet Target 3.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says "30 by 30 means protecting
   30% of every country." What is wrong with that sentence?
   Correct it.

## Application

Take the place from Lesson 04's application (your park, river,
woodlot, or coast). In your notebook, choose the top-ranked
driver from your Lesson 04 list and propose one conservation
action against it, naming: the tool (from this lesson's menu),
one criterion it scores well on, one it scores badly on, and one
refinement you would make after a first evaluation. Bring this
to the project sessions — it is the first draft of the project's
design.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the strategy menu is kept visible
  during matching tasks; "OECM" is always written out in full on
  first use in a session.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may score the matrix
  by placing numbered tokens on a printed table instead of
  writing scores.
- **Extension:** read (adult previews) one country's or
  organization's 30-by-30 progress report and write three
  sentences: the percentage protected now, the largest gap named
  in the report, and whether the report discusses management
  effectiveness or only coverage. (Coverage without management
  is the lesson's standing caution.)

## Teacher note

The matrix is a thinking tool, not a calculator: if a learner's
scores all come out 3s, the criteria are not biting — ask which
option is *worst* on each criterion and why, and make them
defend one low score out loud. The "do nothing" option in guided
practice is deliberate: it usually scores well on cost and
feasibility, and learners need to see a matrix that still rejects
it because the biodiversity criterion is weighted. If the project
designs start to sprawl ("protect everything, restore
everything"), pull the learner back to one driver, one tool, one
refinement — the unit's project rubric rewards a narrow design
done well over a broad design asserted.
