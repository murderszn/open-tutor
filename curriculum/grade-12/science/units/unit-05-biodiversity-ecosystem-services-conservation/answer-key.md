**Adult-only. Never place beside a student question.** Worked
solutions for every lesson item, practice item, quiz question,
assessment task, and project rubric application. All datasets are

## Lesson 01 — What biodiversity measures

**Guided practice**
1. All p = 0.25, ln 0.25 = −1.39; each term 0.25 × (−1.39) =
   −0.35 (four times); sum = −1.39, so H′ = **1.39**, the maximum
   for S = 4 (ln 4 = 1.39). Perfectly even.
2. Zones: open pond water (lily pads, mallard ducks, dragonflies);
   marshy bank (cattails, grey heron); wildflower meadow
   (purple coneflowers, yellow black-eyed susans, tall grasses,
   monarch butterfly, brown rabbit). The meadow holds the most
   nameable types (5, against 3 for the pond and 2 for the marsh).
   That does not prove it is the most diverse zone: the picture
   shows types, not abundances, so H′ cannot be computed from it,
   and it misses hidden species (soil life, nocturnal animals,
   fish below the surface).
3. The woodlot (H′ = 1.80) is more diverse than the garden
   (H′ = 0.90). Same richness (8), so the difference is entirely
   **evenness**: the woodlot's individuals are spread more evenly
   across its species.

**Independent practice**
1. Same as Practice Set A1: p = 0.50, 0.30, 0.10, 0.10.
   Terms: 0.50 × (−0.69) = −0.35; 0.30 × (−1.20) = −0.36;
   0.10 × (−2.30) = −0.23 (twice). Sum = −1.17, so H′ = **1.17**;
   J = 1.17 ÷ 1.39 = **0.84**.
2. Diversity can fall while richness holds: if one species comes
   to dominate (evenness falls), H′ falls even though no species
   is lost. Example: a meadow keeps 5 species but grasses grow
   from 40% to 90% of individuals.
3. The sentence is wrong because H′ has no absolute zero point
   that means "no biodiversity": 0.46 describes a strongly
   uneven 5-species community, not an empty one. Corrected:
   "Meadow B has the same richness as Meadow A (5 species) but
   much lower evenness (H′ = 0.46 against 1.47), because one
   species dominates it."

**Application (scoring notes):** zones named with one organism type
each (full marks); each observation matched to a level — the zone
list is ecosystem diversity, the organism list is species
diversity, and any within-species variation noted (flower color,
size) is genetic diversity. Accept "not observed" for genetic
diversity with a reason.

**Exit check**
1. Genetic diversity — variety of genes within a species; species
   diversity — variety of species and their evenness in a place;
   ecosystem diversity — variety of habitats/ecosystems in a
   region.
2. The H′ = 1.70 plot is more diverse. The difference is
   **evenness** (richness is the same, 6).

**Extension:** counts 60, 30, 10: p = 0.60, 0.30, 0.10.
Terms: 0.60 × (−0.51) = −0.31; 0.30 × (−1.20) = −0.36;
0.10 × (−2.30) = −0.23. Sum = −0.90, so H′ = **0.90**;
J = 0.90 ÷ 1.10 = **0.82** — moderately uneven against the
perfectly even 1.10.

## Lesson 02 — How biodiversity changes

**Guided practice**
1. A = 10: 10^0.30 ≈ 2.00, so S = 6 × 2.00 = **12**. A = 100:
   100^0.30 ≈ 3.98, so S = 6 × 3.98 = **23.9, about 24**. A
   tenfold area increase only about doubles richness — the
   relationship flattens.
2. The curve passes through (A = 16, S = 16) and (A = 81,
   S = 24), matching Example 1. Flattening means each added unit
   of area adds fewer new species than the last.
3. Both changed: richness fell (15 → 8 species) and evenness
   fell (mussels came to dominate the pool).

**Independent practice**
1. Same as Practice Set B1: A = 25: 25^0.20 ≈ 1.90, so
   S = 5 × 1.90 = **9.5, about 10 species**. Halved to 12.5 km²:
   12.5^0.20 ≈ 1.66, so S = 5 × 1.66 = **8.3, about 8 species**.
   Predicted loss: **about 1–2 species**.
2. Extinction is natural at the background rate (about one
   extinction per million species per year), but current rates
   run tens to hundreds of times above that background. The
   argument confuses "happens naturally" with "happens at a
   natural rate" — the rate is the problem, not the existence.
3. Missing: a controlled comparison and evidence for each link.
   Wolves may have contributed, but drought, bears, elk numbers,
   and other factors also changed; a before-and-after is not a
   controlled experiment. Corrected: "After wolves returned,
   willows recovered in some places; how much of that recovery
   was wolves, and how much was other factors, is still being
   measured."

**Application:** Before: S = 8 × 81^0.25 = 8 × 3 = **24
species**. After (40.5 km²): 40.5^0.25 ≈ 2.52, so S = 8 × 2.52 =
**20.2, about 20 species** — a predicted loss of about 4
species. Evidence statement (model answer): "Halving the island
from 81 to 40.5 km² is predicted to cut richness from about 24
to about 20 species under S = 8·A^0.25. The model predicts a
number, not which species are lost, and it ignores edge effects
and habitat quality. The question it cannot answer is which four
species would go — and whether one of them is the one the island
cannot afford to lose."

**Exit check**
1. Speciation adds species; extinction removes them.
2. Before: 100^0.25 ≈ 3.16, so S = 10 × 3.16 = **31.6, about 32
   species**. After: 25^0.25 ≈ 2.24, so S = 10 × 2.24 = **22.4,
   about 22 species**.

**Extension:** A = 1 → S = 8; A = 16 → S = 16; A = 81 → S = 24;
A = 256 → S = 32 (256^0.25 = 4). On log–log axes the four
points lie on a straight line of slope 0.25 — e.g., in base 10,
log S rises from 0.90 to 1.51 while log A rises from 0 to 2.41,
and 0.61 ÷ 2.41 ≈ 0.25.

## Lesson 03 — Ecosystem services

**Guided practice**
1. (a) Provisioning — the harvest test: a product is taken.
   (b) Regulating — the steady-conditions test: the forest does
   work that engineering would otherwise do. (c) Cultural — the
   experience test: the benefit is in the walk itself.
   (d) Supporting — the underneath test: without soil formation
   the other services stop.
2. 25 ha × $8,000/ha/year = **$200,000 per year**. The number
   leaves out the woodland's wildlife, recreation, carbon
   storage, and flood control — it is a partial value.
3. Dependents: orchard farmers, beekeepers, fruit buyers.
   Deciders: landowners, pesticide regulators, developers.
   Disagreement (sample): a farmer may want a pesticide that
   protects the crop this year while the beekeeper needs the
   forage the spray would kill.

**Independent practice**
1. Same as Practice Set C1: (a) provisioning, (b) regulating,
   (c) cultural, (d) supporting, (e) provisioning,
   (f) regulating.
2. Same as Practice Set C2: total $3,000,000 + $500,000 =
   **$3,500,000 per year**; per hectare $3,500,000 ÷ 60 =
   **$58,333, about $58,300 per hectare per year**. Assumptions
   (any two): the avoided-damage figure assumes one storm
   pattern; the fishery value assumes current catch and prices
   hold; neither counts the mangrove's carbon storage or nursery
   habitat.
3. Same as Practice Set C3: draining the wetland would not cost
   exactly $680,000 a year — the figure is a partial estimate
   built on assumptions (a matching treatment plant, one flood
   pattern), it leaves out wildlife, recreation, and carbon
   storage, and the loss would arrive over time, not as a single
   annual bill. Corrected: "The wetland's filtration and flood
   services are estimated at $680,000 a year on stated
   assumptions; draining it would remove at least that much value
   and more we did not count."

**Application (scoring notes):** service named with correct MEA
category and test (full marks); one dependent group and one
decision-maker named; one assumption stated for a valuation.
Accept any local service the learner can observe.

**Exit check**
1. Provisioning (food, water, timber); regulating (flood
   control, pollination, water purification); cultural
   (recreation, beauty, spiritual value, education); supporting
   (photosynthesis, nutrient cycling, soil formation).
2. 15 × $10,000 = **$150,000 per year**. Most important
   assumption: the $10,000 per hectare per year replacement cost
   matches the reed bed's filtration exactly — same water
   quality, same volume, every year.

**Extension (scoring notes):** full marks for naming the service,
the valuation method, and one omission — the figure itself is
not memorized or graded.

## Lesson 05 — Conservation design

**Guided practice**
1. Protected area → land-use change; corridor → fragmentation;
   captive breeding → extinction risk for one species; fishing
   quota → overexploitation.
2. The 15% managed twice as well better serves the word
   **"effectively"** — coverage without management protects
   little. But Target 3 requires both: 30% coverage *and*
   effective management. Neither option alone satisfies the
   target; the town should aim for 30% that is also effectively
   managed.
3. D scores 1 × 2 + 3 + 3 + 1 = **9**. Cost does not save it
   because biodiversity gain is weighted ×2 and scores 1 — doing
   nothing is cheap and easy, and it also does nothing for the
   marsh.

**Independent practice**
1. Same as Practice Set E1: any refinement tied to a finding,
   e.g., "The design scored 1 on feasibility because it needs a
   land purchase; refine it to a voluntary management agreement
   with the landowner and re-score feasibility in the model; if
   the total rises above the alternative, recommend the refined
   design." A refinement with no finding ("add a pond") scores 0.
2. Same as Practice Set E2: ex situ keeps individuals alive away
   from their habitat, so it cannot conserve land or sea areas;
   Target 3 counts areas conserved, not individuals stored; and a
   species kept only in captivity is not a functioning part of an
   ecosystem, so it cannot deliver ecosystem services.
3. Same as Practice Set E3: 30 by 30 is a *global* target — at
   least 30% of the world's land, inland waters, and coastal and
   marine areas — not 30% of every country. Some countries will
   protect more, some less; the commitment is global.

**Application (scoring notes):** one action against the
top-ranked driver (full marks); tool named from the menu; one
criterion it scores well on and one badly; one refinement for
after a first evaluation.

**Exit check**
1. Design — propose a solution from the evidence; evaluate —
   judge it against criteria and first results; refine — change
   it in response to what the evaluation found, then repeat.
2. At least **30%** of terrestrial, inland-water, and coastal
   and marine areas, **by 2030**; the word is **"effectively"**
   ("effectively conserved and managed").

**Extension (scoring notes):** full marks for the current
percentage protected, the largest gap the report names, and a
yes/no on whether it discusses management effectiveness.

## Project — scoring notes

Apply the rubric in `project.md` (40 points). Notes:

- **Survey:** full marks need the effort recorded (time, area,
  method) — a table of counts without effort caps at half marks.
  "Unknown — described" entries count as good practice, not as
  errors.
- **Species–area model:** the halved-area calculation uses the
  project's stated constants (c = 8, z = 0.25); accept any
  correct arithmetic from the learner's own measured area. The
  limit sentence ("the model cannot say which species are lost")
  is required for full marks.
- **Service valuation:** the method must be named and the
  assumptions stated; a total without a method caps at half
  marks. "Cost not estimated," stated honestly, is acceptable.
- **Threat ranking:** five drivers with place evidence; a global
  ranking pasted without place evidence caps at half marks.
- **Design and matrix:** the action must target the top-ranked
  driver; an action against a lower driver caps at half marks.
  Both the design and one alternative must be scored.
- **Refinement:** must answer a named evaluation finding and show
  a before/after model test; decoration without a finding scores 0.
- **Communication:** the tradeoff must name gain, cost, who bears
  it, and what is not fixed. Sources need access dates.
