## Lesson guided practice (short answers)

- **L01:** (1) cos 30° ≈ 0.87 of overhead. (2) Absorbed
  800 × 0.75 = 600 W/m²; reflected 200 W/m². (3) Deflected right
  (NH); joins the northeast trade winds. (4) Hadley air sinks near
  30°; sinking warms and dries, suppressing cloud formation.
  (5) Errors: Coriolis comes from Earth's *rotation* (not its
  orbit), and toilet swirl is set by bowl shape/jets — the Coriolis
  effect is negligible at that scale.
- **L02:** (1) North Equatorial (trades) → Kuroshio (north along
  Asia's east coast) → North Pacific Current (westerlies) →
  California Current (south along North America's west coast).
  (2) Cold — west-coast currents flow equatorward; the Peru
  (Humboldt) Current. (3) Water: 8,360 ÷ (2,000 × 4.18) =
  8,360 ÷ 8,360 = 1.0 °C. Sand: 8,360 ÷ (2,000 × 0.84) =
  8,360 ÷ 1,680 ≈ 4.98 °C ≈ 5.0 °C. (4) Fresher surface water is
  less dense → less sinking → slower deep circulation → less heat
  drawn down in the North Atlantic region. (5) Error: the Gulf
  Stream's warmth comes from its tropical origin; the current
  transports heat poleward, it does not create it by flowing near
  the equator.
- **L03:** (1) cT = continental tropical: hot, dry; summer heat,
  clear skies. (2) Cold front (blue triangles) moving east.
  (3) Cold front passed; the system was in the mature-to-occluded
  transition (warm sector replaced by cold air). (4) The warm
  sector holds warm, moist mT air that, when lifted, condenses and
  releases latent heat — the fuel for severe storms. (5) Errors:
  NH lows spin counterclockwise; the warm front is on the east
  side of the low.
- **L04:** (1) Near the low — closer isobars mean a steeper
  pressure gradient and stronger winds. (2) 18Z = 12:00 CST
  (UTC−6) or 13:00 CDT (UTC−5). (3) A low is approaching: falling
  pressure plus wind veering to southerly (warm-sector inflow).
  (4) Windward: air rises, cools ~9.8 °C/km dry (slower once
  saturated), condenses, rains out. Lee: dry air descends, warms,
  relative humidity falls — rain shadow. (5) Error: isobars join
  equal *pressure*; isotherms join equal temperature. Confusing
  them would misread the pressure gradient and hence the wind.
- **L05:** (1) 14/20 = 70% probability of precipitation; wide
  disagreement on timing means low confidence in timing even
  though the headline probability is 70%. (2) Expected ≈ 20% × 50
  = 10 days; observed 22 — poorly calibrated, over-forecasting
  dryness (the forecaster should raise the stated probabilities).
  (3) Warming → ice melts → darker surface exposed → lower albedo
  → more absorption → more warming (positive feedback).
  (4) Accept any rewrite with a probability or range, a time
  window, and the condition that would change it (for example,
  "60% chance of 3–6 inches Saturday afternoon, less if the storm
  tracks south"). (5) Error: a 20% forecast expects rain about one
  day in five; one rainy day is consistent with it, not proof
  against it.

## Practice Set A

1. cos 45° ≈ 0.71; 1,000 × 0.71 = 710 W/m².
2. Snow: absorbed 600 × (1 − 0.8) = 120 W/m²; reflected 480 W/m².
   Forest: absorbed 600 × (1 − 0.15) = 510 W/m²; reflected 90 W/m².
   The forest warms faster because it absorbs ~4 times more.
3. Southeast trade winds; Coriolis deflects left in the Southern
   Hemisphere; equatorward flow bent left comes from the southeast.
4. Hadley cell air sinks near 30° after rising at the equator;
   sinking air warms and its relative humidity falls, suppressing
   cloud formation and rain.
5. Zero at the equator; strongest at the poles; deflection grows
   with sin(latitude); sin 30° = 0.5.
6. Incorrect. Inflowing air is deflected right in the NH, producing
   counterclockwise rotation around a low.
7. Accept: tilt aims each hemisphere's Sun higher in summer (more
   direct rays, higher energy per square metre) and gives longer
   days; distance from the Sun is not the cause.

## Practice Set B

1. North Equatorial Current (northeast trades) → Gulf Stream
   (turned north along North America's east coast) → North Atlantic
   Current (westerlies) → Canary Current (south along Europe/Africa
   west coasts, rejoining the trades).
2. Warm — the Kuroshio: east coasts carry tropical water poleward.
3. Water: 2,090 ÷ (500 × 4.18) = 2,090 ÷ 2,090 = 1.0 °C. Sand:
   2,090 ÷ (500 × 0.84) = 2,090 ÷ 420 ≈ 4.98 °C ≈ 5.0 °C.
4. Accept a chain: ocean water stores heat (high heat capacity) and
   releases it slowly → coastal air is moderated in both seasons →
   inland ground heats and cools fast → large seasonal swings.
5. Density increases; the water sinks and drives deep (thermohaline)
   circulation. Fresher water is less dense, so less of it sinks and
   the circulation slows.
6. The California Current is cold: it flows equatorward along a
   west coast, carrying cool water from higher latitudes — the
   gyre rule, not California's climate, sets its character.

## Practice Set C

1. mP = maritime polar: cool, moist; cool cloudy weather, drizzle
   or light rain when lifted. cT = continental tropical: hot, dry;
   hot clear weather, heat and drought risk.
2. Warm air is advancing northward; expect gradual cloud
   thickening, steady rain, then warming as the warm air arrives.
3. Order: stationary front → wave → mature → occluded. Mature:
   cold front west of the low, warm front east, warm sector between.
4. Warm front approaching: rising temperature (warm air arriving),
   falling pressure (low approaching), clouds thickening ahead of
   the front.
5. A cold front is a steep wedge: abrupt, intense lifting over a
   narrow band → short, heavy precipitation. A warm front is a
   gentle ramp: gradual lifting over a wide area → prolonged,
   steadier precipitation.
6. Yes. Town A (warm sector) can have showers/thunderstorms from
   warm, moist, unstable air; Town B (behind the cold front) can
   have post-frontal showers then clearing in the cold air mass.
   Same cyclone, different air masses and mechanisms.

## Practice Set D

1. Winds are stronger near the low: closer isobars = steeper
   pressure gradient = stronger wind.
2. 00Z = 18:00 CST (UTC−6) the previous day — Sunday 6:00 p.m. CST
   for a Monday 00Z chart.
3. Day 4: pressure reached its minimum then rose; wind shifted
   SW→NW; temperature fell from 24 °C to 14 °C.
4. Final temperature: 15 − (9.8 × 3) = 15 − 29.4 = −14.4 °C. The
   parcel reaches its 12 °C dew point after (15 − 12) ÷ 9.8 ≈ 0.31
   km of lift, so condensation is expected well before 3 km.
5. Accept four steps: (a) moist air arrives at the range;
   (b) forced ascent cools it (~9.8 °C/km dry, slower when
   saturated), vapor condenses, windward rain falls; (c) dry air
   descends the lee slope and warms by compression; (d) the lee
   side is dry — rain shadow.
6. A high means sinking air, which usually brings clear skies and
   light winds. Stormy weather sits near lows and fronts, where
   air rises.

## Practice Set E

1. 10 ÷ 40 = 25% probability of precipitation. Wide disagreement on
   timing means low confidence in *when* rain falls, even with a
   stated probability.
2. Expected ≈ 10% × 200 = 20 days; observed 19 — well calibrated.
3. Warming → ice melts → darker surface (lower albedo) → more
   sunlight absorbed → more warming. The loop amplifies the
   original change (positive feedback).
4. Accept any rewrite with a probability or range, a time window,
   and the condition that would change it (for example, "50%
   chance of 2–5 inches Saturday, more if the storm tracks north").
5. The 2-day forecast was better *for that day*, but the comparison
   is unfair without lead time: skill declines with lead time, so
   forecasts must be compared at the same lead time or over many
   cases, not on one outcome.

## Unit assessment

1. Hadley cell air rises at the equator and sinks near 30°; sinking
   air warms, dries, and suppresses cloud formation → deserts.
2. The westerlies: Ferrel-cell surface flow heads poleward and is
   deflected right (NH), giving wind from the west, blowing east.
3. Water: 4,180 ÷ (1,000 × 4.18) = 1.0 °C. Soil:
   4,180 ÷ (1,000 × 0.84) = 4,180 ÷ 840 ≈ 4.98 °C ≈ 5.0 °C. Coastal
   cities are moderated by the ocean's heat storage; inland cities
   swing with the land's fast heating and cooling.
4. Cold temperature and high salinity both increase density. Fresher
   surface water is less dense, so less of it sinks and deep
   circulation slows.
5. cP = continental polar: cold, dry air from cold land — cold,
   clear winter weather.
6. A cold front moving east; expect showers/thunderstorms, a sharp
   temperature drop, a wind shift, then clearing and rising
   pressure.
7. Day 4: pressure minimum then rising; wind shift SW→NW;
   temperature fall 23 → 13 °C (any three count).
8. Day 3: mT — warm (22 °C), southerly wind, rain (warm, moist air).
   Day 5: cP (accept mP with justification) — cool (14 °C),
   northwesterly wind, clear skies, rising pressure (cold, dry air
   mass in place).
9. Rain is a symptom of the lifting, not the cause of the cooling:
   the temperature fell because the air mass changed (warm mT air
   replaced by cold air behind the front).
10. Drawing: Hadley 0–30° (rise at equator, sink at 30°), Ferrel
    30–60°, polar 60–90° (sink at pole, rise at 60°); surface winds:
    trades toward the equator, westerlies poleward, polar easterlies
    toward 60°. Sentences must name unequal heating, pressure
    differences, and Coriolis deflection.
11. The first forecaster is better calibrated: 17 ÷ 60 ≈ 28%,
    close to the stated 30%. The second forecaster is right 75% of
    the time but gives no usable probability information — a single
    day's outcome cannot verify or refute a probability forecast;
    only a large sample can.
