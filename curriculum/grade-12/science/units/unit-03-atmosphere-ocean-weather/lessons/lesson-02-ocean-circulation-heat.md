# Lesson 02 — Ocean Circulation: Gyres, Deep Water, and the Climate Flywheel

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can trace a surface gyre from
its winds, explain density-driven deep circulation, and use heat
capacity to explain why oceans moderate climate.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, calculator, a world
map or globe (any atlas or the resource-pack map links).

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** State the Northern Hemisphere
Coriolis rule (deflects right) and name the three surface wind belts
(trades, westerlies, polar easterlies). If either is shaky, re-read
Lesson 01, section 3.

## 1. Wind pushes the surface ocean into gyres

The wind belts of Lesson 01 drag on the ocean surface. Because the
winds blow in different directions at different latitudes, and
because continents block the flow, the surface water organizes into
five great loops called **gyres** — clockwise in the Northern
Hemisphere, counterclockwise in the Southern Hemisphere (the same
Coriolis logic as the atmosphere, applied to water).

Trace the North Atlantic gyre, leg by leg, and check each leg against
a wind belt:

1. **North Equatorial Current** — the northeast trades push warm
   water westward across the tropical Atlantic.
2. **Gulf Stream** — blocked by the Americas, the water turns north
   along the east coast of North America. This is a warm, fast
   current; it carries tropical heat toward higher latitudes and is
   why western Europe is milder than other places at its latitude.
3. **North Atlantic Current** — the westerlies push the water
   eastward toward Europe.
4. **Canary Current** — the water turns south along the west coasts
   of Europe and northwest Africa, cool now, and rejoins the North
   Equatorial Current.

The mirror rule holds everywhere: warm currents flow from the
tropics toward the poles along **east** coasts of continents; cold
currents flow from high latitudes toward the equator along **west**
coasts (for example, the California Current off the west coast of
North America). The Southern Hemisphere gyres run counterclockwise
for the same reason the atmosphere's deflection flips: Coriolis
deflects left there.

Two cautions. First, the "loop" is a long-term average — real
currents meander, shed eddies, and shift with the seasons. Second,
the gyre's center is a calm, converging region: floating material
accumulates there, which is why ocean plastic concentrates in gyre
centers rather than spreading evenly. Circulation explains the
pattern; it does not excuse the pollution.

**Worked example 1 — Predict a current's temperature character.**
Question: a current flows north along the east coast of South
America. Warm or cold? Answer: east coasts carry water from the
tropics poleward, so it is warm — the Brazil Current. The same
reasoning, not a memorized list, answers the question for any coast:
find the gyre's rotation, find the leg, and the direction of travel
tells you where the water came from.

**Worked example 2 — Why western Europe is mild.** London sits near
51° N, roughly the latitude of Calgary, Canada. London's winters are
far milder. Two mechanisms from this unit explain most of the gap:
the Gulf Stream / North Atlantic Current delivers warm water to
Europe's doorstep, and the prevailing westerlies then carry that
ocean-moderated air over the land. Remove either leg — the current or
the wind — and the explanation collapses. (Ocean heat transport is
part of the story, not the whole story; atmospheric transport matters
too, and a good answer says so.)

## 2. Deep circulation: cold and salty sinks

Surface currents are wind-driven and fast (years to cross a basin).
Deep circulation is density-driven and slow (centuries to circle the
globe). Its driver is simple: cold water is denser than warm water,
and salty water is denser than fresh water. Where surface water
becomes both cold and salty, it sinks.

The main sinking region is the North Atlantic. Warm, salty Gulf
Stream water travels north, gives up heat to the cold polar air, and
becomes cold while staying salty. Cold plus salty means dense, so it
sinks to the deep ocean and begins a slow journey southward, around
through the Southern Ocean, and into the Pacific and Indian basins,
where it eventually upwells. This loop is **thermohaline circulation**
("thermo" = temperature, "haline" = salt). The name is sometimes
shortened to the "global conveyor," but treat that as a cartoon, not
a timetable: the real flow has many branches, mixing zones, and
timescales.

Why it matters for climate: the sinking leg is a one-way valve for
heat and carbon. Surface water carries heat and dissolved gases
downward and stores them in the deep ocean for centuries. If the
sinking slowed — for example, if fresher meltwater diluted the North
Atlantic surface water and made it less dense — less heat would be
drawn down, and regional climates (especially around the North
Atlantic) would shift. That is a feedback claim of the HS-ESS2-2
kind: one change (surface salinity) creates changes in other systems
(heat transport, regional temperature). The unit does not claim a
shutdown is imminent; it claims the mechanism is real and the
consequence would be large, which is why scientists watch it.

## 3. Water's heat capacity: the flywheel

**Heat capacity** is how much energy it takes to raise a substance's
temperature: water needs about 4.18 joules per gram per degree
Celsius (J/g·°C), one of the highest of any common substance. Dry
sand needs only about 0.84 J/g·°C. Water therefore warms slowly in
summer and cools slowly in winter — it stores the season's energy
and releases it gradually.

**Worked example 3 — Water vs. sand.** Add 4,180 J of energy to
1 kg (1,000 g) of water and to 1 kg of dry sand.

- Water: temperature change = energy ÷ (mass × specific heat) =
  4,180 ÷ (1,000 × 4.18) = 4,180 ÷ 4,180 = 1.0 °C.
- Sand: 4,180 ÷ (1,000 × 0.84) = 4,180 ÷ 840 ≈ 4.98 °C, about 5.0 °C.

Same energy, five times the temperature swing in sand. Scale that
up: a coastal city sits next to a giant water reservoir that
resists temperature change, while an inland desert city sits on
dry ground that swings wildly. That contrast — **continentality** —
is why San Francisco's seasons are muted and why a continental
interior at the same latitude can swing from −20 °C winters to
35 °C summers. Oceans do not just move heat; they *store* it, which
makes them the climate system's flywheel.

A related number: evaporating water also stores huge energy
(latent heat), which is released when water vapor condenses into
clouds. That is why a tropical storm over warm water can intensify:
warm sea-surface temperature is fuel, not background.

## Guided practice

1. Trace the North Pacific gyre: name its four legs and the wind
   belt driving each.
2. A current flows south along the west coast of South America.
   Warm or cold? Justify with the gyre rule, and name the current
   if you can (the Peru/Humboldt Current).
3. Two identical 2 kg samples, one water and one sand, each absorb
   8,360 J. Compute each temperature change. (Answer in the key;
   show the division.)
4. Explain why adding fresh meltwater to the North Atlantic surface
   could slow deep-water sinking, in a chain of at least three
   cause-and-effect links.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The Gulf Stream is warm
   because it flows near the equator." Correct the error: the Gulf
   Stream *carries* tropical water poleward — its warmth comes from
   where the water originated, and the current's job is transport.

## Application

Pick any coastal city you know. Using the gyre rule and heat
capacity, predict whether its climate is moderated by a warm or a
cold current (or neither), and what that means for its seasonal
temperature range. Write the prediction as a falsifiable sentence:
"If the current offshore is ___, then the city's seasonal range
should be ___ compared with an inland city at the same latitude."
Then check it against a climate-data source from the resource pack
and report whether the prediction held.

## Exit check

In three sentences: (1) what drives surface gyres, (2) what drives
deep circulation, (3) why water's heat capacity makes coastal
climates milder. If sentence 2 mentions wind instead of density,
re-read section 2.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** trace the gyre on a globe with a finger while
  reading section 1 aloud; say each wind belt as you cross it.
- **Alternate response:** draw the gyre as a labeled loop diagram
  instead of writing the trace in sentences.
- **Extension:** investigate upwelling zones (for example, off Peru
  or California): why does deep, cold, nutrient-rich water rise
  there, and why are fisheries productive nearby? Connect the answer
  to wind direction and the Coriolis deflection of surface water
  (Ekman transport, qualitative only).
