# Lesson 03 — Air Masses, Fronts, and the Mid-Latitude Cyclone

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can identify air masses and
fronts, order the stages of a mid-latitude cyclone, and predict the
sequence of weather changes as a cold front passes a station.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook; the front diagram in
`../assets/three-cell-circulation.svg` is not used here — fronts are
drawn by hand in this lesson, which is the point.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Warm air rises and holds more water
vapor than cold air. A low-pressure center in the Northern
Hemisphere spins counterclockwise. If both are firm, proceed.

## 1. Air masses: big bodies of air with one personality

An **air mass** is a large body of air — often 1,000 km or more
across — that has roughly uniform temperature and humidity because
it sat for days over one kind of surface (a "source region"). Air
masses are named by that surface:

- **cP (continental polar):** cold, dry — formed over cold land.
- **mP (maritime polar):** cool, moist — formed over cold ocean.
- **cT (continental tropical):** hot, dry — formed over hot land.
- **mT (maritime tropical):** warm, moist — formed over warm ocean.
- **cA / A (continental arctic):** bitterly cold, dry — formed over
  Arctic ice and snow.

The letters are a code, not decoration: the first letter says
*where* (continental = land, maritime = ocean), the second says
*how warm* (polar, tropical, arctic). When an air mass moves, it
carries its personality with it and modifies the weather of every
place it visits. Most North American weather arguments are really
arguments between cP air from Canada and mT air from the Gulf of
Mexico.

**Worked example 1 — Read the code.** A forecast mentions "mT air
advancing from the Gulf." Decode it: maritime = it picked up
moisture over warm ocean water; tropical = warm. Expect warm, humid
air — and, if it is forced to rise (over a front, a mountain, or by
surface heating), clouds and possibly thunderstorms, because warm
moist air that rises cools to its dew point and condenses. If the
code had been cP instead, the same sentence would predict cold, dry,
clear air. Two letters change the whole forecast.

## 2. Fronts: where air masses meet

Where two air masses meet, they do not mix politely. The boundary is
a **front**, a sloping zone of transition. Four kinds matter:

- **Cold front:** cold air advances and wedges *under* warm air,
  lifting it steeply and abruptly. Expect a narrow band of heavy
  showers or thunderstorms, a sharp temperature drop, and a wind
  shift as it passes. The front moves relatively fast.
- **Warm front:** warm air advances and slides *gently up over*
  retreating cold air. The slope is shallow, so clouds and steady
  precipitation spread far ahead of the surface boundary. Expect
  hours of gradual cloud thickening, then steady rain, then warming.
- **Stationary front:** neither air mass advances; the boundary
  stalls and can sit for days with prolonged cloud and rain along it.
- **Occluded front:** the cyclone matures and the faster cold front
  catches the warm front, lifting the warm air entirely off the
  ground. The warm sector is pinched out at the surface.

On a weather map, cold fronts are drawn blue with triangles pointing
in the direction of motion; warm fronts red with semicircles pointing
in the direction of motion; occluded fronts purple with alternating
triangles and semicircles; stationary fronts alternating red and
blue. The symbols point where the front is *going* — a detail worth
checking every time you read a map.

Why the shapes differ: cold air is dense, so it stays low and pushes
like a wedge; warm air is buoyant, so it rides up a gentle ramp.
Steep wedge → abrupt, violent lifting → narrow, intense weather.
Gentle ramp → gradual lifting over a wide area → broad, steady
weather. One mechanism, two very different forecasts.

## 3. The mid-latitude cyclone: a life story in four stages

Mid-latitude cyclones are born where cold polar air and warm
tropical air meet along the polar front, usually under the polar jet
stream. Their life runs in four stages:

1. **Stationary front stage:** a stationary front separates cold air
   to the north from warm air to the south. Nothing dramatic yet.
2. **Wave stage:** a disturbance bends the front into a wave. Warm
   air pushes north on the east side (a warm front forms); cold air
   pushes south on the west side (a cold front forms). A low-pressure
   center appears at the bend, spinning counterclockwise (Northern
   Hemisphere).
3. **Mature stage:** the cyclone is fully formed with a distinct
   warm sector between the warm front (east) and cold front (west).
   The warm sector is where severe weather is most likely, because
   warm, moist mT air sits there, ready to be lifted.
4. **Occluded stage:** the faster cold front catches the warm front,
   the warm sector is lifted off the ground, and the cyclone weakens
   and dissipates. Energy is spent; the system decays.

**Worked example 2 — Predict a front passage.** A station sits in the
warm sector ahead of an approaching cold front. Predict the sequence:

1. *Before:* warm, humid, southerly winds; clouds building, pressure
   falling.
2. *Passage:* wind shifts abruptly (southwesterly to northwesterly
   in the Northern Hemisphere), temperature drops sharply, a burst
   of showers or thunderstorms, pressure reaches its minimum then
   starts rising.
3. *After:* cooler, drier air; clearing skies; gusty northwesterly
   winds; pressure rising steadily.

Check the logic at each step: the temperature drop happens because
the air mass itself changed (cP replacing mT), not because the Sun
went down. If a prediction says "temperature falls because it
started raining," the mechanism is wrong — rain is a symptom of the
lifting, not the cause of the air-mass change.

**Worked example 3 — Warm-front passage, for contrast.** A station
ahead of an approaching warm front sees: high cirrus clouds thickening
over a day, then lower stratus, then steady light-to-moderate rain,
then a temperature rise and wind shift as the warm air arrives. The
change is gradual because the warm air climbs a shallow ramp. A
learner who predicts a sudden thunderstorm for a warm front has
mixed up the two mechanisms — go back to section 2 and compare the
slopes.

## Guided practice

1. Decode "cT" and predict the weather it brings in summer.
2. A front's blue triangles point east. What kind of front is it,
   and which way is it moving?
3. A station reports: temperature 24 °C falling to 14 °C in one
   hour, wind shifting SW to NW, pressure rising after the shift,
   showers then clearing. Which front passed, and which cyclone
   stage was the system in?
4. Why is the warm sector the most likely place for severe
   thunderstorms? Name the air mass and the lifting mechanism.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate draws a Northern Hemisphere
   cyclone spinning clockwise with the warm front on the west side.
   Identify both errors and fix them (counterclockwise spin; warm
   front on the east side, ahead of the low's eastward motion).

## Application

Write a 24-hour forecast narrative for a fictional town in the warm
sector of a maturing cyclone, hour by hour in four blocks (morning,
afternoon, evening, overnight), as the cold front passes in the
evening. Each block must state temperature trend, wind, cloud, and
precipitation, with a mechanism for each change. Swap narratives
with a partner (or the adult) and check: does the temperature drop
*at* the front, not before it?

## Exit check

Sketch a mid-latitude cyclone from memory: low center, cold front
with triangles, warm front with semicircles, warm sector labeled.
If any symbol is on the wrong side, re-read section 3 before Lesson 04.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** act out the cyclone stages with two colored
  cloths (cold = blue, warm = red) on a table, moving the cold edge
  faster until it overtakes the warm edge.
- **Alternate response:** narrate the front-passage sequence orally
  instead of writing it, using the station data as prompts.
- **Extension:** research why tropical cyclones (hurricanes) are a
  different machine: warm-core, no fronts, fueled by warm ocean
  water, and why they weaken rapidly over land or cold water.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: fronts as "walls" (they are sloping transition
zones, kilometres wide in reality); warm fronts bringing cold weather
(the name says which air mass is *advancing*); cyclones as only
destructive (they also deliver most mid-latitude rainfall). Answer-key
reference: `../answer-key.md` § Set C.
