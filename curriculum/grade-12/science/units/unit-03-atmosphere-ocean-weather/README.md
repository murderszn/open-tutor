# Unit 03 — Atmosphere, Ocean Circulation, and Weather

**Grade 12 · Science · Earth and Space Science pathway · Weeks 11–14 of the course year**
(draft, delivered for issue #55; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Weather is not random, and climate is not just "average weather"
hand-waved. Both come from one engine: sunlight heats Earth unevenly,
the atmosphere and ocean move that heat around, and the spin of the
planet bends the moving fluids into the circulation patterns we live
under. This unit follows that engine in four moves and ends with the
learner's own two-week weather investigation:

1. **Unequal heating and the atmosphere** — solar angle and albedo,
   pressure differences, wind, the Coriolis effect, and the three-cell
   circulation model (Hadley, Ferrel, polar) that explains the trade
   winds, westerlies, and polar easterlies (HS-ESS2-4).
2. **Ocean circulation** — wind-driven surface currents and gyres,
   thermohaline (density-driven) deep circulation, and why water's
   high heat capacity makes oceans the climate system's flywheel
   (HS-ESS2-4).
3. **Weather systems** — air masses, fronts, and the mid-latitude
   cyclone: the machine that delivers most of the day-to-day weather
   change in the mid-latitudes.
4. **Reading weather maps and regional climates** — isobars, fronts,
   and station data on a real surface-analysis chart; why coasts,
   mountains, and ocean currents give places their climates.
5. **Forecasting, models, and uncertainty** — how numerical forecasts
   are made, what "40% chance of rain" actually means, and how to
   judge a forecast honestly instead of calling it "wrong" when one
   outcome differs.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **if the
same Sun, the same spin, and the same oceans drive weather everywhere,
why does weather change so fast where you live — and how far ahead can
anyone honestly predict it?**

The unit's investigation is *"Testing a Front-Passage Prediction"*
(see [project.md](project.md)): the learner records two weeks of local
weather data (temperature, pressure trend, wind direction, cloud,
precipitation) from a free public source or home instruments, states a
circulation-based prediction in advance, and tests it against what
actually happened — including an honest account of where the
prediction failed.

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–02 and the Weeks 1–2 diagnostic:

- Energy transfer (radiation, conduction, convection) and the idea of
  a system with inputs, storage, and outputs.
- Density as mass per volume, and how temperature and dissolved salt
  change water's density.
- Reading graphs, computing a mean, and stating a claim with its
  uncertainty — not just its value.
- From earlier grades (conceptual level): the water cycle, seasons
  from Earth's tilt, and that wind is moving air. This unit makes
  those quantitative and mechanistic; it re-teaches none of them
  from scratch.

No calculus is assumed. Every calculation is worked step by step.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Explain, with a diagram or model, how unequal solar heating plus
   Earth's rotation produces pressure differences, the Coriolis
   deflection, and the three-cell circulation pattern — and predict
   the prevailing surface wind direction in a named latitude band.
   (HS-ESS2-4)
2. Use the Sun-angle and heat-capacity calculations to explain why
   the tropics are warm, why deserts sit near 30° latitude, and why
   coastal climates are milder than continental climates at the same
   latitude. (HS-ESS2-4)
3. Trace a surface-current gyre from its driving winds and Coriolis
   deflection, and explain in qualitative terms how cold, salty water
   sinking in the North Atlantic drives slow deep circulation.
   (HS-ESS2-4)
4. Identify air masses, fronts, and the stages of a mid-latitude
   cyclone on a described weather map, and predict the sequence of
   temperature, wind, and precipitation changes as a cold front
   passes a station. (HS-ESS2-2 strand: one change creating feedbacks
   in other systems)
5. Read a surface-analysis chart: state what isobars, fronts, and
   high/low centers mean, and infer wind strength and likely weather
   from isobar spacing and front positions.
6. Plan and carry out the two-week weather investigation: a
   prediction stated before the data, a data table with units, a
   graph of pressure and temperature, an error discussion, and a
   revised claim — with the adult approving the plan before data
   collection begins. (track objective 12)

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS),
high school (9–12) band, performance expectations for Earth and
Space Science. Codes and descriptions were verified on 2026-10-28
against the California Department of Education NGSS pages and the
NSTA standard listings (both restating the Achieve performance
expectations); descriptions below are short paraphrases for mapping,
not reproductions of the standards text. **This crosswalk is a
planning reference, not a claim of state adoption, accreditation, or
certification of alignment.**

- **HS-ESS2-4** — use a model to describe how variations in the flow
  of energy into and out of Earth's systems result in changes in
  climate (Lessons 01–02, 04–05; project; assessment). Clarification
  note: over 1–10 years, ocean circulation is one of the named
  causes of climate change; over 10–100s of years, ocean circulation
  and solar output join human activity.
- **HS-ESS2-2** — analyze geoscience data to make the claim that one
  change to Earth's surface can create feedbacks that cause changes
  to other Earth systems (Lessons 02–03, 05; assessment). Used here
  for ocean–atmosphere feedbacks (for example, warm sea-surface
  temperatures feeding storm energy) and ice–albedo feedback.
- Crosscutting concepts named explicitly: **systems and system
  models** (the three-cell model, Lessons 01–02); **cause and effect:
  mechanism and explanation** (every wind and current prediction must
  name its mechanism, all lessons); **stability and change**
  (forecast uncertainty and feedback, Lesson 05).

All practice datasets in this unit are labeled fictional. Real data
tasks use public sources named in the resource pack (for example, the
National Weather Service surface-analysis charts); the learner
records the date accessed.

## Safety

All outdoor observation is adult-supervised: the adult checks the
forecast first, and observation stops for lightning, flooding, or
severe-weather warnings. No chasing storms, no sheltering under trees
in lightning, no wading in floodwater, no climbing on roofs to read
instruments. The public-data alternative in `project.md` is the
default whenever the site, weather, or instruments are inadequate.
See scope-and-sequence §6 and `project.md` § Safety for the full rules.
