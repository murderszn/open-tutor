# Lesson 03 — Dating Assumptions and Earth's Age

**Unit 01 · Session S8 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** HS-ESS1-6 (Earth's formation and early history from ancient materials)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can name the three assumptions behind
every radiometric age, predict which direction the age shifts when each
assumption fails, and explain how zircons, meteorites, and concordant
clocks converge on Earth's ~4.54-billion-year age.

## Explicit teaching

**Every radiometric age rests on three assumptions.** A clock equation
is a promise; these are the fine print:

1. **Closed system.** No parent or daughter atoms entered or left the
   mineral since it formed. If the rock was heated later, daughter
   atoms can leak out (argon, for example, is a gas and escapes
   easily). *Failure direction:* losing daughter makes the parent
   fraction look too high → the computed age is **too young**. Gaining
   daughter (contamination) pushes the age **too old**.
2. **Known initial daughter.** The equation assumes you know how much
   daughter was present at formation (Lesson 02 assumed zero). If some
   daughter was already there, N/N₀ is underestimated → the age reads
   **too old**.
3. **Known, constant decay rate.** The half-life must be measured
   correctly and must not have changed. Laboratory measurements pin
   down half-lives precisely, and the physics of nuclear decay does not
   care about pressure, temperature, or chemistry in any way that
   matters at geologic scales — but "measured, not assumed" is why the
   half-life table in Lesson 02 lists real reference values.

**How geologists catch broken assumptions: concordance.** Date the same
rock with two or three *different* isotope systems. Each system has
different chemistry, different half-lives, and different ways of
failing — so if U-Pb says 4.55 Ga and Rb-Sr says 4.56 Ga, the odds that
both broke in exactly the compensating way are tiny. **Concordant
ages** (independent clocks agreeing) are the gold standard: the
assumptions are probably holding.

**The isochron trick (conceptual).** To test assumption 2, geologists
measure several minerals from the *same* rock and plot parent-vs.-
daughter ratios. If the rock stayed closed, the points fall on a
straight line; the line's slope gives the age, and where the line meets
the axis reveals the initial daughter amount — assumption 2 *measured*,
not assumed. You will not compute an isochron in this unit; you need
only the idea: the method checks its own homework.

**How we know Earth's age.** Earth has no surviving rock from its own
formation — the oldest crust was recycled long ago. So the age comes
from three converging lines of evidence (all real reference values):

- **Oldest Earth material:** zircon crystals from Jack Hills,
  Australia, dated to ~**4.4 Ga** by U-Pb. Zircons are tough — they
  survive melting that destroys their host rock.
- **Oldest solar-system material:** primitive meteorites dated to
  ~**4.56 Ga**. Meteorites are leftover building blocks of the
  planets, never melted into a planet — so they kept the original
  clock. Earth formed with them, so Earth cannot be older than they
  are, and the meteorites set the ceiling.
- **Concordance:** many meteorites and ancient rocks dated by
  multiple isotope systems agree with each other.

The accepted value, **~4.54 Ga**, sits between the oldest surviving
Earth crystal and the oldest meteorites, supported by concordant clocks
on both. It is not one measurement — it is an agreement.

**A note on what cannot date Earth.** Oceanic crust nowhere exceeds
~**200 Ma** (real reference value) — the seafloor is young because it
is constantly recycled (Lesson 04). You cannot date a 4.54-Ga planet
with 0.2-Ga seafloor, and C-14 (t½ = 5,730 years) is useless for
anything older than tens of thousands of years. The clock must fit the
interval — Lesson 02's rule, now with a real consequence.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Concordance check with uncertainties.**

A meteorite is dated by two systems (fictional lab report, realistic
values):

- U-Pb: 4.55 Ga ± 0.02 Ga
- Rb-Sr: 4.56 Ga ± 0.03 Ga

Do the clocks agree?

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — the difference: 4.56 − 4.55 = 0.01 Ga = **10 Ma**.

Step 2 — compare with the uncertainties. The U-Pb value could truly be
anywhere in 4.53–4.57 Ga; the Rb-Sr value anywhere in 4.53–4.59 Ga.
The ranges overlap heavily, and the 10-Ma difference is smaller than
either uncertainty.

Step 3 — conclusion: the ages are **concordant**. Two independent
clocks, with different chemistry and different failure modes, agree
within measurement uncertainty. That is strong evidence the rock's
system stayed closed and the age is trustworthy — far stronger than
either number alone.

**Example 2 — Bracketing: relative and absolute evidence combined.**

A shale layer contains an index fossil known to have lived only between
180 and 160 Ma. An igneous intrusion cutting through the shale is
dated by K-Ar at 150 Ma. (Fictional practice scenario; numbers
realistic.)

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — the fossil brackets the layer: the shale was
deposited while the organism lived, so the shale's age is between 160
and 180 Ma.

Step 2 — the intrusion is younger than the shale (cross-cutting,
Lesson 01), and its absolute age is 150 Ma. So the shale is older than
150 Ma — consistent with the 160–180 Ma bracket (150 < 160 ✓).

Step 3 — the combined constraint: 160–180 Ma, with the intrusion
confirming the fossil bracket rather than tightening it. If the
intrusion had dated at 190 Ma, the story would break (an intrusion
cannot be older than the rock it cuts) — and that contradiction would
be the discovery, sending the geologist back to check assumptions.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult nearby; hints are in brackets.

1. A basalt is reheated by a later lava flow; much of its Ar-40
   escapes before a geologist dates it by K-Ar. Is the computed age
   too young, too old, or unaffected? Which assumption failed?
   [Which half of the parent/daughter pair was lost?]
2. A zircon gives 4.40 Ga by U-Pb and 4.39 Ga by a second uranium
   system. The uncertainties are ±0.02 Ga each. Are these concordant?
   Show the difference in Ma and compare. [Fictional practice
   numbers.]
3. A rock's measured parent fraction is 1/4. A second analysis shows
   the rock actually started with daughter equal to half the parent
   amount (not zero). Without computing exact ages, say whether the

   [Which way does extra initial daughter push N/N₀?]
4. Rank these by age, oldest first: 4.4-Ga zircons, 4.56-Ga
   meteorites, 200-Ma oceanic crust, Earth's accepted 4.54-Ga age.
   Then compute, in Ma: (a) meteorites minus zircons; (b) Earth's age
   minus zircons. [0.16 Ga = ? Ma.]

## Application

A field team reports (fictional practice data):

- Sandstone layer S contains index fossil F (lived 120–110 Ma).
- A volcanic ash bed *above* S dates to 105 Ma by K-Ar (±2 Ma).
- An intrusion cutting S dates to 90 Ma by U-Pb (±3 Ma).
- A second mineral in the intrusion gives 91 Ma by Rb-Sr (±4 Ma).

a) Bracket layer S's age as tightly as the evidence allows. Show each
   bound and which evidence sets it.
b) Are the two intrusion ages concordant? Show the arithmetic.
c) Suppose the ash bed's K-Ar age were 130 Ma instead. Which piece of
   evidence would contradict which, and what would you check first?

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "assumptions = guesses, so dates are
opinions" — counter with concordance: independent clocks agreeing is
not an opinion, it is the opposite of one; (2) confusing *precision*
(small ±) with *accuracy* (right answer) — Example 1 exists to separate
them; (3) thinking the 4.54-Ga value came from dating one rock —
stress the convergence story every time; (4) assuming "too young/too
old" directions are memorizable trivia — always re-derive from
"which half of the ratio changed." Answer-key reference:
`answer-key.md`, Lesson 03.
