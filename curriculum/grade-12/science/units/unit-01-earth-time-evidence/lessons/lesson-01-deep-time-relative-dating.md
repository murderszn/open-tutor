## Explicit teaching

**Deep time.** The Earth is about **4.54 billion years old** (real
reference value; Lesson 03 tells how we know). A billion years is not
"a long time" the way a summer feels long — it is a quantity that human
intuition cannot picture directly. The whole span is called **deep
time**, and geologists divide it into named chunks. The four largest
are the **eons**:

| Eon | Rough span (real reference values) | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Hadean | 4.54–4.0 billion years ago | Earth forms; early surface is hot and violent |
| Archean | 4.0–2.5 billion years ago | First life appears (simple, single-celled) |
| Proterozoic | 2.5 billion–541 million years ago | Oxygen builds up; complex cells evolve |
| Phanerozoic | 541 million years ago–today | Animals, plants, dinosaurs, humans — the "visible life" eon |

Notice where we live in that table: the Phanerozoic, the eon of visible
life, is the **shortest** one — 541 million out of 4,540 million years,
about 12%. Everything you have ever seen in a nature documentary fits
in the last eighth of the story.

**Relative vs. absolute age.** A *relative* age says "older than" or
"younger than" without a number: the bottom sandwich layer was placed
first. An *absolute* age says "how long ago" with a number: this rock
is 150 million years old. This lesson is about relative age — ordering
events. Lessons 02–03 turn those orderings into numbers.

**The six stratigraphic principles.** "Stratigraphy" is the study of
layered rocks (strata). Six rules let a geologist read a stack of layers
like a timeline:

1. **Superposition** — in an undisturbed stack, the layer on the bottom
   was deposited first, so it is the **oldest**; each higher layer is
   younger. (The sandwich rule.)
2. **Original horizontality** — sediments are deposited in nearly flat,
   horizontal layers. If layers are tilted or folded now, something
   moved them *after* they formed — so the tilting is younger than the
   layers it tilts.
3. **Lateral continuity** — a layer extends sideways until it thins out
   or meets a barrier. Two cliffs miles apart showing the same sequence
   of layers are probably the *same* layers, once continuous.
4. **Cross-cutting relationships** — anything that cuts across a rock
   body is **younger** than what it cuts. A crack filled with cooled
   magma (an igneous intrusion) is younger than every layer it slices
   through.
5. **Inclusions** — a fragment of rock trapped inside another rock is
   older than the rock holding it. (A pebble inside a concrete wall is
   older than the wall.)
6. **Unconformities** — a surface where rock was eroded away (or never
   deposited) before new layers formed on top. An unconformity is a
   **gap in the record**: time passed, but no rock recorded it. It is
   younger than the rocks below and older than the rocks above.

**Index fossils.** Some organisms were widespread (lived over huge
areas), short-lived (existed only briefly in geologic terms), and
abundant (left many fossils). Their fossils are **index fossils**:
find one in a layer, and you know the layer's age is the brief window
when that organism lived — even if the same layer is exposed hundreds
of miles away. Index fossils are how geologists *correlate* separated
outcrops (match layers of the same age across distances).

### Reading passage — "The Library of Layers"

*Read with the adult in Session S4 (reading session); keep for reference
now.*

Stand at the foot of a canyon and the walls are a library — but the
books are stacked with the oldest on the bottom and the newest on top,
and some volumes are missing entirely, their pages torn out by ancient
rivers. No one labeled the shelves. So geologists learned to read the
shelves themselves: flat layers were laid flat; a slanted layer was
tilted later; a dark band cutting straight through the stack is younger
than everything it crosses. A fossil shell found in one canyon wall and
then again in a wall a hundred miles away is a bookmark — if the
creature lived only briefly, both walls' matching layers were pages
turned at the same time. The canyon does not say "200 million years" in
words. But the order of its pages, and the bookmarks pressed between
them, are exactly the evidence that later lets a laboratory put numbers
on the story. Relative age is the table of contents; absolute age is the
page count.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Order the events in the column above.**

Events to order: deposition of A, B, C; the intrusion I; the
unconformity; deposition of D.

*Reasoning.* Start at the bottom. By **superposition**, A was deposited
first (oldest), then B, then C — each higher layer younger than the one
below. The intrusion I slices through A, B, and C, so by
**cross-cutting relationships** it is younger than all three — it had
to exist as layers before the magma could cut them. The intrusion stops
at the wavy surface: that surface eroded the tops of C and I, so the
**unconformity** is younger than the intrusion. Finally D sits on the
unconformity, so D is youngest of all — younger than the gap it covers.

Oldest → youngest: **A, B, C, intrusion I, unconformity, D.**

Note what the unconformity costs us: we cannot know how much of C and I
was erased, or how long the gap lasted. The record is silent there —
and saying so is part of the science.

**Example 2 — Fit deep time onto a one-meter strip.**

Question: compress all 4.54 billion years of Earth's history into a
strip exactly 1 meter long (today at the 1-m end, Earth's formation at
the 0 end). (a) Where does the oldest oceanic crust (~200 million
years) begin on the strip? (b) Where do the oldest Earth materials, the
4.4-billion-year-old Jack Hills zircons, sit?

*Reasoning.* First convert everything to the same unit: 4.54 billion
years = 4,540 million years. The strip maps 4,540 Myr → 1.000 m, so
1 Myr → 1.000 m / 4,540 = 0.0002203 m = 0.2203 mm.

(a) 200 Myr × 0.2203 mm/Myr = 44.06 mm ≈ **4.4 cm from the "today"
end.** Everything the ocean floor has ever recorded fits in the last
4.4 centimeters.

(b) The zircons formed 4,400 Myr ago, so they sit
(4,540 − 4,400) Myr = 140 Myr after formation:
140 × 0.2203 mm/Myr = 30.8 mm ≈ **3.1 cm from the formation end.**

Sanity check: 200 Ma is 200/4,540 = 4.4% of Earth's history — the strip
answer (4.4 cm of 100 cm) agrees. When two routes agree, the arithmetic
is probably right.

1. In the column diagram: is the intrusion older or younger than layer

3. A tilted stack of layers is cut by a fault (a crack where the rock

4. On the one-meter Earth-history strip: the dinosaurs went extinct
   66 million years ago. How many centimeters from the "today" end is

   Example 2.]

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "deeper is always older" applied to the
intrusion (it is deep but young — cross-cutting beats depth);
(2) treating the unconformity as an event *before* the rocks it
eroded; (3) confusing "the fossil is old" with "the fossil is an
index fossil" — stress the three criteria; (4) deep-time numbness:
learners nod at "4.54 billion" without feeling the scale, so the
one-meter strip arithmetic is the lesson's load-bearing moment — make
them do the multiplication themselves. Answer-key reference:
`answer-key.md`, Lesson 01.
