# Lesson 04 — Plate Tectonics from Crustal Evidence

**Unit 01 · Session S11 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** HS-ESS1-5 (evaluate plate-tectonics evidence to explain the ages of crustal rocks)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can evaluate three lines of evidence —
seafloor rock ages, magnetic striping, and earthquake/volcano belts —
and explain in writing how plate motion accounts for the ages of
crustal rocks, including why no oceanic crust is older than ~200 Ma.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, ruler, calculator. Lesson 02's unit-conversion habit
(km ↔ m ↔ cm; yr ↔ Ma) is used heavily today.

## Explicit teaching

**The seafloor is young — and it gets older away from the ridges.**
Dredged and drilled samples show a striking pattern: rock at a
mid-ocean ridge axis is essentially newborn (0 Ma), and the age
increases steadily with distance from the ridge on *both* sides. The
oldest oceanic crust anywhere is about **200 Ma** (real reference
value) — on a 4.54-Ga planet, the entire ocean floor is younger than
the last 4.4% of Earth's history. Something must be *removing* old
ocean floor as fast as new floor is made.

**Magnetic striping: the tape recorder.** Earth's magnetic field has
reversed many times (north becomes south and back). When lava erupts at
a ridge and cools, magnetic minerals freeze in the field's direction —
like a compass needle set in stone. The result, mapped by ships towing
magnetometers, is **magnetic striping**: bands of normal and reversed
polarity running parallel to the ridge, in a pattern that is
**mirror-symmetric** about the ridge axis. The Vine–Matthews–Morley
hypothesis (1963) explains it: new crust forms at the ridge and moves
away on both sides, recording each reversal as a pair of matching
stripes. One-sided flow cannot make a mirror image — the symmetry is
the fingerprint of spreading.

**Earthquake and volcano belts trace the boundaries.** Plot real
earthquake locations (USGS Earthquake Hazards Program) and volcano
locations (Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program) on a world map and
they do not scatter randomly — they outline narrow belts that match
the plate boundaries drawn from seafloor data. Three independent maps
(seafloor ages, magnetic stripes, quake/volcano belts) draw the same
lines. That convergence is the argument.

**How plate motion explains crustal rock ages.** Put the pieces
together:

- New oceanic crust forms at mid-ocean ridges (youngest rock at the
  axis) and spreads outward — so crustal age increases with distance
  from the ridge. Age = distance ÷ spreading rate.
- Old oceanic crust is destroyed where it sinks back into the mantle
  at **subduction zones**. Creation and destruction balance — that is
  why the oldest seafloor is only ~200 Ma.
- Continental crust is thicker and more buoyant; it resists
  subduction, so continents keep 4-billion-year-old rocks (like the
  Jack Hills zircons' host region) while ocean basins are
  perpetually repaved.

**Full rate vs. half rate.** A quoted spreading rate like "2.5 cm/yr
in the Atlantic" is the **full rate**: the two flanks separate at that
speed. Each flank moves away from the ridge at half of it — the
**half-spreading rate** (1.25 cm/yr for the Atlantic). Age from a
one-sided distance uses the half rate:

> age = (distance from ridge) ÷ (half-spreading rate)

**Older supporting lines.** Two pre-1960s clues pointed the same way
and still count as evidence: the **fit of the continents**
(South America and Africa slot together) and matching **fossil and
rock-type belts** across now-separated continents (organisms and rock
formations that stop at today's coastlines and resume across the
ocean). Plate tectonics explains all of them with one mechanism; no
fixed-continent model does.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Age from distance (Atlantic).**

A dredge sample comes from seafloor 1,250 km from the Mid-Atlantic
Ridge axis. The Atlantic's full spreading rate is 2.5 cm/yr. Estimate
the seafloor's age.

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — use the half rate: each flank moves at
2.5 / 2 = 1.25 cm/yr. Convert to km per million years:
1.25 cm/yr × 1,000,000 yr/Ma = 1,250,000 cm/Ma = 12,500 m/Ma =
**12.5 km/Ma**.

Step 2 — divide distance by rate:
t = 1,250 km ÷ 12.5 km/Ma = **100 Ma**.

Step 3 — cross-check with SI units: 1.25 cm/yr = 0.0125 m/yr;
1,250 km = 1,250,000 m; t = 1,250,000 / 0.0125 = 100,000,000 yr =
100 Ma. Same answer — the arithmetic is consistent.

**Example 2 — Fast ridge, young flank (East Pacific Rise).**

The East Pacific Rise spreads at a full rate of about 10 cm/yr
(faster than the Atlantic; real reference range 2–10 cm/yr). A sample
sits 1,000 km from the ridge axis. Estimate its age, and check it
against the 200-Ma oceanic maximum.

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — half rate: 10 / 2 = 5 cm/yr = 50,000 m/Ma =
**50 km/Ma**. (Check: 5 cm/yr × 10⁶ yr = 5×10⁶ cm = 50,000 m = 50 km
per Ma. ✓)

Step 2 — age: t = 1,000 km ÷ 50 km/Ma = **20 Ma**.

Step 3 — sanity check: 20 Ma < 200 Ma ✓. And reversing the question —
how far could the fastest flank get in 200 Ma? 50 km/Ma × 200 Ma =
10,000 km, far wider than any real ocean basin flank — which tells us
real basins are limited by subduction destroying old crust, not by how
far a flank could theoretically travel. The model and the 200-Ma
observation agree that recycling is happening.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) using the full rate with a one-sided
distance — the single most common arithmetic error; always ask "whose
distance is this — one flank or both?"; (2) reading "youngest rock at
the ridge" as "the ridge is young"; (3) treating magnetic stripes as
painted-on features rather than frozen-in mineral alignments formed at
the ridge; (4) "subduction destroys rock" misread as "destroys the
atoms" — clarify: the *crust* is recycled into the mantle; the matter
is conserved (bridge to Lesson 05's convection). Answer-key
reference: `answer-key.md`, Lesson 04.
