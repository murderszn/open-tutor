# Answer Key — Unit 04: Climate Evidence, Feedbacks, and Uncertainty

**Adult only.** Worked solutions for every lesson practice item,
practice set, quiz question, and assessment task. Open responses
give the acceptable reasoning and its limits; accept any response
that makes the same point with correct numbers.

## Lesson 01 guided practice

1. Reflected: 340 × 0.30 = 102 W/m². Absorbed: 340 − 102 =
   238 W/m².
2. T⁴ = 200 ÷ (5.67 × 10⁻⁸) ≈ 3.53 × 10⁹; T ≈ 244 K =
   244 − 273 = −29 °C. (243–244 K is accepted.)
3. The surface emits 390 W/m², but greenhouse gases absorb much
   of it and re-emit part downward; only 238 W/m² finally
   escapes at the top of the atmosphere, balancing the 238
   absorbed. The two numbers belong to different levels.

## Lesson 01 independent practice

4. Absorbed = 340 × (1 − 0.25) = 340 × 0.75 = 255 W/m²; a rise
   of 255 − 238 = 17 W/m².
5. Same calculation as Earth with no greenhouse effect:
   T ≈ 255 K (−18 °C), far below Earth's observed 288 K
   (15 °C). Instructive because the Moon sits at Earth's
   distance with the same sunlight; not exact because the Moon
   rotates slowly, redistributes heat poorly, and has enormous
   day–night swings that a single mean hides.
6. Accept any ≤ 4-sentence correction making both points:
   CO₂ absorbs in an infrared window water vapor leaves open
   (near 15 micrometres), and it persists for centuries, so its
   level is set by cumulative emissions — abundance alone does
   not measure importance.
7. (Applied task) Accept a diagram with incoming 340, reflected
   102, absorbed 238, outgoing infrared 238, surface emission
   ~390 with a downward return arrow. The arrow a greenhouse
   rise changes first is the outgoing infrared escape: more
   absorption means less escapes at first, so the surface
   warms until balance is restored.
8. (Exit check) 340 in, 102 reflected, 238 absorbed; no-greenhouse
   255 K (−18 °C); observed 288 K (15 °C); the 33 °C gap is the
   greenhouse effect.

## Lesson 02 guided practice

1. Warming → warmer air holds more water vapor (about 7% per
   °C) → more infrared absorbed → more warming. Reinforcing.
2. ΔT = 1.2 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 1.2 ÷ 0.5 = 2.4 °C.
3. Correction: low, bright clouds cool by reflecting sunlight,
   but high, thin clouds warm by trapping infrared; the sign
   depends on cloud type and height, so "clouds always cool"
   is false.

## Lesson 02 independent practice

4. The totals differ by a factor of three (1.07 vs. 3.2 °C)
   from the same forcing, purely through f. Pinning down the
   feedback fraction matters more than refining the direct
   response, because the total is far more sensitive to f —
   especially as f approaches 1.
5. Warming → permafrost thaws → methane released → more
   greenhouse absorption → more warming. Reinforcing. Limit:
   the finite permafrost carbon stock, and/or the balancing
   Planck response as temperature rises.
6. Error: melting ice *lowers* albedo (bright ice replaced by
   dark water), it does not raise it. Corrected loop: warming
   → less ice → lower albedo → more absorption → more
   warming. Reinforcing.
7. (Applied task) Accept any four-arrow loop that returns to
   its starting quantity, with a justified note per arrow and
   a named limit (finite ice, Planck response, saturation).
8. (Exit check) Reinforcing: ice–albedo, water vapor.
   Balancing: Planck emission response (T⁴). No runaway on
   Earth because the balancing Planck response grows steeply
   with temperature and real feedbacks saturate (finite ice,
   finite stocks).

## Lesson 03 guided practice

1. 300 × 2.12 = 636 GtC. 500 ÷ 2.12 ≈ 236 ppm.
2. 1,800 ÷ 60 = 30 years.
3. Airborne: 2.0 × 2.12 = 4.24 GtC/yr. Fraction: 4.24 ÷ 10 =
   0.424 ≈ 42%.

## Lesson 03 independent practice

4. Staying airborne: 11 × 0.46 = 5.06 GtC/yr. In ppm/yr:
   5.06 ÷ 2.12 ≈ 2.39 ppm/yr. Over 10 years: ≈ 24 ppm.
5. The 90 GtC/yr is a two-way exchange (about 90 in, about 90
   out, roughly balanced); the net ocean sink is only about
   2.9 GtC/yr. A gross flux cannot absorb a net addition
   unless the outflow stays fixed while the inflow grows.
6. Errors: (a) 11 GtC/yr is the human *emission* flux, not
   the flux out of the atmosphere — the denominator for
   turnover should be a removal flux (about 120 GtC/yr for
   photosynthesis, giving 7.5 years). (b) Turnover time (how
   long a molecule spends in the air) is not adjustment time
   (how long an added pulse takes to be removed, centuries to
   millennia, because sinks return much of what they take).
7. (Applied task) Accept any five lines that are quantitative
   (using 120 GtC/yr fluxes, the ~900 GtC stock, the 46%
   airborne fraction, or the ~11 GtC/yr emissions) and end in
   a genuine question (scale, duration, additionality,
   saturation). Score the questions, not the verdict.
8. (Exit check) 1 ppm ≈ 2.12 GtC; atmosphere ≈ 900 GtC;
   airborne fraction ≈ 46%. A small one-way flux raises the
   stock every year because the natural two-way fluxes were
   balanced and the human flux has no matching outflow.

## Lesson 04 guided practice

1. Ice cores measure past CO₂ directly from trapped air bubbles
   (and past temperature from ice isotopes). Alone they cannot
   show the present-day cause, and they show CO₂ and
   temperature moving together without by themselves settling
   which led in each transition.
2. Check ocean heat content (a volcanic cause would cool the
   surface briefly, not add sustained deep-ocean heat) and the
   stratospheric fingerprint (volcanic aerosols cool the
   surface for a year or two, then fade; sustained warming
   needs a sustained cause). The claim would need sustained
   undersea heat input matching the observed ocean warming and
   a mechanism that survives the fingerprint checks.
3. Mean of all ten: 154.6 ÷ 10 = 15.46 °C. A single starting
   year at 15.0 °C does not weaken the trend claim: trends are
   judged from multi-year means and the scatter around the
   line, not from any one year's value.

## Lesson 04 independent practice

4. 3 mm/yr × 100 = 300 mm = 30 cm per century at a constant
   rate. "If the rate stayed constant" matters because the
   observed rate is accelerating (thermal expansion plus
   growing ice loss), so a constant-rate projection understates
   the century total.
5. More than 90% of the trapped heat goes into the ocean, so
   ocean heat content measures the budget's storage term
   directly; surface temperature is a small, noisy remainder
   that can pause for years while the total keeps rising.
6. Error: comparing one September to the previous September
   mistakes weather noise for trend. Correct comparison:
   multi-year means for the same month over the whole Arctic
   (the satellite record's decadal decline).
7. (Applied task) Score the structure: claim stated, testing
   evidence line named, result the claim needs stated, result
   actually observed stated (or honestly marked "check in the
   resource pack"). Do not score the side chosen.
8. (Exit check) Any four of: instrumental record (temperature),
   ice cores (past CO₂/temperature), ocean heat content
   (stored heat), sea level (warming + ice loss integrated),
   ice loss/snow (cryosphere response), living world
   (ecological response) — each with one thing it measures
   and one limit, per the lesson.

## Lesson 05 guided practice

1. 1,000 ÷ 100 = 10 cells per side, so 10 × 10 = 100 cells. A
   10 km thunderstorm is far smaller than one 100 km cell, so
   the model cannot resolve it; it parameterizes its average
   effect.
2. Model uncertainty: same scenario, different models — the
   gap reflects differing model physics (especially cloud
   feedback), not different emissions paths.
3. Accept any two-sentence correction making the point: 5.7 °C
   is the top of the very-high-emissions scenario (SSP5-8.5)
   range for 2081–2100, not a prediction; the range depends
   on the emissions path chosen.

## Lesson 05 independent practice

4. Ensemble mean 2.8 °C; range 2.3–3.3 °C. The range mostly
   reflects internal variability plus model differences within
   the one fixed scenario — not scenario uncertainty.
5. Ten-year forecasts: internal variability dominates, because
   the system's own chaos swamps the slow forced trend over
   short windows. Century forecasts: scenario uncertainty
   dominates, because the emissions path chosen over a
   century moves the endpoint far more than any model detail.
6. Error: averaging across scenarios mixes human-choice
   uncertainty with physics uncertainty. Correct reading: the
   spread 1.0–5.7 °C spans different emissions futures; within
   any one future the model range is much narrower.
7. (Applied task) Score by the teacher-guide rubric: every
   sentence checkable; scenario named; impact tied to the
   warming level; uncertainty named by kind.
8. (Exit check) Scenario, model, internal-variability
   uncertainty. Internal variability dominates at 10 years;
   scenario dominates at 100 years. A scenario range is not
   a prediction because it is conditional on an emissions
   path that humans have not yet chosen.

   340.25 ≈ 340 W/m².

4. The surface emits 390, but greenhouse gases absorb much of
   it and re-emit part downward; only 238 escapes at the top,

5. 1,334 ÷ 4 ≈ 334 W/m² (333.5).
6. Absorbed: 800 × (1 − 0.15) = 680 W/m². Reflected: 800 ×

7. The blanket image is wrong because heat *does* leave — 238
   W/m² escapes at the top, balancing the 238 absorbed. The
   greenhouse effect slows the escape (absorption and
   re-emission, part downward), forcing the surface warmer

## Practice Set B

1. Warming → less ice → lower albedo → more absorption →
   more warming. Reinforcing.
2. Warming → more infrared emitted (σT⁴) → more energy leaves
   to space → less warming. Balancing (Planck response).
3. 1.5 ÷ (1 − 0.4) = 1.5 ÷ 0.6 = 2.5 °C.
4. 2.0 ÷ (1 − 0.6) = 2.0 ÷ 0.4 = 5.0 °C. As f approaches 1,
   the total grows without bound in the teaching model — which
   is why the model breaks down there and why real feedbacks
   must saturate below 1.
5. About 14% more (1.07² ≈ 1.14), compounding year on year.
6. Water vapor rains out within days, so its level is set by
   temperature; it cannot start a long-term warming on its own.
   It amplifies a warming started by a long-lived forcing such
   as CO₂, which is why it is classified as a feedback.
7. If the extra clouds are high and thin, they trap infrared
   and the loop flips to reinforcing. The sign depends on
   cloud type and height, not on cloud amount alone.
8. "Positive" means reinforcing (same direction), "negative"
   means balancing (opposite direction). Neither word is a
   value judgment about good or bad.

## Practice Set C

1. 280 × 2.12 = 593.6 ≈ 594 GtC. 426 × 2.12 = 903.1 ≈ 903 GtC.
   Difference ≈ 309 GtC.
2. Staying airborne: 11 × 0.46 = 5.06 ≈ 5.1 GtC/yr. In ppm/yr:
   5.06 ÷ 2.12 ≈ 2.4 ppm/yr.
3. 2,400 ÷ 80 = 30 years.
4. 900 ÷ 120 = 7.5 years. It means the average molecule's
   turnover time against photosynthesis; it does not mean an
   added pulse of CO₂ disappears in 7.5 years (adjustment time
   is centuries to millennia, because sinks return much of
   what they absorb).
5. Not durably, until three questions are answered: how long
   the planted carbon stays stored; whether the planting is
   additional (trees that would not have grown anyway); and
   whether the sink saturates or reverses (fire, harvest,
   decay). A one-year balance is not a permanent offset.
6. 0.60 × 11 = 6.6 GtC/yr; 6.6 ÷ 2.12 ≈ 3.1 ppm/yr.
7. 38,000 ÷ 90 ≈ 422 years. Rough because the ocean is not one
   well-mixed box: the surface mixes in years, the deep ocean
   in centuries to a millennium, so a single residence time
   averages very different real timescales.
8. The 90 GtC/yr is gross two-way exchange, roughly balanced;
   the net sink is only about 2.9 GtC/yr. A balanced exchange
   cannot absorb a net addition unless the balance itself
   shifts — and the observed airborne fraction shows it shifts
   only part-way.

## Practice Set D

1. Any four of the lesson's six lines, each with what it
   measures (see Lesson 04).
2. The fingerprint argues against a brighter Sun: extra
   sunshine would warm the whole atmosphere including the
   stratosphere, but the stratosphere is observed cooling
   while the troposphere warms — the greenhouse pattern.
3. 4 × 20 = 80 mm total. To call it acceleration you need an
   earlier 20-year rate to compare against: a rise alone,
   however large, is a rate, not an acceleration.
4. Independent groups using different methods, stations, and
   adjustments converge on the same warming (within about
   0.1 °C over the century). Convergence across independent
   methods rules out a single method's bias; one group
   publishing twice does not.
5. The comparison shows today's CO₂ is far outside the
   800,000-year natural range and rose far faster than any
   glacial–interglacial swing. It does not by itself prove the
   cause of the rise — that comes from the emissions budget,
   isotopes, and fingerprints (Lessons 03–04).
6. Correction: one town's one winter is weather (short-term,
   local); warming is a claim about 30-year global means. A
   cold local winter is expected somewhere on a warming planet
   and does not test the global claim.
7. No: the energy budget is not broken. The heat is in the
   ocean (more than 90% of trapped heat), which keeps warming
   while surface air temperature pauses for a few years;
   surface temperature is a noisy, small part of the budget.
8. Errors: (a) one station is not the globe; (b) five years is
   weather, not the 30-year climate average. Either alone

## Practice Set E

1. No: a 10 km storm is smaller than a 50 km cell. The model
   parameterizes its average effect with a formula tuned to
   observations instead of resolving it.
2. Scenario uncertainty mostly: the two ranges come from
   different emissions paths (SSP1-1.9 vs. SSP2-4.5).
3. Model uncertainty (plus internal variability) within one
   fixed scenario: different models' physics, especially cloud
   feedback, spread the result.
4. Internal variability: same model family and scenario, yearly
   scatter around the trend from the system's own chaos.
5. Short forecasts: internal variability dominates because
   chaos swamps the slow forced trend over a decade. Century
   forecasts: scenario uncertainty dominates because the
   emissions path chosen over a century moves the endpoint
   more than model details do.
6. Ask: (a) under which scenario? (b) which models and what
   averaging period (and what range, not just what mean)?
7. Scenario B's emissions assumption matched reality, so its
   projection matched; Scenario A's emissions assumption
   overshot, so its projection overshot. The comparison
   separates scenario error from physics error: the physics
   was right in both cases, the emissions path differed.
8. Correction: the ensemble mean is an average across runs,
   smoother than any real future, not a most-likely single
   outcome. No single run should be quoted as *the*
   prediction; the spread is part of the forecast.

## Unit assessment

1. Global-mean incoming: 1,361 ÷ 4 ≈ 340 W/m². Reflected:
   340 × 0.30 = 102 W/m². Absorbed: 340 − 102 = 238 W/m².
2. T⁴ = 238 ÷ σ ≈ 4.20 × 10⁹; T ≈ 255 K = −18 °C.
3. Surface emission (390) and top-of-atmosphere escape (238)
   are different levels: greenhouse gases absorb much of the
   upward infrared and re-emit part downward, so only 238
   finally escapes, balancing the 238 absorbed.
4. (a) Reinforcing: less ice lowers albedo, increasing
   absorption. (b) Balancing: warmer surface emits more
   infrared (T⁴), increasing energy loss to space.
   (c) Reinforcing: warmer air holds more water vapor, which
   absorbs more infrared.
5. 1.4 ÷ (1 − 0.5) = 2.8 °C. Limit: the model compresses many
   feedbacks into one constant fraction; real feedbacks vary
   with temperature, pattern, and timescale, and saturate.
6. 900 ÷ 120 = 7.5 years (turnover time). It is not the
   adjustment time: sinks return much of what they absorb and
   the deep ocean equilibrates slowly, so an added CO₂ pulse
   takes centuries to millennia to be removed.
7. Airborne: 2.4 × 2.12 = 5.088 ≈ 5.1 GtC/yr. Fraction:
   5.1 ÷ 11 ≈ 0.46 (46%).
8. (a) Ice cores. (b) Ocean heat content (Argo floats).
   (c) Sea level (tide gauges + satellite altimetry).
9. The stratosphere cools while the troposphere warms. A
   brighter Sun would warm both; greenhouse gases trap heat
   low down and let the high atmosphere radiate away.
10. SSP5-8.5 (very high emissions). It is not a single
    prediction because it is conditional on that emissions
    path, and the range within it reflects model differences
    and internal variability.
11. (a) First two mean: (7.0 + 6.6) ÷ 2 = 6.8 million km².
    Last two mean: (6.2 + 6.0) ÷ 2 = 6.1 million km².
    (b) Change: 6.1 − 6.8 = −0.7 million km².
    (c) Five Septembers cannot establish a trend: weather
    noise between single years is large, and a trend needs
    multi-year means over decades for the same month and the
    whole Arctic. (d) Ice–albedo feedback: less ice lowers
    albedo, increasing absorption and further melting.
12. Score with the teacher-guide rubric (6 points): checkable
    claims (2), scenario stated and used correctly (1), impact
    tied to the warming level (1), uncertainty named by kind
    (1), what one model run cannot decide (1).

## Project scoring guidance

Score with the `project.md` rubric (20 points). The prediction
criterion is judged on falsifiability, quantification, and
pre-data timing — never on whether the prediction turned out
right. For the public-data alternative, read "data table" as
the CO₂/ice-extent table and "graph" as the plotted series;
the conclusion must distinguish correlation from the feedback
mechanism and name what the data cannot show.
