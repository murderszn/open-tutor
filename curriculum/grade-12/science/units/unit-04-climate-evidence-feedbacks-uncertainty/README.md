# Unit 04 — Climate Evidence, Feedbacks, and Uncertainty

**Grade 12 · Science · Earth and Space Science pathway · Weeks 15–18 of the course year**
(draft, delivered for issue #55; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Unit 03 showed how unequal heating and Earth's rotation move heat
around the planet. This unit asks what happens when the *balance* of
that heat flow changes, and how scientists can tell — honestly — what
is established, what is uncertain, and what no single model run can
decide. It follows five moves:

1. **The energy budget and the greenhouse effect** — the global-mean
   numbers (about 340 W/m² in, about 102 reflected, about 238
   absorbed), the Stefan–Boltzmann calculation that puts a planet
   with no greenhouse effect at about −18 °C instead of the observed
   about 15 °C, and why only some gases absorb infrared
   (HS-ESS2-4).
2. **Feedbacks** — ice–albedo, water vapor, clouds, and the
   carbon-cycle feedback: what amplifies a warming, what damps it,
   and how a simple feedback-gain calculation shows why a small
   initial push can grow (HS-ESS2-2).
3. **The carbon cycle, quantified** — reservoirs, fluxes, and
   residence times in gigatonnes of carbon (GtC); the ppm-to-GtC
   conversion; the airborne fraction; and how to test a sink/source
   claim against the numbers instead of its slogan (HS-ESS2-6, with
   the HS-LS2-5 photosynthesis/respiration bridge).
4. **Climate evidence from independent lines** — the instrumental
   record, ice cores, ocean heat, sea level, ice loss, and the
   fingerprints that separate a greenhouse warming from a brighter
   Sun or a volcanic lull (HS-ESS3-5 strand).
5. **Models, projections, and uncertainty** — what a global climate
   model actually computes, what scenarios are and are not, the
   three kinds of uncertainty, and how to make an evidence-based
   forecast of the current rate of change and its impacts
   (HS-ESS3-5).

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **if the
climate system is full of feedbacks and every model is an
approximation, how can scientists still be confident about some
claims, uncertain about others — and what would change their minds?**

The unit's investigation is *"Testing an Albedo Feedback Claim"*
(see [project.md](project.md)): the learner tests, by a supervised
surface-temperature experiment or by a public-data analysis, whether
a darker surface really warms more and stays warmer — and states
plainly where the test supports the feedback idea and where it does
not.

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–03 and the Weeks 1–2 diagnostic:

- Energy transfer (radiation, conduction, convection); a system
  with inputs, storage, and outputs; the Sun-angle and albedo
  calculations from Unit 03 (cosine factor; albedo as reflected
  fraction).
- Density and heat capacity at the qualitative level; reading
  graphs, computing a mean, and stating a claim with its
  uncertainty — not just its value.
- From earlier grades (conceptual level): the water cycle, seasons
  from Earth's tilt, photosynthesis and respiration as carbon-cycle
  processes. This unit makes the carbon numbers quantitative; it
  re-teaches none of those concepts from scratch.

No calculus is assumed. Every calculation is worked step by step,
including the fourth-root step in Lesson 01 (the learner may use a
calculator's power key or repeated square roots).

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Build the global-mean energy budget from the solar constant:
   state the incoming, reflected, and absorbed fluxes; use the
   Stefan–Boltzmann law to compute the no-greenhouse temperature
   (about 255 K) and explain the about 33 °C gap to the observed
   about 288 K in terms of selective infrared absorption.
   (HS-ESS2-4; track objective 5)
2. Classify a feedback as reinforcing or balancing, trace its loop
   in words and in a diagram, and use the feedback-gain teaching
   model to estimate how a stated feedback fraction amplifies an
   initial warming — naming what the model leaves out.
   (HS-ESS2-2; track objective 5)
3. Use a quantitative carbon-cycle model (reservoirs in GtC, fluxes
   in GtC per year, 1 ppm CO₂ ≈ 2.12 GtC) to compute a residence
   time, convert an emissions rate to a ppm-per-year rate, and
   evaluate a sink/source claim against the airborne fraction.
   (HS-ESS2-6; HS-LS2-5 bridge; track objective 7)
4. Compare at least four independent lines of climate evidence,
   explain what each one measures and what it cannot show on its
   own, and use fingerprint reasoning to judge whether a proposed
   cause fits all the lines or only one. (HS-ESS3-5 strand, HS-ESS2-4;
   track objective 6)
5. Interpret a published model-projection range for a stated
   scenario: name the scenario, separate scenario, model, and
   internal-variability uncertainty, and make an evidence-based
   forecast of the current rate of change and one associated impact
   — stating what is established, what is uncertain, and what one
   model run cannot decide. (HS-ESS3-5; track objective 6)
6. Plan and carry out the albedo investigation: a claim stated
   before the data, a data table with units, a graph, an error
   discussion, and a revised claim — with the adult approving the
   plan before data collection begins. (track objective 12)

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS),
high school (9–12) band, performance expectations for Earth and
Space Science. Codes and descriptions were verified on 2026-11-03
against the California Department of Education NGSS pages and the
NSTA standard listings (both restating the Achieve performance
expectations); descriptions below are short paraphrases for
mapping, not reproductions of the standards text. **This crosswalk
is a planning reference, not a claim of state adoption,
accreditation, or certification of alignment.**

- **HS-ESS2-4** — use a model to describe how variations in the
  flow of energy into and out of Earth's systems result in changes
  in climate (Lessons 01, 04–05; project; assessment). Clarification
  note: over 10–100s of years, changes in human activity, ocean
  circulation, and solar output are among the named causes.
- **HS-ESS2-2** — analyze geoscience data to make the claim that
  one change to Earth's surface can create feedbacks that cause
  changes to other Earth systems (Lessons 02–03; project;
  assessment). The clarification examples include decreasing
  glacial ice reducing reflected sunlight, raising surface
  temperature, and further reducing ice.
- **HS-ESS2-6** — develop a quantitative model of the cycling of
  carbon among the hydrosphere, atmosphere, geosphere, and
  biosphere (Lesson 03; assessment).
- **HS-ESS3-5** — analyze geoscience data and the results from
  global climate models to make an evidence-based forecast of the
  current rate of global or regional climate change and associated
  future impacts to Earth systems (Lessons 04–05; assessment).
  Assessment is limited to one example of a climate change and its
  associated impacts.
- **HS-LS2-5 (bridge)** — develop a model to illustrate the role
  of photosynthesis and cellular respiration in the cycling of
  carbon among the biosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere, and
  geosphere (Lesson 03, as the biological fluxes of the carbon
  model).

All practice datasets in this unit are labeled fictional. Real-data
tasks use the public sources named in the resource pack (for
example, NOAA Mauna Loa CO₂ trends and the IPCC AR6 Summary for
Policymakers headline statements); the learner records the date
accessed, because CO₂ values and projection ranges are updated as
new data arrive. Where the unit quotes a current value — for
example, atmospheric CO₂ around 426 ppm in autumn 2026 (NOAA
Mauna Loa monthly mean, September 2026: 426.07 ppm) and the IPCC
AR6 finding of 1.1 °C of warming in 2011–2020 relative to
1850–1900 — it names its source and date so the adult can check
whether a newer value has superseded it.

## Safety

All outdoor observation is adult-supervised: the adult checks the
forecast first, and observation stops for lightning, flooding, or
severe-weather warnings. The albedo experiment in `project.md`
uses only sun, trays, and thermometers — no open flames, no
hazardous chemicals, no mains electricity — and the public-data
alternative is the default whenever the site, weather, or
instruments are inadequate. See scope-and-sequence §6 and
`project.md` § Safety for the full rules.
