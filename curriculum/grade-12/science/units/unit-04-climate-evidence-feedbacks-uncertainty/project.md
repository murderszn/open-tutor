# Project — Testing an Albedo Feedback Claim

**Claim to test (state it before collecting data):** "A darker
surface absorbs more sunlight, warms more, and stays warmer —
so replacing a bright surface with a dark one reinforces
warming (the surface version of the ice–albedo feedback)."

## Question

Does surface brightness (albedo) measurably change how much a
surface warms in sunlight, and does the difference persist after
the Sun is gone? What does your result support — and what does
it not support — about the real ice–albedo feedback?

## Prediction (state BEFORE collecting data)

Write your prediction in this form, before any data are taken:

- "The [dark / light] tray will warm by about ___ °C more than
  the [other] tray after ___ minutes in direct sun, because
  ___."
- "After ___ minutes in shade, the difference will [persist /
  shrink / vanish], because ___."

The adult initials the prediction. A prediction changed after
seeing data is recorded as changed, with the reason — that is
good science, not failure.

## Materials

- Two identical shallow trays or sheets of card (same size,
  same material), one covered in white paper, one in black
  paper (or white and dark cloth).
- Two identical thermometers (or one thermometer used
  alternately, with the order swapped each round — note the
  method).
- A sunny, wind-sheltered spot; a watch or phone timer; a
  notebook with a data table ready.
- Sun protection and water. The adult approves the site and
  the timing before the experiment begins.

## Procedure

1. **Control the variables.** Same tray size, same paper type
   except color, same Sun angle, same start temperature (leave
   both trays in shade until they read the same). List every
   variable you controlled and one you could not (wind is the
   usual one — note it).
2. **Baseline.** Record both thermometers in shade for
   5 minutes (one reading per minute, or one reading each if
   sharing a thermometer). Both should agree within about
   0.5 °C; if not, note the offset and correct for it.
3. **Sun exposure.** Place both trays side by side in direct
   sun at the same angle to the Sun. Record temperature every
   2 minutes for 20 minutes.
4. **Shade recovery.** Move both trays to shade together.
   Record every 2 minutes for 10 minutes.
5. **Repeat if time allows.** A second run, swapping the
   thermometer positions, is worth more than a longer first
   run: it separates a thermometer bias from a real effect.
6. **Graph.** Plot both temperature curves on the same axes
   (time on the horizontal axis, temperature on the vertical),
   with a mark for the sun-to-shade changeover. Compute the
   mean temperature difference during the last 10 minutes of
   sun exposure.

## Deliverables

1. The prediction, adult-initialed, with any post-data changes
   marked as changes.
2. A data table with units (°C, minutes), including the
   baseline, both runs if done, and the control-variables list.
3. A graph of both curves with labeled axes and the
   changeover marked.
4. A written conclusion (about 300 words): the measured
   difference, whether the prediction held, an error discussion
   (wind, thermometer offset, cloud, tray angle), and a final
   paragraph stating what this tabletop test does and does not
   show about Arctic sea ice — including the scale gap (a tray
   is not an ocean) and the missing ocean-heat and cloud pieces.
5. A feedback-loop diagram for the real ice–albedo feedback,
   with a "limit" box (Lesson 02).

## Rubric (adult scores; 20 points total)

| Criterion | 5 points | 3 points | 1 point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prediction before data | Falsifiable, quantitative, adult-initialed | Stated before data but vague | Written after data or missing |
| Data quality | Table with units, baseline, repeat or swap, controls listed | Table with units but no baseline or controls | Incomplete table, units missing |
| Graph and calculation | Both curves, labeled axes, changeover marked, mean difference computed | Graph present but an element missing | No usable graph or calculation |
| Conclusion and error discussion | Measured difference judged against prediction; ≥ 3 error sources; scale gap to real ice stated | Judgment present but error discussion thin or scale gap missing | Claim restated without evidence or errors |
| Feedback diagram | Loop returns to its start quantity; limit box named | Loop drawn but does not close or limit missing | No diagram or loop broken |

16–20: proceed to the unit assessment. 11–15: revise the
weakest criterion with the adult, then proceed. Below 11:
repeat the data session (or switch to the public-data
alternative) before the assessment.

## Safety

Adult-supervised, outdoor only in safe conditions: the adult
checks the forecast first; work stops for lightning, high
winds, flooding, or extreme heat. Sun protection and water are
carried; no one stares at the Sun. No open flames, no
hazardous chemicals, no mains electricity. Thermometers are
used as labeled; broken glass thermometers (if any are used)
are left for the adult — use alcohol thermometers or digital
probes where possible.

## Alternatives

**Public-data alternative (the default whenever the site,
weather, or instruments are inadequate):** use NOAA Mauna Loa
CO₂ trends and a published Arctic sea-ice extent series
(resource pack) to test a parallel claim: "As CO₂ rose from
about 280 ppm toward about 426 ppm, September Arctic sea-ice
extent fell." Deliverables are the same shape — a stated claim
before looking at the series, a data table (year, CO₂, ice
extent) for at least 10 years, a two-axis or two-panel graph,
and a conclusion that distinguishes correlation from the
feedback mechanism (Lesson 02) and names what the data cannot
show. The rubric applies with "data table" read as the
public-data table and "graph" as the plotted series.
