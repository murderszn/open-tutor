# Lesson 03 — The Carbon Cycle, Quantified

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can use reservoir and
flux numbers to compute a residence time, convert between GtC
and ppm, find the airborne fraction, and test a sink/source claim
against the model instead of its slogan.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, calculator.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From grade-9 biology:
photosynthesis takes CO₂ from the air and stores carbon in
plants; respiration and decay return it. A **reservoir** holds
carbon; a **flux** moves it. If you can say which is which for
"the ocean" and "photosynthesis," you are ready.

## 1. Reservoirs and fluxes in one model

Carbon is counted in **gigatonnes of carbon (GtC)** — one
gigatonne is a billion tonnes. Atmospheric CO₂ is counted in
**parts per million (ppm)**: molecules of CO₂ per million
molecules of dry air. The bridge between them is:

**1 ppm of atmospheric CO₂ ≈ 2.12 GtC.**

The teaching model used all through this unit (rounded values,
consistent with the Global Carbon Budget's published orders of
magnitude; the adult checks the current year's budget in the
resource pack before assigning exact figures):

| Reservoir | Size (GtC, rounded) |
|---|---|
| Atmosphere | ~900 |
| Vegetation | ~550 |
| Soils (including litter) | ~2,300 |
| Ocean (surface + deep) | ~38,000 |
| Fossil fuels still in the ground | large, but not a flux until extracted |

| Flux (GtC per year, rounded) | Size |
|---|---|
| Photosynthesis (air → plants) | ~120 |
| Respiration + decay (plants/soils → air) | ~120 |
| Ocean uptake and release (each way) | ~90 |
| Human fossil-fuel emissions | ~10 |
| Human land-use emissions | ~1 |
| **Total human emissions** | **~11** |

Before industrialization, the natural fluxes roughly balanced:
photosynthesis ≈ respiration + decay, ocean in ≈ ocean out, so
atmospheric CO₂ sat near 280 ppm for centuries. Human emissions
are small *compared with the natural fluxes* — about 11 against
120 — and that comparison is the source of a famous bad
argument, dismantled in section 3. What matters is not the size
of the human flux against the natural fluxes, but its
*imbalance*: nature's in-flows and out-flows matched; humans
added an extra inflow with no matching extra outflow, so the
atmosphere accumulates the difference.

**Worked example 1 — ppm to GtC.** Atmospheric CO₂ in autumn
2026 is about 426 ppm (NOAA Mauna Loa, September 2026 monthly
mean 426.07 ppm). In GtC: 426 × 2.12 = **903 GtC** — the "~900"
in the table. Pre-industrial 280 ppm is 280 × 2.12 = **594 GtC**.
The atmosphere has gained about 903 − 594 = **309 GtC** since
pre-industrial times. Keep that 309 in mind: it is the bill the
fluxes must explain.

**Worked example 2 — Residence time.** Residence time =
reservoir ÷ flux out. For the atmosphere against photosynthesis
alone: 900 ÷ 120 = **7.5 years**. That is the **turnover time**:
an average CO₂ molecule spends only a few years in the air
before photosynthesis or the ocean takes it up. It is *not* the
time for an added pulse of CO₂ to disappear: because the ocean
and land return much of what they absorb, and because the deep
ocean equilibrates slowly, the **adjustment time** for a CO₂
pulse is centuries to millennia. Confusing turnover with
adjustment is the second famous bad argument of this lesson:
"CO₂ only lasts 7 years, so emissions don't matter" mistakes
a molecule's holiday for the stock's lifetime. For the ocean
against its 90 GtC/yr exchange: 38,000 ÷ 90 ≈ **420 years** —
a rough teaching average that hides the real structure (fast
surface mixing, slow deep mixing), which is why it is labeled
rough.

## 2. The airborne fraction

Of each year's ~11 GtC of human emissions, only part stays in
the air. The rest is taken up by the ocean and by land ecosystems
(the "sinks"). The split, rounded, closes the budget:

- Stays in the air: ~5.1 GtC/yr.
- Ocean sink: ~2.9 GtC/yr.
- Land sink: ~3.0 GtC/yr.
- Total: 5.1 + 2.9 + 3.0 = **11.0 GtC/yr** ✓.

**Worked example 3 — From emissions to ppm per year.** If all
11 GtC/yr stayed airborne, the rise would be
11 ÷ 2.12 ≈ **5.2 ppm/yr**. The observed rise in recent years
is about **2.4 ppm/yr**. Convert back: 2.4 × 2.12 = **5.1 GtC/yr**
staying airborne. The **airborne fraction** is therefore
5.1 ÷ 11 ≈ **0.46 — about 46%**. The sinks take the other
~54%. Two cautions: the airborne fraction wobbles year to year
(wildfire years and El Niño years push it up; strong La Niña
years push it down), and there is no guarantee it stays near
46% — if sinks weaken as the ocean warms and acidifies, a larger
share of each tonne emitted would stay airborne, which is itself
a carbon-cycle feedback (Lesson 02's family).

**Worked example 4 — Testing a sink claim.** A company claims
its tree-planting will "offset" 1 GtC of emissions per year.
Test it against the model, not the press release: (a) 1 GtC is
about 9% of one year's human emissions — material, but not a
solution on its own. (b) A forest is a reservoir, not a
permanent vault: when trees die, decay returns carbon unless
the land stays forested and the carbon stays stored. (c) The
claim is only additional if those trees would not have grown
anyway. The model does not decide whether the project is good;
it decides which questions to ask first: *how much, for how
long, and instead of what?* Any sink claim that cannot answer
in GtC and years has not yet been tested.

**Misconception check:** "Human emissions are only 4% of the
carbon flux, so they can't matter." The natural fluxes are
two-way streets: 120 in, 120 out. Human emissions are a
one-way street: 11 in, 0 out, year after year. A bathtub with
equal inflow and outflow stays level; add a small extra inflow
and it rises steadily, however small the extra tap looks next
to the main pipes. The atmosphere's 309 GtC gain is the rising
water level, measured.

## 3. Photosynthesis and respiration as fluxes

The biological fluxes are the same processes from grade-9
biology, now with numbers attached (HS-LS2-5 bridge):
photosynthesis moves about 120 GtC/yr from air to plants;
respiration plus decay moves about 120 back. On a seasonal
scale this shows up directly in the Mauna Loa record: CO₂ dips
each northern summer as northern forests photosynthesize, and
rises each winter as they go dormant — the "sawtooth" on the
rising Keeling curve. The sawtooth is about 6 ppm peak to
trough; the long-term rise is about 2.4 ppm per year, every
year, on top of it. A learner who can point at the sawtooth
and at the trend separately, and say which one is biology's
season and which one is the imbalance, understands this lesson.

## Guided practice

1. Convert 300 ppm of CO₂ to GtC. Then convert 500 GtC to ppm.
   (Answers use 2.12 GtC per ppm.)
2. A reservoir holds 1,800 GtC and loses 60 GtC/yr. What is its
   residence time?
3. In a practice year, human emissions are 10 GtC and the
   observed rise is 2.0 ppm/yr. Compute the airborne amount in
   GtC/yr and the airborne fraction.

## Independent practice

4. If emissions stayed at 11 GtC/yr and the airborne fraction
   stayed at 46%, how much would atmospheric CO₂ rise in ppm
   over 10 years? (First find GtC/yr staying airborne, then
   convert, then multiply by 10.)
5. A news story says: "The ocean absorbs 90 GtC a year, so it
   can easily absorb our 11 GtC." Explain the error, using the
   difference between a two-way exchange flux and a net sink.
6. **Error analysis:** A worksheet computes an atmospheric
   residence time as 900 ÷ 11 = 82 years and calls it "how long
   CO₂ lasts." Identify the two errors: wrong flux in the
   denominator, and turnover confused with adjustment time.

## Applied task

7. Write a five-line "claim test" for this statement: "Planting
   trees will solve the carbon problem." Each line must use a
   number from this lesson's model (a flux, a reservoir, or the
   airborne fraction) and end with the question the number
   forces. There is no single correct verdict — the adult scores
   whether every line is quantitative and whether the questions
   are the right ones.

## Exit check

8. Without notes: state the ppm-to-GtC conversion, the
   atmosphere's size in GtC, and the airborne fraction — and say
   in one sentence why a small human flux can still raise the
   atmospheric stock every year.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** keep the reservoir/flux table open beside
  every calculation; no number in this lesson has to be
  memorized, only the *methods* (convert, divide, compare).
- **Alternate response mode:** the applied task may be
  delivered as a spoken claim test or a poster, not only as
  prose.
- **Extension:** look up the current year's Global Carbon
  Budget (adult previews; see resource pack) and replace the
  rounded 11 / 5.1 / 2.9 / 3.0 split with the published values.
  Does the airborne fraction move much? What changed most?

**Teacher note:** the bathtub analogy does most of the
reteaching work here; return to it whenever a learner compares
a human flux to a natural flux without asking about balance.
