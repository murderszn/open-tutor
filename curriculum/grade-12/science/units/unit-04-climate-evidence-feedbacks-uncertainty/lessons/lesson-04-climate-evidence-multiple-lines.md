# Lesson 04 — Climate Evidence from Independent Lines

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can compare four
independent lines of climate evidence, state what each one
measures and what it cannot show alone, and use fingerprint
reasoning to judge whether a proposed cause fits all the lines
or only one.

**Time:** ~50 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, calculator.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** From Lesson 03: CO₂ was about
280 ppm pre-industrial and about 426 ppm in autumn 2026. If you
can convert that rise to GtC (about 309 GtC), you are ready.

## 1. One claim, many witnesses

A single measurement can always be doubted: an instrument can
drift, a station can move, a record can be short. Climate
science therefore leans on *independent* lines — measurements
made by different instruments, in different parts of the system,
often by different research communities, that would have to be
wrong in the same direction at the same time for the conclusion
to fail. This lesson walks six of them. The assessment asks for
four; learn all six and choose your strongest four.

**Line 1 — The instrumental temperature record.** Thermometers
at land stations, ships, buoys, and satellites, combined by
NASA and NOAA into global-mean series back to about 1880, show
warming of about 1.1 °C in 2011–2020 relative to 1850–1900
(IPCC AR6, high confidence). *Measures:* air and sea-surface
temperature, directly. *Cannot show alone:* why it warmed —
temperature alone does not name a cause — and the early record
is sparser, especially over the oceans and the Arctic, so its
uncertainty bars are wider before about 1950.

**Line 2 — Ice cores.** Bubbles trapped in Antarctic and
Greenland ice preserve ancient air. Over the last 800,000 years,
CO₂ swung between about 180 ppm in glacial periods and about
280–300 ppm in warm interglacials, tracking temperature up and
down. Today's about 426 ppm sits far above that whole range —
and the rise took about 150 years, not thousands. *Measures:*
past CO₂ and temperature (via ice isotopes), directly from
trapped air. *Cannot show alone:* the present-day cause — ice
cores end before the industrial era in most records, and they
show CO₂ and temperature moving together without, by
themselves, saying which led which in each transition.

**Line 3 — Ocean heat content.** The ocean stores more than 90%
of the extra heat trapped by the greenhouse imbalance, because
water's heat capacity is high (Unit 03). Argo floats — about
4,000 autonomous profiling floats — measure temperature down
to 2,000 m, and the upper ocean has warmed steadily since
reliable records began. *Measures:* where the missing heat
actually went — the budget's storage term. *Cannot show alone:*
surface impacts; a warming deep ocean is consistent with a
greenhouse forcing, but it is not itself a heatwave or a
drought.

**Line 4 — Sea level.** Tide gauges and satellite altimetry
show sea level rising roughly 20 cm since 1900, now at a few
millimetres per year and accelerating. Two mechanisms add up:
water expands as it warms (thermal expansion) and land ice
melts into the sea. *Measures:* the integrated result of ocean
warming plus ice loss. *Cannot show alone:* the split between
the two mechanisms — that needs the ocean-heat and ice-loss
records alongside it.

**Line 5 — Ice loss and snow cover.** Satellite records show
Arctic September sea-ice extent declining about 13% per decade
relative to the 1981–2010 average, glaciers retreating on
nearly every continent, and northern snow cover shrinking in
spring. *Measures:* the cryosphere's response, visible from
space. *Cannot show alone:* global temperature — ice also
responds to ocean currents, snowfall changes, and dark particles
(soot) on its surface.

**Line 6 — The living world.** Species ranges shifting
poleward and uphill, spring events (leaf-out, migration, egg
laying) advancing, and coral bleaching events increasing are
documented across many taxa. *Measures:* ecological response
to warming, in systems nobody calibrated to a thermometer.
*Cannot show alone:* attribution — habitat loss and pollution
also move species, so ecology corroborates rather than proves.

**Worked example 1 — Why independence matters.** Suppose the
thermometer record were somehow biased warm by 0.3 °C. That
error would not touch the ice-core CO₂, the Argo heat content,
the satellite sea level, or the glacier photographs: each uses
different physics. To overturn the conclusion that the planet
warmed, a skeptic must explain away *all* the lines, not one.
That is the difference between doubting a measurement and
doubting a conclusion: the first is routine science, the
second needs a rival explanation that fits every line at once.

**Worked example 2 — Reading a trend honestly.** A practice
dataset (fictional, for method only): a station's annual-mean
temperatures for 10 years are 15.0, 15.2, 15.1, 15.4, 15.3,
15.6, 15.5, 15.8, 15.7, 16.0 °C. The mean of the first five is
15.2 °C; of the last five, 15.72 °C — a rise of about 0.5 °C
across the decade. But one hot year does not make a trend, and
one cold year does not break one: the lesson is to compare
multi-year means and to report the scatter (here about ±0.2 °C
around the line) alongside the slope. Climate is a 30-year
average by convention (the WMO climate-normal period) precisely
so that weather noise does not masquerade as signal.

## 2. Fingerprints: telling causes apart

If several causes could warm the planet, evidence must do more
than show warming — it must show *which* warming. Three
fingerprints do that work:

- **A brighter Sun** would warm the whole atmosphere, including
  the stratosphere, and would warm days and nights roughly
  alike. Observed: the stratosphere has *cooled* while the
  troposphere warmed, and nights have warmed faster than days.
  That pattern fits greenhouse gases (which trap heat low down
  and let the high atmosphere radiate away) and does not fit a
  brighter Sun. Solar output, moreover, has been measured from
  space since 1978 and shows no upward trend matching the
  warming.
- **Volcanoes** cool for a year or two after big eruptions
  (sulfate aerosols reflect sunlight) and then fade. They
  explain wiggles, not a century-long rise.
- **Greenhouse gases** predict exactly the observed pattern:
  troposphere warms, stratosphere cools, nights warm faster,
  the Arctic warms fastest (ice–albedo feedback, Lesson 02),
  and the outgoing infrared spectrum shows growing bites taken
  out exactly at CO₂'s absorption wavelengths. That last one is
  the most direct fingerprint of all: satellites watch CO₂
  eating its own wavelengths in real time.

**Worked example 3 — Testing a rival claim.** Claim: "The
warming is just the Sun." Check it against three lines.
(1) Solar output: flat since 1978 while warming continued —
fails. (2) Stratosphere: cooling observed, warming predicted
by a solar cause — fails. (3) Night vs. day: nights warming
faster fits greenhouse trapping, not extra sunshine — fails.
A claim that fails three independent checks is not "one
scientist's opinion against another's"; it is a hypothesis
that has been tested and found wanting. That is also how to
handle the reverse error: a claim that passes one check (say,
a cold winter somewhere) but fails the global lines has not
been confirmed either. Weather is not climate, and a local
cold snap is a data point, not a rebuttal.

**Misconception check:** "Scientists changed the data — the
temperature record has been adjusted!" Adjustments are the
point, not a scandal: stations move, instruments change, and
a station that moved from a valley to a hilltop in 1960 would
show a fake cooling unless corrected. The honest test is
whether independent groups, using different adjustment methods
(NASA, NOAA, and others), get the same warming — they do, to
within about 0.1 °C over the century. If adjustments were
manufacturing warming, different methods would not converge.

## Guided practice

1. Name one thing the ice-core record measures directly and one
   thing it cannot show alone.
2. A rival claim says warming comes from undersea volcanoes.
   Which two evidence lines would you check first, and what
   result would the claim need?
3. Using the practice dataset in section 1, compute the mean of
   all ten years and state whether a single year at 15.0 °C at
   the start would weaken the trend claim.

## Independent practice

4. Sea level rises about 3 mm in a practice year. How many
   centimetres is that per century if the rate stayed constant?
   Why is "if the rate stayed constant" doing heavy lifting in
   that sentence?
5. Explain why ocean heat content is a *stronger* check on the
   energy budget than surface temperature alone. (Hint: where
   does more than 90% of the trapped heat go?)
6. **Error analysis:** A commentator shows a graph of Arctic ice
   for one September that is higher than the previous September
   and declares the decline over. Identify the error (one year
   vs. trend; September-to-September weather noise) and state
   the correct comparison (multi-year means, same month, whole
   Arctic).

## Applied task

7. Pick a climate claim you have heard outside school (any
   claim, from any side). Write a four-line fingerprint test:
   the claim, the evidence line that tests it, the result the
   claim needs, and the result actually observed (use this
   lesson's lines; where you do not know the observed result,
   write "check in the resource pack" instead of guessing).
   The adult scores the structure, not the side you chose.

## Exit check

8. Without notes: name four evidence lines and, for each, one
   thing it measures and one thing it cannot show alone.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the six evidence lines may be learned as
  a spoken list (instrument, ice, ocean, sea, ice-loss, living)
  before any of them is written about.
- **Alternate response mode:** the applied task may be a
  spoken fingerprint test or a table, not only prose.
- **Extension:** find the satellite outgoing-infrared spectrum
  (adult previews; see resource pack) and identify the CO₂
  absorption feature near 15 micrometres. What would the
  spectrum look like if CO₂ had *not* increased?

**Teacher note:** the fingerprint section is the unit's
argumentative core; do not let it become a list of conclusions
to memorize. Insist on the structure: claim → prediction →
observation → verdict. Answers: see `answer-key.md`, Lesson 04.
