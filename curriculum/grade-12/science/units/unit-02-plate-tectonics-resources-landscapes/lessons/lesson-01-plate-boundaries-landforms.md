# Lesson 01 — Plate Boundaries and the Landforms They Build

**Unit 02 · Session S1 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** HS-ESS2-1 (Earth systems and processes: analyze evidence for
plate tectonics — planning reference only; no claim of state adoption)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can look at the motion of a plate boundary —
which plates, what crust type, which direction — and predict the
landforms it produces, explaining the prediction in terms of how the
plates interact.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, ruler, calculator (or the adult's phone calculator).

## Explicit teaching

**A boundary is a collision of processes, not just a line.** A plate
boundary is where two plates interact, and the interaction depends on
three things: the **direction of relative motion** (apart, together,
sideways), the **type of crust** involved (oceanic or continental), and
the **density contrast** between them. Change any one and the landforms
change. This lesson is a catalog of those interactions — learn to read
the motion and predict the landscape.

**Divergent boundaries — plates move apart.** Where plates pull apart,
hot material rises to fill the gap and new crust is built. Under the
ocean this makes the **mid-ocean ridge**: a long underwater mountain
range with a central rift valley where magma erupts and new oceanic
crust spreads outward on both sides. On continents, the same process
starts as a **continental rift valley** — the crust stretches, thins,
drops down along faults, and volcanoes erupt along the rift floor. A
rift valley can eventually flood and become a new ocean basin: the
seafloor ages and magnetic striping you studied in Unit 01 are the
record of this process at work.

**Convergent boundaries — plates move together.** One of the two plates
has to give way, and the denser one loses: it bends down and sinks back
into the mantle. That sinking is **subduction**.

- **Ocean–ocean convergence.** One oceanic plate subducts beneath the
  other. The sinking slab bends downward, carving a **deep-sea trench**,
  and water carried down with the slab lowers the melting point of the
  mantle above it, generating magma. The magma rises through the
  overriding oceanic plate and builds a curved chain of volcanoes — an
  **island arc** — parallel to the trench.
- **Ocean–continent convergence.** Oceanic crust is denser than
  continental crust, so the ocean plate subducts. Again you get a trench
  offshore and magma generation above the sinking slab — but now the
  volcanoes rise through thick continental crust, forming a **coastal
  volcanic mountain range** (the magma also thickens and crumples the
  continent's edge). Trench, offshore; volcanic mountains, on the
  continent.
- **Continent–continent convergence.** Neither plate can subduct —
  continental crust is too buoyant to sink deep. So instead of one plate
  sliding under, the crust **crumples, folds, and thickens**. The result
  is a broad, high **folded mountain range** sitting on a **thickened
  crustal root**, often with a high plateau behind it. And here is the
  key contrast: there are **no subduction volcanoes** in a
  continent–continent collision. Volcanoes at convergent boundaries come
  from magma generated above a sinking slab of *oceanic* crust — water
  released from the sinking slab triggers the melting. With no oceanic
  slab going down, there is no slab to release water and no arc magma.
  The mountains are built by crumpling, not by eruption.

**Transform boundaries — plates slide sideways.** Neither plate is
created nor destroyed; they grind past each other along **faults**. The
rock locks, stress builds, and then it slips — which is why transform
boundaries produce **earthquakes but no volcanoes**: there is no
subducting slab to make magma and no rift opening to let magma up.
Streams, ridges, and roads that cross the fault get **offset** —
visibly bent or displaced sideways — the field signature of a
transform boundary.

**Reading a boundary in three questions.** For any boundary, ask:
(1) *Motion*: apart, together, or sideways? (2) *Crust*: ocean–ocean,
ocean–continent, or continent–continent? (3) *Density consequence*: does
something sink (subduction → trench + volcanoes), does something crumple
(no volcanoes), or does nothing happen but sideways grinding (faults,
earthquakes)?

### Reading passage — "The Trench and the Arc"

*Read with the adult in a reading session; keep for reference
now.*

Stand on the deck of a ship over the western Pacific and drop a line:
beneath you the seafloor falls away into a trench deeper than Everest is
tall, and a few hundred miles to one side a string of volcanic islands
rises from the sea, each one steaming or freshly black with lava. The
two features are one process. The ocean plate bends and dives at the
trench, carrying seawater locked in its minerals down into the heat.
That water does something strange — it makes solid rock melt at a lower
temperature — and the magma that forms is lighter than everything around
it, so it rises. By the time it breaks the surface it has built an
island, and the trench beside it marks exactly where the plate began its
dive. Find a trench on a map and you have found a subduction zone; find
the line of volcanoes parallel to it and you have found where the slab's
water finished its work. Now sail to where two continents are
colliding: the mountains are just as high, but the summits are folded
rock, not craters. Nothing sank, so nothing melted — the range was
raised the way a rug wrinkles when you push it across the floor. Same
planetary engine, two completely different landscapes, and the
difference is written in which crust went down.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Predict landforms from a boundary description.**

*Given:* An oceanic plate and a continental plate are moving toward each
other. *Predict the landforms and justify each one.*

*Reasoning.* Step 1 — motion: the plates converge, so one must give way.
Step 2 — crust and density: oceanic crust is denser than continental
crust, so the **oceanic plate subducts** beneath the continent. Step 3 —
consequences of sinking: the slab bends down at the plate edge, carving
a **deep-sea trench offshore**; water carried down with the slab
triggers melting in the mantle above, the magma rises through the
continent, and builds a **coastal volcanic mountain range**. Prediction:
**trench offshore + volcanic mountains on the continent**, with
earthquakes from the plates grinding as one dives. Check the alternative
answers: an island arc is wrong (that needs ocean on *both* sides of the
trench); a rift valley is wrong (plates are converging, not diverging).

**Example 2 — How far does a spreading ridge push plates apart?**

*Given:* A mid-ocean ridge spreads at a **full spreading rate of
2.5 cm/yr** — meaning the two sides move apart from each other at a
combined 2.5 centimeters per year. (Typical Mid-Atlantic value; real
reference value.) How far apart do two points — one on each side of the
ridge — move in (a) 1 million years, (b) 10 million years? Show every
unit conversion.

*Reasoning.* Distance = rate × time, so the arithmetic is
multiplication — the work is in the units.

(a) 1 million years = 1,000,000 yr.
2.5 cm/yr × 1,000,000 yr = **2,500,000 cm**.
Convert centimeters to kilometers: 100 cm = 1 m, and 1,000 m = 1 km,
so 1 km = 100,000 cm. 2,500,000 cm ÷ 100,000 cm/km = **25 km**.
Answer: **25 kilometers** of new seafloor between the two points.

(b) 10 million years = 10,000,000 yr.
2.5 cm/yr × 10,000,000 yr = **25,000,000 cm**.
25,000,000 ÷ 100,000 = **250 km**. Answer: **250 kilometers**.

*Sanity check.* The real Atlantic is about 5,000–6,000 km wide and the
oldest seafloor there is roughly 180 million years old. At 2.5 cm/yr,
180 Myr gives 2.5 × 180,000,000 = 450,000,000 cm = 4,500 km — the same
order of magnitude as the real ocean width. The model and the map agree
roughly, so the method is sound (real seafloor there is a bit older and
rates vary along the ridge, which is why it is not exact).

**Example 3 — Rule out the wrong boundary.**

*Given:* A field team reports a long, straight fault cutting across a
desert. Dry stream channels that cross the fault are offset sideways by
several meters; nearby hills show cracked rock but there are no lava
flows and no young volcanic cones. Which boundary type fits, and which
two do not?

*Reasoning.* Offset stream channels with no volcanoes is the classic
transform signature: sideways motion on a fault, earthquakes, no magma
involved. Divergent is ruled out — there is no rift valley or new crust
being built, just sideways offset. Convergent is ruled out — there is no
trench, no folded range, no volcanic arc. Fit: **transform boundary**.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult nearby; hints are in brackets.

1. An oceanic plate and another oceanic plate converge. List the two
   landforms you predict and say which plate subducts. [Which crust is
   denser — or are they the same? Does "same" stop subduction?]
2. A continent is splitting: the crust is thinning, the ground is
   dropping along faults, and volcanoes are erupting along the rift
   floor. Name the boundary type and predict what this landscape becomes
   if the process continues for tens of millions of years. [What did
   Unit 01 say new ocean basins start as?]
3. Two continents are colliding and a huge folded mountain range is
   rising. A classmate expects volcanoes to appear soon. Agree or
   disagree, with the mechanism. [What generates arc magma — and is
   that ingredient present here?]
4. At the same 2.5 cm/yr full spreading rate from Example 2, how far
   apart do the two sides move in 500,000 years? Show the conversion to
   kilometers. [Half of the 1-million-year answer — but do the
   multiplication yourself.]

## Independent practice

Complete in the notebook; the adult scores same-day.

1. **Matching.** Write each boundary type and the landforms that belong
   to it. Terms: *divergent; ocean–ocean convergent; ocean–continent
   convergent; continent–continent convergent; transform.*
   Landform bank: *mid-ocean ridge; rift valley; island arc; deep-sea
   trench; coastal volcanic range; folded mountain range; high plateau;
   offset stream channels; thickened crustal root.* (Some landforms,
   like the trench, belong to more than one boundary — say so.)
   (Fictional practice task; the boundary definitions above are the
   reference.)
2. Two plates slide sideways past each other at 3.5 cm/yr (fictional
   practice rate). A fence built straight across the fault in the year
   1900 is offset by 2025. How many centimeters of offset has accumulated,
   and how many meters is that? Show the steps. (Hint: elapsed years ×
   rate, then cm → m.)
3. A map shows a deep trench with a parallel chain of volcanic islands
   200 km away. Identify the boundary type and crust combination, and
   explain in two sentences why the volcanoes sit *parallel to* the
   trench rather than inside it. (Fictional practice map.)
4. **Error analysis.** A classmate claims: "Transform boundaries build
   volcanoes, because all plate boundaries recycle crust and recycling
   makes magma." Identify the two errors — one about what transform
   boundaries do to crust, one about where boundary volcanoes come
   from — and state the correct claim.

## Application

A (fictional) survey team describes a coastline: offshore, the seafloor
drops into a trench; onshore, a chain of active volcanic peaks runs
parallel to the coast; GPS stations show the offshore plate moving
toward the continent at 6 cm/yr (fictional practice data).

a) Identify the boundary type and state which plate subducts and why.
b) The team asks whether a *new* ocean basin could open here. Answer
   with the motion test: what motion would be required, and does this
   boundary have it?
c) Using the spreading-rate method from Example 2 (not the 6 cm/yr
   convergence rate), estimate how wide a *new* ocean would be after
   40 million years of spreading at 2.5 cm/yr. Show the arithmetic in
   centimeters and kilometers.

## Exit check

1. Name the one boundary type that produces volcanoes *without*
   subduction, and the one convergent setting that produces mountains
   *without* volcanoes. One sentence each.
2. Two points on opposite sides of a ridge move apart at 2.5 cm/yr.
   Roughly how many kilometers apart are they after 4 million years?
   Show the multiplication.

  whole ocean?

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "all convergent boundaries make volcanoes" —
the continent–continent case is the deliberate counterexample; press the
mechanism (no subducting oceanic slab → no slab water → no arc magma),
not just the fact; (2) conflating *full* and *half* spreading rates —
this lesson always uses full rates, and the fence problem (Independent
practice 2) is the check that learners multiply rate × time rather than
halving; (3) reading the trench as the volcano site — the volcanoes sit
inland of the trench, above where the slab reaches melting depth, which
is exactly what Application (a) tests; (4) treating transform faults as
"constructive or destructive" — they are conservative: crust is neither
created nor destroyed, only ground sideways. Watch the unit conversions
in Example 2: learners who write "2,500,000 cm = 2,500 km" have divided
by 1,000 instead of 100,000 — make them write the cm→km bridge
(100,000 cm = 1 km) every time until it is automatic.
