# Lesson 02 — Weathering, Erosion, and Deposition: Reading a Landscape

**Unit 02 · Session S6 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards:** HS-ESS2-1 (Earth systems and processes — planning

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can tell weathering, erosion, and deposition
apart; explain how slope, water volume, and material size control what a
river carries and where it drops it; and read a valley's shape to say
where erosion and deposition are happening and why.

## Explicit teaching

**Three different jobs.** *Weathering* breaks rock into smaller pieces
*where it sits* — no movement required. *Erosion* picks those pieces up
and *moves* them. *Deposition* drops them *somewhere new* when the
transporting agent loses energy. A cliff can weather for a thousand
years without eroding; a river can erode without depositing until it
slows down. Keep the three jobs separate and half the confusion in this
topic disappears.

**Weathering comes in two flavors.** *Physical weathering* breaks rock
without changing what it is made of: **freeze–thaw** (water seeps into
cracks, freezes, expands, and wedges the crack wider — like an ice cube
splitting its tray), **abrasion** (particles scrape against each other
and against bedrock, rounding and shrinking as they go), and **root
wedging** (plant roots grow into cracks and pry them open). *Chemical
weathering* changes the minerals themselves: **dissolution** (minerals
dissolve outright — limestone dissolving in slightly acidic rainwater,
the same chemistry that carves caves) and **oxidation** (iron-bearing
minerals react with oxygen and water to form rusty iron oxides — the
reddish staining on old rock surfaces is literally rust). A rounded,
reddish boulder has been physically *and* chemically weathered: abrasion
rounded it, oxidation colored it.

**Erosion and deposition are about energy.** Moving water carries
material only while it has enough energy to keep it moving, and its
energy depends on **slope** (steeper = faster = more energy), **water
volume** (more water = more energy), and **material size** (larger,
heavier grains need more energy to move). When energy drops — the slope
flattens, the flow spreads out, the flood ends — the water can no longer
carry its load, and it deposits the **largest grains first**, the finest
last. That one rule explains most depositional landscapes.

**Where deposition builds land.** A **delta** forms where a river meets
a lake or sea and suddenly slows: it drops its sediment load in a fan,
largest grains nearest the river mouth. A **floodplain** is the flat
land beside a river, built grain by grain each time floodwater spills
over the banks, slows, and drops fine silt. A **beach** is where waves
drop sand they can no longer carry — a depositional feature, rebuilt
constantly. All three are piles of sorted sediment, and the sorting
(coarse near the energy source, fine farther away) is the signature of
deposition.

**One river, two jobs.** The same river erodes upstream and deposits
downstream because its energy changes along its course. Upstream the
valley is steep and narrow: fast water with high energy cuts down into
bedrock, exposing layered rock and carving features like waterfalls
(where the river meets a resistant layer it cannot cut as fast).
Downstream the valley widens and flattens: the same water, now slow,
cannot carry what it picked up upstream, so it drops it — building
floodplains, sandbars, and deltas. Follow one grain of sand from the
waterfall to the delta and you have traced the whole lesson: weathered
off the cliff, eroded by the fast reach, deposited where the river
tired.

### Reading passage — "The River's Two Reaches"

*Read with the adult in a reading session; keep for reference
now.*

Walk a river from its headwaters to its mouth and you walk through an
energy gradient. In the hills the channel is a staircase of rock: the
water is loud, white, and impatient, and it spends its energy the way a
chisel does — downward. It undercuts banks, plucks blocks from the bed,
and where it meets a band of hard rock it pours over the edge in a
waterfall, drilling a plunge pool at the base like a hammer that never
tires. The valley here is narrow because the river's work is vertical.
Then the land flattens, and the river changes its mind. The same volume
of water, spread across a wide shallow channel on a gentle slope, goes
quiet. It can still carry silt, but the gravel it rolled so easily
uphill of here now sinks to the bed. On the outside of each bend the
current still runs fast enough to chew the bank into a steep, undercut
cliff; on the inside of the same bend the water barely moves, and sand
settles into a gentle sloping bar. Bend after bend, flood after flood,
the quiet water builds a flat valley floor out of everything the loud
water tore loose upstream. Erosion and deposition are not two different
rivers. They are one river, spending its energy budget in two different
places.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Stream-table reasoning from practice data.**

*Setup (fictional practice data):* A stream table runs the same water
volume over the same sand-and-gravel mix at two slopes. At a **5°**
slope, tracer grains travel a mean distance of **12 cm** before
stopping. At a **15°** slope, identical grains travel a mean distance of
**38 cm**.

*Question:* How much farther do grains travel on the steeper slope, and
what claim does this support?

*Reasoning.* First the arithmetic: 38 cm − 12 cm = **26 cm farther**
on the 15° slope. The only thing that changed between runs was the
slope — same water volume, same material — so the slope caused the
difference: steeper slope → faster water → more energy → grains carried
farther before the flow could no longer move them. Claim: **with water
volume and material held constant, a steeper slope transports grains
farther.**

*Limits of the claim (say these out loud).* This supports the claim
only inside the tested conditions: one material, one water volume, two
slopes, one table. It does not prove the relationship is a straight
line — we cannot assume 25° would give a proportional jump — and it says
nothing about what a larger water volume would do. Two points show a
direction, not a formula.

**Example 2 — Reading a valley from a text description.**

*Given (text-only; the lesson stands alone on this description):*
"A river bends through a wide flat valley. On the outside of each bend
the bank is steep and undercut; on the inside the bank is a gentle slope
of fresh sand. Upstream, the valley is narrow with exposed layered rock
and a small waterfall."

*Questions:* (a) Which bank of each bend is eroding, and which is
depositing? Why? (b) What does the upstream reach show?

*Reasoning.* (a) Water moves fastest on the **outside** of a bend
(think of a car on a curved track — the outer lane travels farther and
faster). Fast water has the energy to erode, so the **outside bank
erodes**: it is steep and undercut because the current keeps chewing
material away from its base. On the **inside** of the bend the water
moves slowest, energy drops, and the river deposits its load — hence
the **gentle slope of fresh sand**, a point bar built grain by grain.
The "fresh" matters: newly deposited sand has not had time to weather
or grow plants, so it marks *active* deposition.

(b) The upstream reach — narrow valley, exposed layered rock, waterfall —
is the **erosion zone**. Steep slope means high-energy water cutting
*downward* into bedrock; the exposed layers are rock the river has
stripped bare (weathering loosened it, erosion is carrying it away),
and the waterfall is where the river meets resistant rock and pours
over instead of cutting through evenly. Upstream erodes; downstream,
where this description's bends sit, the river both erodes (outer banks)
and deposits (inner banks, and the flat valley floor itself).

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult nearby; hints are in brackets. Then do the
observation activity.

1. Classify each as physical weathering, chemical weathering, erosion,
   or deposition. (a) Wind-blown sand smooths a rock face. (b) Rainwater
   dissolves a limestone statue's details. (c) A glacier drags rocks
   across bedrock, scratching it. (d) A river slows entering a lake and
   drops silt. [Ask two questions per item: did the material *move*?
   Did its *chemistry* change?]
2. A river carries gravel easily during a flood but drops it when the
   flood ends. Explain using energy: what changed, and why does the
   gravel drop *first* rather than last? [What does "flood ends" do to
   water volume and speed? Which grains need the most energy?]
3. **Observation activity — sketch and label a river valley.** In the
   notebook, draw the valley from Example 2 from words alone: a wide
   flat valley with one clear bend, a narrow rocky reach with a
   waterfall upstream. Label: (a) the eroding bank, (b) the depositing
   bank, (c) the upstream erosion zone, (d) one weathering feature on
   the exposed rock. Under the sketch, write two sentences explaining
   why the same river erodes in one place and deposits in another. The
   drawing may be rough — the labels and the reasoning are what count.
4. A student says a delta and a beach are "both just piles of sand, so
   they form the same way." What is right and what is wrong in that
   claim? [Both *are* depositional — but what is the depositing agent,
   and where does each one's energy drop?]

## Application

A (fictional) town sits on a wide flat valley floor beside a bending
river. The town council is choosing between two sites for a new school:
Site A on the steep undercut bank outside a bend, Site B on the gentle
sandy slope inside the same bend, 200 meters downstream.

a) Using Example 2's reasoning, which site is at greater risk from
   erosion over the next 50 years, and why?
b) The council notes the whole valley floor is flat layered silt.
   What does that tell them about this land's history — was it built
   by erosion or deposition, and during what kind of events?
c) Name one weathering process likely acting on the exposed rock of
   the steep bank today, and say whether it is physical or chemical.

## Exit check

1. A hillside rock cracks wider each winter as water in the crack
   freezes. Weathering or erosion — and physical or chemical? One
   sentence of reasoning.
2. In one sentence, explain why the *same* river can erode upstream and
   deposit downstream.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** The three jobs (weathering = break in
  place, erosion = move, deposition = drop) go in the notebook with a
  sketch for each; definitions may be dictated to the adult. The reading
  passage may be read aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** The observation activity (Guided
  practice 3) may be done as a spoken description instead of a drawing:
  the learner describes the valley aloud, pointing to where each label
  would go, and the adult checks the four labels against the
  description.
- **Extension:** After a real rain (with the adult), observe a gutter,
  ditch, or driveway runoff: sketch where water ran fastest, where it
  dropped sand or leaves, and where it is still carrying material.
  Label one erosion spot and one deposition spot and write two
  sentences connecting what you saw to slope and water volume.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) **weathering vs. erosion** — learners merge
them into "wearing away"; enforce the movement test (did material move?
no → weathering; yes → erosion) on every classification until it is
reflex; (2) **"rocks melt"** — heat-softening language sneaks in from
everyday speech; weathering breaks rock down (freeze–thaw, dissolution,
oxidation) but melting is a different process at far higher
temperatures — correct the word every time; (3) **deposition as
"the opposite of erosion"** rather than its consequence — stress the
energy story: deposition happens *because* erosion's transport lost
energy, which is why coarse grains drop first; (4) **the river as two
rivers** — learners treat "eroding river" and "depositing river" as
different rivers; the upstream/downstream comparison in Example 2 and
Application (b) exist to force the one-river-two-energy-zones model.
On the arithmetic: the stream-table differences (26 cm; 32 cm) are
subtractions, but make learners write the claim *and* its limit —
data literacy is the load-bearing skill here, and "two points show a
direction, not a formula" is the sentence to rehearse.
