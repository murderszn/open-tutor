# Lesson 03 — Topographic Maps and Local Relief

**Unit 02 · Session S11 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards (planning reference only, not a claim of state adoption or alignment):** HS-ESS2-1 (Earth's systems and surface processes)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can read a topographic map: I can find a
contour interval, use index contours to work out elevations, apply the
rule of V's to a stream valley, compare steep and gentle slopes from
contour spacing, calculate a gradient with correct unit conversions,
and sketch a topographic profile from a described contour pattern.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, ruler. No printed map is required: every map in this
lesson is described fully in words, so the lesson works with nothing
but this text and a notebook.

## Explicit teaching

**What a topographic map shows.** A topographic map is a flat drawing
of a three-dimensional landscape. It shows the shape of the land using
**contour lines**: lines that connect points of equal elevation above
sea level. If you walked along a single contour line, you would stay
at exactly the same height the whole way — you would neither climb
nor descend. Every point on the 1,000-foot contour line, for example,
is 1,000 feet above sea level.

**Contour interval and index contours.** The **contour interval** is
the elevation difference between one contour line and the next one.
It is printed on every real map, and it is the same everywhere on that
map. If the contour interval is 20 feet, then moving from one contour
line to the next one uphill means you have climbed 20 feet, and
crossing five lines uphill means you have climbed 100 feet.

Some contour lines are drawn darker and bolder than the rest, and they
carry a number giving their elevation. These are **index contours**.
They usually appear every fifth line. On a map with a 20-foot interval,
the index contours might read 1,000 feet, 1,100 feet, 1,200 feet, and
so on. Index contours are your anchors: count ordinary lines from the
nearest index contour, multiply by the interval, and you have the
elevation of any line.

**Three reading rules that never change.**

1. Contour lines never cross or split (except at a vertical cliff,
   where they merge into a single heavy line). Each line is one
   elevation, so two different elevations cannot occupy the same spot.
2. Closely spaced contour lines mean a steep slope: a lot of elevation
   change happens over a short horizontal distance. Widely spaced
   contour lines mean a gentle slope: the same elevation change is
   spread over a long distance.
3. Closed, roughly circular contour lines with higher values toward
   the center mark a hill. Closed contours with lower values toward
   the center mark a depression (a hollow or pit). On many maps a
   depression is flagged with short tick marks (hachures) pointing
   inward, toward the lower ground.

**The rule of V's.** Where a stream valley crosses contour lines, the
lines bend into a V shape. The point of the V always points
**upstream** — toward higher ground, toward the source of the stream.
The open end of the V points downstream, the direction the water
flows. This is a powerful check: if you know which way a stream flows,
the V's confirm it, and if you know the V's, you can work out the flow
direction. The V points uphill because the valley floor is lower than
the valley sides at the same distance along the stream, so the contour
line has to reach upstream to find ground at its elevation.

**Local relief.** **Relief** is the difference in elevation between
the highest and lowest points in an area. **Local relief** is that
difference for one small, defined area — one hill, one map sheet, one
valley. It is a single subtraction: highest elevation minus lowest
elevation. A flat plain has small local relief; a mountain valley has
large local relief. Relief tells you how rugged a place is, but not
how steep any one slope is — that is what gradient is for.

**Gradient.** The **gradient** of a slope is how much elevation changes
per unit of horizontal distance:

> gradient = elevation change / horizontal distance

Both numbers must be in the same unit before you divide. A gradient
can be left as a plain ratio (a decimal), turned into a percentage by
multiplying by 100, or restated as "feet of drop per 1,000 feet of
distance" by multiplying by 1,000. All three say the same thing. A
gradient of 0.076, 7.6%, and 76 feet per 1,000 feet are one slope
described three ways.

**Drawing a topographic profile.** A profile is a side view of the
land along a straight line drawn across the map. To build one: mark
where your line crosses each contour, note each crossing's elevation,
then plot those elevations on a graph whose horizontal axis is
distance along the line and whose vertical axis is elevation, and join
the points with a smooth curve. Hills become humps, valleys become
dips, flat ground becomes a level stretch. A profile turns the map's
code back into the shape your eyes would see standing on the ground.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Gradient with full unit conversion.**

Question: A slope drops 200 feet over a horizontal distance of
0.5 miles. What is the gradient? Give it as a decimal, a percentage,
and feet of drop per 1,000 feet.

*Reasoning.* The two measurements are in different units — feet and
miles — so convert first. The conversion factor is 1 mile = 5,280
feet. Therefore:

0.5 miles × 5,280 feet per mile = 2,640 feet.

Now both numbers are in feet, so divide elevation change by
horizontal distance:

gradient = 200 feet / 2,640 feet = 0.075757… ≈ 0.076.

(The feet cancel, so the gradient has no unit — it is a pure ratio.)

As a percentage: 0.075757… × 100 = 7.5757…% ≈ 7.6%.

As feet per 1,000 feet: 0.075757… × 1,000 = 75.757… ≈ 76 feet of drop
per 1,000 feet of horizontal distance.

Answer: **gradient ≈ 0.076, about 7.6%, or about 76 feet per
1,000 feet.** The most common error here is dividing 200 by 0.5
without converting, which gives 400 — a meaningless number, because
it mixes feet and miles. Convert first, divide second, every time.

**Example 2 — Contour-interval reasoning.**

Question: A map has a contour interval of 40 feet. Two points on the
map are separated by 5 contour intervals. How much do they differ in
elevation?

*Reasoning.* Each interval crossed is 40 feet of elevation change,
and the intervals add up:

5 intervals × 40 feet per interval = 200 feet.

Answer: **the two points differ by 200 feet in elevation** — the
higher point is 200 feet above the lower one. This assumes no
hilltop or depression contour intervenes between the two points: if
the ground rises to a summit and falls again between them, the
counted intervals would overstate the straight elevation difference,
because some intervals would be crossed twice (once going up, once
coming down). Count intervals along the ground you are actually
measuring, and watch for closed contours that signal a summit or a
pit in between.

**Example 3 — Reading slope and a stream from spacing and V's.**

Question: On one map, the east side of a hill has contour lines
packed tightly together, while the west side has the same contour
lines spread far apart. A stream valley on the north side shows V's
whose points aim north. Which side is steeper, and which way does the
stream flow?

*Reasoning.* Tight spacing means much elevation change over little
distance, so the east side is the steep side and the west side is
gentle. For the stream, the rule of V's says the points aim upstream,
toward higher ground — so upstream is north, and the stream flows
the opposite way, toward the south, out of the open ends of the V's.

Answer: **the east side is steeper; the stream flows south.**

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult nearby; hints are in brackets.

1. A map has a contour interval of 20 feet. An index contour reads
   1,000 feet. What is the elevation of the third contour line above
   that index contour? [Each line adds one interval.]
2. A slope drops 160 feet over a horizontal distance of 0.25 miles.
   Convert the distance to feet, then give the gradient as a decimal
   and as a percentage. [1 mile = 5,280 feet; 0.25 × 5,280 = ?]
3. The highest point in a small map area is 1,240 feet and the lowest
   point is 860 feet. What is the local relief? [One subtraction.]
4. A valley's contour V's point east. A hiker claims the stream flows
   east. Is the hiker right? Explain in one sentence. [Where does the
   point of the V aim?]

## Independent practice

Complete in the notebook; the adult scores same-day.

1. In your own words, define contour interval and index contour, and
   explain how the two work together to give the elevation of an
   unnumbered line. (Two to three sentences each.)
2. A gentle slope drops 40 feet over a horizontal distance of 0.5
   miles. Convert, then state the gradient as a decimal, a percentage,
   and feet per 1,000 feet. Compare it with Example 1's slope: which
   is steeper, and by roughly how many times?
3. A map has a contour interval of 10 feet. Point A sits on the
   500-foot index contour. Point B is four intervals uphill from A,
   with no summit or depression between them. What is B's elevation?
   Show the multiplication.
4. Error analysis: a classmate counts 6 contour intervals on a map
   with a 20-foot interval and reports an elevation difference of
   120 feet, but the two points are on opposite sides of a hilltop.
   Explain what is wrong with the classmate's number — is the true
   elevation difference larger, smaller, or impossible to tell from
   the count alone? Justify your answer.
5. Sketch a topographic profile from this fully described contour
   pattern (no image needed — everything is in the description).
   A straight transect runs west to east across a single hill. The
   map's contour interval is 20 feet. From west to east, the transect
   crosses: a stream valley floor at 960 feet; then contour lines at
   980 feet and the 1,000-foot index contour; then 1,020, 1,040,
   1,060, and 1,080 feet; then a rounded summit that lies above the
   1,080-foot contour but below the next contour, 1,100 feet; then,
   descending the east side, 1,080, 1,060, 1,040, 1,020 feet, the
   1,000-foot index contour, and 980 feet, ending at 940 feet on a
   low plain. On the west side of the hill, a stream valley cuts
   across the slope, and its contour V's point east (upstream).
   (a) Draw the profile: horizontal axis = distance west to east,
   vertical axis = elevation from 900 to 1,100 feet, plotting each
   contour crossing and the summit as a smooth hump. (b) Label the
   summit's possible elevation range. (c) In one sentence, state
   which way the west-side stream flows and how the V's tell you.
   (d) The west side's contour lines are spaced farther apart than
   the east side's. Which side is steeper?

## Application

A trail crew is choosing between two routes up the same hill, and
they have only a topographic map (contour interval 20 feet) to decide
with. Route North crosses 8 contour intervals over a horizontal
distance of 0.5 miles. Route South crosses 8 contour intervals over
a horizontal distance of 2 miles.

a) How much elevation does each route gain? Show the multiplication.
b) Convert each horizontal distance to feet and calculate each
   route's gradient as a percentage.
c) Which route would you recommend for a trail meant to be walked by
   families with small children, and why? Use both the gradient
   numbers and what contour spacing would look like on the map to
   justify your recommendation in two to three sentences.
d) A stream crosses Route South, and its contour V's point uphill
   along the route. What does that tell the crew about the stream's
   flow direction relative to a hiker walking uphill, and why does it
   matter for placing a footbridge?

## Exit check

1. State the rule of V's in one sentence.
2. A slope drops 60 feet over 1,200 feet of horizontal distance.
   What is its gradient as a percentage? Show the division.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** The terms contour interval, index
  contour, relief, and gradient go in the notebook with a sketch and
  a one-line example for each; definitions may be dictated to the
  adult.
- **Alternate response mode:** The profile in Independent practice
  question 5 may be built with a strip of paper marked at each contour
  crossing and held against a hand-drawn elevation scale, instead of
  plotted on graph paper; the learner points to each mark and states
  its elevation aloud.
- **Extension:** Find a real topographic map of a familiar place
  (with the adult, from a public mapping source) and identify its
  contour interval, one index contour, the steepest slope visible,
  and one stream valley where the rule of V's holds. Report the
  interval and the steepest slope's gradient if a distance scale is
  available.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) dividing feet by miles without converting
— insist on "convert first, divide second" and on checking that the
units cancel; (2) reading contour spacing backwards, treating widely
spaced lines as steep because the slope "looks bigger" on paper;
(3) pointing the V downstream, with the flow — the V points
upstream, against the flow, and this is worth drilling with a
finger-trace on every valley; (4) treating a hilltop's elevation as
exactly the highest closed contour — it is higher than that contour
but lower than the next one, so it is a range, not a number;
(5) confusing relief (a difference between two points) with gradient
(a rate along one slope). This lesson connects back to Unit 01's
quantitative modeling: every gradient here is a rate with units that
must cancel, and every elevation difference carries the assumption
stated in Example 2.
