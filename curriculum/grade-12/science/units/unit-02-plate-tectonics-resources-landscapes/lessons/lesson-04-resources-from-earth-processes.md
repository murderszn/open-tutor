# Lesson 04 — Where Resources Come From: How Earth Processes Distribute Them

**Unit 02 · Session S12 · ~50 minutes**
**Standards (planning reference only, not a claim of state adoption or alignment):** HS-ESS3-1 (Earth resources and human activity)

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can explain why Earth's useful resources
are unevenly distributed — linking metal ores, oil and gas,
geothermal energy, groundwater, and fertile soils to the specific
Earth processes that create them — and I can weigh a resource
decision by comparing what it offers against its extraction costs
and environmental tradeoffs, using stated criteria.

## Materials

Notebook, pencil, ruler. No other materials are required.

## Explicit teaching

**Resources are not spread evenly, and that is the point.** People
often talk as if the Earth were a warehouse with its goods shelved at
random. It is not. A resource exists in a place because a particular
process ran there, for long enough, under the right conditions — and
that process does not run everywhere. Find the process, and you have
a first guess about where to look. That is why resource maps and
plate-boundary maps look so alike: the boundaries are where the
processes are most active.

**Metal ores near convergent margins.** When an oceanic plate sinks
beneath another plate at a convergent margin, water carried down with
it helps melt the rock above. That magma rises, cools slowly
underground, and releases hot, mineral-rich water. As the water cools
in cracks in the surrounding rock, metals dissolved in it settle out
as ore minerals. Copper deposits of the **porphyry** type — large,
low-grade bodies of copper-bearing rock, named for the speckled
texture of the rock that hosts them — form exactly this way, in the
roots of volcanic mountain chains above sinking plates. The copper
was always present in trace amounts in ordinary rock; the convergent
margin concentrated it, by moving huge volumes of hot water through a
small volume of rock over a long time, until the concentration became
worth mining. Take away the sinking plate and the hot water, and the
same copper stays scattered and useless.

**Oil and gas in sedimentary basins.** Oil and gas do not form at
plate boundaries directly. They form in **sedimentary basins**: broad
low areas where layers of sediment pile up over millions of years.
Buried organic matter — mostly the remains of tiny marine organisms —
is cooked by the Earth's heat as it sinks deeper, turning first into
a waxy substance and then into oil and gas. But oil and gas alone are
not a resource: they are fluids, and fluids migrate upward through
porous rock until something stops them. A usable deposit needs three
things together: a **source rock** where the oil formed, a porous
**reservoir rock** it can collect in, and a **trap** — a folded layer
or a fault sealed by an impermeable **cap rock** — that holds it in
place. Basins that never got the right burial depth, or that lost
their traps to later faulting, hold nothing, however much organic
matter they once contained. That combination of conditions is why
oil-rich regions are patchy, not universal.

**Geothermal energy at divergent boundaries and hot spots.** Where
plates pull apart at a divergent boundary, or where a hot spot feeds
magma close to the surface, the crust is thin and hot. Water pumped
down into that hot rock returns as steam, and the steam can drive a
turbine to make electricity, or heat buildings directly. The resource
here is not a substance to dig up but a temperature difference: hot
rock near the surface. It is strongest where the Earth itself brings
the heat upward, which is why geothermal power clusters along
spreading ridges, rift valleys, and volcanic island chains, and is
weak where the crust is thick, old, and cold.

**Groundwater in porous aquifers.** Rain and snowmelt soak into the
ground wherever the surface rock lets water through. An **aquifer**
is a body of rock or sediment that both holds water in its pore
spaces (**porosity**: how much empty space the rock contains) and
lets water move through it (**permeability**: how well those spaces
connect). Sand and gravel make good aquifers; solid granite and
tight clay generally do not, even though clay can hold water — it
will not give it up to a well. Aquifers refill, or **recharge**, only
as fast as water arrives from above, and only where the surface
allows it in. Pump faster than recharge, and water levels fall,
wells run dry, and the ground can even sink as pore spaces collapse.
Groundwater is therefore a resource with a budget, not a tank that
refills on demand.

**Fertile volcanic soils.** Volcanic ash and lava break down into
soils rich in minerals plants need — potassium, phosphorus, calcium,
and magnesium among them — and the fine ash particles hold water and
nutrients well. That is why some of the most productive farmland on
Earth sits on the flanks of volcanoes, and why farming communities
have lived beside active volcanoes for centuries. The fertility is a
gift of the same process that makes the volcano dangerous, which is
the subject of Lesson 05.

**A cost-benefit frame without taking sides.** Every resource
decision can be laid out the same way, whatever you conclude about
it. Ask four questions: (1) What does this resource offer — what
material, energy, water, or food, and how much of it? (2) What does
extraction cost — money, energy, water, and labor? (3) What are the
environmental tradeoffs — land disturbed, waste produced, water or
air affected, habitats changed, and for how long? (4) Who gains and
who bears the costs — are they the same people? A decision that
looks good on question 1 alone can look different once questions 2
through 4 are answered honestly. This lesson does not tell you which
answer to reach; it asks you to show your criteria and apply them
consistently, so that someone who disagrees can see exactly where
your reasoning differs from theirs.

## Worked examples (modeled reasoning)

**Example 1 — Ranking three candidate mine sites (fictional practice data).**

Question: Three candidate copper sites have been surveyed. All
figures below are fictional practice data, invented for this lesson;
they describe no real mine.

| Site | Ore grade (% copper) | Depth to ore | Distance to water | One environmental constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Site A | 0.8% | 150 m | 2 km | Lies inside a protected watershed: no mine wastewater may be discharged there |
| Site B | 0.4% | 40 m | 12 km | Next to a farming community: dust, noise, and competition for water affect residents |
| Site C | 1.2% | 400 m | 1 km | Steep, unstable slope: landslide risk during and after mining |

Rank the sites and justify the ranking with stated criteria.

*Reasoning.* Start by stating criteria before ranking, so the ranking
can be checked. Suppose the criteria are: (a) economic promise
(grade and depth — richer and shallower is cheaper to mine),
(b) water access, and (c) severity of the environmental constraint.
On grade alone, C beats A beats B (1.2% > 0.8% > 0.4%). On depth,
B is far cheapest to reach (40 m), A is moderate (150 m), and C is
deep and expensive (400 m). On water, C and A are close (1 km and
2 km) while B is far (12 km). On constraints, each site carries a
different kind of problem, not a different size of the same problem:
A's watershed rule may block the project outright no matter how good
the ore is; B's problem is borne by neighbors who do not own the
mine; C's problem threatens the mine itself and anyone downhill.

One reasoned ranking: **A first, C second, B third** — A balances
decent grade, moderate depth, and close water, but only if the
watershed problem can be solved (for example, by treating and
reusing all water on site, with nothing discharged); C has the
richest ore but its depth and landslide risk make it costly and
risky; B is cheapest to dig but poorest in grade, farthest from
water, and pushes its costs onto a farming community.

Notice what this ranking is and is not. It is not the "right" answer:
a decision-maker who weights the watershed rule as an absolute bar
would rank A last, and one who weights landslide danger above all
else would rank C last. What makes a ranking good is not matching a
hidden answer — it is stating criteria, applying them to every site,
and saying plainly which constraint could change the result. A
ranking that hides its criteria cannot be argued with, and therefore
cannot be trusted.

**Example 2 — A groundwater budget check (fictional practice data).**

Question: A small farming valley's aquifer recharges at 120 units
per year and farmers pump 150 units per year. (Here "units" means
arbitrary volume units for practice — think of one unit as one
truckload of water, if that helps. The numbers are fictional practice
data, not measurements from a real aquifer.) Is the aquifer in
balance? What happens if these rates hold for 10 years?

*Reasoning.* A budget is a subtraction: what comes in minus what goes
out. Net change = recharge − pumping = 120 units per year −
150 units per year = −30 units per year. The minus sign is the whole
story: every year the aquifer loses 30 more units than it gains.

Over 10 years at the same rates: −30 units per year × 10 years =
−300 units. If the aquifer held, say, 1,000 units of extractable
water at the start (another fictional figure), it would hold about
700 units after 10 years — and wells that draw from its upper part
would start to fail well before it reached zero, because water
levels, not just total volume, are what a well needs. The fix is
also visible in the arithmetic: either pump less (cut pumping to
120 units per year to break even, or to 100 units per year to gain
20 units per year back), or increase recharge, or both. An aquifer
is sustainable only when the subtraction comes out at zero or
better, year after year — not when one wet year happens to cover a
dry decade.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult nearby; hints are in brackets.

1. A porphyry copper deposit sits in a mountain range above a sinking
   oceanic plate. Name the process that concentrated the copper, and
   explain in one sentence why the same copper would not be worth
   mining if it had stayed scattered through ordinary rock. [Think:
   what did the hot water do?]
2. An oil company finds a thick layer of organic-rich shale in a
   basin, but no oil pool. Name two of the three things (besides the
   source rock) that a usable deposit needs, and explain which one
   might be missing. [Reservoir, trap, cap rock.]
3. Using Example 2's aquifer: if farmers cut pumping to 100 units per
   year while recharge stays at 120 units per year, what is the net
   change per year, and how much would the aquifer gain over 5
   years? Show both calculations. [Net = in − out; then multiply.]
4. A town wants geothermal power but sits on thick, old, cold crust
   far from any plate boundary or hot spot. Using this lesson's
   reasoning, explain why geothermal is a weak option there, and
   name one resource from this lesson that might fit the town
   better if it has porous sandstone underground. [What does
   geothermal need that this town lacks?]

## Independent practice

Complete in the notebook; the adult scores same-day.

1. In your own words, explain why metal ores cluster near convergent
   margins while geothermal energy clusters at divergent boundaries

## Application

A fictional county council must choose between two proposals, and
has asked for a comparison, not a verdict. Proposal 1: open a small
copper mine at a site like Example 1's Site B — shallow ore (40 m),
low grade (0.4%), 12 km from water, beside a farming community.
Proposal 2: build a geothermal plant at a hot spot site — hot rock
close to the surface, steady output, but high upfront drilling cost
and a small land footprint near a scenic valley.

a) For each proposal, list what it offers and what extraction or
   construction costs, using the four questions from the lesson.
b) Compare the two proposals on two criteria: (i) how long the
   benefit lasts (a mine depletes its ore; a geothermal field can
   run for decades if managed within its heat budget), and (ii) who
   bears the main burden in each case. Write one paragraph per
   criterion.
c) State which proposal you would recommend to the council and why,
   naming the criterion that mattered most to you — and name one
   fact that, if it turned out differently, would change your
   recommendation. (There is no single correct recommendation;
   the reasoning and the stated condition are what are assessed.)

## Exit check

1. Give one resource and the Earth process that concentrates or
   creates it, in a single sentence.
2. An aquifer recharges at 90 units per year and is pumped at
   90 units per year. Is it in balance? What if pumping rises to
   110 units per year — what is the net change?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** The terms porphyry, reservoir rock,
  trap, cap rock, aquifer, porosity, and permeability go in the
  notebook with a sketch and a one-line example for each;
  definitions may be dictated to the adult.
- **Alternate response mode:** The ranking in Example 1 and the
  Application may be presented as a spoken argument with a simple
  table on paper (one row per site or proposal, one column per
  criterion), instead of a written essay; the learner points to
  each cell and explains it aloud.
- **Extension:** With the adult, look up one real resource region
  (for example, the copper country of the Andes or a geothermal
  field in Iceland) and write a paragraph: which plate setting is
  it in, and how does that setting explain the resource? Cite the
  source you used.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "resources are everywhere, we just have
not found them" — stress that concentration processes, not
exploration effort, decide where deposits exist; (2) confusing
porosity with permeability — a clay can be porous yet useless as an
aquifer because its pores do not connect; (3) treating grade as the
whole economics of a mine — depth, water distance, and constraints
routinely outweigh grade, as Example 1 shows; (4) reading the
cost-benefit frame as a verdict — the frame is a discipline for
comparing options, and learners who reach different conclusions
from the same table have used it correctly if their criteria are
stated and applied consistently. Keep the tone neutral throughout:
describe tradeoffs and who bears them, and let the learner's stated
criteria carry the judgment. This lesson's quantitative thread
continues Unit 01's modeling work: the water budget is a rate
balance with units that must be labeled, and the ore-tonnage
calculation is a percentage-of-a-total with the same unit discipline
as the gradient work in Lesson 03.
