## Lesson 01 — Plate boundaries and landforms

**Readiness check:** 1. Oceanic and continental crust; oceanic is
denser. 2. The oldest ocean floor (about 200 million years old, real
reference value) is subducted — it sinks back into the mantle at
convergent boundaries and is recycled.

**Guided practice:**
1. Ocean–ocean convergent. Both plates are oceanic, so the older,
   denser slab subducts beneath the younger one. Landforms: a
   deep-sea trench where the slab bends down, and an island arc of
   volcanoes parallel to the trench (slab water triggers melting).
2. Divergent. If it continues for tens of millions of years the rift
   floods and becomes a new ocean basin — this is how Unit 01's new
   ocean basins start.
3. Disagree. Continent–continent collision has no subducting oceanic
   slab, so there is no slab water to trigger arc melting — the
   mountains are built by crumpling and folding, not by eruption.
4. 2.5 cm/yr × 500,000 yr = 1,250,000 cm. 1,250,000 ÷ 100,000 =
   12.5 km.

**Independent practice:**
1. Divergent → mid-ocean ridge, rift valley. Ocean–ocean convergent
   → island arc, deep-sea trench. Ocean–continent convergent →
   deep-sea trench (offshore), coastal volcanic range. (Trench
   belongs to both ocean-involving convergent types.) Continent–
   continent convergent → folded mountain range, high plateau,
   thickened crustal root. Transform → offset stream channels (and
   roads/ridges), fault system.
2. Elapsed: 2025 − 1900 = 125 yr. 125 yr × 3.5 cm/yr = 437.5 cm.
   437.5 cm ÷ 100 = 4.375 m.
3. Ocean–ocean convergent. The volcanoes sit parallel to the trench,
   not inside it, because magma rises above the point where the
   sinking slab reaches melting depth — inland of (behind) the
   trench, not at the trench itself.
4. Two errors: (a) transform boundaries are conservative — crust is
   neither created nor destroyed there, only ground sideways;
   (b) boundary volcanoes come from magma generated above a
   subducting slab (or at divergent rifts), not from "recycling" in
   general. Correct claim: transform boundaries produce earthquakes
   and offset features, not volcanoes.

**Application:** (a) Ocean–continent convergent; the oceanic plate
subducts because it is denser than the continental plate. (b) No —
a new ocean basin requires divergent motion (plates moving apart);
this boundary converges, so the motion test fails. (c) 2.5 cm/yr ×
40,000,000 yr = 100,000,000 cm; 100,000,000 ÷ 100,000 = 1,000 km.

**Exit check:** 1. Divergent boundaries build volcanoes without
subduction (mid-ocean ridges, rift volcanoes); continent–continent
convergence builds folded mountains without volcanoes. 2. 2.5 cm/yr
× 4,000,000 yr = 10,000,000 cm = 100 km.

## Lesson 02 — Weathering, erosion, deposition

**Readiness check:** 1. Any real wearing-down process, e.g. rain
washing soil, frost cracking pavement, a river carrying pebbles.
2. The grain of sand — smaller and lighter grains need less energy
to move, so the same current carries them farther.

**Guided practice:**
1. (a) Physical weathering — abrasion; the rock is worn in place,
   chemistry unchanged. (b) Chemical weathering — dissolution; the
   mineral dissolves, changing composition. (c) Erosion — the
   glacier picks up and moves rock (movement test: yes). (d)
   Deposition — the slowing river drops its load in a new place.
2. When the flood ends, water volume and speed fall, so the river's
   energy falls. Gravel drops first because the largest, heaviest
   grains need the most energy to stay moving — when energy drops,
   they are the first the water can no longer carry.
3. Sketch check: (a) steep undercut bank labeled on the outside of
   the bend; (b) gentle sandy bar on the inside; (c) narrow rocky
   reach with waterfall labeled upstream erosion zone; (d) one
   weathering feature on the exposed rock (e.g. cracked/frost-wedged
   blocks, rust staining). Two sentences must state the energy
   reason: fast water upstream and on outer bends has energy to
   erode; slow water on inner bends and downstream lacks energy and
   deposits.
4. Right: both are depositional piles of sorted sediment. Wrong:
   they form differently — a delta forms where a river slows at a
   lake or sea mouth; a beach forms where waves drop sand along a
   shore. Different agents, different energy-drop settings.

**Image observation:** 1. Eroding bank: the steep undercut bank on
the outside of the bend — fastest water there. Depositing bank: the
gentle sandy beach on the inside of the bend — slowest water drops
its load. 2. Eroding: the layered cliffs show rock stripped bare
by downward cutting, and the waterfall marks where the river meets
resistant rock and pours over rather than cutting evenly. 3. Built
by deposition — the flat layered silt accumulated flood by flood,
each flood spreading, slowing, and dropping fine sediment. 4. A
convergent boundary (subduction) can put an arc volcano on the
horizon; it adds ashfall and lahar/flood hazards for the farms,
reaching far beyond the volcano itself.

**Independent practice:**
1. Definitions in the learner's own words: weathering = breaking
   rock down where it sits; erosion = picking up and moving the
   pieces; deposition = dropping them somewhere new. Original
   examples required (accept any that fit the definitions).
2. 41 cm − 9 cm = 32 cm farther for fine sand. Claim: with slope
   and water volume held constant, finer material travels farther.
   Limit: one material pair, one slope, one volume — a direction,
   not a formula.
3. (a) Erosion on the left (steep undercut bank); deposition on
   the right (gentle sandy slope). (b) Slow water — fine silt
   settles only when the water lacks the energy to carry it; fast
   water would keep silt moving and leave coarser material behind.
4. Errors: "melted" (heat-softening language — the rock was broken
   down, not melted) and the process assignment (weathering does
   not move material). Rewrite: "Freeze–thaw weathered the rock,
   breaking off fragments, and erosion carried the fragments
   downhill."

**Application:** (a) Site A — the steep undercut bank outside the
bend, where the fastest water keeps chewing material from the
bank's base. (b) The flat layered silt floor was built by
deposition, during repeated floods that spilled over the banks,
slowed, and dropped fine sediment layer by layer. (c) Any one:
freeze–thaw or root wedging (physical), or dissolution/oxidation
(chemical) on the exposed rock.

**Exit check:** 1. Weathering — physical (freeze–thaw); the rock
breaks in place with no chemistry change and no movement. 2. The
river's energy changes along its course: steep upstream reaches
have fast, high-energy water that erodes, while flat downstream
reaches have slow water that cannot carry its load and deposits it.

**Guided practice:**

2. 0.25 mi = 1,320 ft. 160 ÷ 1,320 = 0.1212… ≈ 0.12; × 100 =
   12.1%.
3. 1,240 − 860 = 380 ft.
4. No — the point of the V aims upstream, so upstream is east and

**Independent practice:**

   contour lines, constant across one map. Index contour: the
   darker, numbered every-fifth line. Together: read the nearest

3. 500 ft + 4 × 10 ft = 540 ft.
4. The true elevation difference is smaller than 120 ft. Counting
   intervals across a hilltop counts some intervals twice (up one
   side, down the other), so the counted 120 ft overstates the
   straight difference between the two points.
5. (a) Profile: smooth hump peaking between 1,080 and 1,100 ft,
   falling to 960 ft at the west stream and 940 ft on the east
   plain. (b) Summit elevation: above 1,080 ft and below 1,100 ft.
   (c) The stream flows west — the V's point east, and V's point
   upstream, so upstream is east. (d) The east side is steeper
   (tighter contour spacing).

**Application:** (a) Each route gains 8 × 20 ft = 160 ft.
(b) North: 0.5 mi = 2,640 ft; 160 ÷ 2,640 = 0.0606… ≈ 6.1%.
South: 2 mi = 10,560 ft; 160 ÷ 10,560 = 0.01515… ≈ 1.5%.
(c) Route South — about one-quarter the gradient, and its contours
would be spaced about four times farther apart (gentler on the
map). (d) The V's point uphill along the route, so upstream is
uphill and the stream flows downhill — toward a hiker walking up.
The bridge must span the active channel where the crew can see the

**Exit check:** 1. Where a stream valley crosses contours, the

## Lesson 04 — Resources from Earth processes

**Readiness check:** 1. Convergent: one plate sinks, water from the
slab helps melt rock above. Divergent: plates pull apart, magma
rises, new crust forms. Hot spot: a mantle plume brings heat close
to the surface far from plate edges. 2. Open — the lesson tests it:
resources depend on processes that run only in some places.

**Guided practice:**
1. Hot, mineral-rich water released from cooling magma above the
   sinking slab carried dissolved copper through cracks and dropped
   it as ore minerals as it cooled. Scattered trace copper is not
   worth mining because mining needs concentration — the process
   collected diffuse copper into one rich body.
2. Two of: a porous reservoir rock and a trap sealed by cap rock.
   The trap (or its cap rock) is likely missing — without a seal,
   the oil migrates upward and escapes instead of pooling.
3. Net = 120 − 100 = +20 units per year. Over 5 years: +20 × 5 =
   +100 units gained.
4. Geothermal needs thin, hot crust near the surface; this town's
   crust is thick, old, and cold, so the temperature difference is
   weak. A better fit: groundwater — porous sandstone underground
   can form an aquifer if it is permeable and recharged.

**Independent practice:**
1. Metal ores: at convergent margins, water from the sinking slab
   helps generate magma; cooling magma releases hot water that
   carries and concentrates metals in cracks. Geothermal: at
   divergent boundaries and hot spots the crust is thin and heat is
   near the surface, so the resource is the temperature difference
   itself — no concentration of a substance needed.
2. The fault broke the cap rock, so the trap no longer seals.
   Oil, being a fluid, migrates upward through the porous
   reservoir rock and escapes instead of pooling — two ingredients
   without a working trap leave no usable deposit.
3. Deposit X: 2,000,000 × 0.008 = 16,000 tonnes of copper.
   Deposit Y: 1,000,000 × 0.012 = 12,000 tonnes of copper. Deposit
   X holds more total copper — tonnage can outweigh grade, so grade
   alone does not decide which deposit is bigger.
4. Table (neutral language required): Offers — heat/electricity
   from Earth's interior, steady output where available. Costs —
   high upfront drilling cost; needs suitable geology. Tradeoffs —
   small land footprint; possible induced seismicity and
   groundwater effects; steam/gas handling. Who gains/bears — nearby
   communities gain power and jobs; drillers and nearby residents
   bear construction disruption and any subsurface effects.
5. Error: rain is recharge, but water levels depend on the budget
   — recharge minus pumping. Example (fictional, labeled):
   recharge 100 units/yr, pumping 140 units/yr → net −40 units/yr;
   levels fall even though it rained.

**Application:** Score the four-question frame per proposal
(complete and neutral), the two-criterion comparison (one paragraph
each: duration of benefit — depleting ore vs. a heat budget managed
over decades; who bears the burden — farming neighbors vs. scenic-
valley residents and ratepayers), and the recommendation (any
option accepted if it names the deciding criterion and one fact
that would change it).

**Exit check:** 1. Any one correct pair, e.g. "Porphyry copper is
concentrated by hot mineral-rich water above a sinking plate."
2. Yes, in balance (net 0). At 110 units/yr pumping: 90 − 110 =
   −20 units per year.

## Lesson 05 — Hazards and human settlement

**Readiness check:** 1. Volcano → lava/ash/lahar; sudden slip →
earthquake (and tsunami under the ocean); slope failure →
landslide; overflowing river → flood. 2. Open hook — any honest
answer with one decision-changing factor.

**Guided practice:**
1. Attraction: fertile volcanic soil (Lesson 04 — ash breaks down
   into mineral-rich soil). Hazard: eruption — ash, lava, or
   lahars from the same volcano.
2. It is mitigation (it reduces exposure by moving people before
   the event). No — it does not prevent the tsunami; it buys
   evacuation time.
3. People who live or work in the older buildings — the code only
   governs new construction, and retrofitting costs money owners
   may not have.
4. "About every 20 years" is an average, not a schedule. With the
   latest flood 18 years ago, the record says a flood is about due
   — not 20 years away.

**Independent practice:**
1. Definitions in the learner's own words: mitigation reduces the
   hazard or the exposure to it; adaptation changes how people live
   with the hazard that remains. One new example of each required
   (accept any that fit).
2. Paragraph must name at least two of: water and trade (the port),
   jobs (most residents employed there), cost (cheap land), cultural
   ties — applied to the coastal town, not just listed.
3. Two paragraphs (cost; who benefits/bears risk), one per
   criterion, following Example 2's structure; must state what
   each option leaves unresolved; must not declare a universal
   winner.
4. Two errors: (a) a levee protects only up to its design level —
   a bigger flood can overtop or breach it; (b) building behind a
   levee increases the damage when that happens, because more now
   stands in the flood's path.
5. (a) Drainage pipes = mitigation (they reduce the hazard by
   removing water from the slope); they cannot stop a slide if rain
   exceeds their capacity or if the slope fails for another reason.
   (b) Insurance = adaptation (it spreads the financial cost); it
   cannot prevent damage, protect people, or rebuild what was lost.

**Application:** (a) Two paragraphs in Example 1's style, from the
young family's viewpoint, citing at least two evidence items
(fertile soil, mill jobs, flood record, family ties, trade route).
(b) Comparison on cost and who benefits/bears risk, ending with a
recommendation that names the deciding criterion and one fact that
would change it. (c) One limit of the recommended option plus one
covering measure (e.g. levee → beyond-design flood covered by
evacuation planning; relocation → remaining residents covered by a
warning system or flood-proofing).

**Exit check:** 1. Any one: fertile soil, water and trade, jobs,
lower cost, or cultural and family ties — framed as a real reason,
not ignorance. 2. No — the volcano is exactly as dangerous; the
system only buys warning time for evacuation and shutdowns.
