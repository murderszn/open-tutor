# Lesson 01 — Scarcity and Opportunity Cost

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain what scarcity is and find the
opportunity cost — the best alternative I gave up — in any decision.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, this lesson
**Readiness check (3 min):** Write one decision you made this week where you
could not have both options. What did you pick, and what did you give up?

## 1. Explicit teaching

**Scarcity** means resources are limited while wants are not. Time is the
clearest example: you get 24 hours a day, no more, no matter what you want.
Money, land, labor, and equipment are the same story at larger scale — a
city has one budget, a factory has one shift of workers, a country has one
set of natural resources. Scarcity is not the same as poverty. A wealthy
person and a poor person both face scarcity: both have the same 24 hours,
and both must choose.

Because of scarcity, **choice is unavoidable**. Every "yes" to one thing is a
"no" to something else. Economists give that "no" a name: the **opportunity
cost** of a decision is the value of the **best alternative you gave up**.
Note the word *best*: if you give up three things to do one, the opportunity
cost is only the single most valuable thing you sacrificed — not all three
added together. That is the most common mistake beginners make.

### Worked example A — Maya's Saturday

Maya has a free Saturday afternoon. Her options:

- Work a 4-hour shift at the grocery store: earn $60.
- Help her neighbor move: the neighbor would pay her $40.
- Go to the park with friends: she values the fun at about $30 worth of
  enjoyment (this is a rough personal estimate — it does not have to be
  exact to be useful).

Maya chooses the grocery shift. Her opportunity cost is **$40** — the best
forgone alternative (helping the neighbor). It is NOT $40 + $30 = $70,
because she could never have done both of those things in the same
afternoon. Reasoning check: ask "what would she have done if the shift were
unavailable?" — the answer, helping the neighbor, is the opportunity cost.

### Worked example B — the city's budget vote

A city council has $2 million left in this year's budget and three proposals:

- Repave Main Street: engineers estimate $2 million of road-repair value.
- Build a splash pad at the community pool: estimated $1.6 million of
  recreation value.
- Fund after-school tutoring for a year: estimated $1.5 million of
  education value.

The council chooses repaving. The opportunity cost is the splash pad at
$1.6 million — the best alternative given up. Now imagine the council
chooses the tutoring program instead: the opportunity cost becomes the
repaving at $2 million, because *that* is now the best forgone option. The
opportunity cost always depends on what you actually chose. Reasoning check:
the two alternatives you did not take are not both the cost — only the best
one.

### The sunk-cost trap

Money, time, or effort you have already spent and cannot recover is a **sunk
cost** — and it should not change your decision. You paid $15 for a movie
ticket, the movie is terrible 20 minutes in, and staying costs you two more
hours. The $15 is gone either way; the real choice is between two more bad
hours and leaving. If you stay "because I paid for it," you are letting a
sunk cost decide instead of comparing your *current* options. Opportunity
cost looks forward (what will I give up next?); sunk costs look backward
(what did I already spend?). Only the forward-looking comparison matters.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

Work these with your adult or a partner before trying the independent set.

1. **Jamal's evening.** Jamal has two hours free. He could (a) do an extra
   shift task for his dad's shop worth $25, (b) study for a test (he thinks
   the studying is worth about $20 in future earnings... no — in grade
   value, roughly), or (c) play video games, worth about $10 of fun to him.
   He studies. What is the opportunity cost?
   *Hint: list the options he did NOT take, then pick the best one.*
2. **The wrong total.** A classmate says: "I chose the $25 task over the
   $20 study session and the $10 games, so my opportunity cost is $30."
   Explain the error in one or two sentences.
   *Hint: can you do both forgone things at once?*
3. **The concert ticket.** Priya bought a $50 concert ticket last month. The
   night of the concert she feels sick. Going means a miserable evening and
   possibly getting friends sick; staying home means resting. A friend says
   "you can't waste $50." What should Priya compare, and what role does the
   $50 play?
   *Hint: which costs are recoverable and which are not?*

## 3. Independent practice

4. **Your own scarcity.** Name one resource (time, money, space, attention)
   that is scarce for you this week. Describe two things competing for it
   and state which one you chose and the opportunity cost of your choice.
5. **Three-way choice.** A school club has $800 and one free Saturday. It
   can (a) run a car wash expected to raise $1,200, (b) host a bake sale
   expected to raise $900, or (c) volunteer at the food bank (members value
   the service at roughly $700 of satisfaction). The club picks the car wash.
   State the opportunity cost and show why it is not the sum of the other
   two.
6. **Sunk or not?** For each, say whether the past spending is a sunk cost
   and what the decision-maker should compare instead:
   - a. A family prepaid $300 for a beach rental; a storm is forecast all
     week. Go or stay home?
   - b. A student spent 3 hours on an essay draft, then learns the
     assignment changed. Keep polishing the old draft or start over?
7. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "There is no such thing as a free lunch."
   Explain what this saying means using *scarcity* and *opportunity cost* in
   your own words, with one original example. (Someone always pays — if not
   you, then the restaurant, the taxpayer, or the donor — and that payment
   had its own best alternative.)

## 4. Application

**The 24-hour audit.** Track tomorrow (or reconstruct yesterday) hour by
hour in a simple log: sleep, school, meals, work, phone, friends, and so on.
Pick the two hours you would most like to reallocate. For each, name what
you would do instead and state the opportunity cost honestly — including the
value of what you would give up. Write a 5–6 sentence reflection: what does
your log show about where your scarcest resource (your time) actually goes?

## 5. Exit check

Answer in your own words:

1. Define scarcity in one sentence and give one example not used in this
   lesson.
2. Define opportunity cost. Why is it the *best* forgone alternative rather
   than everything you gave up?
3. You choose option A ($100 value) over option B ($80) and option C ($60).
   What is the opportunity cost? What is it NOT?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** key terms are bolded on first use; say each
  term aloud and restate it in your own words before moving on. Home-language
  labels for the terms are welcome in your notebook.
- **Alternate response mode:** you may answer practice items orally to your
  adult, who records your exact words; the 24-hour audit may be a spoken
  walkthrough instead of a written log.
- **Extension:** research one real policy debate (adult-approved, current or
  historical) and identify the opportunity cost each side emphasizes. Whose
  "best alternative" is being left out of the argument? Write one paragraph.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) adding up all forgone alternatives as the cost —
re-teach with "you could never have done both"; (2) treating the chosen
option's own price as its opportunity cost; (3) calling any past expense a
reason to continue (sunk cost). If the learner struggles, return to Worked
example A and change the numbers. Answer-key reference: every item is solved
in [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 01 section.

*All dollar figures in this lesson are fictional practice data for learning
the reasoning — not real wages, prices, or budgets.*
