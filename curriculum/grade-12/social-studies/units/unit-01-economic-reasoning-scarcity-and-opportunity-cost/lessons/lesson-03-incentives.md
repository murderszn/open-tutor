# Lesson 03 — Incentives

**Goal (student-readable):** I can identify positive and negative incentives
in real situations and explain how a policy's incentives create different
costs and benefits for different groups.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, this lesson
**Readiness check (3 min):** Name one rule at your school or job that changed
what people do. Was it a reward, a punishment, or something else?

## 1. Explicit teaching

An **incentive** is anything that encourages or discourages a choice — a
reward for doing something or a penalty for doing it. **Positive incentives**
offer a gain (a discount, a bonus, a tax credit); **negative incentives**
impose a cost (a fine, a fee, a tax). Incentives work because people respond
to them: make an action cheaper or more rewarding and more people do it;
make it costlier and fewer do.

Incentives act on three kinds of decision-makers:

- **Consumers** respond to prices, discounts, rebates, and fees. A store
  coupon is a positive incentive to buy; a bag fee is a negative incentive
  to take a disposable bag.
- **Producers** (firms) respond to profits, subsidies, taxes, and
  regulations. A subsidy for installing equipment is a positive incentive to
  invest; a per-unit tax is a negative incentive to produce.
- **Policymakers** respond to votes, budgets, and rules too. A grant program
  is a positive incentive for a city to apply; a funding penalty is a
  negative incentive to miss a target.

### Worked example A — the litter fine

A city introduces a $50 fine for littering in parks.

- **Incentive:** negative — littering now costs $50 on top of the effort.
- **Predicted response:** less littering, because the action got more
  expensive. (The prediction is *ceteris paribus* — all else equal. If
  enforcement is zero, the real incentive is weaker than the written one.)
- **Who bears what:** park visitors who litter bear the cost; all park users
  gain cleaner parks; the city gains fine revenue but pays enforcement
  costs.
- **Reasoning check:** ask "who changes behavior, and what do they gain or
  lose?" If you cannot name the behavior change, you have not found the
  incentive yet.

### Worked example B — the solar tax credit

A government offers households a 30% tax credit for installing rooftop solar
panels (fictional rate for this example).

- **Incentive:** positive — installing solar now costs the household 30%
  less after the credit.
- **Predicted response:** more installations, especially by households that
  were close to deciding already.
- **Who bears what:** installing households gain lower energy bills;
  installers gain business; taxpayers who do not install bear the cost of
  the credit through the budget — a different group from the one that
  benefits. That split is the point of D2.Eco.1.9-12: **one policy's
  incentives routinely create different costs and benefits for different
  groups.**
- **Reasoning check:** a policy that "helps everyone" still has an
  opportunity cost — the credit's money could have funded something else
  (Lesson 01 returns).

### When incentives misfire

Incentives do not always work as intended, because people respond to the
*actual* incentive, not the intended one:

- A fine for late daycare pickups led some parents to treat it as a *price*
  for extra childcare — lateness rose. (The classic case: the penalty became
  a fee people were willing to pay.)
- Paying blood donors can *reduce* donations if it crowds out the
  good-feeling motive. The incentive changed the meaning of the act.
- A subsidy with no verification invites claims from people who would have
  acted anyway — the incentive pays for behavior that was already happening.

The lesson: always ask (1) what behavior the incentive rewards or punishes,
(2) whether the target audience can respond as hoped, and (3) who actually
pays. Good analysis predicts the response *and* checks it.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. **Name the incentive.** For each, say positive or negative, who it
   targets, and the predicted behavior change: (a) a $2 discount for
   bringing your own cup; (b) a $200 traffic ticket for running a red light;
   (c) a college scholarship for students with a 3.5 GPA.
   *Hint: who gains or loses what, and what will they do differently?*
2. **Split costs and benefits.** A city subsidizes bus fares for teenagers
   (fictional policy). List one group that gains, one group that bears a
   cost, and the opportunity cost of the subsidy money.
   *Hint: follow the money — who pays for the subsidy?*
3. **Spot the misfire risk.** A school offers $5 gift cards for every book
   read over summer. Name one way students might respond that the school did
   NOT intend. *Hint: what is the easiest way to collect the reward?*

## 3. Independent practice

4. **Three decision-makers.** Write one original example each of an
   incentive aimed at a consumer, a producer, and a policymaker. For each:
   positive or negative, the predicted behavior change, and one group that
   gains and one that bears a cost.
5. **Enforcement matters.** Two towns pass identical $100 litter fines. Town
   A hires park rangers who issue tickets daily; Town B issues none. Predict
   the littering outcome in each town and explain in 3–4 sentences why the
   written rule is not the real incentive.
6. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "If we want less of something, tax it; if we
   want more of it, subsidize it." Explain the logic in your own words using
   *incentive* and *opportunity cost*, then give one reason this rule of
   thumb can fail. (Answers will vary; acceptable: people may lack good
   alternatives, enforcement may be weak, or the incentive may be too small
   to matter.)

## 4. Application

**Incentive field observation.** With your adult's approval, observe one
real incentive in action: a store's sale signs, a parking sign with fines, a
school reward poster, a utility bill's pricing tiers. (Documentary
alternative: analyze a photographed sign or an official policy page your
adult selects.) Record: what behavior is rewarded/punished, who the target
is, what response you predict, and one group that gains and one that bears
a cost. 6–8 sentences plus the observation notes.

## 5. Exit check

1. Define positive and negative incentives, one example each (not from this
   lesson).
2. Why can the same policy create benefits for one group and costs for
   another? Give a one-sentence example.
3. A fine is meant to reduce a behavior but the behavior rises. Name two
   possible explanations using incentive reasoning.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** "positive" here means "adds something
  desirable," not "good" — a subsidy can still be badly designed. Keep a
  running two-column list (positive / negative) in your notebook.
- **Alternate response mode:** you may present practice answers as a table
  (situation → incentive type → target → predicted change → who gains / who
  pays) instead of paragraphs.
- **Extension:** choose one real local policy (adult-approved: a bag fee, a
  parking rule, a school incentive program). Find the official rule text,
  map its incentives across two groups, and write one paragraph on whether
  the evidence suggests it works as intended.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "positive incentive" = morally good — re-teach
the technical meaning; (2) assuming the intended response is the actual
response — push the enforcement question every time; (3) forgetting the
opportunity cost of the incentive itself (subsidies are paid for). Keep
examples institutional and nonpartisan: analyze roles and rules, not parties
or candidates. Answer-key reference: every item solved in
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 03 section.
