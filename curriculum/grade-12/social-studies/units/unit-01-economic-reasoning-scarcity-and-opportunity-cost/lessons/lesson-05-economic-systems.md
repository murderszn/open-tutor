# Lesson 05 — Economic Systems

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain how market, command, traditional,
and mixed economies answer "what, how, and for whom" — and compare them with
evidence instead of slogans.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, this lesson
**Readiness check (3 min):** Who decided what your school cafeteria serves
this week? Who decided the price? Keep your answer — it is a clue about your
economic system.

## 1. Explicit teaching

Every society must answer three questions: **what** goods and services to
produce, **how** to produce them (with what methods and resources), and
**for whom** (who gets what). An **economic system** is the set of
institutions and rules a society uses to answer them. Four ideal types:

- **Traditional economy:** custom and habit decide. What/how/for whom follow
  long-standing roles — families farm, hunt, or craft as their ancestors
  did, and distribute within the community by custom. Found today in parts
  of rural and Indigenous communities, usually alongside larger market
  systems.
- **Command economy:** a central authority decides. The state sets
  production targets, assigns resources, and fixes prices. The textbook
  example is the former Soviet Union; the closest large-scale current case
  is North Korea, where the state directs most production decisions.
- **Market economy:** decentralized decisions through prices. Firms and
  households respond to incentives (Lesson 03): what gets produced follows
  what people will pay for; how follows cost competition; for whom follows
  income. No pure market economy exists — every real one has government
  rules, taxes, and public goods.
- **Mixed economy:** every real modern economy. Markets allocate most goods
  and services; governments set the rules (property rights, contract
  enforcement), provide public goods, stabilize the economy, and run safety
  nets. The United States, Germany, Sweden, Japan, and China all mix market
  and command features in different proportions — the interesting question
  is never "market or command?" but "**how much of each, where, and why?**"

Two notes before the examples. First, the sketches below are **simplified
models for analysis, not full country profiles** — real economies are more
complex than any paragraph. Second, comparison is not endorsement: the task
is to describe how each system answers the three questions and what the
evidence says about the results, not to declare a winner.

### Worked example A — who answers the three questions?

**Bread in a market-leaning mixed economy (the United States, simplified):**

- *What:* bakeries decide what bread to bake by watching what customers buy
  — sourdough booms, plain white declines. No official orders bread types.
- *How:* each bakery chooses its methods — one uses a wood-fired oven, one
  uses an industrial line — chasing lower cost and better quality.
- *For whom:* bread goes to whoever can pay the price; people who cannot
  afford it rely on wages, charity, or public food assistance — which is
  itself a government program inside the mixed system.
- Reasoning check: the *price* coordinates all three answers without anyone
  planning the whole outcome. That coordination is the market mechanism U02
  will model.

### Worked example B — the same three questions, command-leaning

**Grain in a command-leaning economy (North Korea, simplified):**

- *What:* state plans set grain production targets for regions and
  workplaces.
- *How:* methods and inputs are assigned through the plan rather than chosen
  by competing producers.
- *For whom:* distribution runs through a state rationing and allocation
  system rather than market prices.
- Reasoning check: here a *plan* coordinates the three answers. The
  comparison with Example A is about **mechanisms** (prices vs. plans), not
  about which country is "better" — keep every comparison at the mechanism
  level.

### Property rights: the quiet foundation

Whichever mix a country chooses, economists emphasize one institution:
**clearly defined property rights** — rules saying who owns what and how
ownership transfers (D2.Eco.9.9-12). When ownership is secure and contracts
are enforced, people invest, lend, and trade with confidence; where it is
not, economic activity shrinks toward what can be protected personally.
The factors of production — **land, labor, and capital** (tools, machines,
buildings) — are allocated very differently depending on who can own them
and how securely.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. **Three questions, one good.** Pick smartphones. For a market-leaning
   system and a command-leaning system, say who answers what, how, and for
   whom in one or two sentences each. *Hint: copy the bread/grain structure
   from the worked examples.*
2. **Find the mix.** A country has private shops and farms, but the
   government owns the railways, sets fuel prices, and runs free public
   schools. Is this a "pure" anything? Name the system type and point to one
   market feature and one command feature. *Hint: no pure systems exist —
   describe the mix.*
3. **Mechanism, not slogan.** A classmate says, "Market economies are fair
   and command economies are unfair." Rewrite the claim as a mechanism-level
   comparison using the three questions. *Hint: "fair" is a verdict —
   replace it with who decides and how.*

## 3. Independent practice

4. **Cafeteria economics.** Return to the readiness check: who decided what
   your cafeteria serves, how it is made, and who gets it (and at what
   price)? Classify each answer as market-like or command-like and write one
   paragraph: what mix does your cafeteria run on?
5. **Sort the sketches.** Your adult gives you five short economy sketches
   (or use the investigation session's set). Rank them from most
   market-reliant to most command-reliant, citing one feature per sketch
   that justifies its position.
6. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "In a traditional economy, the three
   questions are answered before anyone asks them." Explain what this means
   in 3–4 sentences, then name one strength and one limitation of answering
   by custom rather than by prices or plans.

## 4. Application

**The desert-island constitution (thought experiment).** Thirty people are
stranded with tools, seeds, and fishing gear. In 8–10 sentences, design
their economic system: who decides what is produced, how work is organized,
and how output is shared — and why you chose that mix of custom, command,
and market mechanisms. Then name one problem your design would face in year
two and how the system would adapt. There is no single right answer; the
grading is on mechanism-level reasoning and acknowledged tradeoffs.

## 5. Exit check

1. Name the three economic questions and the four system types in your own
   words.
2. Give one real-world example of a mixed economy and name one market
   feature and one command feature it has.
3. Why do economists say property rights matter regardless of a country's
   system mix? Two sentences.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** keep a four-column chart (traditional /
  command / market / mixed) and file every example under the right column
  as you read. Say "what, how, for whom" aloud before answering any
  comparison question.
- **Alternate response mode:** you may answer practice items as labeled
  diagrams or tables (system → what → how → for whom) instead of
  paragraphs.
- **Extension:** research how one country's system mix changed over time
  (adult-approved topic, e.g., China's reforms after 1978 or Sweden's
  welfare-state buildout — sources dated). Write one paragraph: what moved
  toward markets, what stayed state-directed, and what the evidence says
  changed as a result.

## Teacher note

Guardrails: keep every comparison at the mechanism level (who decides, by
what process, with what evidence of results). Redirect slogans, partisan
framing, and present-day political commentary back to institutions and
evidence; contested claims get multiple sourced perspectives or are set
aside. If the learner reaches for "which system is best," reframe to "which
questions does each system answer well, and at what cost?" — the W6S4
deliberation runs on exactly that question. Answer-key reference: every
item solved in [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 05 section.

*Country sketches are simplified models for analysis. Economic facts change;
re-verify any current claim with a dated source before learner-facing use.*
