# Lesson 02 — The Production Possibilities Frontier

**Goal (student-readable):** I can read and build a production possibilities
frontier, find the opportunity cost between any two points on it, and explain
what moves the curve.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, graph paper or spreadsheet,
[this unit's farm illustration](../assets/two-good-farm-ppf.png)
**Readiness check (3 min):** In one sentence, define opportunity cost. Then
name one tradeoff a farmer faces.

## 1. Explicit teaching

The **production possibilities frontier (PPF)** is a model of scarcity for
a whole economy (or a farm, or a factory): it shows every combination of two
goods that can be produced when all resources are used fully and efficiently.
Because resources are limited, producing more of one good means producing
less of the other — the curve *is* the tradeoff, drawn as a picture.

Start with the activity, then the model.

### Activity A — Read the two-good farm

![An AI-generated illustration: one farm, two possible crops. Left: wheat
fields and a harvester. Right: corn fields. The farmer stands at the
boundary. If the farm expands its wheat half, what must it give
up?](../assets/two-good-farm-ppf.png)

Study the image (caption: an AI illustration of one farm split between wheat
and corn). Answer:

1. What two goods could this farm produce?
2. The farmer currently grows wheat on the left half and corn on the right.
   If the farm expands the wheat half into the corn half, what exactly must
   it give up? (Answer in terms of the other good.)
3. The fence is a visual metaphor, not a real farm plan. In one sentence,
   what does the boundary represent economically?

*Text-only alternative:* the left half of the farm grows wheat; the right
half grows corn; a farmer stands at the boundary between them. All three
questions can be answered from this description.

### The Greenfield Farm schedule (fictional practice data)

Now put numbers on the farm. Greenfield Farm has fixed land, labor, and
equipment for one season. Its possible wheat/corn combinations are:

| Combination | Wheat (bushels) | Corn (bushels) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 100 | 0 |
| B | 80 | 30 |
| C | 50 | 50 |
| D | 20 | 65 |
| E | 0 | 75 |

Plot wheat on the horizontal axis and corn on the vertical axis, then connect
the points. That curve is the farm's PPF.

### Worked example A — opportunity cost between two points

Move from **B to C**: corn rises from 30 to 50 (+20 bushels) while wheat
falls from 80 to 50 (−30 bushels). The opportunity cost of those 20 extra
bushels of corn is 30 bushels of wheat, or **1.5 bushels of wheat per bushel
of corn** (30 ÷ 20). Reasoning check: corn gained ÷ wheat given up, per unit.
Now move from **D to E**: corn rises 65 → 75 (+10) while wheat falls 20 → 0
(−20). The per-unit opportunity cost is **2 bushels of wheat per bushel of
corn** (20 ÷ 10). Notice it *rose*: the last bushels of corn cost more wheat
than the first ones did. That is **increasing opportunity cost** — the
resources best suited to wheat get converted last, so each extra bushel of
corn costs more. The bowed-out (concave) shape of the curve shows it.

### Worked example B — reading points on and off the curve

Take the same PPF.

- **Point C (50 wheat, 50 corn)** sits *on* the curve: the farm uses all
  resources fully. Call this **efficient**.
- **Point F (40 wheat, 30 corn)** sits *inside* the curve: the farm could
  produce more of both (e.g., reach B or C) without giving anything up. Call
  this **inefficient** — resources are idle or wasted. Moving from F to C
  costs *nothing* in opportunity-cost terms, because nothing is sacrificed.
- **Point G (80 wheat, 60 corn)** sits *outside* the curve: **unattainable**
  with current resources and technology. The farm cannot reach it today.

Reasoning check: inside the curve, more of one good does not require less of
the other — so there is no tradeoff *yet*. The tradeoff only bites on the
curve itself.

### What shifts the frontier

The PPF moves **outward** (more of both goods possible) when resources grow
or technology improves: new farmland, a better seed variety, more workers,
a more efficient harvester. It moves **inward** when resources are lost: a
drought destroys part of the crop, workers leave, equipment breaks down.
A movement *along* the curve is a tradeoff with fixed resources; a
*shift* of the curve is a change in what is possible. Keep those two ideas
separate — confusing them is the classic error.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. **Per-unit cost, A to B.** Using the schedule, moving from A (100 wheat,
   0 corn) to B (80 wheat, 30 corn): how many bushels of wheat does each
   bushel of corn cost? *Hint: wheat given up ÷ corn gained.*
2. **Find the cheapest corn.** Which move has the *lowest* per-unit
   opportunity cost of corn: A→B, B→C, C→D, or D→E? What does that tell you
   about which land gets converted first? *Hint: compute all four; the
   smallest number wins.*
3. **Classify the points.** On the Greenfield PPF, classify each as
   efficient, inefficient, or unattainable: (60 wheat, 40 corn); (50 wheat,
   50 corn); (90 wheat, 70 corn); (30 wheat, 20 corn). *Hint: plot mentally
   — inside, on, or outside the curve?*
4. **Shift or slide?** For each event, say whether Greenfield's PPF shifts
   (and which direction) or the farm moves along its curve: (a) a new
   irrigation system doubles water efficiency; (b) the farmer plants more
   corn and less wheat this season; (c) a flood washes away a third of the
   fields. *Hint: did total resources/technology change, or just the mix?*

## 3. Independent practice

5. **Build it.** On graph paper or a spreadsheet, plot the Greenfield
   schedule and draw the PPF. Label one efficient, one inefficient, and one
   unattainable point of your choosing (not the ones from the lesson).
6. **Reverse the cost.** Moving from C (50 wheat, 50 corn) back to B
   (80 wheat, 30 corn): what is the per-unit opportunity cost of *wheat* in
   terms of corn? Show the division. (Answer: 30 more wheat costs 20 corn →
   2/3 bushel of corn per bushel of wheat.)
7. **Explain-your-reasoning.** A classmate says, "Point F inside the curve
   proves opportunity cost is zero everywhere." Explain the error: when is
   the opportunity cost of producing more actually zero, and when is it
   positive? Use points F and C in your answer.
8. **Shift story.** Write a 4–5 sentence story about a small bakery (two
   goods: bread and cakes) in which its PPF first shifts outward, then the
   bakery moves along the new curve. Name the cause of the shift and the
   tradeoff of the movement.

## 4. Application

**Your personal PPF.** Pick two activities competing for your Saturday
(e.g., paid work hours and study hours; practice and rest). Sketch a PPF
with plausible numbers (label it fictional), mark where you actually landed
last Saturday, and classify your point. Then describe one realistic change
that would shift your personal frontier outward. One paragraph plus the
sketch.

## 5. Exit check

1. In your own words: what does the PPF show, and why is it bowed outward?
2. From the schedule: moving C→D, what is the per-unit opportunity cost of
   corn in wheat? (Show the arithmetic.)
3. Give one example of a PPF *shift* and one of a movement *along* the
   curve, and explain the difference.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the schedule table is the lesson's anchor —
  re-read it before each practice item; say "wheat given up divided by corn
  gained" aloud as the per-unit formula.
- **Alternate response mode:** you may describe the plotted points in a
  table (combination → classification → reason) instead of drawing the
  graph; the adult checks the table against the schedule.
- **Extension:** look up your country's actual wheat and corn output for
  one recent year (adult-supervised; source and date recorded). Is a real
  country's two-crop tradeoff as clean as Greenfield's? Write one paragraph
  on what the model leaves out (weather, prices, soil, trade).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) reading "inside the curve" as a tradeoff —
re-teach that tradeoffs bite only *on* the frontier; (2) adding the two
goods' totals instead of computing per-unit cost; (3) calling any change a
"shift." The image activity primes the model before the numbers arrive;
if the learner struggles with the arithmetic, do the A→B computation
together first. Answer-key reference: every item solved in
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 02 section.

*The Greenfield Farm schedule is fictional practice data for learning the
model — not real farm output.*
