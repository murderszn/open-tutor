# Lesson 04 — Marginal Analysis

**Goal (student-readable):** I can use marginal benefit and marginal cost to
decide the right *amount* of something — and argue for or against an approach
using that reasoning.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, this lesson
**Readiness check (3 min):** "Should you study one more hour?" What two
things would you compare to answer? (Not total study vs. total fun — the
*next* hour of each.)

## 1. Explicit teaching

Lessons 1–3 answered *whether* to do something. Most real decisions are
**how much**: how many hours to study, how many workers to hire, how strict
a regulation to set. The tool is **marginal analysis** — compare the
**marginal benefit (MB)** of the *next* unit with its **marginal cost (MC)**,
and keep going only while MB ≥ MC. Stop when the next step costs more than
it gains.

Why the *next* unit and not the total? Because totals include steps you
already decided. If the first three study hours were clearly worth it, that
does not make the fourth worth it — the fourth hour has its own, smaller
benefit (you are tired; the easy gains are taken) and its own, larger cost
(sleep you now desperately need). Economists call the shrinking benefit
**diminishing marginal benefit** and the growing cost **increasing marginal
cost**. The optimal amount is where they cross.

### Worked example A — the study-hours schedule (fictional practice data)

A student estimates, for each *additional* hour of studying tonight:

| Hour | Marginal benefit (expected test points gained) | Marginal cost (value of the next-best use, in the same points) |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 12 | 3 |
| 2nd | 10 | 5 |
| 3rd | 8 | 7 |
| 4th | 5 | 9 |
| 5th | 2 | 11 |

Decide hour by hour:

- 1st hour: MB 12 ≥ MC 3 → **study**.
- 2nd hour: 10 ≥ 5 → **study**.
- 3rd hour: 8 ≥ 7 → **study** (close, but still worth it).
- 4th hour: 5 < 9 → **stop**.
- 5th hour: 2 < 11 → **stop** (even worse).

**Optimal: 3 hours.** Reasoning check: the 4th hour is not "bad studying" —
it would still gain 5 points. It is simply not worth its 9-point cost. And
notice what does *not* matter: the total points from the first three hours
(30) are irrelevant to the 4th-hour decision. Only the margin counts.

### Worked example B — the bakery's extra worker (fictional practice data)

A bakery considers hiring one more Saturday worker at $120 for the shift
(the marginal cost). The extra worker would let the bakery sell 40 more
loaves at $4 profit each = $160 marginal benefit. MB ($160) ≥ MC ($120) →
**hire**. Now suppose a second extra worker would add only 20 more loaves
($80 MB) at the same $120 wage. $80 < $120 → **do not hire the second**.
Same wage, different decision — because the *marginal* benefit fell while
the marginal cost stayed flat. Reasoning check: the question is never "are
workers profitable in general?" but "is the *next* worker worth it?"

### Building a marginal argument (D2.Eco.2.9-12)

Marginal analysis becomes an argument when you use it to recommend for or
against an approach:

> **Claim:** The city should expand its free summer-meals program from 10
> sites to 12, but not to 15.
> **Marginal evidence:** Sites 11–12 would each serve ~200 additional
> children at ~$8,000 per site (MB high, MC moderate). Sites 13–15 would
> each serve ~40 additional children at the same $8,000 cost (MB low, MC
> unchanged) — the money would feed more children if spent on the existing
> sites' weekend backpack program instead.
> **Conclusion:** expand to 12, then redirect remaining funds.

The structure — claim, marginal comparison with numbers, acknowledgment of
the forgone alternative, conclusion — is the template for this week's
argument session and the project.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. **Find the optimum.** A runner considers extra weekly miles. MB per added
   mile (fitness points): 20, 16, 12, 8, 4. MC per added mile (injury risk
   + time, same points): 6, 8, 10, 12, 14. How many extra miles is optimal?
   *Hint: go mile by mile; stop at the first mile where MB < MC.*
2. **The irrelevant total.** A friend says, "I've already studied 3 hours
   and gained 30 points, so obviously the 4th hour is worth it." Using the
   study schedule, explain the error in two sentences.
   *Hint: what are the 4th hour's own MB and MC?*
3. **Hire or not?** A car wash considers a second Sunday shift. MC: $300
   wages and supplies. MB: 90 extra washes × $5 profit = $450. A third shift
   would add 40 washes ($200 MB) at the same $300 MC. Decide each shift.
   *Hint: decide the second and third shifts separately.*

## 3. Independent practice

4. **Build a marginal table.** Choose one "how much" decision from your own
   life (practice hours, work shifts, pages read, episodes watched). Make a
   4-row marginal table with your honest estimates (label it fictional),
   decide the optimal amount, and write 3–4 sentences defending the
   decision — including which step you stopped at and why.
5. **Policy at the margin.** A town considers lowering its speed limit from
   35 to 25 mph on Main Street (fictional scenario). List two marginal
   benefits and two marginal costs of the *change* (not of speed limits in
   general). Then write one paragraph arguing for or against the change
   using the claim → marginal comparison → conclusion structure.
6. **Explain-your-reasoning.** "The first slice of pizza is the best, the
   fifth makes you sick, and the price never changed." Explain this with
   *marginal benefit* and *marginal cost* in 3–4 sentences, then state the
   optimal number of slices for someone with these preferences and why the
   *total* enjoyment of all five slices does not answer the question.

## 4. Application

**Marginal audit of a real rule.** Pick one rule you live under (a curfew, a
screen-time limit, a workplace break policy, a school tardy policy —
adult-approved). Analyze it at the margin: what is the MB and MC of making
the rule *slightly stricter*? Slightly looser? Write a 6–8 sentence
recommendation (keep, tighten, or loosen) with marginal reasoning and one
acknowledged tradeoff.

## 5. Exit check

1. Define marginal benefit and marginal cost in your own words.
2. MB per unit: 15, 12, 9, 6. MC per unit: 5, 7, 10, 13. What is the optimal
   number of units? Show the comparison.
3. Why do economists compare the *next* unit rather than the totals? One or
   two sentences.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** "marginal" here means "the next one" — write
  "MB of the NEXT unit vs. MC of the NEXT unit" at the top of your notes.
  The tables are the lesson's anchor; re-read them before each item.
- **Alternate response mode:** you may answer the exit check orally; the
  marginal table in item 4 may be dictated to your adult.
- **Extension:** find one real cost-benefit analysis summary (adult-approved
  source, e.g., a government agency's regulatory review). Identify the MB
  and MC being compared and write one paragraph on what the analysis leaves
  out.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) using total benefit/total cost instead of the
margin — the readiness check and item 2 target this directly; (2) treating
sunk costs as marginal costs — re-teach Lesson 01's forward-looking rule;
(3) thinking "MB ≥ MC, keep going" means "more is always better" — the
crossing point is the whole lesson. If the arithmetic is the barrier, do
Guided practice 1 together aloud before releasing the independent set.
