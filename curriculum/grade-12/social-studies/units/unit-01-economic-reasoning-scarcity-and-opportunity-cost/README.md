# Unit 01 — Economic Reasoning: Scarcity, Incentives, and Opportunity Cost

**Grade:** 12 · **Subject:** Social Studies (Economics and policy inquiry)
**Track weeks:** 3–6 · **Backlog:** [issue #57](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/57), section U01
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Every choice is a trade: you cannot take every path, spend every dollar twice,
or plant a whole field in two crops at once. This unit builds the three tools
economists use to reason about any decision — **scarcity** (resources are
limited, so choice is unavoidable), **opportunity cost** (the value of the best
alternative you give up), and **incentives** (rewards and penalties that shift
choices) — then adds **marginal analysis** (deciding "how much" by comparing
the next step's benefits and costs) and compares how **economic systems** answer
the questions every society must settle: what to make, how to make it, and for
whom.

## Essential question

When every choice costs something, how do people — and whole societies — decide
what's worth it?

## Prerequisites

From the track diagnostic weeks (weeks 1–2) and the assumed grade-11
social-studies and Algebra II background:

- Frame a claim supported by at least two pieces of evidence; identify a
  tradeoff and its opportunity cost in a simple scenario.
- Graph and interpret linear functions, compute and interpret slope, read
  scatterplots, work with percentages and ratios, read two-variable tables.
- Classify sources by kind and evaluate evidence with attention to
  limitations (used from Week 5 onward in this unit).

No cross-grade learner links: these are described skills, not references to
other grades' lessons. Opportunity cost and marginal reasoning are re-taught
from scratch in Lessons 1 and 4 rather than assumed.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain** scarcity and give original examples of how it forces choice
   for individuals, firms, and governments. (D2.Eco.1.9-12)
2. **Compute and interpret opportunity cost** from a production schedule,
   identifying the best forgone alternative rather than all forgone
   alternatives. (D2.Eco.1.9-12)
3. **Read and build a production possibilities frontier (PPF):** identify
   efficient, inefficient, and unattainable points; find opportunity cost
   between points; explain what shifts the curve. (D2.Eco.3.9-12)
4. **Analyze** how positive and negative incentives shape the choices of
   consumers, producers, and policymakers — and explain why a policy's
   incentives produce different costs and benefits for different groups.
   (D2.Eco.1.9-12)
5. **Use marginal analysis:** compare marginal benefit and marginal cost to
   decide the optimal level of an activity and construct an argument for or
   against an economic approach. (D2.Eco.2.9-12)
6. **Compare** how market, command, traditional, and mixed economies answer
   what, how, and for whom — using evidence, without endorsing any system.
   (D2.Eco.3.9-12 applied)

In every unit the learner also develops compelling questions (D1.1–D1.5.9-12),
evaluates sources and their limits (D3.1–D3.4.9-12), and communicates
evidence-based conclusions (D4.1–D4.4.9-12).

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: the **College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework
for Social Studies State Standards**, National Council for the Social
Studies — the inquiry arc and the 9–12 economics indicators cited in the
track audit (2026-10-09). **Indicator codes are exact; the short descriptions
below are brief paraphrases — check the authoritative framework for exact
wording.** No state adoption, accreditation, or alignment certification is
claimed. This course is a proposed pathway, not a graduation requirement.

| Unit objective | Indicator | Paraphrase |
|---|---|---|
| 1, 2, 4 | D2.Eco.1.9-12 | Analyzing how incentives influence choices that can produce policies with a range of costs and benefits for different groups |
| 5 | D2.Eco.2.9-12 | Using marginal benefits and marginal costs to construct an argument for or against an economic approach |
| 3, 6 | D2.Eco.3.9-12 | Analyzing how incentives influence what is produced and distributed in a market system |

All numerical schedules in the unit are **fictional practice data, clearly
labeled** — they model reasoning, not real observations. Real-data tasks
begin in U05. The course is nonpartisan: incentives and systems are analyzed
through roles, rules, and evidence, not partisan commentary. Financial
examples are **educational, not investment advice**.

## 20-session pacing (4 weeks × 5 sessions, ~50 minutes each)

Sessions rotate: core lesson → practice → investigation/reading →
argument/deliberation → review. The project launches in Week 4, Session 3
and runs across investigation and argument sessions; the economic-systems
deliberation (W6S4) doubles as the project defense.

| Session | Type | Content |
|---|---|---|
| W3S1 | Lesson | [L1 — Scarcity and opportunity cost](lessons/lesson-01-scarcity-and-opportunity-cost.md): choice is unavoidable; the best forgone alternative is the cost |
| W3S2 | Practice | Opportunity-cost scenarios; sunk-cost error analysis; retrieval on definitions |
| W3S3 | Lesson | [L2 — The production possibilities frontier](lessons/lesson-02-production-possibilities-frontier.md): the two-good model, the farm illustration, points on and off the curve |
| W3S4 | Investigation | Build a PPF from the Greenfield Farm schedule; compute per-unit opportunity costs; find increasing cost |
| W3S5 | Review | Week 3 review + formative check (scarcity, opportunity cost, PPF reading) |
| W4S1 | Lesson | [L3 — Incentives](lessons/lesson-03-incentives.md): positive and negative incentives for consumers, producers, policymakers |
| W4S2 | Practice | Identify-the-incentive scenarios; predict behavioral responses with evidence |
| W4S3 | Investigation | **Project launch** — [One Decision, Three Lenses](project.md): choose a decision, plan the PPF, incentive, and marginal analyses |
| W4S4 | Investigation | One policy's incentives: who bears the costs and benefits across groups (documentary alternative provided) |
| W4S5 | Deliberation + review | Do incentives always work as intended? Week 4 review |
| W5S1 | Lesson | [L4 — Marginal analysis](lessons/lesson-04-marginal-analysis.md): the next step's benefits vs. costs; the study-hours schedule |
| W5S2 | Practice | Compute optimal levels from marginal schedules; error analysis on sunk costs vs. marginal costs |
| W5S3 | Investigation | Apply marginal reasoning to one business or policy decision; update project evidence logs |
| W5S4 | Argument | Construct a marginal case for or against an economic approach (D2.Eco.2.9-12); peer-style critique with the rubric |
| W5S5 | Review | Week 5 review + formative check (marginal math; marginal argument structure) |
| W6S1 | Lesson | [L5 — Economic systems](lessons/lesson-05-economic-systems.md): market, command, traditional, mixed — what/how/for whom |
| W6S2 | Investigation | Sort five economy sketches by market-vs-command features; source check on the sketches |
| W6S3 | Project | Drafting session — [One Decision, Three Lenses](project.md) due at W6S4 |
| W6S4 | Deliberation | **Project defense + deliberation:** "Which questions does each system answer best?" — argued from evidence |
| W6S5 | Assessment | Unit review + [U01 assessment](assessments/unit-assessment.md); [quiz](assessments/week-06_quiz.md) taken the session before |

Success criteria: by W3S5 the learner can define opportunity cost and read a
PPF; by W4S5 the learner can identify incentives in a policy and explain who
bears its costs; by W5S5 the learner can compute an optimal level from a
marginal schedule and argue a marginal case; by W6S5 the learner can compare
economic systems with evidence and has delivered the project.
