# Answer Key — Unit 01: Economic Reasoning

Adult only. Every practice, quiz, and assessment item is solved here;
student files contain the questions without answers. Accept equivalent
reasoning stated in the learner's own words.

## Lesson 01 — Scarcity and opportunity cost

**Guided 1 (Jamal's evening):** Jamal studies. Options not taken: the $25
task and the $10 games. Opportunity cost = **$25** (the best forgone
alternative).

**Guided 2 (the wrong total):** The classmate added both forgone options
($20 + $10 = $30). But Jamal could never have done *both* — doing the task
meant giving up either studying or games, and the best of those is the $20
study session... (for the classmate's own numbers: $25 task chosen over $20
study and $10 games → opportunity cost is $20, not $30).

**Guided 3 (concert ticket):** Priya should compare going (miserable evening,
possibly infecting friends) vs. staying home (rest). The $50 is a **sunk
cost** — unrecoverable either way — and should not affect the decision.

**Independent 4:** Answers vary. Check: one scarce resource named, two
competing uses described, choice stated, opportunity cost = best forgone
use.

**Independent 5:** The club picks the car wash ($1,200). Opportunity cost =
**$900** (the bake sale, the best forgone alternative). It is not $900 +
$700 = $1,600 because the club could never have run *both* the bake sale
and the food-bank shift on the same Saturday.

**Independent 6:**
- a. The $300 is a **sunk cost** (prepaid, unrecoverable). Compare: a
  storm-ruined week at the rental vs. staying home — the forward-looking
  options, ignoring the $300.
- b. The 3 hours are a **sunk cost**. Compare: polishing a draft for the
  wrong assignment vs. starting over for the right one.

**Independent 7:** "No free lunch" means every good or service consumes
scarce resources, so providing it free to you means someone gave up the
best alternative use of those resources. Example (any original one
accepted): a "free" school laptop was paid for by taxpayers, whose money
could otherwise have funded textbooks — the textbooks are the opportunity
cost.

**Exit check:**
1. Scarcity: resources are limited while wants are unlimited (any
equivalent sentence + original example).
2. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative given up. It is the
*best* one because you could only ever have chosen one alternative — the
others were never simultaneously available.
3. Opportunity cost = **$80** (option B). It is NOT $140 ($80 + $60).

## Lesson 02 — Production possibilities frontier

**Activity A (farm image):**
1. Wheat and corn.
2. It must give up corn — specifically, the corn that the converted land
would have grown.
3. The boundary represents the tradeoff: with fixed land, more of one crop
means less of the other (any equivalent sentence).

**Guided 1 (A→B per-unit cost):** Wheat given up: 100 − 80 = 20. Corn
gained: 30 − 0 = 30. Per-unit cost = 20 ÷ 30 = **2/3 bushel of wheat per
bushel of corn** (≈0.67).

**Guided 2 (cheapest corn):** A→B: 20/30 ≈ 0.67; B→C: 30/20 = 1.5; C→D:
30/15 = 2; D→E: 20/10 = 2. **A→B is cheapest.** The land converted first is
the land best suited to corn (lowest wheat sacrificed); increasingly
wheat-suited land converts later.

**Guided 3 (classify):**
- (60 wheat, 40 corn): between B(80,30) and C(50,50) — on segment B→C,
corn 30→50 while wheat 80→50; at corn 40, wheat = 80 − (10/20)×30 = 65.
60 < 65 → **inside → inefficient**.
- (50 wheat, 50 corn) = point C → **on the curve → efficient**.
- (90 wheat, 70 corn) → beyond A(100,0)/B(80,30) region; at wheat 90,
frontier corn = 15 (segment A→B: corn = (100−wheat)×30/20... precisely:
corn = 30×(100−90)/20 = 15). 70 > 15 → **outside → unattainable**.
- (30 wheat, 20 corn): segment C→D: at corn 20... use wheat: between
C(50,50) and D(20,65) is corn-heavy; better check segment B→C at wheat 30:
below B. Simplest: maximum wheat at corn 20 is on segment B→C:
wheat = 80 − ((20−30+... let parameter t: corn 30→50, wheat 80→50; at corn
40, wheat 65 (above). At corn 20 (below B's 30), use segment A→B: at corn
20, wheat = 100 − 20×(20/30) = 100 − 13.33 = 86.67. 30 < 86.67 → **inside
→ inefficient**.

**Guided 4 (shift or slide):**
- (a) New irrigation doubles water efficiency → more output from same land
→ **PPF shifts outward**.
- (b) Planting more corn, less wheat → same resources, new mix →
**movement along the curve**.
- (c) Flood destroys a third of the fields → fewer resources → **PPF
shifts inward**.

**Independent 5:** Check the plot: 5 points plotted and connected, one
point labeled in each region with a correct classification and reason.

**Independent 6 (reverse cost C→B):** Wheat gained: 80 − 50 = 30. Corn
given up: 50 − 30 = 20. Per-unit opportunity cost of wheat = 20 ÷ 30 =
**2/3 bushel of corn per bushel of wheat**.

**Independent 7:** The classmate is wrong because opportunity cost is zero
only when resources are idle — moving from F (inside) to C (on the curve)
sacrifices nothing. Once *on* the frontier (point C), producing more of one
good requires producing less of the other, so opportunity cost is positive.
Zero inside (slack exists); positive on the curve (tradeoff binds).

**Independent 8:** Check: a story in which a cause (new oven, new baker,
better recipe) expands what is possible = shift; then a choice to bake more
bread and fewer cakes at the new capacity = movement along the new curve.
Both labeled.

**Exit check:**
1. The PPF shows all maximum combinations of two goods producible with
fixed resources. It bows outward because opportunity cost increases — the
resources converted later are less suited to the expanding good.
2. C→D: corn +15 (50→65), wheat −30 (50→20). 30 ÷ 15 = **2 bushels of
wheat per bushel of corn**.
3. Shift example: new technology or more resources move the whole curve
(e.g., the irrigation system). Movement example: planting more corn and
less wheat with the same resources. Difference: a shift changes what is
possible; a movement changes the chosen mix within what is possible.

## Lesson 03 — Incentives

**Guided 1:**
- (a) **Positive** incentive for **consumers** (shoppers): bring your own
cup → save $2 → more reusable-cup use predicted.
- (b) **Negative** incentive for **drivers**: running a red light now costs
$200 → fewer red-light runs predicted.
- (c) **Positive** incentive for **students**: 3.5 GPA earns a scholarship
→ more study effort predicted.

**Guided 2:** Gains: teenagers (cheaper rides); the city/transit agency
(more ridership, less congestion — one group suffices, two preferred).
Bears the cost: taxpayers funding the subsidy; drivers facing more bus
traffic (either accepted). Opportunity cost of the subsidy money: whatever
the city would otherwise have funded (road repair, libraries, etc.).

**Guided 3:** Students might skim very short books (or re-read easy ones,
or skim without comprehending) to collect cards. Accept any plausible
reward-chasing response the school did not intend.

**Independent 4:** Check each: incentive typed correctly, target named,
behavior predicted, one gaining and one cost-bearing group identified.
(Any original examples accepted.)

**Independent 5:** Town A: real enforcement → the $100 fine is a genuine
negative incentive → less littering. Town B: no tickets → the real
incentive is ~$0 regardless of the written rule → little change. The
written rule is not the real incentive; the *expected* penalty (fine ×
probability of being caught) is.

**Independent 6:** Logic: taxes raise the cost of an activity (negative
incentive → less of it); subsidies lower the cost (positive incentive →
more of it); both work through opportunity cost — the taxed activity's
alternatives just got relatively cheaper. Why it can fail (any one):
people may have no good alternative; enforcement may be weak; the
tax/subsidy may be too small to change behavior; or it may crowd out
non-monetary motives.

**Exit check:**
1. Positive incentive adds something desirable (example: a discount);
negative incentive imposes a cost (example: a fine). Original examples
required.
2. Because the group responding to the incentive is often not the group
paying for it — e.g., a solar subsidy benefits installing households while
taxpayers fund it.
3. Two of: enforcement was too weak to make the penalty real; people treated
the fine as an acceptable price; the incentive was too small; it crowded
out a stronger non-monetary motive.

## Lesson 04 — Marginal analysis

**Guided 1:** Mile 1: 20 ≥ 6 ✓; mile 2: 16 ≥ 8 ✓; mile 3: 12 ≥ 10 ✓;
mile 4: 8 < 12 ✗ → stop. **Optimal: 3 extra miles.**

**Guided 2:** The 30 total points came from hours 1–3, which are already
decided. The 4th hour must be judged on its own: MB 5 vs. MC 9 — and 5 < 9,
so it is not worth it. Totals do not decide the next step.

**Guided 3:** Second shift: MB $450 ≥ MC $300 → **run it**. Third shift:
MB $200 < MC $300 → **do not run it**.

**Independent 4:** Check: a 4-row marginal table with honest labeled
estimates, the optimum found by MB ≥ MC step by step, and a defense naming
the stopping step and its MB/MC.

**Independent 5:** Check: two marginal benefits of the *change* (e.g.,
fewer pedestrian injuries; calmer street) and two marginal costs of the
*change* (e.g., longer commutes; delivery delays) — not of speed limits in
general. The paragraph follows claim → marginal comparison → conclusion.

**Independent 6:** The first slices have high MB (hunger) and the price
(MC) is constant, so early slices pass MB ≥ MC easily; by the fifth slice
MB has fallen below the price (and below zero — discomfort). The optimal
number is the last slice with MB ≥ price (for typical preferences, 2–3).
Total enjoyment is irrelevant because the eaten slices are sunk — only the
*next* slice's benefit vs. its price decides.

**Exit check:**
1. Marginal benefit: the extra benefit of one more unit. Marginal cost:
the extra cost of one more unit. (Any equivalent.)
2. Unit 1: 15 ≥ 5 ✓; unit 2: 12 ≥ 7 ✓; unit 3: 9 < 10 ✗ → **2 units.**
3. Because the earlier units are already decided (their benefits are sunk
into the total); only the next unit's own benefit and cost can change the
decision.

## Lesson 05 — Economic systems

**Guided 1:** Market-leaning: *what* — phone makers decide models by what
customers buy; *how* — each firm picks its production methods chasing lower
costs; *for whom* — phones go to buyers who can pay. Command-leaning:
*what* — the state plan sets phone output targets; *how* — methods assigned
through the plan; *for whom* — distribution by the allocation system, not
price. (Structure matters more than the specific good.)

**Guided 2:** A **mixed economy**: market feature — private shops and farms
respond to prices; command feature — state-owned railways / state-set fuel
prices (either accepted).

**Guided 3:** Accept any rewrite at mechanism level, e.g.: "In a market
economy, *what* is produced follows what buyers will pay for and *for
whom* follows income — so people with little income get little; in a
command economy, the plan's priorities decide *for whom* — so outcomes
follow planners' choices, including their errors." The verdict ("fair /
unfair") is replaced by who decides and how.

**Independent 4:** Check: each of what/how/for whom answered for the
cafeteria with a market-like or command-like classification and a one-
paragraph mix description. (Typical: *what* is command-like — a menu set by
the school; *price* may be set administratively; *how* is market-like —
vendors compete for the contract.)

**Independent 5:** Check: five sketches ranked with one cited feature each
justifying the position. Order must be internally consistent.

**Independent 6:** "Answered before anyone asks" means custom — not prices
or plans — allocates roles and shares: a fisher fishes because fishers
fish, and shares follow tradition. Strength: stability, low decision costs,
social cohesion (any one). Limitation: slow adaptation when conditions
change; little room for individual choice or innovation (any one).

**Exit check:**
1. The three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, for whom. The
four types: traditional, command, market, mixed. (Own words.)
2. Any real mixed economy with one market feature (private firms, price
signals) and one command feature (state-owned sector, administered prices,
public provision) correctly identified.
3. Secure property rights and contract enforcement let people invest, lend,
and trade with confidence; without them, activity shrinks to what can be
protected personally — whatever the system's mix.

## Practice set

**A1:** Opportunity cost = **$55** (the robotics workshop, the best forgone
alternative). Not $80 ($55 + $25), because Lena could not have done both.

**A2:** Opportunity cost of the garden = **$40,000/year** (the parking lot).
If the town had built the parking lot, the opportunity cost would have been
**$28,000/year** (the garden — the best forgone alternative in that case).

**B1:** Wheat given up: 30 + 30 = **60 bushels**. Corn gained: 20 + 15 =
**35 bushels**. Average per-unit cost = 60 ÷ 35 ≈ **1.71 bushels of wheat
per bushel of corn**.

**B2:** Every combination yields less with the same land and labor → the
**PPF shifts inward** (a loss of effective resources), not a movement along
it. The new curve sits closer to the origin at every point.

**C1:** **Negative** incentive for **library patrons**: overdue books now
cost $1/day → fewer overdue books predicted. Gains: other patrons (books
return faster); the library (fine revenue). Bears the cost: forgetful
borrowers.

**C2:** For the three already-planned hires, the grant pays for behavior
that was happening anyway — pure transfer, no behavior change. This
resembles opportunity-cost thinking: the grant money's best alternative use
(e.g., training genuinely new hires) is the real cost of the windfall.

**D1:** Unit 1: 30 ≥ 10 ✓; unit 2: 24 ≥ 14 ✓; unit 3: 18 ≥ 18 ✓ (breaks
even — do it by the standard classroom rule; "indifferent" also accepted
with explanation); unit 4: 12 < 22 ✗. **Optimal: 3 units.**

**D2:** The error: sitting at the desk is sunk. The marginal cost of the
5th problem is the value of the next-best use of that time (rest, another
subject, sleep) — whatever is given up *for this problem*, not the time
already spent.

**E1:** Traditional: the catch goes by custom — e.g., shares by family
need or elder allotment, as done for generations. Market: the catch goes
to whoever pays the dock price; the fisher sells to the highest bidder.
(2–3 sentences each; custom vs. price mechanism is the graded contrast.)

**E2:** **Mixed economy.** Market feature: private package firms compete on
price/service. Command feature: the state runs the postal service.

**E3:** (a) Opportunity cost = **$150/week** (the internship's future-
earnings value — the best forgone alternative). (b) **No.** Opportunity
cost is judged on the information available *at decision time*; later
hindsight does not rewrite the original tradeoff. (Common trap — award
credit for the explanation, not just "no.")

**E4:** A→B: corn +20, wheat −15 → 15 ÷ 20 = **0.75 bushel of wheat per
bushel of corn**. D→E: corn +5, wheat −15 → 15 ÷ 5 = **3 bushels of wheat
per bushel of corn**. Yes — increasing (0.75 < 3). On shared axes,
Greenfield's curve lies entirely outside the second farm's: Greenfield has
the **absolute advantage in both** wheat (100 > 60) and corn (75 > 50).

**E5:** Check: one incentive stated with target, predicted response, one
gaining group, one cost-bearing group, and one plausible dodge (e.g.,
students dump trays to avoid a "clean plate" reward being measured, or take
extra food to game a per-item count).

**E6:** Date 1: 12,000 ≥ 5,000 ✓; date 2: 9,000 ≥ 5,000 ✓; date 3:
6,000 ≥ 5,000 ✓; date 4: 4,000 < 5,000 ✗. **3 dates.** Decision rule: add
dates while the next date's expected profit covers its cost; stop before
the first date that does not.

**E7:** Check: one paragraph using what/how/for whom to show the mix is
deliberate (e.g., public schools: *what* — education guaranteed; *how* —
publicly provided; *for whom* — all children regardless of income — inside
an otherwise market economy), arguing the mix is a choice, not indecision.

**R1–R3:** Self-check items — the learner compares against Lessons 1–4.
(R2: a PPF shows efficient/inefficient/unattainable points, opportunity
cost between points, and what shifts the curve. R3: because only one
alternative could ever have been chosen.)

## Unit assessment (80 pts)

**Task 1 (20):**
- a. A→B: tables +12 (0→12), chairs −12 (60→48): 12 ÷ 12 = **1 chair per
table**. D→E: tables +4 (26→30), chairs −16 (16→0): 16 ÷ 4 = **4 chairs per
table**. **Increasing**: later tables cost more chairs (1 < 4) because the
most table-suited resources convert first. (8)
- b. At 10 tables the frontier allows 50 chairs (segment A→B: chairs =
60 − tables); 30 < 50, so the point is **inside → inefficient**. One
reachable frontier combination with at least as much of both: **B (48
