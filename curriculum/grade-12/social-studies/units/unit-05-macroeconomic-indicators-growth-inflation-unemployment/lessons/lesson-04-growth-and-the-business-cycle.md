## Explicit teaching

### The business cycle: economies breathe

Real GDP does not grow smoothly. It rises, peaks, falls, bottoms out,
and rises again. That repeating pattern is the **business cycle**, and
it has four phases:

- **Expansion** — real GDP is rising; jobs are added; unemployment
  generally falls.
- **Peak** — the high point before a downturn: the last quarter of
  growth before output starts falling.
- **Contraction** — real GDP is falling; jobs are lost; unemployment
  generally rises. A contraction that is deep and long enough is called
  a **recession**.
- **Trough** — the low point before the next expansion begins.

The diagram below is a schematic — a drawing of the idea, not a chart
of real data. The wavy line is real GDP; the straight dashed line is
the long-run trend (potential GDP, the path output would follow if
resources were fully used). The gap between the two is the **output
gap**: positive at peaks, negative at troughs.

The phases are labeled: expansion on the rising part of the wave, peak at the crest, contraction on the falling part, and trough at the lowest point. The vertical axis is labeled Real GDP and the horizontal axis is labeled Time.](../assets/business-cycle-diagram.svg)

*Caption: Schematic diagram of the business cycle (not real data).
Real GDP fluctuates around its long-run trend; the phases are
expansion, peak, contraction, and trough.*

Two cautions belong with the picture. First, the cycle is irregular:
expansions in the United States have lasted from about a year to about
ten years; contractions are usually shorter. The diagram's even waves
are a teaching simplification. Second, "recession" has two meanings in
common use. The shorthand — two consecutive quarters of falling real
GDP — is a rule of thumb, not the official definition. In the United
States, the National Bureau of Economic Research's Business Cycle
Dating Committee declares recessions from monthly data on output,
income, employment, and sales, and it can call a downturn a recession
even without two negative quarters (it did so in 2020), or decline to
call two negative quarters a recession. When a learner says "we are in
a recession," the honest follow-up is: "by which definition, and who
decides?"

Economists sort indicators by timing: **leading indicators** move
before the economy does (new orders, building permits, stock prices);
**coincident indicators** move with it (output, employment, sales);
**lagging indicators** move after it (the unemployment rate, which
keeps rising even after a recovery begins, because firms hire late).
The October 2026 data illustrate the lag: payrolls rose 165,000 and
unemployment sat at 4.1 percent while GDP was still being revised.

### Worked example 1 — Find the peak and the trough

*Fictional practice data: quarterly real GDP growth, annualized
percent change.*

| Quarter | Q1 | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 | Q5 | Q6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth | +2.1% | +1.4% | −0.8% | −1.6% | −0.4% | +1.2% |

Step 1 — Mark the sign of each quarter: growth, growth, decline,
decline, decline, growth.

Step 2 — The **peak** is the end of Q2: the last quarter of growth
before output starts falling. (The peak is a point in time — the end
of the expansion — not the highest growth rate.)

Step 3 — The **trough** is the end of Q5: the last quarter of decline
before growth resumes in Q6.

Step 4 — The contraction runs Q3 through Q5: three consecutive quarters
of decline. By the two-quarter shorthand, this is a recession; by the
official definition, the dating committee would look at monthly
employment and income data before deciding. Either way, the learner
states the definition being used.

Step 5 — The expansion resumes in Q6 (+1.2%). Note that one quarter of
growth does not prove the contraction is over — later data can revise
the picture, as the BEA's advance estimate is revised twice (the Q3
2026 advance estimate of +3.4 percent will be revised on November 25,
2026, and again in December).

**A common error:** calling the quarter with the fastest growth the
peak, or the quarter with the steepest fall the trough. Peaks and
troughs are *turning points* — the last good quarter before the fall,
and the last bad quarter before the rise. Read the signs in order.

### What makes an economy grow in the long run?

The cycle is the wobble; **economic growth** is the trend. Over
decades, what raises output per person — and therefore the material
standard of living — is not working longer hours but producing more
per hour: **productivity**. Three drivers matter most:

1. **Technology** — better ways of doing things: new machines, new
   processes, new knowledge. A farmer with a tractor produces more
   than a farmer with a hoe, and the difference is technology, not
   effort.
2. **Physical capital** — the stock of produced means of production:
   factories, machines, roads, computers. More capital per worker
   raises output per worker — but with diminishing returns: the tenth
   machine helps less than the first.
3. **Human capital** — the skills, education, and health of workers.
   Schooling and training raise what a worker can produce, and unlike a
   machine, human capital is carried by the person.

These three compound. A small difference in the growth rate becomes a
large difference in living standards over a generation, because growth
multiplies on itself.

### Worked example 2 — The rule of 70

The **rule of 70** estimates how long a quantity growing at a steady
rate takes to double:

**Doubling time in years ≈ 70 ÷ annual growth rate in percent**

Step 1 — At 2 percent growth: 70 ÷ 2 = **35 years**. An economy growing
2 percent a year doubles in about 35 years. (Check the readiness
guess: the answer is "double," and it takes 35 years to get there.)

Step 2 — At 4 percent growth: 70 ÷ 4 = **17.5 years**. The same
doubling in half the time.

Step 3 — At 1 percent growth: 70 ÷ 1 = **70 years**.

Step 4 — Apply it to living standards. Fictional practice data: a
country's GDP per capita is $12,000. At 2 percent growth it reaches
about $24,000 in 35 years; at 4 percent it reaches $24,000 in about
17.5 years. The growth rate is the difference between one generation's
living standards and two generations' — which is why economists care
so much about a single percentage point.

**A common error:** treating the rule of 70 as exact. It is an
approximation (it comes from the mathematics of compounding: ln 2 ≈
0.693). For classroom purposes, "about" is part of the answer, and
rates above about 10 percent make the approximation noticeably worse.

### Growth, cycles, and jobs: how the indicators connect

Two rough relationships tie the indicators together. **Okun's law**
states, as a rule of thumb, that when real GDP grows about 2 percentage
points faster than its trend, the unemployment rate falls by about 1
percentage point — output and jobs move together, but not one-for-one,
because firms first raise hours and productivity before hiring. And the
short-run **Phillips curve** states that, in the short run, lower
unemployment tends to come with higher inflation, and higher
unemployment with lower inflation — because a hot labor market pushes
wages and prices up. In the long run, the tradeoff fades: the economy
returns to its natural rate of unemployment, and the long-run Phillips
curve is vertical at that rate. Both relationships are tendencies with
exceptions, not mechanical laws; supply shocks (an oil price spike, a
pandemic) can raise unemployment *and* inflation together, which is why
Lesson 05 insists on reading all three indicators at once.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the diagram stays visible while the
  learner works the quarterly series; phase names are matched to the
  picture before any numbers are computed.
- **Alternate response mode:** the quarterly-series task may be
  answered by coloring the expansion quarters one color and the
  contraction quarters another on a drawn timeline.
- **Support:** if the rule of 70 is the sticking point, compute 70 ÷ 2
  and 70 ÷ 7 with a calculator first, then interpret the answers as
  years.
- **Extension:** look up one real long-run series (FRED: real GDP per
  capita, United States) and compute the average annual growth rate
  over a decade the adult selects. Compare the result with the rule
  of 70. Or research one historical U.S. recession's dates as declared
  by the NBER and check them against the two-quarter shorthand.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "The peak is the fastest growth" — no, it
is the turning point. (2) "Two negative quarters always means a
recession" — no, that is a shorthand; the NBER decides from monthly
data. (3) "Growth means everyone is better off" — no, GDP per capita
is an average and GDP misses distribution, leisure, and environmental
costs. (4) "The rule of 70 is exact" — no, it is an approximation.
