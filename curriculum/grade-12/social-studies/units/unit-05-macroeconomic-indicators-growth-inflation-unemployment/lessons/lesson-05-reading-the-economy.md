# Lesson 05 — Reading the Economy

**Unit 05 · Week 23 · Sessions S4 · ~50 minutes each** (S3 is the
data investigation; S5 is unit review and assessment)
**Standards:** D2.Eco.10.9-12, D2.Eco.11.9-12

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can combine GDP, inflation, and
unemployment into a sourced judgment about the state of an economy,
state each indicator's period, units, and revision status, name one
limitation of each indicator, and argue what "economic success" should
count.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, calculator, the three releases read in Weeks
20–22 (BEA GDP Q3 2026 advance; BLS CPI October 2026; BLS Employment
Situation October 2026) or newer ones the adult verifies.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. A country reports: GDP growing 3.4 percent, prices rising 0.4
   percent a month, unemployment 4.1 percent. Is the economy "doing
   well"? Write one sentence of judgment — then write one sentence
   about what could make that judgment wrong.
2. Which of the three indicators is reported as an *annual rate*, which
   as a *monthly change*, and which as a *level* (a percentage of a
   group)? Why does mixing those up produce nonsense?

(Adult: question 2 is the lesson's technical core. A 3.4 percent annual
rate is not a 3.4 percent quarterly rise; a 0.4 percent monthly rise is
not a 0.4 percent annual rise; a 4.1 percent unemployment rate is a
share of the labor force, not a growth rate.)

## Explicit teaching

### One economy, three instruments

GDP, inflation, and unemployment are three instruments pointed at the
same economy, and they disagree the way three instruments do: each
measures something real, each misses something, and each is reported
on a different clock.

| Indicator | What it measures | Reported as | Its clock | Its source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Real GDP growth | Change in production | Percent change, annual rate | Quarterly | BEA |
| CPI inflation | Change in consumer prices | Percent change, monthly (SA) and index level | Monthly | BLS |
| Unemployment rate | Share of the labor force without work and looking | Percent (a level, not a change) | Monthly | BLS |

A judgment that uses only one column is a guess with a citation. The
skill this lesson teaches is the combination: read all three, check
their dates, and say what they agree on before saying what they imply.

### Worked example 1 — Read the three releases together

*Real, verified data (checked 2026-11-13; the adult re-verifies at
teaching time).*

- **GDP:** BEA advance estimate, Q3 2026 (released October 29, 2026):
  real GDP rose at a **3.4 percent annual rate** (0.8 percent in the
  quarter), after 2.2 percent in Q2. Current-dollar GDP rose 5.6
  percent. The PCE price index rose 2.1 percent in Q3 after 5.0
  percent in Q2. Next release: November 25, 2026 (second estimate).
- **Inflation:** BLS CPI, October 2026 (released November 10, 2026):
  CPI-U rose **0.4 percent** seasonally adjusted in October after 0.6
  percent in September; shelter +0.3 percent; food +0.5 percent;
  energy +0.7 percent after +5.2 percent in September; core +0.3
  percent. Index level 337.511 (1982–84 = 100). **No 12-month change
  published** (October 2025 data not collected).
- **Jobs:** BLS Employment Situation, October 2026 (released November
  6, 2026): payrolls rose **165,000**; unemployment rate **4.1
  percent**; 7.1 million unemployed; the rate has sat between 4.1 and
  4.3 percent since March. Teenagers 13.9 percent; Black workers 6.6
  percent.

Step 1 — Align the periods. GDP covers July–September (a quarter).
CPI and employment cover October (a month). The releases describe
overlapping but different windows: the GDP release is about the summer;
the October releases are about October. A learner who writes "in
October, GDP grew 3.4 percent" has misread the clock.

Step 2 — Convert to comparable ideas. The 3.4 percent GDP figure is an
*annual rate*: the quarter's 0.8 percent growth projected as if it
continued for a year. The 0.4 percent CPI figure is a *monthly* change:
compounded over twelve months it would be about 4.9 percent a year.
The 4.1 percent unemployment figure is a *level*: a share of the labor
force, not a change at all. Three different units — annual rate,
monthly change, level — must be named as such before they are compared.

Step 3 — Combine, carefully. The three releases agree on a picture:
output grew faster in the summer than in the spring; consumer prices
kept rising in October but more slowly than in September; the labor
market was steady, with low unemployment and continued job gains. A
defensible one-sentence judgment: "In late 2026 the U.S. economy was
growing, with inflation still rising month to month but moderating,
and a steady labor market near 4 percent unemployment."

Step 4 — State what the judgment cannot say. It cannot say how the
year-on-year inflation rate stood (the 12-month CPI change is missing).
It cannot say whether the 3.4 percent GDP figure will survive revision
(the second estimate arrives November 25). It cannot say how the gains
were distributed (GDP is a total; the unemployment rate differs by
group — teenagers 13.9 percent against 4.1 percent overall). And it
cannot say whether people felt better off (GDP misses leisure,
household work, and environmental costs; the CPI basket is not any one
person's basket).

**A common error:** quoting the most dramatic number as the whole
story — "GDP up 3.4 percent, so the economy is booming" or "energy up
5.2 percent, so inflation is out of control." Both errors take one
column of the table and ignore the other two. The discipline is: three
indicators, three dates, three limitations, then the judgment.

### Worked example 2 — What should count as economic success?

Two economists look at the same three releases and disagree about what
"success" means. The disagreement is not about the numbers; it is
about the goal.

- **Position A (production):** success is rising real GDP per capita.
  If output per person grows, the economy can provide more of
  everything — food, housing, medicine — and the rest follows. The Q3
  figure (+3.4 percent annual rate) is the headline that matters.
- **Position B (well-being):** success is rising well-being: secure
  jobs, stable prices, time with family, a clean environment. GDP per
  capita can rise while leisure falls, inequality widens, and rivers
  are polluted. The unemployment rate, the CPI's effect on real wages,
  and what GDP leaves out are the numbers that matter.

Step 1 — Steelman each position: state it in its strongest form, as
above, before criticizing it.

Step 2 — Find the shared ground: both positions need the indicators.
Position A needs GDP; Position B needs GDP *plus* the other two
indicators *plus* distributional and environmental data GDP does not
contain.

Step 3 — Take a position with a named tradeoff: for example, "GDP per
capita is the best single summary of material living standards, but it
is not a well-being score; a success claim needs the unemployment rate
by group, the real wage, and one measure GDP misses." That sentence
uses the indicators and names their limits — the move this unit has
been practicing.

**A common error:** treating the argument as a matter of taste ("I
think GDP matters most"). A position is only as strong as the evidence
and the acknowledged tradeoff behind it. "GDP matters most because it
is the only indicator" is false — the other two exist. "GDP matters
most for material living standards, and here is what it misses" is a
claim that can be checked.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult. Hints are given; answers are in the answer
key.

1. A headline says: "Economy grows 3.4%!" A second says: "Prices up
   0.4%!" A third says: "Unemployment 4.1%!" Rewrite all three so a
   careful reader can tell them apart: name each figure's period,
   units, and source.
2. Using the three releases above: (a) Was inflation in October 2026
   faster or slower than in September, and by how much in percentage
   points? (b) Did the unemployment rate change much in October?
   Quote the release's own words.
3. A learner writes: "The economy is doing great because GDP grew 3.4
   percent." Name two facts from the other two releases that this
   sentence ignores, and one limitation of the GDP figure itself.

## Independent practice

Do these alone; check against the answer key afterward.

4. A fictional country reports: real GDP growth +1.0 percent (annual
   rate), CPI +0.9 percent in the month, unemployment 9.2 percent.
   Write a three-sentence judgment: one sentence per indicator, then
   one combined sentence. Name one limitation of the judgment.
5. Explain, in one paragraph, why the October 2026 CPI release's
   missing 12-month change matters for a learner who wants to know
   whether inflation is "high" — and why the monthly change (0.4
   percent) is not a substitute for it.
6. Take Position A or Position B from Worked example 2 and argue it in
   one paragraph, using at least one figure from each of the three
   releases and naming one tradeoff your position accepts.

## Applied task

Build a one-page "indicator dashboard" for the U.S. economy as of
November 13, 2026 (or a newer date the adult verifies). It must
contain, for each indicator: the figure, the period it covers, the
source and release date, the units, and one limitation. Add a
three-sentence judgment and a one-sentence statement of what would
change your mind. This dashboard is the draft of the project brief.

## Exit check

1. GDP is reported as an annual rate, the CPI monthly change as a
   monthly change, and the unemployment rate as a level. In one
   sentence each, say what each unit means.
2. Name one limitation of GDP, one of the CPI, and one of the
   unemployment rate.
3. What is the next release date for the Q3 2026 GDP estimate, and why
   does it matter?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the three releases are pre-marked at
  the headline paragraph; the table above stays visible during the
  combination task.
- **Alternate response mode:** the dashboard may be a table instead of
  prose; the Position A/B argument may be delivered orally.
- **Support:** if the units are the sticking point, rewrite each
  headline three ways (as a rate, as a change, as a level) and cross
  out the two that are wrong.
- **Extension:** compare the U.S. dashboard with one other country's
  latest releases (adult-selected, verified and dated) and write a
  comparability note: different baskets, different surveys, different
  base years. Or track the November 25, 2026 GDP second estimate and
  record how much the 3.4 percent figure moved — a live lesson in
  revision.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "3.4 percent GDP growth means output rose
3.4 percent in the quarter" — no, it is an annual rate; the quarterly
rise was 0.8 percent. (2) "0.4 percent monthly inflation is small" —
no, compounded it is about 4.9 percent a year. (3) "The three releases
describe the same month" — no, GDP is quarterly (Jul–Sep) while CPI
and employment are monthly (October). (4) "One good indicator settles
the question" — no, each indicator has a named limitation; the
judgment comes from the combination. Answer-key reference: Lesson 05
items in the answer key.
