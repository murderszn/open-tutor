## Explicit teaching

### What GDP is — and what it is not

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the market value of all **final**
goods and services produced **inside a country** in a given period,
usually a quarter or a year. Every word in that definition does work:

- **Market value** — things are counted at the prices they sell for, so
  different goods can be added together in dollars.
- **Final** — only goods and services sold to their final user count. A
  tire sold to a car factory is an **intermediate good**: its value is
  already inside the price of the finished car. Counting both would
  double-count.
- **Produced inside a country** — where the production happens matters,
  not who owns the factory. A car built in Ohio counts in U.S. GDP even
  if the carmaker is foreign-owned; a car built in Mexico by a U.S.
  company does not.
- **In a given period** — GDP is a flow over time, like income, not a
  stock at a moment, like wealth.

The economy is a loop — the **circular flow**: households sell labor to
firms, firms pay wages, households spend wages on firms' goods, and the
government taxes and spends alongside. GDP measures the value flowing
around that loop in one period. Three ways to measure the same loop give
the same total in principle: add up spending, add up incomes, or add up
the value each producer adds. This lesson uses spending, because its
components are the ones the news reports.

### The expenditure approach: C + I + G + NX

Total spending on final goods and services has four parts:

- **Consumption (C)** — household spending on goods and services: food,
  rent, haircuts, medical care. The largest part in the United States.
- **Investment (I)** — spending on things that produce future output:
  factories, machines, new houses, and business inventories. Note the
  economist's sense of the word: buying a share of stock is **not**
  investment here, because no new productive asset is created; building
  a new house **is**.
- **Government purchases (G)** — government spending on goods and
  services: roads, schools, defense. Transfer payments such as Social
  Security checks are **not** in G, because the government buys nothing
  with them; the spending counts later, when the recipient spends the
  money (as consumption).
- **Net exports (NX)** — exports minus imports. Exports are production
  inside the country bought by foreigners, so they add. Imports are
  spending on production abroad, so they subtract: GDP counts only what
  is produced domestically.

The formula is:

**GDP = C + I + G + NX**

### Worked example 1 — Compute GDP from an expenditure table

*Fictional practice data for the country of Meridia, in billions of
dollars, one year.*

| Component | Amount ($ billions) |
|---|---|
| Household consumption (C) | 12,000 |
| Private investment (I) | 3,600 |
| Government purchases (G) | 3,900 |
| Exports | 2,400 |
| Imports | 3,000 |

Step 1 — Net exports: NX = Exports − Imports = 2,400 − 3,000 = −600.
Meridia imports more than it exports, so NX is negative.

Step 2 — Add the four parts: GDP = 12,000 + 3,600 + 3,900 + (−600) =
18,900.

Step 3 — Check the shares: consumption is 12,000 ÷ 18,900 ≈ 63.5% of
GDP; investment ≈ 19.0%; government purchases ≈ 20.6%; net exports ≈
−3.2%. The shares add to 100% (63.5 + 19.0 + 20.6 − 3.2 = 100.0, up to
rounding). A share over 100% for one part is fine when another part is
negative — the check is that the parts add to the total.

**A common error:** adding exports and subtracting imports twice, or
adding transfer payments into G. NX is already exports minus imports;
enter it once. And a $1,000 government pension check is not a purchase
— it becomes consumption only when the pensioner spends it.

### Nominal vs. real: prices can lie about output

**Nominal GDP** values output at current-year prices. **Real GDP**
values output at base-year prices, so it tracks how much was actually
produced. The link is the **GDP deflator**, a price index with the base
year set to 100:

**Real GDP = (Nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator) × 100**

If prices rise and output does not, nominal GDP rises while real GDP
stays flat. That is why the news always quotes *real* GDP growth: it is
the change in production, not the change in the price tags.

### Worked example 2 — From nominal to real

*Fictional practice data.*

- Year 1: nominal GDP = $20,000 billion; GDP deflator = 100 (base year).
- Year 2: nominal GDP = $21,200 billion; GDP deflator = 104.

Step 1 — Real GDP in Year 1: (20,000 ÷ 100) × 100 = $20,000 billion.
In the base year, nominal and real GDP are equal by construction.

Step 2 — Real GDP in Year 2: (21,200 ÷ 104) × 100 = $20,384.6
billion. Check: 21,200 ÷ 1.04 = 20,384.6.

Step 3 — Growth rates. Nominal growth: (21,200 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 =
6.0%. Real growth: (20,384.6 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 = 1.9%. Prices rose
4.0%, so most of the 6.0% nominal rise was inflation, not more output.

Step 4 — Say it in a sentence: "Output grew about 1.9 percent; the
rest of the dollar increase was higher prices." A learner who reports
"the economy grew 6 percent" from these numbers has confused nominal
with real.

**A common error:** subtracting the inflation rate from the nominal
growth rate and calling the result exact (6.0 − 4.0 = 2.0). That is a
rough approximation, not the calculation. The exact real growth here is
1.9%, and the gap grows when the numbers are large. Always divide by
the index.

### What GDP misses

GDP is a measure of market production, not of well-being. Five gaps
matter for every judgment this unit will make:

1. **Household production.** Cooking, cleaning, childcare, and mowing
   your own lawn create value but are not sold, so they are not counted.
   Two countries with the same market output can differ greatly in
   unpaid work.
2. **The underground economy.** Cash work done off the books and illegal
   activity are not recorded, so GDP understates production where
   informal work is common.
3. **Leisure.** A country where everyone works 60-hour weeks can have
   higher GDP than one where everyone works 35-hour weeks and enjoys
   the extra time. GDP records the output, not the lost leisure.
4. **Environmental costs.** A factory that pollutes a river adds its
   output to GDP; the polluted river subtracts nothing. Cleanup spending
   then *adds* to GDP — the damage and the repair both count positively.
5. **Distribution.** GDP is a total. It does not say who gets the
   output. GDP per capita — GDP divided by population — is a better
   first proxy for the average person's material standard of living, but
   it is still an average: it hides inequality.

None of this makes GDP useless. It makes it a **narrow instrument**:
excellent for tracking production over time, misleading when quoted as
a score for how well people live.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) GDP counts all money that changes hands —
no, only final production; used goods, financial trades, and transfers
are excluded. (2) A bigger nominal GDP number always means a bigger
economy — no, compare real GDP, and compare per capita for living
standards. (3) Government spending means all government outlays — no,
only purchases of goods and services; transfers are excluded from G.
