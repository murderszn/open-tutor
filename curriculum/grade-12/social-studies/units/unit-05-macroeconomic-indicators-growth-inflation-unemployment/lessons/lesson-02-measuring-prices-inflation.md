# Lesson 02 — Measuring Prices: Inflation

**Unit 05 · Week 21 · Sessions S1–S2 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** D2.Eco.11.9-12

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can explain what a price index measures,
compute a CPI-style index and an inflation rate from a basket, convert
a nominal wage to a real wage, and explain who inflation helps and who
it hurts — including what the October 2026 CPI release could and could
not say.

Notebook, pen/pencil, calculator.

## Explicit teaching

### The price level is an average, not a price

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the **price level** — the average
of prices across the economy — not a rise in one price. A single good
getting more expensive while others get cheaper is a *relative* price
change, not inflation. **Deflation** is a sustained fall in the price
level; **disinflation** is inflation slowing down (prices still rising,
but more slowly). The distinction matters: in October 2026, U.S.
consumer prices rose 0.4 percent after rising 0.6 percent in
September — that is disinflation, not deflation, because prices were
still rising.

Economists track the price level with a **price index**: a number that
compares the cost of a fixed set of goods and services over time. The
best-known one is the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**, published monthly
by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). The CPI tracks a
**market basket** — a representative selection of what urban consumers
buy: food, housing, transport, medical care, clothing, recreation, and
more — and asks, "How much does this same basket cost now, compared
with the base period?"

The formula, with the base period set to 100:

**CPI = (Cost of the basket in the current period ÷ Cost of the basket
in the base period) × 100**

The **inflation rate** is the percentage change in the index:

**Inflation rate = (CPI this period − CPI last period) ÷ CPI last
period × 100**

Two details the release always states: the CPI is published **not
seasonally adjusted** and **seasonally adjusted** (seasonal adjustment
removes regular patterns, such as holiday prices, so one month can be
compared with the next); and the headline monthly change is usually
quoted seasonally adjusted, while the index *level* is quoted
unadjusted. In October 2026 the CPI-U index level was 337.511
(1982–84 = 100) and the seasonally adjusted monthly change was +0.4
percent.

### Worked example 1 — Build a basket, compute the index

*Fictional practice data. A small basket, priced in two years.*

| Item | Quantity | Year 1 price | Year 2 price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loaf of bread | 100 | $2.00 | $2.20 |
| Bus ticket | 50 | $1.50 | $1.50 |
| Haircut | 12 | $20.00 | $22.00 |

Step 1 — Cost of the basket in Year 1: (100 × $2.00) + (50 × $1.50) +
(12 × $20.00) = $200 + $75 + $240 = **$515.00**.

Step 2 — Cost of the same basket in Year 2: (100 × $2.20) + (50 ×
$1.50) + (12 × $22.00) = $220 + $75 + $264 = **$559.00**. Note the
quantities are fixed: the basket is the same in both years. That is the
whole point of an index — only prices move.

Step 3 — CPI in Year 2 (Year 1 is the base, CPI = 100): (559.00 ÷
515.00) × 100 = 108.54.

Step 4 — Inflation rate: (108.54 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = **8.5%**
(8.54%, rounded). Equivalently: (559 − 515) ÷ 515 = 8.5%.

Step 5 — Read it back: the basket that cost $515 now costs $559. The
bus ticket did not change price, the bread rose 10 percent, the haircut
rose 10 percent — and the basket as a whole rose 8.5 percent, because
the unchanged bus tickets dilute the increases. A single price never
tells you the inflation rate; the basket does.

**A common error:** using the new year's quantities in the Year 2
basket (people buy less of what got expensive). That is a real
measurement problem — called *substitution bias* — but the CPI's
definition fixes the basket on purpose, so the learner must use the
same quantities in both years for this computation. The bias is a
limitation to discuss, not a reason to change the arithmetic mid-way.

### Who wins, who loses

Inflation redistributes purchasing power. The rule is simple: inflation
helps people who **owe** fixed amounts of money and hurts people who
are **owed** fixed amounts.

- **Borrowers gain, lenders lose.** A $1,000 loan repaid in dollars
  that buy less is a smaller real burden on the borrower and a smaller
  real return for the lender. This is why unexpected inflation transfers
  wealth from savers to borrowers.
- **People on fixed incomes lose** unless their payments adjust. A
  pension of $2,000 a month buys less after a year of 5 percent
  inflation. A **cost-of-living adjustment (COLA)** — an automatic
  increase tied to the CPI — is designed to prevent exactly this.
- **Workers gain or lose depending on wages.** What matters is the
  **real wage**: the nominal wage divided by the price level. If pay
  rises faster than prices, the real wage rises; if prices rise faster,
  it falls — even though the paycheck got bigger.

### Worked example 2 — The real wage

*Fictional practice data.*

- Last year: hourly wage $20.00; CPI = 100.
- This year: hourly wage $20.80 (a 4.0 percent raise); CPI = 105
  (prices up 5.0 percent).

Step 1 — Nominal wage change: (20.80 − 20.00) ÷ 20.00 = +4.0%. The
paycheck grew.

Step 2 — Real wage this year, in last year's dollars: $20.80 ÷ 1.05 =
$19.81. (Divide by the price level as a ratio: 105 ÷ 100 = 1.05.)

Step 3 — Real wage change: (19.81 − 20.00) ÷ 20.00 = −0.95%, about
**−1.0%**. The worker's pay rose, but prices rose faster, so what the
pay buys fell by about one percent.

Step 4 — Say it in a sentence: "A 4 percent raise during 5 percent
inflation is a pay cut of about 1 percent in real terms." A learner
who reports "pay rose 4 percent, so the worker is better off" has
confused nominal with real — the same error as in Lesson 01, in a new
setting.

**A common error:** subtracting 5 − 4 = 1 and stopping there. The
subtraction is a good approximation for small numbers, but the exact
answer comes from dividing by the price ratio (Step 2). Use the
division; cite the subtraction only as a check.

### What the October 2026 CPI release shows — and what it cannot

The BLS release for October 2026 (published November 10, 2026,
USDL-26-1685) is a good first release to read, because it contains a
visible hole. Its headline facts:

- CPI-U rose **0.4 percent** on a seasonally adjusted basis in October,
  after rising 0.6 percent in September.
- Shelter rose 0.3 percent and accounted for over thirty percent of the
  monthly increase. Food rose 0.5 percent. Energy rose 0.7 percent
  after a 5.2 percent jump in September.
- Core inflation (all items less food and energy) was **0.3 percent**,
  unchanged from September and August.
- The index level was **337.511** (1982–84 = 100); unadjusted, the
  index rose 0.2 percent in October.

And the hole: **BLS did not publish 12-month percent changes for
October 2026**, because it did not collect survey data for October 2025
(a lapse in appropriations), so most October 2025 indexes do not
exist. The release says so plainly. A careful reader therefore cannot
say "inflation over the last year was X percent" from this release —
the comparison point is missing. That is not a flaw in the learner's
reading; it is a fact about the data. Every indicator has a
provenance, and a missing comparison is information, not a blank to
fill by guessing. The November 2026 release was scheduled for
December 10, 2026.

Two more limits to carry into the project: the CPI tracks *urban*
consumers, so it does not directly measure rural price experience; and
the fixed basket understates how people substitute toward cheaper
goods when a price rises (the substitution bias from Worked example 1).

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult. Hints are given; answers are in the answer
key.

1. Fictional practice data: a basket costs $180.50 in the base year
   and $189.10 this year. Compute the CPI and the inflation rate.
   (Hint: the index is the ratio times 100.)
2. A worker's wage rises from $25.00 to $26.00 an hour while the CPI
   rises from 200 to 210. Did the real wage rise or fall? Show the
   division.
3. A retiree receives $1,500 a month with no COLA. Prices rise 4
   percent in a year. What is the retiree's monthly payment worth in
   last year's dollars? Who gains from this inflation, and why?
4. Energy prices rose 5.2 percent in September 2026 and 0.7 percent
   in October 2026. A headline says "energy inflation collapsed."
   Is that the right word? Use the disinflation/deflation distinction.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the two formulas (index; inflation
  rate) stay visible; every computation is written as numbered steps
  with the numbers substituted before the arithmetic.
- **Alternate response mode:** basket computations may be shown as a
  table (item, base cost, current cost) instead of prose; the applied
  task may be answered as a labeled list instead of sentences.
- **Support:** if the ratio step is the sticking point, compute the
  basket costs first and check them by addition before dividing.
- **Extension:** compare the CPI with the GDP deflator as a measure of
  inflation (the deflator covers all domestically produced goods and
  services; the CPI covers consumer purchases, including imports).
  Which one would you use to judge what households feel, and why?
  Cite the BEA Q3 2026 release's PCE price index (+2.1 percent in Q3
  after +5.0 percent in Q2) as a third measure and explain why three
  measures of "inflation" can disagree.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "Inflation means everything gets more
expensive" — no, it is the average; some prices fall during inflation.
(2) "A 0.4 percent monthly rise is small" — compounded, 0.4 percent a
month is about 4.9 percent a year; always annualize before judging.
(3) "The CPI measures my cost of living" — no, it measures a fixed
urban basket; an individual's basket differs. (4) "Disinflation means
prices fell" — no, prices rose more slowly. Answer-key reference:
Lesson 02 items in the answer key.
