# Lesson 03 — Measuring Work: Unemployment

**Unit 05 · Week 22 · Sessions S1–S2 · ~50 minutes each**
**Standards:** D2.Eco.11.9-12

## Student goal

By the end of this lesson I can classify people as employed, unemployed,
or out of the labor force; compute the unemployment rate and the
labor-force participation rate; distinguish frictional, structural, and
cyclical unemployment; and explain what the headline unemployment rate
hides.

## Materials

Notebook, pen/pencil, calculator.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Answer in the notebook before the lesson starts:

1. A retired teacher, a college student who is not looking for work, a
   parent staying home with a toddler, and a worker laid off last week
   who is applying for jobs. Which of these four people count as
   "unemployed" in the official sense? Which are "out of the labor
   force"? Guess first; the lesson will check you.
2. If 100 people are in the labor force and 5 are unemployed, the
   unemployment rate is 5 percent. If 5 more people stop looking for
   work, what happens to the rate — and has the jobs picture improved?

(Adult: question 2 is the lesson's central trap. The rate falls when
people leave the labor force, which is why the participation rate is
always read alongside it.)

## Explicit teaching

### Three buckets, not two

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) sorts the civilian,
noninstitutional population aged 16 and over into three buckets, using
the monthly Current Population Survey of households:

- **Employed** — worked for pay (or profit, if self-employed) during
  the survey week, even for one hour; or had a job but were temporarily
  absent (illness, vacation).
- **Unemployed** — did not work, were available for work, and actively
  looked for work in the last four weeks (or were on temporary layoff
  expecting recall).
- **Out of the labor force** — everyone else: retirees, students not
  seeking work, people caring for family full time, people who have
  stopped looking, and people unable to work.

The **labor force** is employed plus unemployed. The **unemployment
rate** is:

**Unemployment rate = (Unemployed ÷ Labor force) × 100**

The **labor-force participation rate** is:

**Participation rate = (Labor force ÷ Working-age population) × 100**

The second rate is the guardrail for the first. If unemployed people
stop looking, they leave the labor force: the numerator and the
denominator both shrink, and the unemployment rate can *fall* while the
jobs picture gets worse. A falling unemployment rate means something
good only if participation is steady or rising.

### Worked example 1 — Compute both rates

*Fictional practice data for the town of Meridia.*

| Group | People |
|---|---|
| Working-age population | 80,000 |
| Employed | 47,500 |
| Unemployed (actively looking) | 2,500 |
| Out of the labor force | 30,000 |

Step 1 — Labor force: 47,500 + 2,500 = 50,000. Check: 50,000 +
30,000 = 80,000, the whole working-age population. The three buckets
add up.

Step 2 — Unemployment rate: (2,500 ÷ 50,000) × 100 = **5.0%**.

Step 3 — Participation rate: (50,000 ÷ 80,000) × 100 = **62.5%**.

Step 4 — Say it in a sentence: "Five percent of Meridia's labor force
is unemployed, and about three in five working-age residents are in the
labor force at all."

**A common error:** dividing the unemployed by the whole population
(2,500 ÷ 80,000 = 3.1%). That number is not the unemployment rate. The
rate divides by the labor force — the people who are actually in the
job market. Always find the labor force first.

### Why people are unemployed: three types

- **Frictional unemployment** — short spells between jobs: a graduate
  searching for a first job, a worker who quit to find a better match.
  It exists even in a healthy economy, because matching takes time, and
  some of it is a sign of a *dynamic* labor market, not a sick one.
- **Structural unemployment** — a mismatch of skills or place: the
  jobs exist, but not where the workers are, or not for the skills they
  have. A coal-region worker when the mines close, or a typist when the
  work is automated, faces structural unemployment. It lasts longer and
  is fixed by retraining, relocation, or time — not by a quick boost in
  spending.
- **Cyclical unemployment** — job loss because total spending fell: a
  restaurant lays off servers in a recession. It rises in contractions
  and falls in expansions, and it is the type that demand-side policy
  (Unit 06) targets.

**Full employment** does not mean zero unemployment. It means
unemployment is at its **natural rate**: frictional plus structural
only, with no cyclical unemployment. The natural rate is not directly
observable; economists estimate it from history, and the estimate moves
as the labor market changes. When the actual rate is near the natural
rate, the economy is roughly at full employment — which is why the
October 2026 U.S. rate of 4.1 percent was read as a fairly tight labor
market, not as a jobs crisis.

### What the headline hides

Four groups the headline rate does not count as unemployed:

1. **Discouraged workers** — people who want work but stopped looking
   because they believe no job is available. They are out of the labor
   force, so they lower the rate. The BLS calls them, with people who
   looked recently but not in the last four weeks, **marginally
   attached** to the labor force.
2. **Underemployed workers** — people working part time who want full
   time, and people working below their skill level. They count as
   employed, so the rate misses their slack.
3. **People in institutions and the military** — the civilian
   noninstitutional population excludes them by definition.
4. **People who want work but never enter the count** because the survey
   week or the four-week search window misses them.

None of this makes the rate useless — it is consistent, monthly, and
comparable across decades. It makes it **one** indicator, to be read
with the participation rate and with payroll employment (the
establishment survey's count of jobs, which rose by 165,000 in October
2026).

### Worked example 2 — What discouraged workers do to the rate

*Fictional practice data.*

- Labor force: 120,000. Unemployed: 6,000. Rate: 6,000 ÷ 120,000 ×
  100 = **5.0%**.
- Now suppose 3,000 discouraged workers — people who want jobs but
  gave up looking — are counted as unemployed, as some critics argue
  they should be.

Step 1 — New unemployed: 6,000 + 3,000 = 9,000.
Step 2 — New labor force: 120,000 + 3,000 = 123,000 (the discouraged
are added to the labor force too — they are in the job market in this
counting).
Step 3 — New rate: 9,000 ÷ 123,000 × 100 = **7.3%**.

The official 5.0% and the adjusted 7.3% describe the *same* labor
market; they differ only in who counts. A learner who quotes one
number without saying which definition it uses has not finished the
job. The BLS publishes broader measures (the U-4, U-5, and U-6 series)
that count discouraged and underemployed workers; the headline rate is
U-3. The adult can show the U-6 table in the same release — the point
is that the choice of measure is a choice, and honest reporting names
it.

**A common error:** adding discouraged workers to the numerator but
not the denominator (9,000 ÷ 120,000 = 7.5%). If a person counts as
unemployed, they count as in the labor force. Both sides move together.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult. Hints are given; answers are in the answer
key.

1. Classify each person as employed, unemployed, or out of the labor
   force, with one reason each: (a) a nurse working 40 hours a week;
   (b) a laid-off machinist applying for jobs daily; (c) a retiree;
   (d) a full-time student not seeking work; (e) a worker who stopped
   looking after a year without an offer; (f) a parent on paid leave
   from a job they will return to.
2. Fictional practice data: working-age population 200,000; employed
   118,000; unemployed 6,000. Compute the labor force, the
   unemployment rate, and the participation rate. (Hint: find the labor
   force first; the out-of-labor-force count is whatever is left.)
3. A factory automates and lays off 200 welders; the town's other
   employers are hiring welders, but for robot-tending work the laid-off
   workers are not trained for. Frictional, structural, or cyclical?
   Defend the classification in two sentences.
4. The unemployment rate falls from 6 percent to 5 percent while the
   participation rate falls from 64 percent to 61 percent. Is the jobs
   picture clearly better? Explain why not, in two sentences.

## Independent practice

Do these alone; check against the answer key afterward.

5. Fictional practice data: a country has 90 million employed and 6
   million unemployed, with a working-age population of 160 million.
   Compute the unemployment rate and the participation rate. Then
   suppose 2 million discouraged workers are added to both the
   unemployed and the labor force. Recompute the rate and state how
   much it changed.
6. Give one example each of frictional, structural, and cyclical
   unemployment, set in the same town, and explain in one sentence each
   why the type fits.
7. The October 2026 Employment Situation reported an unemployment rate
   of 4.1 percent, with teenagers at 13.9 percent and Black workers at
   6.6 percent. Write three sentences: what does the gap between the
   overall rate and a group rate tell you, and what does it *not* tell
   you without more evidence?

## Applied task

Your adult supplies the October 2026 Employment Situation release (or
a newer one, verified and dated). Find and write down: (a) the headline
unemployment rate and the number of unemployed people; (b) the change
in nonfarm payroll employment; (c) the unemployment rate for one group
(teenagers, or one racial/ethnic group) and the overall rate; (d) one
sentence from the release about what "changed little" means for the
month. Then write two sentences: does this release, on its own, show a
strong labor market, a weak one, or an uncertain one — and what other
indicator would you check next?

## Exit check

1. In one sentence, define the labor force.
2. 8,000 people are unemployed in a labor force of 160,000. What is
   the unemployment rate? Show the arithmetic in one line.
3. Name the three types of unemployment and give a one-phrase example
   of each.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the two formulas stay visible; every
  computation begins by naming the three buckets and checking they add
  to the working-age population.
- **Alternate response mode:** classifications may be given as a
  three-column table instead of sentences; the applied task may be
  answered as a labeled list.
- **Support:** if the rate formula is the sticking point, work with a
  small town first (labor force 100, unemployed 5 → 5 percent) and
  scale up.
- **Extension:** read the BLS "alternative measures of labor
  underutilization" table (U-1 through U-6) in the same release and
  explain, with numbers, how much slack the headline rate misses. Or
  research how the Current Population Survey is conducted (about 60,000
  households) and explain why the monthly rate is an estimate with
  sampling error, not an exact count.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "Unemployed means anyone without a job" —
no, only those available and actively looking; students, retirees, and
discouraged workers are out of the labor force. (2) "A falling
unemployment rate always means more jobs" — no, it can fall because
people stopped looking; check participation and payroll employment.
(3) "Full employment means everyone has a job" — no, frictional and
structural unemployment remain at the natural rate. (4) "The
unemployment rate counts everyone who wants work" — no, discouraged
and marginally attached workers are excluded from U-3. Answer-key
reference: Lesson 03 items in the answer key.
