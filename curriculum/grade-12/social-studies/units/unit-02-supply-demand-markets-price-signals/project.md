# Project — Design a Market, Then Test a Price Control

**The task.** You are the analyst for a fictional town, Riverside,
which is opening a new Saturday market. Your job, in a 3–4 page
brief plus one graph page, is to (1) model the town's apple market,
(2) predict what happens when something changes, and (3) evaluate a
proposed price control on that market — with the winners, the
losers, and the strongest argument against your own recommendation.
Use the unit's fictional Riverside schedules as your starting
point; any additional numbers you invent must be labeled as
illustrative, and any real-world comparison must use
adult-selected, dated, sourced figures.

## Part 1 — The market model (Lessons 1–2)

Present the Riverside apple market from the unit overview (prices
$1–$5; quantities demanded 100 down to 20 lb/week; quantities
supplied 20 up to 100 lb/week) as a schedule and as a graph, with
price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.
Mark the equilibrium ($3, 60 lb/week) and show, with numbers, the
surplus at $4 and the shortage at $2. In one paragraph, explain the
price-signal mechanism: what sellers do with unsold stock, what
buyers do when apples run short, and why the market returns to
$3 rather than staying at $2 or $4.

## Part 2 — One shock, fully traced (Lesson 3)

Choose **one** shock from this list (or propose another with your
adult's approval): a health report praises apples; a drought raises
orchard costs; the price of pears falls; a new picking machine is
invented; more growers join the market; buyers expect apple prices
to rise next month.

For your shock: (a) name the curve and the shift direction, with a
one-sentence mechanism; (b) draw the shift on a copy of your Part 1
graph; (c) predict the new equilibrium price and quantity
*directions*, and compute a new equilibrium if you state a new
schedule or equation (show the algebra, as in Lesson 3's worked
examples); (d) explain the adjustment path — what temporary
surplus or shortage appears first, and how price moves the market
to the new equilibrium.

## Part 3 — A price control, evaluated (Lessons 4–5)

The town council proposes either a price ceiling of $2/lb on apples
or a price floor of $4/lb on apples. Pick one and evaluate it:

1. **Binding test.** Show, with the equilibrium price, whether
   your control binds. (If you pick a control that does not bind,
   say so plainly and explain what would have to change for it to
   bind — and then evaluate a binding version instead, so the
   analysis is substantive.)
2. **The gap.** Compute the shortage or surplus at the controlled
   price from the Riverside schedules.
3. **Winners and losers.** Name at least two gaining groups and
   two losing groups, with a reason for each.
4. **Elasticity note.** Using Lesson 4, explain how elasticity
   would change the size of the effects in a real market: if apple
   demand were very inelastic, would the shortage be larger or
   smaller than in a market with elastic demand? (Think about how
   much quantity demanded moves when price is held low.)
5. **Evidence and judgment.** Compare your fictional case with one
   real price-control case your adult selects from sourced material
   (rent regulation, a minimum wage, or another cap/floor). In one
   paragraph, say what the simple model got right about the real
   case and what it missed. Then recommend for or against the
   Riverside control, and state the strongest argument against your
   recommendation in one paragraph. A recommendation that cannot
   name its best counterargument is incomplete.

## Deliverables

1. A 3–4 page brief covering Parts 1–3, with all numbers labeled
   as fictional Riverside data or as dated, sourced real figures.
2. A graph page: the Part 1 market graph (labeled, with
   equilibrium, surplus, and shortage marked) and the Part 2 shift
   graph.
3. A sources note: for the real case in Part 3, list each source
   with author or publisher, date, and what you took from it. Do
   not copy source text; paraphrase and cite.

## Rubric (40 points)

| Criterion | Full marks | Partial | Missing / weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market model (10) | Schedule and graph correct; equilibrium, surplus, shortage computed; price-signal mechanism explained | One computation or label wrong, or mechanism thin | Graph or equilibrium missing; gaps not computed |
| Shock analysis (10) | Correct curve and direction; mechanism stated; adjustment path traced through a temporary gap | Direction right but mechanism or path missing | Wrong curve, or prediction without mechanism |
| Price-control evaluation (12) | Binding test shown; gap computed at the control price; 2+ winners and 2+ losers with reasons; elasticity note correct | Gap right but winners/losers thin, or elasticity note missing | Binding test skipped, or gap computed from equilibrium quantity |
| Real-case comparison and judgment (8) | Real case sourced and dated; model strengths and misses named; recommendation given with its strongest counterargument | Real case unsourced, or counterargument missing | No real case, or recommendation asserted without evidence |

## Adult notes

- Approve the shock choice and the real case before the learner
  writes; preview sources for reading level, captions, and
  nonpartisan framing, and check any real price or wage figure
  against its dated source.
- Accept a table-only version of the graph page if graphing is the
  barrier; the economics (equilibrium, gaps, shift direction) is
  what is scored, not draftsmanship.
- If the learner picks the non-binding version of a control and
  stops there, return the brief: the rubric requires a binding
  evaluation.
