# Lesson 04 — Price Elasticity of Demand: How Strongly Do Buyers React?

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can compute price elasticity
of demand with the midpoint formula, classify demand as elastic,
inelastic, or unit elastic, and explain why elasticity decides
whether a price rise raises or lowers a seller's revenue.

**Time:** ~50 minutes (taught across W9S1–W9S2; the W9S3
investigation compares necessities and luxuries) · **Materials:**
a calculator · **Readiness check (2 min):** A coffee shop raises its
price 10%. Would you expect the number of cups sold to fall by a lot
or a little? What about a life-saving medicine raised 10%? Hold
both intuitions — today you will measure them.

## 1. Explicit teaching

The law of demand says quantity demanded falls when price rises. It
does not say *by how much*. **Price elasticity of demand** measures
exactly that: the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by
the percentage change in price.

> Elasticity of demand = (% change in quantity demanded) ÷ (% change in price)

We use absolute values and report elasticity as a positive number;
the minus sign on the quantity change is understood (price up,
quantity down). Then:

- **Elastic (Ed > 1):** quantity responds *more* than proportionally.
  A 10% price rise cuts quantity by more than 10%. Buyers are
  price-sensitive.
- **Inelastic (Ed < 1):** quantity responds *less* than
  proportionally. A 10% price rise cuts quantity by less than 10%.
  Buyers keep buying.
- **Unit elastic (Ed = 1):** quantity changes by the same percentage
  as price.

**The midpoint formula.** Percentages depend on where you start:
a rise from 80 to 100 is +25% from 80 but a fall from 100 to 80 is
−20% from 100. To avoid that starting-point problem, divide each
change by the *average* (midpoint) of the start and end values:

> % change in quantity = (Q2 − Q1) ÷ ((Q1 + Q2) ÷ 2) × 100
> % change in price = (P2 − P1) ÷ ((P1 + P2) ÷ 2) × 100

Work in decimals or percentages consistently; the ratio is the same.
Round elasticities to two decimal places and show both percentage
changes — an elasticity with no visible percentage changes cannot be
checked, and in this unit an unchecked number earns no credit.

**What makes demand more or less elastic?** Four determinants, and
you should be able to apply each:

1. **Availability of close substitutes.** Many substitutes → elastic
   (buyers switch away). Few substitutes → inelastic.
2. **Necessity vs. luxury.** Necessities (things buyers feel they
   must have) → inelastic. Luxuries (things buyers can postpone or
   skip) → elastic.
3. **Share of the budget.** A good that takes a large share of
   spending → more elastic; a tiny share (a pinch of salt) →
   inelastic, because even a doubled price barely dents the budget.
4. **Time.** Demand is more elastic over a long horizon than a short
   one: given time, buyers find substitutes, change habits, and buy
   different equipment.

**The revenue test — why firms care.** A seller's **total revenue**
is price × quantity sold. When price rises, two forces fight: each
unit brings more money, but fewer units sell. Elasticity decides
the winner:

- If demand is **inelastic**, the quantity loss is small, so the
  higher price wins: revenue **rises** when price rises (and falls
  when price falls).
- If demand is **elastic**, the quantity loss is large, so the lost
  sales win: revenue **falls** when price rises (and rises when
  price falls).
- If demand is **unit elastic**, the two forces balance: revenue is
  **unchanged**.

Predict the revenue direction *before* you multiply price by
quantity — that prediction is the proof you understand elasticity
rather than just arithmetic.

### Worked example A — apples: inelastic (Riverside numbers)

Riverside apple price rises from $2 to $3 per pound; quantity
demanded falls from 80 to 60 lb/week.

- % change in quantity = (60 − 80) ÷ ((80 + 60) ÷ 2) = −20 ÷ 70 =
  −28.57%.
- % change in price = (3 − 2) ÷ ((2 + 3) ÷ 2) = 1 ÷ 2.5 = 40%.
- Elasticity = 28.57 ÷ 40 = **0.71** (absolute value) → **inelastic**.

Revenue check: at $2, revenue = $2 × 80 = $160/week; at $3,
revenue = $3 × 60 = $180/week. Revenue rose, as the inelastic
prediction requires. Note the trap: revenue rose even though fewer
apples sold — that is precisely what inelastic means.

### Worked example B — concert tickets: elastic (fictional data)

A concert ticket price rises from $50 to $60; tickets sold fall
from 1,000 to 600.

- % change in quantity = (600 − 1,000) ÷ 800 = −50%.
- % change in price = (60 − 50) ÷ 55 = 18.18%.
- Elasticity = 50 ÷ 18.18 = **2.75** → **elastic**.

Revenue check: at $50, revenue = $50,000; at $60, revenue =
$36,000. Revenue fell sharply, as the elastic prediction requires.
The promoter who raised prices expecting more money got less — a
mistake an elasticity estimate would have prevented. (These ticket
numbers are fictional practice data, not a real promoter's figures.)

**Common error:** computing the percentage change from the starting
value only (for example, calling the apple quantity change
−20 ÷ 80 = −25% instead of the midpoint −28.57%). That gives a
different elasticity depending on direction, which is exactly what
the midpoint formula exists to prevent. Always divide by the average.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. Price rises from $10 to $12; quantity falls from 100 to 90.
   Compute elasticity with the midpoint formula and classify.
   *Hint: % change in price = 2 ÷ 11.*
2. Price falls from $20 to $16; quantity rises from 40 to 60.
   Compute elasticity and classify. Then predict whether revenue
   rose or fell, and verify by multiplying.
   *Hint: work with absolute values for the classification.*
3. A good has elasticity 0.25. A seller raises its price 8%.
   Approximately how much will quantity demanded fall, and will
   revenue rise or fall? *Hint: rearrange the elasticity
   definition — you do not need a schedule.*
4. Two goods: tap water in a town with no alternative source, and
   one brand of bottled water among twenty brands. Which demand is
   more elastic, and which determinant explains it?
5. **Error analysis.** A classmate computes elasticity for
   Worked example A as 20 ÷ 80 ÷ (1 ÷ 2) = 0.50 and calls demand
   inelastic. The classification happens to match — explain why
   the method is still wrong, and what value the midpoint formula
   gives.

## 3. Independent practice

1. Price $8 → $10; quantity 120 → 90. Compute elasticity,
   classify, predict the revenue direction, and verify.
2. Price $30 → $27; quantity 50 → 65. Compute elasticity and
   classify. Was this price cut a good revenue move? Show the
   revenue numbers.
3. A medicine (fictional data): price $20 → $30; quantity
   100 → 95 per week. Compute elasticity, classify, and explain
   the result with two determinants.
4. Explain, without numbers, why a seller facing inelastic demand
   can raise revenue by raising price, while a seller facing
   elastic demand cannot. Use the two fighting forces from the
   lesson.
5. Gasoline: explain why demand is more elastic over five years
   than over five days, naming the adjustments buyers make given
   time. (No computation required.)

## 4. Application

You run a small business selling a good you choose. Write a
paragraph advising yourself: estimate — with reasons, not a made-up
elasticity value — whether your demand is elastic or inelastic,
using at least two determinants. Then state whether you would raise
or lower your price to raise revenue, and what evidence would make
you change your mind. If you invent numbers, label them as
illustrative, not as real data about a real business.

## 5. Exit check

1. Elasticity is 1.40. A price rise of 10% will change quantity
   demanded by about how much, and will revenue rise or fall?
2. In one sentence, why does the midpoint formula divide by the
   average instead of the starting value?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult sets out the four-step computation
  (quantity change, quantity average, price change, price average)
  as a template; the learner fills one step at a time.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer elasticity questions with a
  worked table instead of prose, and explain classifications orally.
- **Extension:** verify the unit-elastic case on the Riverside
  curve — move price from $2 (quantity 80, revenue $160) to $4
  (quantity 40, revenue $160). Revenue is unchanged. Compute the
  midpoint elasticity for that move and confirm it equals 1.00.
  Then explain why revenue is maximized at $3 (revenue $180):
  solve revenue = P × (120 − 20P) and find its maximum.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (a) starting-value percentages instead of
midpoints; (b) forgetting the absolute value and reporting a
negative elasticity, then misclassifying; (c) "elastic" confused
with "stretchy" in the everyday sense — anchor it to the number
line: above 1, elastic; below 1, inelastic. If the exit check fails,
re-teach with Worked example A, covering the answers and revealing
one step at a time. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
