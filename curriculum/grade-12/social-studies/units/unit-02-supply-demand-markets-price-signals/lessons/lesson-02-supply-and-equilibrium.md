# Lesson 02 — Supply and Equilibrium: Where the Two Curves Meet

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can build a supply schedule
and curve, find equilibrium price and quantity three ways (schedule,
graph, equation), and explain why a market moves toward equilibrium
rather than staying in surplus or shortage.

**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** graph paper or a graphing
tool, a ruler, a calculator · **Readiness check (2 min):** From
Lesson 1, what happens to quantity demanded when price rises? Now
guess: what happens to quantity *supplied* when price rises? Write
your guess before reading on.

## 1. Explicit teaching

**Supply** is the relationship between price and the quantities
sellers are *willing and able* to sell over a stated period, all else
equal. **The law of supply:** all else equal, when the price of a
good rises, the quantity supplied rises; when the price falls, the
quantity supplied falls. Price and quantity supplied move in the
*same* direction, so a supply curve slopes upward.

Why? At a higher price, producing and selling is more profitable:
firms run extra shifts, bring higher-cost orchards or wells into use,
and new sellers enter. At a low price, only the lowest-cost sellers
can profit, so less is offered. Again, distinguish **supply** (the
whole curve) from **quantity supplied** (one point on it): a price
change moves you *along* the supply curve; it does not shift supply.
(Lesson 3 covers what does shift it.)

Now put the two sides together. **Equilibrium** is the price at
which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, and the
**equilibrium quantity** is that shared amount. At equilibrium,
no buyer who wants the good at that price goes without, and no
seller who wants to sell at that price goes unsold — in the model.
Equilibrium is not a judgment that the outcome is fair or good; it
is the point where the two plans match.

What if price is *not* at equilibrium? The gap itself moves the
price:

- **Surplus:** quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. Sellers
  hold unsold stock. To clear it, they cut prices; lower prices raise
  quantity demanded and lower quantity supplied, shrinking the
  surplus until it disappears.
- **Shortage:** quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. Buyers
  compete for scarce units; sellers notice they can charge more, so
  prices rise; higher prices lower quantity demanded and raise
  quantity supplied, shrinking the shortage.

That is the **price signal** at work: a surplus is a signal saying
"too high — lower the price," and a shortage says "too low — raise
it." No planner needs to compute anything; the signal does the work.
This is the mechanism behind the unit's essential question, and it is
also why Lesson 5's price controls matter: a control that blocks the
signal leaves the surplus or shortage in place.

### Worked example A — equilibrium from the Riverside schedule

| Price ($/lb) | Quantity demanded (lb/week) | Quantity supplied (lb/week) | Gap |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| $1 | 100 | 20 | Shortage 80 |
| $2 | 80 | 40 | Shortage 40 |
| $3 | 60 | 60 | **Equilibrium** |
| $4 | 40 | 80 | Surplus 40 |
| $5 | 20 | 100 | Surplus 80 |

Equilibrium price is $3 and equilibrium quantity is 60 lb/week —
the only row where the two quantities match. At $4, sellers offer
80 lb but buyers take only 40 lb: 40 lb sit unsold, so sellers cut
the price toward $3. At $2, buyers want 80 lb but sellers offer
only 40 lb: 40 lb of demand go unmet, buyers bid the price up
toward $3. Check the direction every time: surplus → price falls;
shortage → price rises.

### Worked example B — equilibrium from equations

The Riverside curves as equations are: quantity demanded
Qd = 120 − 20P, and quantity supplied Qs = 20P. Set them equal:

120 − 20P = 20P → 120 = 40P → P = $3.

Plug back into either equation: Q = 20 × 3 = 60 lb/week. Same
answer as the table — as it must be, since the equations describe
the same lines. Now test a non-equilibrium price with the
equations: at P = $4, Qd = 120 − 80 = 40 and Qs = 80, a surplus
of 40 — matching the table. If your equation answer ever
disagrees with your table answer, one of them has an arithmetic
error; find it before moving on.

**Common error:** calling the equilibrium quantity "the amount
sellers want to sell" or "the amount buyers want to buy" alone. It
is both, simultaneously — that equality is the whole point.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. A market: at $6, Qd = 30 and Qs = 90. (a) Surplus or shortage,
   and how large? (b) Which way will price move, and why in one
   sentence? *Hint: unsold stock forces sellers' hands.*
2. A market: at $2, Qd = 200 and Qs = 120. (a) Gap and name.
   (b) Describe the two movements (quantity demanded, quantity
   supplied) as price rises toward equilibrium.
3. Qd = 100 − 10P and Qs = 10P + 20. Solve for equilibrium price
   and quantity, then check your answer in both equations.
   *Hint: collect the P terms on one side first.*
4. Draw the Riverside demand and supply curves on one set of axes
   from the Lesson 1 / Lesson 2 tables. Label the equilibrium
   point (3, 60) and shade or mark the surplus at $4 and the
   shortage at $2.
5. **Error analysis.** A classmate says, "At $5 there is a surplus
   of 80, so the market will stay there because sellers like high
   prices." Explain why sellers cannot "stay there" — what happens
   to the unsold 80 lb, and what does each seller do next?

## 3. Independent practice

1. Schedule: prices $10/$8/$6/$4 with Qd 10/20/30/40 and Qs
   40/30/20/10. Find equilibrium price and quantity; name and size
   the gap at $10 and at $4.
2. Qd = 80 − 5P, Qs = 15P. Find equilibrium price and quantity.
   Then find Qd, Qs, and the gap at P = 5, and say which way price
   will move.
3. Explain in three sentences why equilibrium in this model is
   *stable*: what happens after a small push above it, and after a
   small push below it?
4. A friend says equilibrium means "everyone is happy with the
   price." Give a one-paragraph correction: who might still be
   unhappy at equilibrium, and why the model still calls it
   equilibrium?
5. Suppose sellers at the Riverside market could store apples
   costlessly forever. Would a surplus still push the price down?
   Argue both sides in two or three sentences, then give your
   verdict. (This is a model-limits question: the basic model
   assumes sellers want to clear stock.)

## 4. Application

Pick one market from your own life (snacks at school, a game item
traded online, tickets to a school event). Sketch its demand and
supply curves, mark a plausible equilibrium, and describe in a
paragraph what a surplus would look like in that market and what a
shortage would look like. Label any numbers you invent as example
numbers; do not present them as real market data.

## 5. Exit check

1. At some price, Qd = 70 and Qs = 50. Name the situation, size
   the gap, and state the direction price will move.
2. In one sentence, what does a price signal tell a seller who
   holds unsold stock?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** answer every graph question from the
  schedule table instead; the adult checks the table arithmetic
  before any plotting is required.
- **Alternate response mode:** explain the surplus/shortage
  mechanism orally with the apple stall image from Lesson 1, or act
  it out: the adult plays a seller with unsold apples and the
  learner decides the seller's next move.
- **Extension:** prove that the Riverside equilibrium is unique
  for straight-line curves: show that Qd − Qs falls by 40 for every
  $1 rise in price, so it can cross zero only once. Then find the
  equilibrium if demand becomes Qd = 140 − 20P (what changed in
  words?).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (a) surplus/shortage named backwards;
(b) price moving the wrong way ("shortage → sellers lower prices
to sell more" — no: they raise prices because buyers are
competing); (c) equilibrium read as fairness. Drill the pair
"surplus → down, shortage → up" with fresh numbers until it is
automatic, and re-teach with Worked example A if the exit check
fails. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 02.
