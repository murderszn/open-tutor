# Lesson 05 — Price Floors and Ceilings: When the Signal Is Blocked

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can analyze a price floor
and a price ceiling, including non-binding cases; compute the
resulting surplus or shortage; name who gains and who loses; and
evaluate the policy with benefits and costs.

**Time:** ~50 minutes (taught across W10S1–W10S2; the W10S3
evidence lab and W10S4 deliberation follow it) · **Materials:**
graph paper or a graphing tool, a ruler · **Readiness check
(2 min):** From Lesson 2, a shortage pushes price up. What if a law
forbids the price from rising? Write one predicted consequence
before reading on.

## 1. Explicit teaching

A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum price: sellers may not
charge above it. A **price floor** is a legal minimum price:
buyers may not pay below it. Governments use ceilings to keep
essentials affordable (the classic case is a rent ceiling) and
floors to protect sellers' incomes (the classic case is a minimum
wage — a floor on the price of labor).

The binding test decides whether the control does anything at all:

- A ceiling **binds** only if it is set **below** the equilibrium
  price. A ceiling above equilibrium is **non-binding**: the market
  price is already below the legal maximum, so nothing changes.
- A floor **binds** only if it is set **above** the equilibrium
  price. A floor below equilibrium is **non-binding**: the market
  price is already above the legal minimum, so nothing changes.

If you skip the binding test, every later number will be wrong —
the most common error in this lesson is computing a "shortage" from
a ceiling that does not bind.

When a control binds, the market cannot reach equilibrium, so the
gap from Lesson 2 persists:

- A **binding ceiling** (price held below equilibrium) creates a
  **shortage**: quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the
  controlled price. The price signal that would have rationed
  demand and called forth supply is blocked.
- A **binding floor** (price held above equilibrium) creates a
  **surplus**: quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the
  controlled price. In a labor market, that surplus is people
  willing to work at the floor wage who cannot find jobs at it.

Shortages and surpluses are not the end of the story; they are the
beginning of the evaluation. A control always creates winners and
losers, and a serious evaluation names both:

- With a rent ceiling, sitting tenants who keep their apartments
  pay less (winners), while would-be tenants who cannot find a
  place, landlords who earn less, and future builders who build
  less may lose.
- With a minimum wage above equilibrium, workers who keep jobs at
  the higher wage gain, while workers who lose hours or jobs, and
  employers (and possibly customers, through higher prices) lose.

That is the benefit–cost evaluation the C3 Framework asks for in
**D2.Eco.7.9-12** ("Use benefits and costs to evaluate the
effectiveness of government policies to improve market outcomes"),
and the unintended consequences — reduced maintenance under rent
ceilings, reduced hiring under high minimum wages, black markets,
lower quality — are **D2.Eco.8.9-12** ("Describe the possible
consequences, both intended and unintended, of government policies
to improve market outcomes"). This unit *previews* those indicators;
Unit 04 develops them. Two cautions: first, the size of each effect
depends on elasticities and on the real market's details, so no
lesson here states a real-world effect size as a fact — the Week 10
evidence lab uses adult-selected sources for that. Second, this
analysis is nonpartisan: it describes mechanisms and tradeoffs; it
does not tell the learner which policy to favor. The deliberation
asks the learner to weigh the tradeoff and defend a judgment with
evidence, including evidence against their own position.

### Worked example A — a binding rent ceiling (fictional data)

A city's apartment market (fictional figures): equilibrium rent
$1,200/month, equilibrium quantity 600 apartments. The city sets a
rent ceiling of $900/month.

Binding test: $900 < $1,200, so the ceiling binds. At $900,
quantity demanded is 800 apartments and quantity supplied is 450
apartments (both read from the market's schedules, supplied with
this example). Shortage = 800 − 450 = **350 apartments**: 350
households who want an apartment at $900 cannot find one.

Who gains and loses? Tenants in the 450 rented apartments pay $300
less than equilibrium — they gain. The 350 unhoused-at-this-price
households lose, as do landlords receiving less rent and anyone
hoping new building would add supply. Note what the shortage
number does *not* say: it does not say 350 people are homeless —
they rent elsewhere, share housing, or leave the city, and the
evidence lab is where the learner checks what actually happens in
a real case.

### Worked example B — a binding minimum wage (fictional data)

A local labor market (fictional figures): equilibrium wage $12/hour,
equilibrium employment 500 workers. A minimum wage of $15/hour is set.

Binding test: $15 > $12, so the floor binds. At $15, quantity of
labor demanded (jobs employers offer) is 400, and quantity of labor
supplied (people willing to work) is 650. Surplus = 650 − 400 =
**250 workers**: 250 people willing to work at $15 cannot find
jobs at that wage.

Who gains and loses? The 400 employed workers earn $3/hour more —
they gain. The 250 surplus workers lose, employers paying more
lose (or pass costs on), and some workers may lose hours even
while keeping jobs — an effect the simple model does not show,
which is why the evidence lab matters.

**Non-binding check (do not skip):** if the same city set a rent
ceiling of $1,500 (above the $1,200 equilibrium), the market would
simply trade at $1,200 and there would be no shortage. If the
minimum wage were $10 (below the $12 equilibrium), the market
would trade at $12 and there would be no surplus. A control that
does not bind is a law with no market effect — until equilibrium
moves and it starts to bind.

**Common error:** computing the gap from equilibrium quantity
instead of from the quantities *at the controlled price*. The
shortage in Example A is 800 − 450 = 350, not 800 − 600 or
600 − 450. Always read both quantities at the control price.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. Equilibrium rent $1,000, 800 apartments. A ceiling of $1,200 is
   proposed. (a) Binding or non-binding? (b) What is the market
   outcome? *Hint: apply the binding test first.*
2. Equilibrium wage $11/hour, 900 workers. A minimum wage of
   $14/hour is set; at $14, labor demanded is 700 and labor
   supplied is 1,050. (a) Binding? (b) Surplus or shortage, and
   how large? (c) Name one gaining group and one losing group.
3. Riverside apples: the town sets a price ceiling of $2/lb.
   (a) Binding? (b) Quantities demanded and supplied at $2?
   (c) Shortage size? (d) What would sellers do if the ceiling
   were lifted?
4. Riverside apples: the town sets a price floor of $4/lb.
   (a) Binding? (b) Surplus size? (c) If the town then buys up
   the surplus, who pays for that purchase? (Think about where a
   town's money comes from.)
5. **Error analysis.** A classmate says, "A rent ceiling of $900
   helps all renters." Using Worked example A, explain the two
   groups of renters the claim ignores, and why the claim fails
   even on its own terms.

## 3. Independent practice

1. Equilibrium price $8, quantity 300. A ceiling of $6 is set; at
   $6, Qd = 420 and Qs = 210. (a) Shortage? (b) Two likely
   unintended consequences — name and explain each in one
   sentence.
2. Equilibrium wage $13, 2,000 workers. A floor of $13 is set.
   Explain why this floor changes nothing, and say what would
   have to change for it to start binding.
3. A city debates a rent ceiling. Write two short paragraphs: one
   making the strongest case *for* it, one making the strongest
   case *against* it, each using at least one benefit–cost argument
   and naming who gains and who loses. (Your own view goes in the
   deliberation, not here.)
4. A minimum wage rises in a market where labor demand is very
   inelastic (employers keep hiring almost the same number).
   Predict: is the employment loss large or small? Explain with
   the elasticity idea from Lesson 4.
5. Sketch the Riverside market with a $2 ceiling: demand curve,
   supply curve, equilibrium point, ceiling line, and the shortage
   bracket between quantity supplied and quantity demanded at $2.
   Label every line.

## 4. Application

Choose one real price-control debate — rent regulation, a minimum
wage, or a price cap on another good — and, with your adult's help,
find two sourced assessments that disagree (the resource pack
explains how to select and preview them). Write a one-page brief:
the market and its approximate equilibrium (with source and date),
the control and its binding status, the predicted gap, the claimed
benefits and costs from each source, and one unintended consequence
each source mentions or misses. End with your judgment and the
strongest argument against it. Do not copy source text; paraphrase
and cite.

## 5. Exit check

1. A floor is set below equilibrium. What happens to quantity
   traded? Answer in one sentence.
2. A ceiling creates a shortage of 120 units. At the ceiling price,
   quantity supplied is 380. What is quantity demanded?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads each control scenario aloud;
  the learner answers the binding test before any gap is computed.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer gap questions from schedule
  tables only, and explain winners/losers orally with the Lesson 1
  market image as a prompt (who in the picture gains if apple
  prices are capped below equilibrium?).
- **Extension:** research, with your adult, one real jurisdiction's
  rent-regulation or minimum-wage history from two sources that
  disagree, and write a paragraph on what the simple model got
  right and what it missed (for example, quality changes, hours
  changes, or long-run supply responses). Keep the paragraph
  nonpartisan: describe mechanisms, not villains.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (a) skipping the binding test; (b) gap
computed from equilibrium quantity; (c) "helps renters/workers"
stated without losers. Reteach with the binding test as a chant —
"ceiling below, floor above, or it does nothing" — and re-work
Examples A and B with fresh numbers if the exit check fails.
