# Lesson 03 — Shifts vs. Movements: What Moves a Curve, and What Merely Moves Along It

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can tell a shift of a curve
from a movement along it, name the demand and supply shifters, and
predict the new equilibrium price and quantity for any single shift.

**Time:** ~50 minutes (taught across W8S1–W8S2; the W8S3
investigation classifies eight shocks) · **Materials:** graph paper
or a graphing tool, a ruler · **Readiness check (2 min):** The price
of apples falls from $4 to $3 and quantity demanded rises from 40 to
60 lb. Did demand change? Answer before reading on — this lesson
turns on getting that question right.

## 1. Explicit teaching

The readiness answer is: no, demand did not change. Only the price
changed, so buyers moved *along* the same demand curve from one point
to another. Quantity demanded changed; demand did not. Everything in
this lesson is a variation on that distinction.

**A movement along a curve** happens when the good's *own price*
changes and nothing else does. Quantity demanded or quantity
supplied changes; the curve stays put.

**A shift of a curve** happens when something *other than the good's
own price* changes, so that at every price, buyers want a different
quantity (demand shifts) or sellers offer a different quantity
(supply shifts). The whole curve moves left (decrease) or right
(increase).

### Demand shifters — what moves demand

| Shifter | Demand increases (shifts right) when… | Demand decreases (shifts left) when… |
|---|---|---|
| Income | Incomes rise, for a **normal good** (most goods: buyers buy more as they get richer) | Incomes rise, for an **inferior good** (buyers buy less as they get richer — e.g., instant noodles for some buyers) |
| Tastes and preferences | The good becomes more popular | The good falls out of favor |
| Price of a **substitute** (an alternative that replaces the good) | The substitute's price rises | The substitute's price falls |
| Price of a **complement** (a good used together with it) | The complement's price falls | The complement's price rises |
| Expectations | Buyers expect the good's price to rise, or their incomes to rise | Buyers expect the price to fall, or their incomes to fall |
| Number of buyers | More buyers enter the market | Buyers leave the market |

### Supply shifters — what moves supply

| Shifter | Supply increases (shifts right) when… | Supply decreases (shifts left) when… |
|---|---|---|
| Input prices | Inputs (labor, materials, energy) get cheaper | Inputs get more expensive |
| Technology | A better technique lowers production cost | (A lost technique is rare; treat cost-raising regulation here only if it raises cost — see taxes) |
| Taxes and subsidies | A subsidy (a payment to sellers) lowers effective cost | A tax on sellers raises effective cost |
| Number of sellers | More sellers enter the market | Sellers exit the market |
| Expectations | Sellers expect prices to fall, so they sell now | Sellers expect prices to rise, so they hold stock back |

A memory check that catches most errors: **for demand, the shifter
is about buyers; for supply, about sellers and their costs.** If the
story is about buyers' incomes, tastes, or alternatives, it cannot
shift supply. If it is about production cost, it cannot shift demand.

### Predicting the new equilibrium — the four single-shift results

Hold the other curve fixed and shift one curve. (The learner should
be able to reproduce this table from the graph, not just memorize
it — trace each case on paper.)

| Shift | New equilibrium price | New equilibrium quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Demand increases (right) | Rises | Rises |
| Demand decreases (left) | Falls | Falls |
| Supply increases (right) | Falls | Rises |
| Supply decreases (left) | Rises | Falls |

Why does a demand increase raise both price and quantity? The
rightward demand curve meets the upward supply curve at a higher
point: buyers chasing more of the good bid the price up, and the
higher price calls forth more supply. Why does a supply increase
*lower* price but *raise* quantity? More sellers competing push the
price down, and at the lower price buyers take more. Trace each row
the same way before trusting it.

**Two-shift warning:** if both curves shift at once, one outcome is
certain and the other depends on which shift is bigger. Demand up
*and* supply up: quantity definitely rises, but price could rise,
fall, or stay put. The unit assessment tests exactly this case, so
learn to say "ambiguous" confidently instead of guessing.

### Worked example A — a demand shift, step by step

A health report praises apples. Tastes shift: at every price,
buyers now want 20 lb/week more. The Riverside demand curve shifts
right: at $3, quantity demanded rises from 60 to 80 lb, while supply
at $3 is still 60 lb. A shortage of 20 lb appears, price is bid up,
and the new equilibrium lands where the new demand meets supply.
New demand: Qd = 140 − 20P. Set equal to supply Qs = 20P:
140 − 20P = 20P → P = $3.50, Q = 70 lb/week. Price rose ($3 → $3.50)
and quantity rose (60 → 70), exactly as the table predicts for a
demand increase. Notice the sequence: shift → temporary shortage →
price adjustment → new equilibrium. The shortage is the *mechanism*,
not just a label.

### Worked example B — a supply shift, and a non-shift for contrast

A frost damages orchards, raising apple-growing costs. Supply
shifts left: at every price sellers offer less. Suppose supply
becomes Qs = 20P − 20 (20 lb less at each price). With original
demand Qd = 120 − 20P: 120 − 20P = 20P − 20 → 140 = 40P →
P = $3.50, Q = 50 lb/week. Price rose and quantity fell — the
supply-decrease row of the table.

Contrast: the apple price itself rises from $3 to $4 *because of
that frost*. Quantity demanded falls from 60 to 40 lb — but demand
did not shift. The frost shifted supply; the price rise then moved
buyers along their (unchanged) demand curve. If you can say which
curve moved, why, and what then moved along the other curve, you
understand this lesson.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. Incomes rise in a town and residents buy more fresh apples.
   (a) Which curve shifts, and which way? (b) Predict the price
   and quantity directions. *Hint: apples are a normal good here.*
2. The price of pears falls sharply. (a) What happens to demand
   for apples? (b) What happens to equilibrium price and quantity
   of apples? *Hint: pears substitute for apples.*
3. A new apple-picking machine halves harvest labor time.
   (a) Which curve shifts, and which way? (b) Predict price and
   quantity directions.
4. A tax is placed on apple sellers. (a) Which curve shifts, and
   which way? (b) Why is this not a demand shift? (Answer in one
   sentence.)
5. **Error analysis.** A classmate says, "Apple prices rose, so
   supply decreased." Give two different stories that could both
   produce a higher apple price — one supply-side, one
   demand-side — and say what evidence would tell them apart.
   *Hint: check what happened to quantity: it falls in one story
   and rises in the other.*

## 3. Independent practice

1. For each event, name the curve, the shift direction, and the
   predicted price/quantity directions: (a) a celebrity endorses
   oat milk; (b) the price of oats rises; (c) more oat-milk firms
   open; (d) buyers expect oat-milk prices to fall next month.
2. Demand rises and supply rises by the same amount. What can you
   say for certain about the new equilibrium, and what is
   ambiguous? Explain with a sketch.
3. The price of gasoline rises. A student says demand for
   gasoline fell. Correct the statement, then say what *would*
   count as demand for gasoline falling.
4. Incomes fall during a recession. For a normal good and for an
   inferior good, predict each market's price and quantity
   directions, and explain the difference in one sentence each.
5. A drought hits wheat farms and bread prices rise. A bakery
   owner blames "greedy bakers raising prices." Using shift
   language, give a better explanation in three sentences, and say
   what happened to the quantity of bread sold.

## 4. Application

Find a recent price change in the news (your adult helps you pick a
sourced, nonpartisan business report — no opinion columns). Write
two competing explanations: one demand-shift story and one
supply-shift story. For each, state what would have happened to
quantity sold, and name one piece of evidence — a harvest report, a
sales figure, a cost figure — that would support or undermine it.
Conclude with which story you find stronger and why. Do not present
any unsourced price or quantity as a fact.

## 5. Exit check

1. The price of a good rises and quantity sold rises too. Could
   this be a demand increase? Could it be a supply decrease?
   Explain in two sentences.
2. Name the six demand shifters without looking back.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads each shock story aloud; the
  learner answers "demand or supply, left or right" before any
  price/quantity prediction is asked.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort eight printed shock cards into
  "demand," "supply," and "movement along" piles, then explain one
  card from each pile.
- **Extension:** work the two-shift case numerically — Riverside
  demand becomes Qd = 140 − 20P *and* supply becomes Qs = 20P + 20 —
  and show that quantity rises while price is unchanged. Then
  change one shift's size and show price moving.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (a) any price change is called a shift;
(b) "supply" confused with "quantity supplied" under a tax (a tax
shifts supply because it changes sellers' costs at every price);
(c) inferior goods handled backwards. The card-sort support is the
reteach if the exit check fails: insist on the sentence frame
"_____ changed, so buyers/sellers want _____ at every price, so
the _____ curve shifts _____." Answer-key reference:
