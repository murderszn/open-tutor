# Lesson 01 — Demand: the Law, the Schedule, and the Curve

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can build a demand schedule and
a demand curve from a description of buyers, and explain the law of
demand in your own words.

**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** graph paper or a graphing tool,
a ruler, the market-scene image below · **Readiness check (2 min):**
Name one thing you bought less of when its price rose, and one thing
you kept buying anyway. Keep those examples; you will test them
against today's law.

## 1. Explicit teaching

A **market** is any arrangement where buyers and sellers meet to trade
a particular good or service — a farmers' market stall, a website, a
stock exchange, or a whole city's apartment rentals. **Demand** is not
"how much people want" in general. It is a precise relationship: the
quantities buyers are *willing and able* to buy at each possible
price, over a stated period, with everything else held fixed.

That last clause matters. Economists say ***ceteris paribus***, Latin
for "all else equal." A demand curve answers: *if only the price
changes, and incomes, tastes, and the prices of other goods stay put,
how does the quantity buyers take change?* If incomes also rise, you
are no longer moving along one demand curve — you are looking at a
different one. Lesson 3 takes that up.

**The law of demand:** all else equal, when the price of a good rises,
the quantity demanded falls; when the price falls, the quantity
demanded rises. Price and quantity demanded move in opposite
directions, so a demand curve slopes downward.

Why? Three reasons, and you should be able to give at least two:

1. **Substitution.** When apples get expensive, some buyers switch to
   pears. When apples get cheap, some pear buyers switch back.
2. **Income effect.** A higher price means a dollar buys less, so the
   buyer's real purchasing power falls and they buy less of the good.
3. **Diminishing satisfaction from extra units.** The first apple after
   a long walk is wonderful; the tenth apple today is worth much less
   to you, so you will only take it at a low price.

Two distinctions that prevent most errors in this unit:

- **Demand vs. quantity demanded.** Demand is the whole relationship
  (the whole schedule, the whole curve). Quantity demanded is one
  number on it — the amount at one particular price. Saying "demand
  fell from 80 to 60" when only the price changed is wrong; quantity
  demanded fell, and you moved *along* the curve.
- **Willing *and* able.** Wishing for a sports car at $200,000 is not
  demand unless you would and could pay $200,000 for it.

alternative).** *Text-only alternative: A farmers' market stall
displays baskets of red apples. A stall keeper arranges apples while
several shoppers browse; one shopper carries an empty basket and is
deciding how many apples to buy. Two blank signs hang above the stall;
they show no prices.* Question: (a) Who are the buyers and who is the
seller in this scene? (b) What good is being traded, and what would
you need to know before you could draw this stall's demand curve?
(c) If the stall keeper raised the apple price tomorrow and nothing
else changed, what does the law of demand predict — and what would
have to change for that prediction to fail?

### Worked example A — one buyer's demand schedule

Priya buys apples by the pound each week. At $5/lb she buys 2 lb; at
$4 she buys 4 lb; at $3, 6 lb; at $2, 8 lb; at $1, 10 lb. Her demand
schedule is:

| Price ($/lb) | Quantity demanded (lb/week) |
|---:|---:|
| 5 | 2 |
| 4 | 4 |
| 3 | 6 |
| 2 | 8 |
| 1 | 10 |

Plot price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis,
mark the five points, and join them: a downward-sloping line. Check
the law: every time price falls by $1, quantity demanded rises by
2 lb. Note what the schedule does *not* say: it does not say Priya
buys 10 lb at $5, and it does not say what she would do if her income
doubled. Those are different questions.

### Worked example B — from individual to market demand

Three buyers — Priya (above), Marco, and Ana — shop at the same
market. At $3/lb, Priya buys 6 lb, Marco buys 10 lb, and Ana buys
4 lb. Market quantity demanded at $3 is 6 + 10 + 4 = 20 lb. Repeat
at each price and you have **market demand**: the horizontal sum of
the individual demands. That is why a market demand curve is flatter
than any one buyer's: a price change moves *every* buyer a little.

**Common error:** drawing price on the horizontal axis. By
convention in economics, price goes on the vertical axis even though
it is the "cause" — the opposite of most science graphs. Check your
axes before you plot.

## 2. Guided practice (with hints)

1. A market's buyers take 30 units at $10, 50 at $8, and 70 at $6.
   (a) Does this obey the law of demand? (b) What is quantity
   demanded at $8? (c) What is "demand" here, as opposed to quantity
   demanded? *Hint: demand is the whole set of three rows.*
2. Jamal says, "When concert tickets got cheaper, demand went up
   from 400 to 900." Correct his wording, and say what actually
   changed. *Hint: only the price changed.*
3. A shopper says she would buy a jacket at $40 but cannot afford
   it. Does her wish count as demand at $40? Why or why not?
4. Using the Riverside market demand numbers from the unit overview
   (100 lb at $1 down to 20 lb at $5), plot the market demand curve
   and read off quantity demanded at $2.50. *Hint: the points lie on
   a straight line — extend it between $2 and $3.*
5. **Error analysis.** A classmate draws a demand curve sloping
   upward and labels it "people want more of expensive things, like
   luxury brands." Explain the two mistakes: the slope, and the
   confusion between a status effect and the law of demand under
   *ceteris paribus*.

## 3. Independent practice

1. Build a demand schedule for a good you choose, with five prices
   and quantities that obey the law of demand. Plot it.
2. At $12, market quantity demanded for bus rides is 500/day; at
   $9 it is 800/day. (a) Is this consistent with the law of demand?
   (b) Give one substitution reason and one income-effect reason for
   the change.
3. Two buyers: at $4, A buys 3 and B buys 9; at $3, A buys 5 and B
   buys 12. Find market quantity demanded at each price and say
   whether market demand obeys the law.
4. Explain in two sentences why "everyone wants a cheaper phone"
   is not, by itself, a demand schedule.
5. A news report says "demand for ice cream soared in the heatwave."
   Is that a movement along a demand curve or something else?
   Defend your answer in two sentences. (You will confirm the
   vocabulary for this in Lesson 3.)

## 4. Application

Write a short paragraph (5–7 sentences) about a real market you know
— school lunches, ride-share rides, streaming subscriptions, or
another — explaining how the law of demand shows up in it. Name the
good, give a plausible price change, say what happens to quantity
demanded, and name one substitute buyers would switch to. Do not
state any real current price as a fact unless your adult has verified
it with a dated source; invented, clearly labeled example numbers are
fine.

## 5. Exit check

1. State the law of demand in one sentence, including "all else
   equal."
2. At $4, quantity demanded is 40; at $3, it is 60. Did demand change
   or did quantity demanded change? How do you know?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the worked examples aloud;
  the learner answers from the tables alone, without plotting, if
  graphing is the barrier today.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer the image activity orally, or
  point to the buyer, the seller, and the good in the picture.
- **Extension:** find the demand equation for Priya's schedule
  (quantity demanded = 12 − 2 × price) and check it at $4 and $2.
  Then explain why a real demand curve is usually drawn as a
  straight line only as an approximation.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (a) demand = desire, without ability to pay;
(b) "demand rose" used for any quantity increase; (c) price on the
wrong axis. Reteach with Worked example A if the exit check fails:
cover the schedule's price column and ask the learner to recover each
quantity from the curve, then reverse. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 01.
