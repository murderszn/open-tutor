# Answer Key — Unit 02: Supply, Demand, Markets, and Price Signals

For the guiding adult. Every computation below was solved
independently and reconciled with the lesson, practice, quiz, and
assessment texts. Accept equivalent wording that states the same
curve, direction, mechanism, or number.

## Lesson 01 — Demand

**Image activity.** (a) Buyers: the shoppers; seller: the stall
keeper. (b) Apples are traded; to draw the demand curve you need
the quantities buyers would take at each price (a schedule).
(c) The law of demand predicts quantity demanded falls; the
prediction fails if something else changes at the same time
(income, tastes, substitute prices) — the *ceteris paribus*
condition is broken.

**Guided practice.** 1. (a) Yes — price falls, quantity rises.
(b) 50 units. (c) Demand is the whole three-row relationship;
quantity demanded at $8 is 50. 2. Correction: "quantity demanded
rose from 400 to 900" — only the price changed, so this is a
movement along the demand curve, not a change in demand.
3. No: she is willing but not able, so it is not demand at $40.
4. Riverside demand is Qd = 120 − 20P; at $2.50, Qd = 120 − 50 =
70 lb/week. 5. Errors: (i) a demand curve slopes downward under
*ceteris paribus*; (ii) a status/luxury appeal is a tastes
difference (a different demand relationship), not a movement along
one curve caused by price.

**Independent practice.** 1. Accept any five-price schedule with
quantity falling as price rises, correctly plotted. 2. (a) Yes.
(b) Substitution: cheaper rides replace driving/taxis; income
effect: a lower fare leaves more purchasing power for rides.
3. At $4: 3 + 9 = 12; at $3: 5 + 12 = 17; market demand obeys the
law. 4. "Everyone wants a cheaper phone" states no quantities at
prices and no ability to pay — it is not a schedule. 5. A movement
is wrong: the heatwave changed buyers' wants at every price, so
demand shifted right; the price of ice cream did not cause it.

**Exit check.** 1. "All else equal, when price rises, quantity
demanded falls (and vice versa)." 2. Quantity demanded changed —
only the price changed, so buyers moved along the same curve.

## Lesson 02 — Supply and equilibrium

**Guided practice.** 1. (a) Surplus of 60 (90 − 30). (b) Price
falls: unsold stock forces sellers to cut prices. 2. (a) Shortage
of 80 (200 − 120). (b) As price rises, quantity demanded falls and
quantity supplied rises until they meet. 3. 100 − 10P = 10P + 20 →
P = $4, Q = 60. Check: Qd = 100 − 40 = 60; Qs = 40 + 20 = 60.
4. Graph: demand down-sloping, supply up-sloping, crossing at
(3, 60); surplus bracket at $4 (40 to 80), shortage bracket at $2
(40 to 80). 5. Sellers cannot stay: the unsold 80 lb accumulate;
each seller cuts price to clear stock, so price falls toward
equilibrium.

**Independent practice.** 1. Qd = 60 − 5P; Qs = 5P − 10;
equilibrium $7, 25 units. At $10: surplus 30 (40 − 10). At $4:
shortage 30 (40 − 10). 2. Equilibrium $4, 60 units. At $5: Qd = 55,
Qs = 75, surplus 20; price falls. 3. Above equilibrium, surplus
pushes price down; below, shortage pushes price up — so small
pushes reverse themselves. 4. Accept a paragraph noting that
buyers who value the good below the equilibrium price do not buy,
and sellers whose costs exceed it do not sell; equilibrium means
plans match at the margin, not that everyone is satisfied.
5. Accept a reasoned verdict: costless storage weakens the
clearance pressure, but sellers still prefer cash to stock and
storage is never truly free, so downward pressure remains, only
slower.

**Exit check.** 1. Shortage of 20; price rises. 2. A surplus
signal tells the seller to lower the price to clear stock.

## Lesson 03 — Shifts vs. movements

**Guided practice.** 1. (a) Demand increases (normal good,
incomes up). (b) Price rises, quantity rises. 2. (a) Demand for
apples decreases (pears are a substitute, now cheaper).
(b) Apple price falls, quantity falls. 3. (a) Supply increases.
(b) Price falls, quantity rises. 4. (a) Supply decreases.
(b) A tax changes sellers' costs at every price; it does not
change buyers' wants. 5. Demand-side story: a health craze raises
apple demand (price up, quantity up). Supply-side story: a frost
cuts supply (price up, quantity down). The quantity direction
separates them.

**Independent practice.** 1. (a) Demand increases (tastes).
(b) Supply decreases (input cost). (c) Supply increases (more
sellers). (d) Demand decreases now (buyers wait for the expected
lower price). 2. Quantity rises for certain; price is ambiguous
in general — it depends on the relative shift sizes (with
equal-sized parallel shifts, price is unchanged). 3. Correction:
quantity demanded fell (a movement along the curve). Demand
falling would mean less wanted at every price — e.g., more remote
work reducing driving. 4. Normal good: demand falls → price falls,
quantity falls. Inferior good: demand rises → price rises,
quantity rises. 5. Accept three sentences: drought raises wheat
costs, supply shifts left, bread price rises and quantity sold
falls; bakers are passing on costs, not creating the shift.

**Exit check.** 1. Yes to a demand increase (price up, quantity
up); no to a supply decrease alone (that lowers quantity). 2. The
six demand shifters: income, tastes/preferences, prices of
substitutes, prices of complements, expectations, number of
buyers.

## Lesson 04 — Elasticity

**Guided practice.** 1. %ΔQ = (90 − 100) ÷ 95 = −10.53%;
%ΔP = 2 ÷ 11 = 18.18%; Ed = 0.58 → inelastic. 2. %ΔQ =
(60 − 40) ÷ 50 = 40%; %ΔP = (16 − 20) ÷ 18 = −22.22%;
Ed = 1.80 → elastic. Revenue: $20 × 40 = $800 → $16 × 60 = $960,
so revenue rose — as expected for a price cut with elastic demand.
3. Quantity falls about 2% (0.25 × 8%); revenue rises (inelastic
demand, price rise). 4. The single bottled-water brand is more
elastic: many close substitutes. Tap water with no alternative is
inelastic. 5. The method is wrong because it divides by starting
values, making elasticity direction-dependent; the midpoint
formula gives 0.71 for Worked example A.

**Independent practice.** 1. %ΔQ = −30 ÷ 105 = −28.57%;
%ΔP = 2 ÷ 9 = 22.22%; Ed = 1.29 → elastic. Revenue: $8 × 120 =
$960 → $10 × 90 = $900, so revenue fell, as predicted.
2. %ΔQ = 15 ÷ 57.5 = 26.09%; %ΔP = −3 ÷ 28.5 = −10.53%;
Ed = 2.48 → elastic. Revenue: $30 × 50 = $1,500 →
$27 × 65 = $1,755, so the price cut raised revenue — a good move.
3. %ΔQ = −5 ÷ 97.5 = −5.13%; %ΔP = 10 ÷ 25 = 40%;
Ed = 0.13 → inelastic. Determinants: necessity and few close
substitutes. 4. Accept an explanation using the two fighting
forces: with inelastic demand the per-unit gain outweighs the
small quantity loss; with elastic demand the large quantity loss
outweighs it. 5. Accept: over five days, habits and equipment are
fixed; over five years, buyers buy efficient cars, move closer to
work, or switch modes — more substitutes and more time.

**Exit check.** 1. Quantity falls about 14% (1.40 × 10%);
revenue falls (elastic demand, price rise). 2. Dividing by the
average makes the percentage change the same in both directions,
so elasticity does not depend on the starting point.

**Extension.** Riverside $2 → $4: %ΔQ = (40 − 80) ÷ 60 =
−66.67%; %ΔP = 2 ÷ 3 = 66.67%; Ed = 1.00 exactly, and revenue is
$160 at both prices. Revenue = P × (120 − 20P) is maximized at
P = $3 (revenue $180).

## Lesson 05 — Price floors and ceilings

**Guided practice.** 1. (a) Non-binding ($1,200 > $1,000
equilibrium). (b) The market trades at $1,000, 800 apartments;
no shortage. 2. (a) Binding ($14 > $11). (b) Surplus of 350
(1,050 − 700). (c) Gainers: workers employed at $14; losers: the
350 surplus workers and/or employers paying more. 3. (a) Binding
($2 < $3). (b) Qd = 80, Qs = 40. (c) Shortage 40 lb/week.
(d) Price rises back to $3 and the shortage clears. 4. (a) Binding
($4 > $3). (b) Surplus 40 lb/week. (c) Taxpayers pay: the town's
money comes from taxes and fees, so the purchase cost belongs in
the policy's benefit–cost evaluation. 5. The claim ignores
would-be renters who cannot find apartments at the ceiling (the
shortage) and landlords/future supply; "all renters" wrongly
counts only sitting tenants.

**Independent practice.** 1. (a) Shortage 210 (420 − 210).
(b) Accept two of: reduced maintenance, lower-quality units,
black markets or side payments, reduced new building,
misallocation of apartments. 2. A floor at equilibrium does not
bind: the market already trades at $13. It starts binding if the
equilibrium wage falls below $13 or the floor is raised above the
equilibrium wage. 3. Accept two paragraphs with benefit–cost
arguments and named gainers/losers on each side. 4. Employment
loss is small: with very inelastic labor demand, employers cut
few jobs when the wage rises. 5. Sketch: demand and supply
crossing at (3, 60), a horizontal ceiling line at $2, and a
bracket from 40 to 80 at $2 labeled "shortage 40."

**Exit check.** 1. A floor below equilibrium is non-binding, so
quantity traded stays at the equilibrium quantity. 2. Quantity
demanded = 380 + 120 = 500 units.

## Practice set

**Guided.** A1. Yes, the curve slopes downward as price falls.
A2. Equilibrium $6, 40 units (Qd = 100 − 10P; Qs = 10P − 20).
At $9: surplus 60 (70 − 10). At $3: shortage 60 (70 − 10).
A3. Demand slopes down (substitution, income effect, diminishing
satisfaction); supply slopes up (higher prices make more
production profitable). B4. (a) Movement along demand.
(b) Demand increases. (c) Supply increases. (d) Demand increases
(inferior good, incomes fell). (e) Supply decreases.
(f) Demand increases. B5. Demand increase: price rises, quantity
rises. Supply decrease: price rises, quantity falls. B6. Accept
one demand story and one supply story with the evidence that
separates them (quantity direction; cost or taste reports).
C7. %ΔQ = −60 ÷ 170 = −35.29%; %ΔP = 3 ÷ 7.5 = 40%;
Ed = 0.88 → inelastic. Revenue: $6 × 200 = $1,200 →
$9 × 140 = $1,260, so revenue rose. C8. Revenue falls: with
inelastic demand, a price cut loses more per unit than it gains
in units. C9. The vacation traveler is more elastic: substitutes
(alternative destinations) and time flexibility; the business
traveler faces a necessity with no time to substitute. D10.
Shortage 250 (550 − 300); gainers: sitting tenants paying $700;
losers: would-be tenants and landlords. D11. Non-binding
($12 < $14): the market trades at $14, 1,200 workers. D12.
Taxpayers pay for the surplus purchase, which must count as a
cost of the policy.

**Extra.** 1. Equilibrium $5, 40 units (90 − 10P = 10P − 10).
At $7: Qd = 20, Qs = 60, surplus 40; price falls. 2. Quantity
rises for certain; price is ambiguous. 3. %ΔQ = −30 ÷ 285 =
−10.53%; %ΔP = 3 ÷ 16.5 = 18.18%; Ed = 0.58 → inelastic.
Revenue: $15 × 300 = $4,500 → $18 × 270 = $4,860, so revenue
rose. 4. A ceiling exactly at equilibrium does not bind; the
market trades at equilibrium and the shortage is zero.
5. With elastic demand, a price rise loses more units than the
higher price gains, so revenue falls. 6. Errors: the price rise
caused quantity demanded to fall (a movement along the curve),
not demand to fall; a leftward demand shift means less wanted at
every price, which an own-price change cannot cause. Rewrite:
"When the price of bread rose, quantity demanded fell."

**Retrieval.** 1. Law of demand: all else equal, price up →
quantity demanded down. Law of supply: all else equal, price up →
quantity supplied up. 2. Demand ↑: P↑, Q↑. Demand ↓: P↓, Q↓.
Supply ↑: P↓, Q↑. Supply ↓: P↑, Q↓. 3. Price falls; quantity
demanded rises and quantity supplied falls. 4. Quantity falls
about 4%; revenue rises. 5. Non-binding; shortage zero.

## Week 10 quiz (30 pts)

1. (a) Equilibrium $3, 60 lb/week. (b) Surplus 40 lb/week
(80 − 40). (c) Shortage 40 lb/week (80 − 40).
2. (a) Demand increases; price rises, quantity rises. (b) Supply
decreases. (c) Demand increases (complement cheaper); price
rises, quantity rises. (d) No shift: a movement along the demand
curve (quantity demanded falls).
3. (a) %ΔQ = (70 − 90) ÷ 80 = −25%; %ΔP = 2 ÷ 6 = 33.33%;
Ed = 0.75. (b) Inelastic. (c) Revenue: $5 × 90 = $450 →
$7 × 70 = $490, so revenue rose.
4. (a) Price rises for certain: both shifts push price up.
(b) Quantity is ambiguous: the demand increase pushes quantity
up while the supply decrease pushes it down; the net effect
depends on the relative shift sizes.
5. (a) Error: "demand fell" should be "quantity demanded fell"
— only the price changed, so this is a movement along the curve.
(b) Accept any genuine demand decrease: e.g., more people work
from home (fewer commutes), a cheaper substitute appears, or
incomes fall for this normal good — less wanted at every price.

## Unit assessment (80 pts)

**Task 1 (20).** Qd = 120 − 10P; Qs = 10P − 20. (a) Equilibrium:
120 − 10P = 10P − 20 → P = $7, Q = 50 bottles/day. (b) At $10:
Qd = 20, Qs = 80, surplus 60. At $4: Qd = 80, Qs = 20, shortage 60.
(c) At $10, unsold stock forces sellers to cut prices; as price
falls toward $7, quantity demanded rises toward 50 and quantity
supplied falls toward 50. (d) Accept a labeled sketch with axes,
both curves, and the equilibrium point (7, 50).

**Task 2 (16; 4 pts each).** (a) Demand increases; price rises,
quantity rises. (b) Supply decreases (higher input cost); price
rises, quantity falls. (c) Demand decreases (substitute cheaper);
price falls, quantity falls. (d) Supply increases; price falls,
quantity rises.

**Task 3 (12; 6 pts each).** (a) %ΔQ = (170 − 200) ÷ 185 =
−16.22%; %ΔP = 3 ÷ 13.5 = 22.22%; Ed = 0.73 → inelastic.
Revenue: $12 × 200 = $2,400 → $15 × 170 = $2,550, so revenue
rose. (b) %ΔQ = (650 − 500) ÷ 575 = 26.09%; %ΔP =
(36 − 40) ÷ 38 = −10.53%; Ed = 2.48 → elastic. Revenue:
$40 × 500 = $20,000 → $36 × 650 = $23,400, so revenue rose.

**Task 4 (16).** (a) Binding ($850 < $1,100); shortage 450
(950 − 500). (b) Binding ($16 > $13); surplus 350
(1,000 − 650). (c) Non-binding ($1,300 > $1,100): the market
trades at $1,100, 700 apartments. It starts binding if the
equilibrium rent rises above $1,300 or the ceiling is lowered
below the equilibrium rent.

**Task 5 (16).** (a) Price rises for certain (both shifts push
price up); quantity is ambiguous (demand up pushes quantity up,
supply down pushes it down). Accept a correct sketch description.
(b) Accept any reasoned recommendation: the ceiling binds
($2 < $3) with a shortage of 40 lb/week (80 − 40); gainers
include buyers who obtain apples at $2, losers include buyers
who cannot find apples at $2 and sellers earning less. The
counterargument must be the strongest case for the other side
(e.g., for a "recommend against" brief: affordable access for
low-income buyers and the equity case; for a "recommend for"
brief: the shortage and reduced future supply).
