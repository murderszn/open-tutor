# Unit 02 — Supply, Demand, Markets, and Price Signals

**Grade:** 12 · **Subject:** Social Studies (Economics and policy inquiry)
**Track weeks:** 7–10 · **Backlog:** [issue #57](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/57), section U02
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Prices are signals. When apples get expensive, shoppers buy fewer of them
and growers plant more trees; when apartments are cheap, more people want
them than landlords offer. This unit builds the market model that explains
those movements: **demand** and **supply** schedules and curves,
**equilibrium** (the price and quantity where the two meet), **shifts**
versus **movements along a curve**, **price elasticity of demand**
(how strongly buyers respond to a price change), and **price floors and
ceilings** (what happens when a price is not allowed to reach
equilibrium). Every worked number in this unit comes from one fictional
practice dataset — the Riverside farmers' market apple market — so the
learner can check every step instead of memorizing disconnected examples.
Real-world prices, rents, and wages are never stated as current facts in
this unit; the adult supplies dated, sourced figures whenever a real case
is discussed.

## Essential question

If no one is in charge of a market, how does a price end up telling
buyers and sellers what to do — and what happens when a price is not
allowed to do that job?

## Prerequisites

From Unit 01 and the diagnostic weeks, described as skills (no
cross-grade learner links):

- Compute and interpret opportunity cost; read a two-variable table;
  graph and interpret a linear function and its slope; work with
  percentages and ratios.
- Frame a claim with at least two pieces of evidence; distinguish a
  claim from its evidence and from an interpretation.
- Explain, in one paragraph, how incentives change choices (Unit 01,
  Lesson 3). This unit re-teaches every market concept from scratch
  rather than assuming it.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Build and read demand and supply schedules and curves** for a
   market, and state the law of demand and the law of supply in the
   learner's own words, with the *ceteris paribus* (all else equal)
   condition stated. (D2.Eco.3.9-12)
2. **Find equilibrium price and quantity** from schedules, curves, and
   equations; explain, with numbers, how a surplus pushes price down
   and a shortage pushes price up toward equilibrium.
   (D2.Eco.3.9-12)
3. **Distinguish a shift of a curve from a movement along a curve**;
   name the demand shifters and supply shifters, and predict the
   direction of the new equilibrium price and quantity for any single
   shift. (D2.Eco.3.9-12)
4. **Compute price elasticity of demand with the midpoint formula**,
   classify demand as elastic, inelastic, or unit elastic, and explain
   how elasticity determines whether a price rise raises or lowers a
   seller's total revenue. (D2.Eco.3.9-12, applied)
5. **Analyze price floors and ceilings**, including non-binding cases:
   compute the resulting surplus or shortage from a schedule, name
   who gains and who loses, and evaluate the policy with benefits
   and costs, including possible unintended consequences.
   (D2.Eco.7.9-12; D2.Eco.8.9-12 previewed)

In every unit the learner also develops compelling questions
(D1.1–D1.5.9-12), evaluates sources and their limits
(D3.1–D3.4.9-12), and communicates evidence-based conclusions
(D4.1–D4.4.9-12).

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: the **College, Career, and Civic Life (C3)
Framework for Social Studies State Standards**, National Council for
the Social Studies, Dimension 2, Economics, grades 9–12. The indicator
wording below was verified 2026-10-22 against two independent published
mirrors of the C3 indicator list (a Smithsonian Learning Lab standards
page and a school-district curriculum document reproducing the full
D2.Eco.1–D2.Eco.15.9-12 list); codes are exact and the quoted phrases
match those mirrors. No state adoption, accreditation, or alignment
certification is claimed; the C3 Framework is a reference framework,
not a national grade-by-grade content sequence.

- **D2.Eco.3.9-12:** "Analyze the ways in which incentives influence
  what is produced and distributed in a market system." — the core
  indicator for Objectives 1–4: prices are the incentives that steer
  production and distribution in a market system.
- **D2.Eco.7.9-12:** "Use benefits and costs to evaluate the
  effectiveness of government policies to improve market outcomes."
  — Objective 5 and the Week 10 evidence lab.
- **D2.Eco.8.9-12:** "Describe the possible consequences, both
  intended and unintended, of government policies to improve market
  outcomes." — previewed in Objective 5 and Lesson 5; developed fully
  in Unit 04.
- **D1, D3, D4 (9–12):** the inquiry arc — compelling questions,
  source evaluation, and evidence-based communication — runs through
  the project and the Week 10 deliberation.

Internal reference: the repository's
[supply and demand economics guide](../../../../../resources/supply_and_demand_economics.md)
is an adult-side reference only. Its "kid-friendly" framing targets
grades 5–8, so the adult re-levels every explanation for grade 12 and
never assigns it as lesson text. Its shift lists and market vocabulary
were cross-checked against the lessons here; where the guide simplifies
(e.g., it presents no elasticity formula), this unit's treatment
governs.

**Fictional practice data — read this before any lesson.** All apple
schedules, rent figures, and wage figures in the lessons, practice,
quiz, and assessment are invented for practice and are labeled as
such wherever they appear. They are not observations, not market data,
and not current prices. The one dataset used throughout is the
Riverside farmers' market apple market, per week:

| Price per pound | Quantity demanded (lb/week) | Quantity supplied (lb/week) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| $1 | 100 | 20 |
| $2 | 80 | 40 |
| $3 | 60 | 60 |
| $4 | 40 | 80 |
| $5 | 20 | 100 |

As equations: quantity demanded = 120 − 20 × price; quantity supplied
= 20 × price. Equilibrium: price $3, quantity 60 lb/week. At $4 the
surplus is 40 lb/week; at $2 the shortage is 40 lb/week. Any real
market the learner studies in the project must use adult-selected,
dated, sourced figures instead.

## 20-session pacing (4 weeks × 5 sessions, ~50 minutes each)

Sessions follow the track scope-and-sequence for Weeks 7–10. Lessons
1–5 carry the new teaching; the remaining sessions are investigations,
writing, review, and assessment — each with a named activity and a
success criterion, not a generic "practice" label.

| Session | Activity | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| W7S1 | Lesson 1 — demand: schedules, curves, the law of demand | Learner plots a demand curve from a schedule and explains one row in words |
| W7S2 | Lesson 2 — supply and equilibrium | Learner finds equilibrium from schedules and explains why it is stable |
| W7S3 | Investigation — build both curves from the Riverside schedule and find equilibrium | A labeled graph with equilibrium marked and the surplus/shortage at $2 and $4 computed |
| W7S4 | Writing — one paragraph explaining what equilibrium means, using the Riverside numbers | Paragraph states the mechanism (surplus → price falls; shortage → price rises), not just the definition |
| W7S5 | Review + check — demand/supply vocabulary and curve-reading | Learner reads quantities off both curves at three prices without error |
| W8S1 | Lesson 3, part 1 — demand shifters | Learner classifies four demand shocks and predicts price/quantity direction |
| W8S2 | Lesson 3, part 2 — supply shifters; shifts vs. movements | Learner explains why an own-price change moves along a curve instead of shifting it |
| W8S3 | Investigation — classify eight shocks as shifters or movements and predict price/quantity effects | All eight classified with a one-sentence mechanism each |
| W8S4 | Argument — one current price change, two competing explanations | Short argument names a demand-side and a supply-side explanation and says what evidence would separate them |
| W8S5 | Review + check — shift-prediction table from memory, then corrected | Learner reproduces the four single-shift predictions (demand ↑/↓, supply ↑/↓) |
| W9S1 | Lesson 4, part 1 — elasticity as responsiveness; the midpoint formula | Learner computes one elasticity step by step, showing both percentage changes |
| W9S2 | Lesson 4, part 2 — elastic vs. inelastic; the revenue test | Learner predicts, before computing revenue, whether a price rise helps or hurts revenue |
| W9S3 | Investigation — why necessities differ from luxuries: compute and explain | Two elasticities computed and the necessity/luxury difference explained with substitutes and time |
| W9S4 | Writing — one paragraph on how elasticity changes a firm's pricing decision | Paragraph ties the elasticity value to a revenue prediction |
| W9S5 | Review + check — elasticity computations and classifications | Three fresh computations, all classified correctly |
| W10S1 | Lesson 5, part 1 — price floors and ceilings, binding vs. non-binding | Learner states the binding test (ceiling below equilibrium; floor above it) |
| W10S2 | Lesson 5, part 2 — worked examples: a rent ceiling's shortage, a minimum wage's surplus | Both gaps computed from schedules and graphed |
| W10S3 | Evidence lab — two sourced assessments of one price-control policy | A two-column note: what each source claims, what evidence it offers, what it does not show |
| W10S4 | Deliberation — who gains and who loses under the control? | Learner names at least two gaining groups and two losing groups with reasons |
| W10S5 | Unit review + unit assessment | See [assessments/unit-assessment.md](assessments/unit-assessment.md) |

Retrieval is built in: each week's review session re-asks one prior
week's idea (W8S5 re-asks equilibrium; W9S5 re-asks a shift prediction;
W10S5 re-asks an elasticity computation), and the unit assessment
mixes all five objectives.

## Accessibility and supports

- Every graph task has a table alternative: the learner may answer
  from the schedule alone, and every curve question is also asked as
  a numbers question.
- The Lesson 1 image activity has a full text-only alternative with
  the same question and the same data.
- Key terms are defined where they first appear and again in the
  vocabulary list; the adult reads lesson text aloud on request and
  accepts spoken, drawn, or typed responses.
- Extensions in each lesson add a second shock, a revenue proof, or
  a real-data version of the fictional case for learners who finish
  early; they are never prerequisites for the next lesson.
