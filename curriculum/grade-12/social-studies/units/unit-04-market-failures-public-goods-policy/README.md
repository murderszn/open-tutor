# Unit 04 — Market Failures, Public Goods, and Policy Tradeoffs

**Grade:** 12 · **Subject:** Social Studies (Economics and policy inquiry)
**Track weeks:** 15–18 · **Backlog:** [issue #57](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/57), section U04
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Markets coordinate most production well — Units 01–03 showed how. This
unit asks where that coordination breaks down, and what, if anything,
should be done about it. First, externalities: when a buyer or seller
does not pay the full cost, or does not capture the full benefit, of a
choice, the market quantity is wrong, and the gap can be drawn, measured,
and priced. Then public goods and common resources: two tests —
excludability and rivalry — explain why some valuable things are
under-provided and others are overused. Then information failures and
market power: what happens when one side knows more than the other, or
when one firm can set the price. Then the policy toolkit — taxes,
subsidies, regulation, cap-and-trade, direct provision — judged not by
intentions but by benefits, costs, and marginal checks. Finally, who
pays and who benefits: tax incidence, equity versus efficiency, and the
institutions (property rights, the rule of law, contract enforcement)
without which neither markets nor policies work well.

## Essential question

When the market gets a quantity wrong, how do you tell — and how do you
decide whether a policy fix is worth its own costs and side effects?

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–03 and the diagnostic weeks, described as skills (no
cross-grade learner links):

- Graph a linear demand and supply relationship, find equilibrium, and
  explain surplus and shortage pressure (Unit 02).
- Use marginal reasoning: expand an activity while marginal benefit
  exceeds marginal cost; stop when they are equal (Unit 01, Lesson 4).
- Compute and interpret a percentage change and a share of income
  (diagnostic weeks; Unit 02, Lesson 4).
- Classify a market by number of sellers, product differences, and
  barriers to entry, and explain why a monopolist restricts output
  (Unit 03, Lesson 4).
- Frame a claim with at least two pieces of evidence; distinguish a
  claim from its evidence and from an interpretation.
- Explain, in one paragraph, how a single demand or supply shift moves
  equilibrium price and quantity (Unit 02, Lesson 3). This unit
  re-teaches every market-failure and policy concept from scratch
  rather than assuming it.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Diagnose an externality** — classify it as positive or negative,
   and as arising in production or consumption; draw the private and
   social curves, find the market and efficient quantities, compute
   the deadweight-loss triangle, and state the Pigouvian tax or subsidy
   that restores efficiency. (D2.Eco.6.9-12)
2. **Classify goods with the two tests** — excludability and rivalry —
   into private goods, public goods, club goods, and common resources;
   explain the free-rider problem and the tragedy of the commons, and
   explain why markets under-provide public goods and overuse common
   resources. (D2.Eco.6.9-12)
3. **Explain information failures and market power** — adverse
   selection, moral hazard, signaling and screening, natural monopoly,
   and network effects — and show, with a worked example, how each one
   can shrink or unravel a market. (D2.Eco.6.9-12; D2.Eco.4.9-12)
4. **Evaluate a policy with benefit–cost reasoning** — compare taxes,
   subsidies, regulation, cap-and-trade, and direct provision on the
   same failure; apply a marginal check to an expansion decision; and
   name at least one intended and one unintended consequence of a real
   policy, from two sourced perspectives. (D2.Eco.7.9-12; D2.Eco.8.9-12)
5. **Analyze who pays and who benefits** — compute statutory versus
   economic tax incidence from a price change, judge a tax as
   regressive, proportional, or progressive as a share of income, and
   defend a policy choice that names its equity–efficiency tradeoff
   explicitly. (D2.Eco.7.9-12; D2.Eco.8.9-12)
6. **Explain the institutional precondition** — how clearly defined
   property rights, the rule of law, and contract enforcement make
   both markets and policy remedies possible, and what fails when any
   of the three is weak. (D2.Eco.9.9-12)

In every unit the learner also develops compelling questions
(D1.1–D1.5.9-12), evaluates sources and their limits
(D3.1–D3.4.9-12), and communicates evidence-based conclusions
(D4.1–D4.4.9-12; D4.6.9-12).

## Standards and source notes

Reference framework: the **College, Career, and Civic Life (C3)
Framework for Social Studies State Standards**, National Council for
the Social Studies, Dimension 2, Economics, grades 9–12. The C3
Framework landing page was opened 2026-11-04
(<https://www.socialstudies.org/standards/c3>); the indicator
descriptions below follow the wording in the track audit's
source-backed crosswalk (2026-10-09), which the Unit 02 author
re-verified 2026-10-22 against published mirrors of the C3 indicator
list. No state adoption, accreditation, or alignment certification is
claimed; the C3 Framework is a reference framework, not a national
grade-by-grade content sequence.

- **D2.Eco.6.9-12:** generate explanations for a government role in
  markets when market inefficiencies exist — Objectives 1–3: the
  inefficiencies diagnosed here are externalities, public goods,
  common resources, information failures, and market power.
- **D2.Eco.7.9-12:** use benefits and costs to evaluate whether
  government policies improve market outcomes — Objectives 4–5: the
  policy toolkit is judged by total and marginal benefit–cost checks,
  not by intentions.
- **D2.Eco.8.9-12:** describe intended and unintended consequences of
  government policies to improve market outcomes — Objectives 4–5:
  every policy evaluation in this unit names both.
- **D2.Eco.9.9-12:** describe the roles of institutions such as
  clearly defined property rights and the rule of law in a market
  economy — Objective 6: institutions are the precondition for both
  market and policy solutions.
- **D2.Eco.4.9-12:** evaluate how much competition exists among sellers
  and among buyers in specific markets — Objective 3: natural monopoly
  and network effects extend the Unit 03 market-structures analysis.
- **D1.1–D1.5.9-12; D3.1–D3.4.9-12; D4.1–D4.4.9-12; D4.6.9-12:**
  inquiry, evidence, and communication, practiced in the Week 16 case
  investigation, the Week 17 evidence lab, and the project.

All numbers in lessons, practice, quiz, and assessment are labeled
**fictional practice data** unless a source is named. No current price,
rate, or policy figure is asserted: the adult re-verifies any live
figure at teaching time and dates it. All learner-facing example
sentences and scenarios are original. Policy examples are presented
without endorsing parties, candidates, or causes; contested questions
get multiple sourced perspectives, and the adult selects the Week 16
case and the Week 17 policy for age fit.

## Differentiation, supports, and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** every lesson defines vocabulary in
  context before using it in a computation; graph-reading steps are
  numbered; the adult may read a worked example aloud while the
  learner tracks the curves.
- **Alternate response modes:** classifications and incidence splits
  may be answered in a table, a labeled graph, or a short paragraph;
  the Week 16 argument and the project may be delivered orally with
  the adult scribing the evidence table.
- **Support:** a learner who cannot yet read a linear graph starts
  from the schedule (table) version of each worked example and plots
  two points before reading the curves.
- **Extension:** derive the Pigouvian tax when the external cost rises
  with quantity (a sloped MEC curve); research one real cap-and-trade
  program's permit price history and explain, with dates, why the
  price moved; or extend the project brief with a second policy
  option and a sensitivity check (what if benefits are 20% lower?).
