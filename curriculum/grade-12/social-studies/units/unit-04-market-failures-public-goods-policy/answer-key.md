## Lesson 01 — Guided practice

1. MSC = MPC + MEC = (20 + Q) + 10 = 30 + Q. Market: 80 − Q = 20 + Q →
   Qm = 30, Pm = 50. Efficient: 80 − Q = 30 + Q → Q\* = 25, P\* = 55.
   DWL: base (30 − 25) = 5, height = MEC = 10 → ½ × 5 × 10 = $25.
2. MSB = (60 − Q) + 10 = 70 − Q. Market: 60 − Q = 10 + Q → Qm = 25,
   Pm = 35. Efficient: 70 − Q = 10 + Q → Q\* = 30, P\* = 40. DWL:
   base 5, height 10 → $25. Pigouvian subsidy = MEB = $10 per shot.
3. Acceptable answer: "A negative externality puts MSC above MPC, so
   the market settles where MPB = MPC — to the right of (above) the
   efficient quantity. The market overproduces, because producers
   ignore the cost they impose on others." The error is the direction:
   over-, not under-, production.

## Lesson 02 — Illustration activity

1. (a) Fish in the lake: **common resource** — rival (each fish caught
   leaves one fewer) and nonexcludable (open access, no ticket shown).
   (b) Picnic food: **private good** — rival (eaten food is gone) and
   excludable (the family controls its own basket). (c) Playground at
   a crowded time: **common-resource-like / congestible** — effectively
   rival when crowded (a swing in use is unavailable) and nonexcludable
   in an open park; accept "club good if gated and ticketed" with that
   reasoning. (d) Lamp light: **public good** — nonrival (one more
   person seeing by it dims nothing) and nonexcludable (passersby
   cannot be shut out of the light).
2. Most likely overused: the fish (rival + open access → commons
   logic). Most likely under-provided by a private firm: the lamp
   light (nonexcludable → free riding blocks payment). Each explanation
   must invoke the matching mechanism, not merely the box name.
3. The permit addresses **overuse**: it limits fishing (and can fund
   management). It makes *access* excludable, but the fish remain
   rival, so the fishery becomes closer to a managed club resource,
   not a classic private good — anglers still do not own the fish
   until caught.

## Lesson 02 — Guided practice

1. (i) Paywalled article: excludable, nonrival → club good. (ii) Public
   fireworks: nonexcludable, nonrival → public good. (iii) Congested
   free highway at rush hour: nonexcludable, rival (each car slows the
   rest) → common resource. (iv) Clean air over a city: nonexcludable,
   nonrival → public good.
2. Acceptable correction: "Public" describes how a good is consumed
   (nonexcludable and nonrival), not who provides it. A government can
   provide a private good, and a firm can provide a near-public good.
3. Total value = 80 × $800 = $64,000 > cost $50,000 → provision is
   efficient (net benefit $14,000). A private firm would still not
   reliably provide it: beachgoers who do not pay cannot be excluded
   from the lifeguards' protection, so free riding blocks collection.

## Lesson 03 — Guided practice

2. (a) Moral hazard — behavior changed *after* coverage began.
   (b) Adverse selection — the riskiest borrowers select in *before*

## Lesson 04 — Guided practice

1. Equal cuts: 100 × $20 + 100 × $80 = $2,000 + $8,000 = $10,000.
   Cheapest: Firm X cuts all 200 tons: 200 × $20 = $4,000. Saving:
   $10,000 − $4,000 = $6,000.
2. P net = $120 − $50 = $70. Q net = $200 − $150 = $50. Marginal
   P → Q: Δcost $100, Δbenefit $80 → marginal net −$20. Efficient:
   P (expanding to Q destroys $20 of value).
3. Acceptable answer: "Q's total benefits exceed its total costs, so Q
   beats doing nothing — but the choice is between P and Q, and the
   extra step from P to Q adds $100 of cost for only $80 of benefit.
   A positive total net benefit does not make the largest program
   best; the marginal check decides."

## Lesson 05 — Guided practice

1. Buyers pay seller receipt + tax = $17 + $6 = $23. Buyers' burden:
   $23 − $20 = $3 (50%). Sellers' burden: $20 − $17 = $3 (50%).
2. $900 ÷ $45,000 = 2.0%. $3,000 ÷ $200,000 = 1.5%. The share falls as
   income rises → **regressive**.
3. Acceptable correction: "Progressivity is judged by the tax as a
   share of income, not by dollars paid. A higher-income household
   almost always pays more dollars; what matters is whether its share
   is larger."

## Practice Set A

- **A1.** MSC = 30 + Q. Qm: 90 − Q = 10 + Q → 40, Pm = 50. Q\*:
  90 − Q = 30 + Q → 30, P\* = 60. DWL: base 10, height 20 → $100.
- **A2.** MSB = 96 − 2Q. Qm: 80 − 2Q = 20 + 2Q → 15. Q\*:
  96 − 2Q = 20 + 2Q → 19. DWL: base 4, height 16 → ½ × 4 × 16 = $32.
  Subsidy = MEB = $16 per session.
- **A3.** (i) Negative, production → overproduction. (ii) Positive,
  production → underproduction. (iii) Negative, consumption →
  overproduction. (iv) Positive, consumption → underproduction.
- **A4.** In Example A, total damage at 40 units = 40 × $20 = $800,
  but the Pigouvian tax is $20 per unit — the *marginal* external
  cost at the efficient quantity — not $800. A tax equal to total
  damage would be $800 per unit and would shut the market down, far
  past efficiency.
- **A5.** Acceptable answer: at the market quantity, MSC exceeds MSB,
  so the last units cost society more than they benefit it; total
  surplus is maximized at the lower quantity where MSB = MSC.
- **A6.** (a) Effective MPC = (10 + Q) + 10 = 20 + Q. 90 − Q = 20 + Q
  → Q = 35. (b) Above the efficient quantity (30) by 5 units.
  (c) Remaining gap at Q = 35: MSC − MSB = (30 + 35) − (90 − 35) =
  65 − 55 = 10 → DWL = ½ × 5 × 10 = $25.

## Practice Set B

- **B1.** (i) National defense: nonexcludable, nonrival → public good.
  (ii) Apple: excludable, rival → private good. (iii) Streaming
  subscription: excludable, nonrival → club good. (iv) Beach at peak:
  nonexcludable, rival when crowded → common resource (congestible;
  accept "club good if access is ticketed" with reasoning).
  (v) Streetlamp light: nonexcludable, nonrival → public good.
  (vi) Open-access aquifer water: nonexcludable, rival → common
  resource.
- **B2.** (a) Total value = 12 × $25 = $300 > $250 cost → efficient;
  net benefit $50. (b) No — a firm cannot exclude non-payers from the
  light, so free riding blocks reliable collection.
- **B3.** (a) Taken = 8 × 12 = 96; exceeds the sustainable yield by
  96 − 80 = 16. (b) 80 ÷ 8 = 10 per fisher. (c) Tragedy of the
  commons; remedy: catch quotas, licenses, or fees (a use limit, not
  a provision subsidy).
- **B4.** At peak hours the gym becomes rival (machines occupied,
  crowding) while staying excludable — it moves from club good toward
  a private-good-like service, which is why gyms use peak pricing,
  capacity limits, or off-peak discounts.
- **B5.** Correction: common resources suffer **overuse**, not
  under-provision. Under-provision (free riding blocks payment) is the
  public-good problem; overuse (rivalry + open access) is the commons
  problem.

## Practice Set D

- **D1.** (a) Equal: 30 × $5 + 30 × $45 = $150 + $1,350 = $1,500.
  (b) Cheapest: A cuts all 60: 60 × $5 = $300. Saving: $1,200.
- **D2.** (a) X: net $60, ratio 2.50. Y: net $110, ratio 2.10. Z: net
  $100, ratio 1.50. (b) X → Y: ΔC $60, ΔB $110 → +$50. Y → Z: ΔC
  $100, ΔB $90 → −$10. (c) Y is efficient: expanding X → Y adds
  value, expanding Y → Z destroys value.
- **D3.** Score the mechanism, not the chosen example: the scenario
  must show *how* the mechanism worsens the outcome (capture: the
  regulated industry writes the rule; non-additional subsidy: money
  goes to actors who would have acted anyway; mismeasurement: the tax

  split the gain from the efficient outcome, so bargaining plausibly
  reaches it. (b) 10,000 residents face holdouts, free riding, and
  bargaining costs that swamp the gain; choose a tax, regulation, or
  cap-and-trade instead (any one, with a reason).
- **D5.** Acceptable condition: when damage jumps past a threshold
  (a lake tips into dead zone, a pollutant becomes lethal above a
  concentration), a cap guarantees the threshold is not crossed,
  while a tax set slightly wrong could allow a catastrophic quantity.
  Full credit states the condition and the guarantee-vs-price logic.

## Practice Set E

- **E1.** (a) Buyers: $56 − $50 = $6 (60%). Sellers: $50 − $46 = $4
  (40%). Check: $6 + $4 = $10. ✓ (b) Buyers are more inelastic here:
  they absorb more of the tax rather than cutting back.
- **E2.** (a) $800 ÷ $40,000 = 2.0%; $2,400 ÷ $160,000 = 1.5%.
  (b) Regressive (share falls with income). (c) $1,000 is 2.5% of
  $40,000 and 0.625% of $160,000: equal dollars are proportionally
  much larger at low incomes.
- **E3.** (a) $400 and $4,800. (b) Progressive (share rises with
  income). Dollars alone mislead because higher incomes pay more
  dollars under almost any tax; only shares reveal the pattern.
- **E4.** Under equal treatment, the rebate is fair (identical rules,
  identical dollars). Under narrowing outcomes, it is weakly
  equalizing in proportional terms ($200 matters more at low incomes)
  but leaves absolute gaps unchanged. Full credit states both verdicts
  and why they differ (same dollars vs. proportional impact).
- **E5.** Catches stay above the quota (or rise toward the open-access
  level) and the quota's market value collapses toward zero, because
  an unchecked paper limit binds nobody. Institutional weakness:
  enforcement/monitoring (with records) is missing.
- **E6.** Acceptable pairing: return the revenue as equal lump-sum
  rebates (or targeted rebates to low-income households). Tradeoff:
  administration cost, and — for targeted rebates — eligibility
  errors or reduced work incentives at phase-out ranges. A lump-sum
  rebate preserves the price signal because it does not change the
  per-unit tax: the marginal incentive to pollute less stays intact
  while incomes are compensated.
