# Lesson 05 — Who Pays, Who Benefits: Distribution, Equity, and Institutions

**Student goal:** I can split a tax between buyers and sellers from
price changes, judge a tax as regressive, proportional, or progressive
as a share of income, and defend a policy that names its
equity–efficiency tradeoff and its institutional preconditions.

**Time:** two ~50-minute sessions (Week 18 S1–S2) · **Materials:**
this lesson, Practice Set E.

**Readiness check (5 minutes).** Lesson 04 picked the efficient
policy. But "efficient" answers *how big is the pie?* It does not
answer *who gets which slice?* A policy can be efficient and still
unfair — or fair and still wasteful. Both questions must be asked,
and they must not be confused with each other.

## 1. Statutory incidence is not economic incidence

When a government levies a tax, the law names who must *hand the money
over* — the **statutory incidence**. Economics asks who *really*
bears the burden — the **economic incidence** — and the answer comes
from prices, not from the law. A tax on sellers can be passed to
buyers in higher prices; a tax on buyers can be pushed back onto
sellers in lower net receipts. The split follows elasticity, the
Unit 02 concept: the side that can least easily walk away — the more
**inelastic** side — bears more of the tax, because it changes its
behavior least when the price moves.

**Worked example A — a $4 tax, split by prices (fictional practice
data).** Before the tax, a good sells at $10. The government levies
$4 per unit on sellers. After the tax, buyers pay $13 and sellers keep
$13 − $4 = $9.

- Buyers' burden: $13 − $10 = **$3** (75% of the tax).
- Sellers' burden: $10 − $9 = **$1** (25% of the tax).
- Check: $3 + $1 = $4. ✓

Buyers bear three-quarters here because demand is more inelastic than
supply in this market: buyers cut back little when the price rises, so
sellers can pass most of the tax forward. If the law had instead taxed
*buyers* $4, the same prices would result — $13 paid, $9 kept — and
the same split. Statutory incidence is a paperwork choice; economic
incidence is a market outcome. A common error is to report "sellers
pay the tax" from the statute alone. Always recompute from prices.

## 2. Regressive, proportional, progressive — as a share of income

A tax is judged by the burden *as a share of income*, not by the
dollars paid:

- **Regressive:** the share falls as income rises (the poor pay a
  larger share of their income).
- **Proportional:** the share is the same at every income.
- **Progressive:** the share rises as income rises.

**Worked example B — a $2-per-unit tax (fictional practice data).**
Two households buy a taxed good:

| Household | Income | Units bought | Tax paid ($2 × units) | Tax as share of income |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lower-income | $30,000 | 100 | $200 | $200 ÷ $30,000 = 0.67% |
| Higher-income | $150,000 | 120 | $240 | $240 ÷ $150,000 = 0.16% |

The higher-income household pays *more dollars* ($240 vs. $200) but a
*much smaller share* (0.16% vs. 0.67%). As a share of income, this tax
is **regressive**: necessities are bought in similar quantities at
every income, so a per-unit tax on a necessity takes a larger slice of
a small income. The mirror image: a $500 cash transfer is 1.67% of a
$30,000 income but only 0.33% of a $150,000 income — the same dollars
matter more, proportionally, at the bottom. Distributional analysis is
exactly this table, done carefully, for a real policy with dated
figures: who pays, who benefits, and in what shares.

## 3. The equity–efficiency tradeoff — and its limits

**Efficiency** asks whether total net benefit is maximized.
**Equity** asks whether the distribution of benefits and burdens is
fair — a question economics informs but does not settle, because
fairness is also a values judgment. The classic tradeoff: redistributing
income can reduce work or investment incentives (an efficiency cost),
while a purely efficient policy can leave some people much worse off.
A lump-sum transfer would avoid distorting behavior, but governments
cannot observe who "deserves" what without distorting something, so
real policies trade one against the other.

Two honest limits on the tradeoff framing. First, some policies
improve both: a pollution tax that cleans the air in a low-income
neighborhood can raise efficiency *and* equity at once, if the
pollution burden was itself unequal. Second, "equity" has rival
meanings — equal outcomes, equal opportunity, equal treatment under
the same rules — and a policy that is fair by one meaning can be
unfair by another. A defensible policy argument therefore does three
things: it states which meaning of fairness it uses, it shows the
numbers (who pays, who benefits, in shares), and it names the
efficiency cost it accepts. Lesson 04's project rubric scores exactly
those three moves.

## 4. Institutions: the precondition underneath every lesson

Unit 03 introduced institutions; this unit shows why they are not
optional background. **Property rights** (clear, enforceable ownership)
let a fishery quota, a pollution permit, or a contract mean something.
The **rule of law** (rules applied predictably, including to the
powerful) lets firms invest without fear that the rules change after
the investment. **Contract enforcement** lets a warranty, a permit
trade, or a Coase bargain be trusted. When any leg is weak, both
market and policy remedies wobble: permits cannot trade if ownership
is unclear; a tax cannot be collected if enforcement is arbitrary; a
subsidy cannot reach its target if records are unreliable. That is why
the track's audit lists institutions (D2.Eco.9.9-12) as a track
objective, not a footnote: before asking "which tool?", ask "can any
tool be enforced here, and for whom?"

**Worked example C — the same permit, two institutional settings
(fictional illustration, not a real case).** Two towns adopt the same
fishing-permit system. In Town A, permits are recorded, transferable,
and checked at the dock: fishing falls to the sustainable level and
efficient fishers buy permits from others. In Town B, permits are
paper slips nobody checks: fishing continues past the sustainable
level and the permit price collapses to near zero. Same tool,
opposite outcomes — the difference is enforcement, not economics.
Policy evaluation that ignores institutions will keep recommending
Town A's tool to Town B.

## 5. Guided practice

1. Before a tax, a good sells at $20. A $6 tax on buyers leaves
   sellers receiving $17 per unit. What do buyers pay? Split the $6
   between buyers and sellers, in dollars and percentages.
2. A tax takes $900 from a $45,000 income and $3,000 from a $200,000
   income. Compute each share and classify the tax.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says, "This tax is progressive because
   rich people pay more dollars." Correct the claim in two sentences.

## 7. Applied task — the fair-policy argument (Week 18 S4)

Draft the argument the project requires: choose a policy from this
unit's toolkit, state which meaning of fairness you are using, show a
small who-pays/who-benefits table (in shares, with fictional or
dated real figures labeled as such), and name one efficiency cost you
accept and one group that loses under your policy. An argument that
names no loser is unfinished: revise it until the tradeoff is visible.

## 8. Exit check

Without looking back: (a) define statutory and economic incidence in
one sentence each; (b) state the test that separates regressive from
progressive; (c) name the three institutions and one thing that fails
when each is weak. If any part is missing, re-read the matching
section before the project workshop.

**Teacher note.** Likely misconceptions: (a) reading incidence off
the statute; (b) judging progressivity by dollars instead of shares;
(c) treating equity as a purely technical question with one right
