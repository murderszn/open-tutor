# Lesson 04 — The Policy Toolkit and Benefit–Cost Evaluation

**Student goal:** I can match each policy tool to the failure it
targets, evaluate options with total and marginal benefit–cost checks,
and name intended and unintended consequences from two perspectives.

**Time:** two ~50-minute sessions (Week 17 S1–S2) · **Materials:**
this lesson, Practice Set D. The Week 17 S3 evidence lab and S4
deliberation build directly on §4.

**Readiness check (5 minutes).** Lessons 01–03 diagnosed failures.
Diagnosis is not a prescription: every remedy has costs, and some
remedies cost more than the disease. Today's rule: judge a policy by
its effects, measured against the next-best alternative — not by its
intentions.

## 1. The toolkit, matched to the failure

| Tool | How it works | Failure it targets | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pigouvian tax** | Charge per unit equal to the marginal external cost | Negative externality | Hard to measure MEC; tax may fall on consumers (Lesson 05) |
| **Pigouvian subsidy** | Pay per unit equal to the marginal external benefit | Positive externality | Paying for what would have happened anyway; budget cost |
| **Regulation (command-and-control)** | Set a limit or standard every firm must meet | Externality, safety, information | One standard for unlike firms wastes money; enforcement cost |
| **Cap-and-trade** | Cap total emissions; firms trade permits to emit | Negative externality (pollution) | Permit allocation fights; price swings; monitoring |
| **Direct provision** | Government supplies the good, funded by taxes | Public good | Deciding how much, and for whom; cost overruns |
| **Property rights / liability** | Assign ownership and let parties bargain or sue | Externality (Coase), commons | Bargaining is costly when many parties are involved |
| **Information rules** | Disclosure, labeling, licensing | Asymmetric information | Disclosure nobody reads; compliance cost |

One special idea deserves a sentence: the **Coase insight** (Ronald
Coase, 1960). If property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap,
the parties can negotiate to the efficient outcome themselves, with or
without a tax — the factory can pay residents, or residents can pay
the factory, depending on who holds the right. The catch is the
condition: with thousands of affected residents, bargaining is *not*
cheap, and the insight stops being a practical remedy. Coase does not
say "government is unnecessary"; it says "check whether bargaining
can actually happen before choosing a tool."

## 2. Worked example A — cap-and-trade beats equal cuts (fictional practice data)

Two firms must cut 100 tons of emissions in total. Cutting one ton
costs Firm A $10 and Firm B $50 — different technologies, different
costs.

- **Equal regulation:** each cuts 50 tons.
  Cost = 50 × $10 + 50 × $50 = $500 + $2,500 = **$3,000**.
- **Cap-and-trade:** the cap is 100 tons of cuts, permits trade.
  Firm A cuts all 100 tons (cost 100 × $10 = $1,000); Firm B cuts
  nothing but buys permits from A at a price between $10 and $50 per
  ton. Total cost = **$1,000**. Saving versus equal regulation:
  $3,000 − $1,000 = **$2,000**.

The environmental result is identical — 100 tons cut — but the cost is
one-third as much, because the cheap cutter does the cutting. The
lesson generalizes: when firms' costs differ, a uniform rule wastes
resources, and a price or permit system lets the cheapest reductions
happen first. It does *not* generalize to "trading always works": it
needs a measured pollutant, a enforced cap, and a market with enough
traders to price permits honestly.

## 3. Worked example B — benefit–cost evaluation with a marginal check

A town considers three pollution-control options (fictional practice
data; all figures are annual):

| Option | Total cost | Total benefit | Net benefit (B − C) | Benefit–cost ratio (B ÷ C) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C: basic filters | $60 | $90 | $30 | 1.50 |
| A: filters + scrubbers | $120 | $200 | $80 | 1.67 |
| B: full rebuild | $300 | $360 | $60 | 1.20 |

**Step 1 — total check.** All three have benefits above costs, so all
three beat doing nothing. The largest net benefit is Option A ($80).

**Step 2 — marginal check (the step most evaluations skip).** Moving
from A to B adds cost $300 − $120 = $180 and adds benefit
$360 − $200 = $160. Marginal net benefit = $160 − $180 = **−$20**.
Expanding from A to B destroys $20 of value, even though B's total
benefits exceed its total costs. The efficient choice is A, not the
biggest program. Similarly, moving from C to A adds $60 of cost and
$110 of benefit (+$50), so A beats C as well. Rule: expand only while
marginal benefit exceeds marginal cost; a positive total net benefit
is necessary but not sufficient for the *largest* program.

**Step 3 — what benefit–cost analysis cannot do alone.** It needs
benefits in dollars, which forces hard valuations (what is a cleaner
river worth? a quieter street?). It hides distribution: Option A
might help one neighborhood and burden another, which is Lesson 05's
question. And its numbers are estimates: a good evaluation reports
what happens if benefits come in 20% lower — here, A's benefit falls
to $160, net benefit to $40, still positive but no longer obviously
best. Always ask: *whose* benefits, *whose* costs, and *how sure are
we?*

## 4. Government failure: the cure can fail too

Diagnosing a market failure does not prove a policy will fix it.
**Government failure** is when an intervention makes the outcome
worse than the market failure it targeted, or achieves its goal at a
cost above its benefit. Common routes: regulators captured by the
industry they regulate; policies designed for one group and exploited
by another (a subsidy claimed mostly by people who would have acted
anyway); enforcement so weak the rule is decorative; and simple
ignorance — the external cost was mismeasured, so the tax is set
wrong. Public-choice reasoning adds a political mechanism: officials
and voters respond to incentives too, so concentrated benefits and
dispersed costs tend to win support even when total costs exceed total
benefits. None of this argues against all intervention — it argues for
the marginal check in §3 and for comparing the policy against the
market outcome *and* against other policies, not against perfection.

## 5. Guided practice

1. A city must cut 200 tons of emissions. Firm X's abatement cost is
   $20/ton, Firm Y's is $80/ton. Compute the cost of equal cuts (100
   each) and of the cheapest allocation, and state the saving.
2. Two road-safety options (fictional data): Option P costs $50,
   benefits $120; Option Q costs $150, benefits $200. Compute net
   benefits and the marginal net benefit of moving P → Q. Which
   option is efficient?
3. Error analysis: a classmate says, "Option Q's benefits exceed its
   costs, so Q is the best choice." Explain the mistake using the
   marginal check.

## 7. Applied task — evidence lab preparation (Week 17 S3)

With your adult, choose one real policy that responds to a market
failure (a local bag fee, a congestion charge, a vaccination subsidy,
a fishing quota — anything age-appropriate and documentable). Find
two sources that disagree about its effects, or one source describing
an intended effect and one describing an unintended effect. Bring one
sentence from each source, with its publication and date, to the
evidence lab. If you cannot find two perspectives, bring the single
source and write down what perspective is missing.

## 8. Exit check

State the marginal rule for expanding a program in one sentence, and
give one example of government failure in your own words. If you
cannot do both, re-read §3 and §4 before the evidence lab.

**Teacher note.** Likely misconceptions: (a) picking the biggest
program because its total benefits exceed costs; (b) treating
cap-and-trade as "permission to pollute" rather than a capped total
with a price; (c) treating government failure as proof that no policy
ever helps. Full solutions and reteaching moves are in the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and [answer key](../answer-key.md).
