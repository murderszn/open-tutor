# Lesson 01 — Externalities: When Private and Social Costs Part Ways

**Student goal:** I can tell whether a spillover is positive or
negative, draw the private and social curves, and find the market
quantity, the efficient quantity, the deadweight loss, and the tax or
subsidy that fixes it.

**Time:** two ~50-minute sessions (Week 15 S1–S2) · **Materials:**
this lesson, graph paper or a blank graph, Practice Set A.

**Readiness check (5 minutes).** From Unit 02: a market is in
equilibrium where demand meets supply, and that quantity maximizes
total surplus *when* every cost and benefit of the trade falls on the
buyer and seller. What if some cost falls on a neighbor who never
bought or sold anything? That is today's question.

## 1. The missing-party test

An **externality** is a cost or benefit of a transaction that falls on
someone who is not the buyer or seller — a third party, a bystander.
The test is simple: *is someone affected who did not choose the deal
and is not paid for the effect?* If yes, there is an externality. If
the effect is a cost, it is a **negative externality** (pollution from
a factory, noise from a late-night venue, congestion from one more car
on a crowded road). If it is a benefit, it is a **positive
externality** (a vaccination that also protects neighbors, a neighbor's
well-kept garden that brightens your street, research whose findings
other firms build on).

Two vocabulary pairs carry the whole lesson:

- **Marginal private cost (MPC)** — what the producer pays for one
  more unit. In a competitive market, the supply curve *is* the MPC
  curve.
- **Marginal social cost (MSC)** — what *everyone* pays for one more
  unit: MPC plus the **marginal external cost (MEC)**, the extra cost
  imposed on third parties. So MSC = MPC + MEC.
- **Marginal private benefit (MPB)** — what the buyer gains from one
  more unit. The demand curve *is* the MPB curve.
- **Marginal social benefit (MSB)** — what *everyone* gains: MPB plus
  the **marginal external benefit (MEB)**. So MSB = MPB + MEB.

Efficiency — the quantity where society as a whole gains most — is
where **MSB = MSC**: the last unit's full benefit equals its full
cost. The market, left alone, settles where **MPB = MPC**: private
benefit equals private cost. When an externality exists, those two
equations give *different* quantities, and the difference is the
market failure.

A common error: calling every bad side effect an externality. A
factory that undercuts a rival's price harms the rival, but through the
market — the rival loses a sale, not a cost imposed outside a trade.
That is competition, not an externality. The missing-party test is
about costs and benefits that bypass the price entirely.

## 2. Worked example A — a negative externality (pollution)

All numbers in this unit are **fictional practice data** for
computation, not measurements of any real factory.

A town has one competitive industry. Its demand (marginal private
benefit) is:

> MPB: P = 100 − Q

Its producers' marginal private cost is:

> MPC: P = 20 + Q

Each unit produced also imposes $20 of pollution cost on residents
downwind who buy nothing. So MEC = $20, and:

> MSC = MPC + MEC = (20 + Q) + 20 = 40 + Q

**Step 1 — market quantity.** The market equates private benefit and
private cost: MPB = MPC.

> 100 − Q = 20 + Q → 80 = 2Q → Qm = 40. Price: P = 100 − 40 = 60.

So the market produces 40 units at $60.

**Step 2 — efficient quantity.** Efficiency equates social benefit and
social cost: MSB = MSC. There is no external benefit here, so
MSB = MPB = 100 − Q.

> 100 − Q = 40 + Q → 60 = 2Q → Q\* = 30. Price at that quantity:
> P = 100 − 30 = 70.

Society wants 30 units; the market makes 40. The market **overproduces**
by 10 units, because producers ignore the $20 they impose on others.

**Step 3 — deadweight loss.** Units 31 through 40 each cost society
more than they benefit it. At the market quantity (Q = 40), the gap
between social cost and social benefit is MSC − MSB = (40 + 40) −
(100 − 40) = 80 − 60 = 20, which is exactly the MEC. The loss is the
triangle with base (Qm − Q\*) = 10 and height 20:

> DWL = ½ × 10 × 20 = $100.

Read the triangle correctly: its three corners are the efficient point
(30, 70), the market point on the demand curve (40, 60), and the market
point on the social-cost curve (40, 80). The triangle is *not* the
pollution cost itself — total pollution damage at 40 units is
40 × $20 = $800. The $100 is only the *excess*: the value lost by
producing units whose social cost exceeds their social benefit.

**Step 4 — the Pigouvian fix.** A **Pigouvian tax** charges the
producer the external cost per unit, so the producer's cost curve
becomes the social cost curve: MPC + tax = MSC. Here the tax is
$20 per unit — exactly the MEC. Producers now face P = 40 + Q, meet
demand at Q = 30, and the market quantity becomes the efficient
quantity. A Pigouvian tax does not "punish" production; it makes the
price tell the truth.

## 3. Worked example B — a positive externality (a tutoring program)

A tutoring service has marginal private benefit:

> MPB: P = 90 − 3Q

Each tutored student also raises classmates' learning and later
workplace productivity, worth $20 per session to others. So
MEB = $20, and:

> MSB = MPB + MEB = 110 − 3Q

Supply (marginal cost) is:

> MC: P = 30 + Q

**Step 1 — market quantity.** MPB = MC:

> 90 − 3Q = 30 + Q → 60 = 4Q → Qm = 15. Price: P = 30 + 15 = 45.

**Step 2 — efficient quantity.** MSB = MC:

> 110 − 3Q = 30 + Q → 80 = 4Q → Q\* = 20. Price: P = 30 + 20 = 50.

The market **underproduces** by 5 sessions: buyers ignore $20 of
benefit that falls on others, so they buy too little.

**Step 3 — deadweight loss.** At Qm = 15, MSB − MC = (110 − 45) −
(30 + 15) = 65 − 45 = 20 = MEB. Triangle: base (20 − 15) = 5, height 20:

> DWL = ½ × 5 × 20 = $50.

**Step 4 — the Pigouvian fix.** A **Pigouvian subsidy** of $20 per
session — exactly the MEB — pays buyers (or the provider) for the
benefit others receive, shifting effective demand to MSB and moving
quantity to 20. Note the symmetry: tax the negative spillover, subsidize
the positive one, and in both cases set the charge equal to the
marginal external effect.

A second common error: assuming the fix must be a tax or subsidy.
Lesson 04 compares regulation, cap-and-trade, and direct provision,
and shows when each beats a price fix — including when the external
cost is hard to measure, which is often.

## 4. Guided practice

Work these with a partner; the adult checks the setup, not just the
answer.

1. A good's MPB is P = 80 − Q and its MPC is P = 20 + Q. Each unit
   imposes a $10 external cost. Write MSC, then find Qm, Q\*, and DWL.
2. A flu-shot clinic's MPB is P = 60 − Q, its MC is P = 10 + Q, and
   each shot gives $10 of benefit to others. Find Qm, Q\*, DWL, and
   the Pigouvian subsidy.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says, "A negative externality means
   the market underproduces, because pollution makes people worse
   off." Explain the mistake in two sentences, using the direction of
   the MSC shift.

## 6. Applied task

Name one negative and one positive externality from your own week
(traffic, noise, a shared ride, a skill you taught a friend). For each,
name the third party, say whether the market over- or under-produces,
and state whether a tax, a subsidy, or something else fits best — and
why, in one sentence. No real person's private information is needed;
use situations, not names.

## 7. Exit check

Without looking back: write MSC = MPC + MEC and MSB = MPB + MEB from
memory, then state in one sentence why a negative externality leads
to overproduction. If you cannot, re-read §1 and §2 before Session S2.
