## Set A — Externalities (Lesson 01)

**A1.** Demand is P = 90 − Q and marginal private cost is
P = 10 + Q. Each unit imposes a $20 external cost.
(a) Write MSC. (b) Find the market quantity and price.
(c) Find the efficient quantity and the price at that quantity.
(d) Compute deadweight loss.

**A2.** A community tutoring program has MPB: P = 80 − 2Q and
marginal cost P = 20 + 2Q. Each session gives $16 of benefit to
people other than the buyer. (a) Write MSB. (b) Find the market and
efficient quantities. (c) Compute deadweight loss and state the
Pigouvian subsidy.

**A3.** For each case, classify the externality (positive or
negative; production or consumption) and state whether the market
over- or under-produces: (i) a factory's night noise disturbs
neighbors; (ii) a beekeeper's bees pollinate a neighbor's orchard;
(iii) a driver's loud music at 2 a.m. disturbs a street;
(iv) one household's security lighting deters crime for the whole
street.

**A4.** Error analysis: "The Pigouvian tax should equal the total
pollution damage." Explain why this is wrong, and state what the tax
should equal instead. Use Lesson 01's Example A numbers ($20 MEC,
40 units) to show the difference between the two amounts.

**A5.** A graph shows MSC above MPC and demand below MSC at the
market quantity. In words, explain why the last units produced destroy
value, and what quantity would maximize total surplus.

**A6.** Return to A1's market (demand P = 90 − Q, MPC P = 10 + Q,
true MEC $20). The government instead levies a $10 tax. (a) Find the
new quantity. (b) Is it above or below the efficient quantity, and by
how much? (c) Compute the remaining deadweight loss.

## Set B — Public goods and common resources (Lesson 02)

**B1.** Classify each good with both tests (excludable? rival?) and
name its box: (i) national defense; (ii) an apple you eat;
(iii) a streaming subscription; (iv) a public beach on a packed
holiday; (v) light from a streetlamp; (vi) water in an open-access
aquifer.

**B2.** Twelve households would each pay up to $25 for a neighborhood
streetlight costing $250. (a) Is provision efficient? Show the total
value and the net benefit. (b) Would a private firm reliably provide
it? Explain with the free-rider logic.

**B3.** A lake sustainably yields 80 fish per year (fictional data).
Eight fishers, with no limit, each take 12 fish. (a) How many fish are
taken, and by how much does the take exceed the sustainable yield?
(b) What is the sustainable take per fisher? (c) Name the failure and
one remedy that limits use rather than subsidizing provision.

**B4.** A gym membership is excludable and nonrival when the gym is
nearly empty. Explain what happens to its classification when the gym
is packed at peak hours, and why that matters for pricing.

**B5.** Error analysis: "Common resources are under-provided because
nobody owns them." Correct the statement: name the actual problem for
common resources and contrast it with the public-good problem.

## Set C — Information failures and market power (Lesson 03)

**C1.** Used phones: working phones are worth $300 to buyers and
$260 to sellers; broken phones are worth $50 to buyers and $40 to
sellers. 60% of phones are working, and buyers cannot tell which is
which. (a) Compute the expected value a buyer will pay.
(b) Do working-phone sellers stay in the market? (c) Name the failure.

**C2.** Classify each as adverse selection or moral hazard, using the
timing test (before or after the deal): (a) after premiums rise,
healthy people drop their insurance and the pool gets sicker;
(b) an insured homeowner stops locking a side gate; (c) an employer
requires a costly certification for a job. For (c), also name the
response type (signaling or screening) and say whose action it is.

**C3.** A water network costs $2,000,000 to build (fixed) and $2 per
unit to deliver. (a) Compute average cost at 20,000 units and at
200,000 units. (b) Explain why one firm serving 200,000 units is
cheaper than two firms serving 100,000 each.

**C4.** A new messaging app is technically better than the dominant
app, but almost nobody switches. Explain this with network effects,
in three sentences.

**C5.** An insurer adds a $500 deductible to a policy. (a) How does
the deductible change the insured person's incentives (moral hazard)?
(b) Name one cost or downside of the deductible itself.

## Set D — Policy toolkit and benefit–cost evaluation (Lesson 04)

**D1.** Two firms must cut 60 tons of emissions. Firm A's abatement
cost is $5/ton; Firm B's is $45/ton. (a) Compute the cost of equal
cuts (30 tons each). (b) Compute the cost of the cheapest allocation
and the saving versus equal cuts.

**D2.** Three road-safety options (fictional annual data):
X costs $40, benefits $100; Y costs $100, benefits $210; Z costs
$200, benefits $300. (a) Compute each option's net benefit and
benefit–cost ratio. (b) Compute the marginal net benefit of moving
X → Y and of moving Y → Z. (c) Which option is efficient, and why?

**D3.** Pick one government-failure mechanism — regulatory capture,
a subsidy claimed by people who would have acted anyway, or a
mismeasured external cost — and write a short scenario (4–6
sentences) showing how it makes the outcome worse than the policy
intended.

**D4.** Coase in two cases: (a) one factory and one laundry can
bargain cheaply over smoke damage; (b) one factory affects 10,000
residents. Explain why bargaining plausibly reaches efficiency in (a)
but not in (b), and name the tool you would choose for (b) instead.

**D5.** When would a strict regulatory cap beat a tax, even though a
tax lets the cheapest reductions happen first? Give one reasoned
condition (for example, a threshold beyond which damage jumps) and
explain it in 3–4 sentences.

## Set E — Distribution, equity, and institutions (Lesson 05)

**E1.** Before a tax, a good sells at $50. A $10 tax on sellers leaves
buyers paying $56 and sellers keeping $46. (a) Split the $10 between
buyers and sellers in dollars and percentages. (b) Which side is more
inelastic here, and how do you know?

**E2.** A tax takes $800 from a $40,000 income and $2,400 from a
$160,000 income. (a) Compute each share of income. (b) Classify the
tax. (c) A $1,000 transfer is what share of each income, and what does
that show about equal dollar amounts?

**E3.** A tax takes 1% of a $40,000 income and 3% of a $160,000 income.
(a) Compute the dollars paid at each income. (b) Classify the tax and
explain why the dollar comparison alone would mislead.

**E4.** A flat $200 rebate is paid to every household. Judge it under
two meanings of fairness — equal treatment (same rules for all) and
equal outcomes (narrowing gaps) — and explain why the two verdicts
differ.

**E5.** A town adopts a fishing quota but never checks catches at the
dock. Using Lesson 05's Town A / Town B logic, predict what happens
to the catch and to the quota's value, and name the institutional
weakness.

**E6.** A pollution tax is efficient but regressive: it cleans the
air while taking a larger income share from low-income households.
Propose one pairing (for example, a lump-sum rebate of the revenue)
that softens the regressivity, and name one cost or tradeoff of your
pairing. Explain why a lump-sum rebate preserves the price signal.

## Challenge set

**CH1.** Demand is P = 100 − Q and MPC is P = 20 + Q. The marginal
external cost rises with quantity: MEC = 0.5Q. (a) Write MSC.
(b) Find the market quantity. (c) Find the efficient quantity.
(d) Find the Pigouvian tax that restores efficiency, and verify it by
showing that MPC + tax meets demand at the efficient quantity.
(e) Compute deadweight loss at the market quantity.

**CH2.** Sensitivity check: suppose every benefit in Lesson 04's
Worked example B falls 20% (costs unchanged). Recompute all three
options' net benefits and state whether the efficient choice changes.

**CH3.** Design brief (one page): choose a real, local, adult-approved
market failure; diagnose it (type, curves direction, over- or
under-production); recommend one policy tool with a benefit–cost
sketch; show a small who-pays/who-benefits table in income shares;
and name one intended consequence, one unintended consequence, and
one group that loses under your recommendation. Label every figure
fictional or dated-real; do not present an estimate as a measurement.
