# Teacher Guide — Unit 04: Market Failures, Public Goods, and Policy Tradeoffs

**Audience:** the guiding adult. This guide contains pacing,
misconception, and reteaching guidance. All worked solutions live in
the [answer key](answer-key.md); this guide refers to it by section
and does not duplicate student answers beside student questions.

## Pacing and session notes

Follow the 20-session plan in the [unit README](README.md). Notes per
week:

- **Week 15 (externalities, goods).** Spend the full two sessions on
  Lesson 01: the graph setup (private vs. social curves) is the skill
  every later session reuses. Do not let learners skip drawing the
  curves — a learner who only memorizes "negative means overproduce"
  will reverse the direction on the first unfamiliar case. Lesson 02's
  image activity works best as a paired discussion before writing.
- **Week 16 (information, market power, case).** The case
  investigation (S3) needs an adult-selected case: choose one with
  accessible sources and a clear mechanism (a local congestion, litter,
  or licensing example usually beats a national controversy). The S4
  argument ("hardest to self-correct") has no single right answer;
  score the evidence and the counterargument, not the conclusion.
- **Week 17 (policy toolkit, evidence lab, quiz).** The evidence lab
  (S3) is the unit's nonpartisanship checkpoint: if both perspectives
  come from one side's sources, send the learner back for a second
  perspective before S4. The quiz (S5) is formative — use it to
  reteach, not to rank; record graphing, classification, and
  marginal-check gaps for Week 18.
- **Week 18 (distribution, project, assessment).** The project
  workshop (S3) should catch two failures early: a brief with no
  named loser, and figures presented as measurements. Return both for
  revision before scoring. The assessment (S5) is open-notes by design;
  it tests setup and reasoning, not memorization.

## Misconceptions and reteaching

| Misconception | What to say / do |
|---|---|
| Deadweight loss = total external damage | Re-draw Lesson 01 Example A: total damage at 40 units is 40 × $20 = $800; DWL is only the $100 triangle on units 31–40, where social cost exceeds social benefit. Ask: "Which units should never have been produced?" |
| Negative externality → underproduction (or reversed) | Point to the curve shift: MSC sits *above* MPC, so the private intersection lands to the *right* of the social one. Have the learner state the direction from the graph, not from memory, three times with different numbers. |
| "Public good" = "provided by government" | Use the table test: a government clinic selling glasses provides a private good; a free radio broadcast is close to a public good. Classify by consumption (excludable? rival?), never by provider. |
| Common resources are "under-provided" | Contrast: public goods suffer under-provision (free riding blocks payment); common resources suffer overuse (rivalry + open access). Ask which problem a catch limit solves — and which it does not. |
| Adverse selection vs. moral hazard swapped | Apply the timing test every time: before the deal = selection; after the deal = hazard. Drill with Lesson 03 guided practice until the timing sentence comes first. |
| Signaling = advertising | A signal must be costly to fake: a warranty hurts a lemon seller and barely costs a peach seller. Ask: "Could a lemon seller copy this cheaply? If yes, it is not a signal." |
| Biggest program wins if benefits exceed costs | Re-run Lesson 04 Example B: Option B's total net benefit is positive ($60) yet expanding A → B destroys $20. The question is always marginal: does the *next* step add more benefit than cost? |
| "Sellers pay the tax" (from the statute) | Recompute from prices: buyer price minus old price, and old price minus seller receipt. The two burdens must sum to the tax — use that sum as a self-check. |
| Progressivity judged by dollars | Recompute as shares of income. The higher-income household in Lesson 05 pays more dollars ($240) yet a smaller share (0.16% vs. 0.67%): dollars mislead; shares decide. |
| "Government failure proves no policy helps" | Government failure is a comparison, not a verdict: compare the policy against the market outcome *and* against other policies. A failed tool can still leave a better tool available. |

## Scoring guidance

- **Practice Sets A–E and Challenge:** score setups, not just final
  numbers. A correct setup with an arithmetic slip earns at least
  half credit; a bare correct number earns at most half.
- **Week-17 quiz (20 points):** see the answer key's quiz section for
  point splits. Reteach any item missed by the learner before the
  Week 18 assessment; the assessment re-tests the same skills with
  new numbers.
- **Culminating assessment (40 points):** point splits are printed on
  the assessment. Open responses (Tasks 2, 3b–c, 4c, 5c) are scored
  against the acceptable elements in the answer key; a response that
  meets the elements in different words earns full credit.
- **Project (40 points):** use the rubric in [project.md](project.md).
  Return — do not silently mark down — a brief that endorses a party,
  candidate, or cause, so the learner can reframe it in institutional
  terms. A "do nothing" recommendation argued with numbers is a valid
  recommendation.
- **Readiness signals.** The learner is ready for Unit 05 when they
  can: draw an externality graph from curves and compute Qm, Q\*, and
  DWL; classify a good with both tests; split a tax from prices; and
  run a marginal benefit–cost check without prompting. If graphing is
  still shaky, use the Week 19 midyear review's modeling clinic
  before Unit 05's data labs.
- **Feedback language.** Name the specific move ("your MSC shift is
  correct; your DWL base is the quantity gap, not the price gap")
  rather than a general judgment. Keep all learner work, names, and
  scores in private storage, never in this repository.

## Accessibility and safety notes

- Graph tasks may be completed from schedule (table) versions of the
  curves, or dictated orally; the image activity always has the
  text-only alternative printed in Lesson 02.
- Field observation for the project (a park, a road, a market) is
  adult-supervised; observation and simulation alternatives replace
  any hazardous or inaccessible visit.
- All current-data claims must be dated and re-verified at teaching
  time; the unit's figures are fictional practice data unless a source
  is named.
