# Unit 03 — Firms, Labor, Competition, and Market Structures

**Grade:** 12 · **Subject:** Social Studies (Economics and policy inquiry)
**Track weeks:** 11–14 · **Backlog:** [issue #57](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/57), section U03
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Who decides what a firm makes, what it charges, and what its workers are
paid — and why does that answer change so much from one market to the
next? This unit follows the firm inside the market model built in Unit 02.
First, what a firm *is*: the legal forms it can take, how it counts cost
and profit, and how it picks an output level. Then the labor market, where
wages are the price of work and human capital, productivity, and demand
for a skill all push that price around. Then the four market structures —
perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly —
and what each one does to prices, output, quality, and innovation.
Finally, the entrepreneur and the institutions (property rights, the rule
of law, contract enforcement) that let firms and markets function at all.

## Essential question

If competition disciplines firms, why do some firms earn lasting profits
while others are pushed to the edge — and what, besides competition,
makes an economy productive?

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–02 and the diagnostic weeks, described as skills (no
cross-grade learner links):

- Compute and interpret opportunity cost; distinguish accounting profit
  from a decision that includes opportunity cost (Unit 01).
- Read a schedule, graph a linear relationship, and compute percentage
  changes; find equilibrium and explain surplus and shortage pressure
  (Unit 02).
- Frame a claim with at least two pieces of evidence; distinguish a
  claim from its evidence and from an interpretation.
- Explain, in one paragraph, how a single demand or supply shift moves
  equilibrium price and quantity (Unit 02, Lesson 3). This unit
  re-teaches every firm and labor concept from scratch rather than
  assuming it.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Compare business organizations** — sole proprietorship, partnership,
   and corporation (with limited liability companies noted) — on
   ownership, control, liability, and ability to raise capital, and
   explain why a founder might choose each form. (D2.Eco.3.9-12, applied)
2. **Compute revenue, cost, and profit from a schedule**; find the
   profit-maximizing output with the marginal rule (expand while
   marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost; stop when they are equal);
   and apply the short-run shutdown rule (operate only if price covers
   average variable cost). (D2.Eco.3.9-12)
3. **Explain wage determination** in a labor market: how labor demand
   (derived from the value of what workers produce) and labor supply
   meet, how human capital and productivity shift that meeting point,
   and how to read real earnings-by-education data without mistaking
   correlation for a single cause. (D2.Eco.3.9-12; D2.Eco.13.9-12 previewed)
4. **Classify a market** as perfect competition, monopolistic
   competition, oligopoly, or monopoly from evidence about the number
   of sellers, product differences, and barriers to entry.
   (D2.Eco.4.9-12)
5. **Explain the consequences of competition** in specific markets for
   prices, output, product variety, quality, and innovation — including
   why competition tends to push price toward marginal cost and why a
   monopolist restricts output. (D2.Eco.5.9-12)
6. **Explain the entrepreneur's role and the role of institutions**:
   how entrepreneurs combine resources in new ways (creative
   destruction), and how clearly defined property rights, the rule of
   law, and contract enforcement support investment and growth.
   (D2.Eco.9.9-12; D2.Eco.13.9-12)

In every unit the learner also develops compelling questions
(D1.1–D1.5.9-12), evaluates sources and their limits
(D3.1–D3.4.9-12), and communicates evidence-based conclusions
(D4.1–D4.4.9-12).

**Fictional practice data — read this before any lesson.** All firm
schedules, café figures, and market illustrations in the lessons,
practice, quiz, and assessment are invented for practice and are
labeled as such wherever they appear. They are not observations, not
firm data, and not current prices. The one firm dataset used throughout
is the Harbor Bakery, per week, selling loaves at a market price of $9:

| Loaves per week (Q) | Fixed cost | Variable cost | Total cost | Marginal cost per loaf | Total revenue at $9 | Profit |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | $100 | $0 | $100 | — | $0 | −$100 |
| 10 | $100 | $50 | $150 | $5.00 | $90 | −$60 |
| 20 | $100 | $90 | $190 | $4.00 | $180 | −$10 |
| 30 | $100 | $120 | $220 | $3.00 | $270 | $50 |
| 40 | $100 | $160 | $260 | $4.00 | $360 | $100 |
| 50 | $100 | $220 | $320 | $6.00 | $450 | $130 |
| 60 | $100 | $300 | $400 | $8.00 | $540 | $140 |
| 70 | $100 | $400 | $500 | $10.00 | $630 | $130 |

Profit is highest at 60 loaves ($140); the 70th-loaf step costs $10 per
loaf but earns only $9, so profit falls. Marginal cost is computed per
10-loaf step (for example, from 50 to 60 loaves: ($400 − $320) ÷ 10 =
$8.00 per loaf). One real dataset is used in Lesson 3 and the project:
the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) median usual weekly earnings
for full-time workers age 25 and over, 2024 annual averages — less than
a high school diploma $738; high school graduates, no college $930;
some college or associate degree $1,056; bachelor's degree only $1,543;
advanced degree $1,901. That table is real, dated, and sourced in
[resource-pack.md](resource-pack.md); every other number in this unit
is fictional practice data. Any real market the learner studies in the
project must use adult-selected, dated, sourced figures instead.

## 20-session pacing (4 weeks × 5 sessions, ~50 minutes each)

Sessions follow the track scope-and-sequence for Weeks 11–14. Lessons
1–5 carry the new teaching; the remaining sessions are investigations,
writing, review, and assessment — each with a named activity and a
success criterion, not a generic "practice" label.

| Session | Activity | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| W11S1 | Lesson 1 — business organizations: forms, liability, raising capital | Learner matches three founder situations to forms, giving a liability reason each time |
| W11S2 | Lesson 2, part 1 — revenue, costs, profit; the Harbor Bakery schedule | Learner completes the cost/profit table and states the profit-maximizing output with its profit |
| W11S3 | Investigation — build the bakery's cost curves and test two prices ($9 and $3) | A labeled cost/revenue table showing profit-maximizing output at $9 and the shutdown call at $3 |
| W11S4 | Writing — one paragraph explaining why a firm stops expanding | Paragraph uses the marginal rule, not just "costs got too high" |
| W11S5 | Review + check — forms, cost terms, marginal reasoning | Learner defines fixed vs. variable cost and applies MR = MC to a fresh schedule without error |
| W12S1 | Lesson 3, part 1 — wages as a price: labor demand and supply | Learner explains why a labor-demand rise lifts wages, using a graph or a schedule |
| W12S2 | Lesson 3, part 2 — human capital, productivity, and the BLS earnings table | Learner computes two earnings gaps from the BLS table and names two non-education explanations for them |
| W12S3 | Investigation — one occupation's wages after a demand shock; the café hiring decision | A hiring decision justified by value of marginal product vs. wage, step by step |
| W12S4 | Argument — what explains wage differences? | Short argument weighing at least three explanations, with the correlation-causation caution stated |
| W12S5 | Review + check — labor vocabulary and wage-shock predictions | Learner predicts wage and employment direction for four shocks (demand/supply, up/down) |
| W13S1 | Lesson 4, part 1 — the four market structures and their tests | Learner states the three tests (sellers, differences, barriers) and applies them to two markets |
| W13S2 | Lesson 4, part 2 — consequences: price, output, variety, innovation | Learner explains, with the monopoly-restriction example, why P > MC under monopoly |
| W13S3 | Investigation — classify the four scenes in the generated street image, then two real markets | Four image classifications with one evidence phrase each, plus two real-market classifications with sources |
| W13S4 | Deliberation — when is bigness a problem? | Learner names a case where size helps consumers and a case where it harms them, with reasons |
| W13S5 | Formative quiz (objectives 1–4) | See [assessments/week-13_quiz.md](assessments/week-13_quiz.md) |
| W14S1 | Lesson 5, part 1 — the entrepreneur: risk, new combinations, creative destruction | Learner gives one original example of creative destruction and names what was displaced |
| W14S2 | Lesson 5, part 2 — institutions: property rights, rule of law, contract enforcement | Learner explains, for a hypothetical case pair, why weak enforcement discourages investment |
| W14S3 | Research lab — one entrepreneur's venture (adult-selected source) | A source note: market, innovation, outcome, and what the source does not prove |
| W14S4 | Argument — what matters more for prosperity: resources or institutions? | Argument answers with evidence and concedes the strongest point for the other side |
| W14S5 | Unit review + unit assessment | See [assessments/unit-assessment.md](assessments/unit-assessment.md) |

Retrieval is built in: each week's review session re-asks one prior
week's idea (W12S5 re-asks the marginal rule; W13S5 re-asks a labor
shock; W14S5 re-asks a market-structure classification), and the unit
assessment mixes all six objectives.

## Accessibility and supports

- Every graph and table task has a numbers alternative: the learner
  may answer from the schedule alone, and every curve question is also
  asked as a numbers question.
- The Lesson 4 image activity has a full text-only alternative with
  the same question and the same evidence.
- Key terms are defined where they first appear and again in the
  vocabulary list; the adult reads lesson text aloud on request and
  accepts spoken, drawn, or typed responses.
- Extensions in each lesson add a second price, a second shock, or a
  real-data version of the fictional case for learners who finish
  early; they are never prerequisites for the next lesson.
