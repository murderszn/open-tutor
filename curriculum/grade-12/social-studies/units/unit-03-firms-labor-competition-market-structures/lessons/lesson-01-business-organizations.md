# Lesson 1 — Business Organizations: Who Owns the Firm, Who Bears the Risk?

**Goal:** Compare sole proprietorships, partnerships, and corporations
(and note LLCs) on ownership, control, liability, and raising capital,
and match a founder's situation to a form for a stated reason.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Readiness check:** In one sentence, what does a firm's owner get if
the firm succeeds, and what does the owner risk if it fails? (There is
no single right answer yet — the lesson gives you the vocabulary to
answer precisely.)

## 1. What a firm is

A **firm** is an organization that combines resources — workers,
equipment, materials, ideas — to produce goods or services for sale.
The firm is not the same thing as its owner, but the law has to decide
how closely they are tied. That decision is the firm's **business
organization** (also called its legal form), and it answers four
questions:

1. **Who owns it?** One person, a few people, or many shareholders?
2. **Who controls it?** Who makes the decisions day to day?
3. **Who bears the losses?** If the firm owes more than it owns, whose
   money — or house, or savings — pays?
4. **How does it raise capital?** Capital is money for equipment,
   buildings, and starting costs. Can the firm borrow easily, sell
   ownership shares, or only spend what its owners already have?

The key word for question 3 is **liability**: legal responsibility for
debts. **Unlimited liability** means the owner's personal assets are on
the line. **Limited liability** means the owner's loss is limited to
what the owner put into the firm; personal assets are shielded. That
one distinction drives most of the choice of form.

## 2. Three forms, side by side

### Sole proprietorship

One person owns and runs the business: a freelance designer, a food
stall, a tutor. It is the simplest form to start — often no special
filing beyond local permits — and the owner keeps all the profit and
makes all the decisions. The tradeoff is stark: the owner has unlimited
liability. If the business is sued or cannot pay its debts, the owner's
personal savings can be claimed. Raising capital is also limited: the
owner can borrow, but lenders look at one person's credit, and there
are no shares to sell.

### Partnership

Two or more people own and run the business together, sharing profit,
decisions, and — in a **general partnership** — unlimited liability.
Each partner can bind the firm: one partner's business decision can
create a debt the other partners share. That is why partnerships run on
written agreements spelling out who contributes what, who decides what,
and how a partner leaves. A partnership pools more capital and more
skills than one person alone, but it adds a new risk: your partner's
mistake can become your debt. (Some partnership variants limit
liability for some partners; the general partnership is the baseline
case taught here, and the adult notes local variants if they matter.)

### Corporation

A corporation is a separate legal person: it can own property, sign
contracts, borrow, and be sued in its own name. Its owners are
**shareholders** who own shares of stock; they elect a board, which
hires managers to run the firm. Shareholders have limited liability:
if the corporation fails, a shareholder loses at most the value of the
shares. That shield is what lets a corporation raise large amounts of
capital by selling shares to many strangers — no one would buy a share
if doing so risked their house. The tradeoffs are complexity (charters,
records, boards, regulations), separation of ownership from control
(shareholders do not run the shop floor), and — for an ordinary
"C corporation" — double taxation: the corporation pays tax on its
profit, and shareholders pay tax again on dividends. Other corporate
tax treatments (such as "S corporation" status) pass profit through to
owners' personal tax returns instead; the adult explains the local
version if the learner asks, but the exam point is the tradeoff, not
the tax code.

### A fourth form to recognize: the LLC

A **limited liability company (LLC)** blends features: like a
corporation, it generally shields owners' personal assets; like a
partnership, its profit usually passes through to owners for tax
purposes without a second corporate-level tax. Many small firms choose
it for exactly that combination. This unit asks you to recognize it and
state its blend; it does not ask you to choose it over the other three
in every case, because the right choice depends on the founder's goals,
state rules, and tax situation — all adult-checked, real-world details.

## 3. Worked examples

**Example A — Maya's cart.** Maya runs a juice cart alone, owns it
outright, and keeps every dollar of profit. A customer is injured by a
falling sign and wins a claim larger than the cart's savings. Maya's
form is a sole proprietorship, so her liability is unlimited: her
personal savings can be claimed, not just the cart's money. The
reasoning step: ask "who is legally separate from the firm?" Here, no
one — Maya *is* the firm in the law's eyes. A common error is to say
"the business pays, not Maya." For a sole proprietorship, there is no
"business" separate from Maya to pay.

**Example B — Two founders, one big order.** Dan and Priya form a
general partnership to run a catering firm. Priya signs a contract for
a large event without telling Dan; the event loses money and the firm
owes the venue. Both partners share the debt, because in a general
partnership each partner can bind the firm and liability is unlimited.
The lesson is not "never partner" — it is "write the agreement first":
who may sign, up to what amount, and how disputes are settled. If Dan
and Priya had incorporated instead, the *corporation* would owe the
venue, and their personal savings would generally be shielded (up to
their investment) — but they would also face the corporation's
formalities and costs.

## 4. Guided practice

1. A founder wants to open three shops quickly, needs $200,000 from
   outside investors, and refuses to risk her house. Which form fits
   best, and which feature of that form does the deciding work?
2. A founder wants the simplest possible start, full control, and is
   willing to accept personal risk for a tiny side business. Which
   form, and what is its main danger?
3. Why might two skilled friends still hesitate before forming a
   general partnership, even though it pools their capital?

## 5. Independent practice

4. A shareholder owns $5,000 of stock in a corporation that goes
   bankrupt owing $2 million. How much can she lose as a shareholder,
   and why?
5. Name one advantage a corporation has over a sole proprietorship in
   raising capital, and one disadvantage in control or complexity.
6. A firm is an LLC. In one sentence each, state what it borrows from
   the corporation and what it borrows from the partnership.
7. **Error analysis:** A learner writes, "Corporations are always the
   best form because owners can never lose money." Identify the two
   errors in that sentence and correct each.

## 6. Application

A founder comes to you with three facts: she is starting alone, she
has $8,000 of personal savings she cannot afford to lose, and she
expects to stay small for two years. Recommend a form, give two
reasons, and name one risk she still bears under your recommendation.
(There is a defensible case for a sole proprietorship with insurance
and careful borrowing, and a defensible case for an LLC; the rubric in
the teacher guide credits either, if the liability reasoning is right.)

## 7. Exit check

In your own words: what does limited liability protect, and what does
it *not* protect? (Hint: it limits what an owner can lose — it does
not make the firm itself unable to fail, and it does not shield fraud
or personal wrongdoing, which the law treats separately.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** The four questions in §1 are the whole lesson
  in miniature. Answer them for each form in a four-row table before
  writing anything else.
- **Alternate response mode:** Answer guided practice orally, or by
  completing the table, instead of in sentences.
- **Extension:** Research, with the adult, one real local rule: does
  your state require a filing fee or publication step for an LLC or a
  corporation? Report the rule, its source, and its date — and note
  that rules change, so a dated source is part of the answer.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "limited liability means the firm cannot go
bankrupt." Correct it now: limited liability limits the *owner's*
loss, not the firm's failure. The firm can still collapse, workers can
still lose jobs, and creditors can still lose money. See
[teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).
