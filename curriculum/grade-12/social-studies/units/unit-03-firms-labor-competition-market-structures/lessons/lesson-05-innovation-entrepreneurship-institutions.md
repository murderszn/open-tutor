# Lesson 5 — Innovation, Entrepreneurship, and Institutions

**Goal:** Explain the entrepreneur's role (risk, new combinations,
creative destruction) and explain how property rights, the rule of
law, and contract enforcement support investment and growth.
**Time:** ~50 minutes (plus the W14S3 research lab) · **Materials:**
this lesson; one adult-selected source for the research lab.
**Readiness check:** Think of a product or service that did not exist
when your parents were children, and one that has largely disappeared.
What replaced the disappeared one, and who did the replacing? That
replacement process is this lesson's subject.

## 1. The entrepreneur: a distinct role

Workers supply labor, lenders supply capital, and landowners supply
land. The **entrepreneur** supplies something else: the decision to
combine those resources in a *new* way, bearing the risk that the
combination fails. The economist Joseph Schumpeter (writing in the
early 1940s) called the entrepreneur the agent of **creative
destruction**: new products, processes, and business models destroy
old ones, even as they create new value. Streaming services largely
replacing video-rental shops is a plain example: the new combination
(delivery over the internet, subscription pricing) did not merely add
a competitor to rental shops; it made their model unprofitable.

Three features define the role:

- **Risk-bearing under uncertainty.** An entrepreneur commits
  resources before knowing whether customers will come. That is
  different from a manager running a known process, and different
  from an investor who spreads risk across many bets.
- **New combinations.** Innovation is not only invention. It can be
  a new product, a new production method, a new market, a new source
  of supply, or a new way of organizing a firm. The lesson's test for
  "innovation" is whether the combination changes what is produced or
  how, not whether it uses new technology.
- **Displacement is part of the story.** Creative destruction helps
  consumers on average and hurts specific workers, firms, and towns.
  A complete account names both: cheaper, better options for buyers,
  and lost jobs and stranded skills for the displaced. Unit 04's
  policy discussion (who bears adjustment costs) starts here.

Entrepreneurship is not a synonym for "starting any business." A new
bakery identical to ten others is a firm, and a useful one; the
entrepreneurial element is larger when the combination is new — a
new product, a new market, or a new method — and the risk is real.

## 2. Institutions: the rules that make markets possible

A market is not just buyers and sellers meeting. It runs on
**institutions**: the rules, norms, and enforcement systems that let
strangers trust each other enough to trade, invest, and lend. Three
matter most for this unit:

- **Property rights:** clearly defined rights to own, use, exclude
  others from, and sell an asset. If you cannot be sure you own what
  you built, you will build less, or build only what you can guard
  yourself.
- **Rule of law:** rules that apply predictably and impartially,
  including to the powerful — so that a deal's outcome does not
  depend on whom you know or who governs this year.
- **Contract enforcement:** courts or equivalent mechanisms that make
  agreements stick, at a cost and speed that ordinary firms can use.
  A contract nobody enforces is a hope, not a commitment.

**Worked example — two hypothetical cases (not real countries).**
Case A: contracts are enforced quickly and impartially; property
records are public and reliable. Case B: contracts are often
unenforced, and property claims are uncertain. In Case A, a lender can
lend against a workshop's equipment, a supplier can deliver on credit,
and a founder can sign a five-year lease — all because each party can
count on the agreement. In Case B, the same transactions shrink:
lenders demand larger collateral or refuse, suppliers demand cash in
advance, and founders invest only in what pays back fast. Output is
lower in Case B not because its people or resources are worse, but
because its institutions raise the cost of trusting. That is the
mechanism behind the track's claim that institutions are a growth
condition — and it is why the research lab asks *how* an entrepreneur's
venture depended on institutions, not just *what* the venture sold.

A caution in both directions: institutions help, but they are not a
complete explanation of prosperity. Resources, geography, education,
technology, and history all matter, and strong institutions can
coexist with inequality and exclusion. The Week 14 argument task
("resources or institutions?") is graded on acknowledging both sides,
not on picking the "right" one.

## 3. Worked examples

**Example A — Is it entrepreneurship?** Three cases: (1) A cook opens
a tenth identical sandwich shop in a town. (2) A cook invents a
low-cost method to keep meals hot for delivery and builds a delivery
business around it, risking her savings. (3) A manager cuts costs at
an existing chain by 5%. Case (2) is the clearest entrepreneurship:
a new combination (method + business model) under risk. Case (1) is
firm formation with little new combination; case (3) is management
improving a known process. The distinction is a matter of degree, and
the teacher guide credits answers that argue the borderline cases
with the three features in §1.

**Example B — An institution in one transaction.** A farmer wants a
loan to buy a tractor, offering the farm as collateral. Walk the
chain: the loan works only if (a) the farmer's ownership is recorded
and undisputed (property rights), (b) the loan contract will be
enforced if either side defaults (contract enforcement), and (c) the
rules will not change arbitrarily mid-loan (rule of law). Break any
one link and the loan shrinks or disappears — and with it, the
tractor, the extra output, and the income it would have produced.
That chain is the model to reuse in the argument task.

## 4. Guided practice

1. Give one original example of creative destruction (not streaming
   or video rental) and name what was displaced.
2. Which institution is most directly at stake if a court takes ten
   years to hear a contract dispute? Explain in one sentence.
3. Why would weak property rights push founders toward short-payback
   projects? Use the Case A / Case B reasoning.
4. Is every new business an act of entrepreneurship, in this lesson's
   sense? Answer yes or no, with the "new combination" test.

## 5. Independent practice

5. A town's property records are unreliable: two people claim the
   same plot. Explain two economic consequences (lending, investment,
   or trade — pick two) in two sentences each.
6. **Error analysis:** A learner writes, "Creative destruction means
   innovation is always good for everyone." Identify what is missing
   (the displaced) and rewrite the sentence fairly.
7. An inventor patents a device, then licenses it to manufacturers.
   Which institution makes the license valuable, and what would
   happen to the inventor's incentive without it?
8. Compare: a country rich in natural resources but with weak
   contract enforcement, and a country poor in resources but with
   strong institutions. Which does this lesson predict will attract
   more long-term investment, and what is the mechanism?

## 6. Application

For the Week 14 research lab, choose (with the adult) one
entrepreneur from a verified source. Write a source note answering:
What was the new combination? What risk did the entrepreneur bear?
What did it displace, if anything? And which institution (property
rights, rule of law, or contract enforcement) did the venture most
depend on — with one fact from the source? If the source does not
answer a question, say so; do not invent the answer.

## 7. Exit check

In your own words: what does an entrepreneur do that a manager of an
existing process does not, and why would weak contract enforcement
make an entrepreneur invest less?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** The Case A / Case B pair in §2 is the whole
  institutions argument in miniature. Reuse its chain (lend → deliver
  on credit → sign a long lease) for any institutions question.
- **Alternate response mode:** Answer guided practice as a two-column
  table (Case A vs. Case B) instead of in sentences.
- **Extension:** Research, with the adult, one real institutional
  reform (for example, a country's move to public, searchable property
  records) from a verified source. Report what changed, for whom, and
  what the source does *not* claim about its effects.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "entrepreneur = anyone who starts a business";
"creative destruction means everyone gains"; "institutions = only
government" (norms, business customs, and private arbitration are
institutions too, though this unit emphasizes the three named ones).
