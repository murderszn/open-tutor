# Lesson 3 — Labor Markets: Wages as the Price of Work

**Goal:** Explain wage determination with labor demand and supply;
use human capital and productivity to explain wage differences; and
read real earnings-by-education data without mistaking correlation for
a single cause.
**Time:** ~50 minutes (plus the W12S3 investigation) · **Materials:**
this lesson, a calculator.
**Readiness check:** Why might two jobs that both take 40 hours a week
pay very different wages? List as many reasons as you can in two
minutes; keep the list — this lesson tests each reason against the
model.

## 1. Labor demand is derived demand

Firms do not hire workers because they like employing people. They
hire because workers produce things customers buy. So **labor demand
is derived demand**: it is derived from demand for the firm's product
and from what an extra worker adds. The key concept is the **value of
marginal product (VMP)**: the extra revenue from one more worker's
extra output.

VMP = (extra output from one more worker) × (price of that output).

A profit-seeking firm hires an extra worker if VMP exceeds the wage,
and stops when the next worker's VMP falls to the wage. Because of
**diminishing marginal product** — each extra worker adds less than
the one before, as equipment, space, and customers are fixed in the
short run — VMP falls as hiring rises. That is why the labor-demand
curve slopes down: at a lower wage, more workers are worth hiring.

**Labor supply** slopes up: at a higher wage, more people are willing
to work in that occupation (and current workers may work more hours),
while at a lower wage some choose other occupations, schooling, or
leisure. Equilibrium wage and employment are where the two meet —
exactly the Unit 02 mechanism, with wages on the price axis.

## 2. What shifts wages: four shocks, four predictions

- **Product demand rises** → labor demand rises → wage rises,
  employment rises. (A popular restaurant chain's sales boom lifts
  cooks' wages in its town.)
- **Productivity rises** (better tools, training, technology) → each
  worker produces more → VMP rises → labor demand rises → wage rises,
  employment rises.
- **More workers enter the occupation** (a new training program,
  migration, a licensing change) → labor supply rises → wage falls,
  employment rises.
- **Workers leave the occupation** → labor supply falls → wage rises,
  employment falls.

Note the discipline of the model: a wage rise can come from demand
*or* from a supply fall, and the two have opposite employment effects.
That is why the investigation asks for wage *and* employment
predictions, never wage alone.

## 3. Human capital and the earnings table

**Human capital** is the productive skill and knowledge a worker
carries: schooling, training, experience, health, and work habits.
More human capital generally raises productivity, which raises VMP,
which raises the wage a firm can profitably pay. It also works as a
**signal**: completing a demanding program tells employers something
about persistence and trainability, even before the worker produces
anything. Both mechanisms point the same way — more schooling is
associated with higher earnings — but they imply different policies,
and the data below cannot by themselves separate them.

Here is a real, dated dataset: median usual weekly earnings for
full-time wage and salary workers age 25 and over, 2024 annual
averages, U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), Current Population
Survey, table 37b (checked 2026-10-28; see the resource pack for the
link):

| Educational attainment | Median weekly earnings, 2024 |
|---|---:|
| Less than a high school diploma | $738 |
| High school graduates, no college | $930 |
| Some college or associate degree | $1,056 |
| Bachelor's degree only | $1,543 |
| Advanced degree | $1,901 |

These are **medians** (the middle worker), for **full-time** workers
**age 25 and over**, in **2024**. They describe groups, not destinies:
half of bachelor's-only workers earned less than $1,543 a week, and
many high school graduates earned more than many graduates.

## 4. Worked examples

**Example A — The café hiring decision (fictional).** A café sells
coffee at $4 a cup. One extra worker adds 40 cups a day: VMP =
40 × $4 = $160 a day. A second extra worker adds only 25 cups:
VMP = 25 × $4 = $100 a day. The daily wage is $120. Hire the first
worker ($160 > $120) and not the second ($100 < $120). The reasoning
step is comparison at the margin: never compare the *total* output of
all workers with the wage, and never hire on average product. The
common error is to hire the second worker because "the café still
makes money overall." Overall profit is not the hiring rule; the
marginal worker's VMP against the wage is.

**Example B — Reading the BLS table without overclaiming.** A
bachelor's-only worker's median ($1,543) exceeds a high school
graduate's median ($930) by $613 a week — about 1.66 times as much,
or roughly $31,900 a year if you multiply by 52 weeks (an
approximation, since weekly earnings are medians, not annual salaries,
and not everyone works 52 weeks). What does that gap *prove*? It proves
that earnings differ across education groups. It does not prove that
one more degree *causes* the whole gap: degree holders also differ in
occupation, hours, experience, location, and family background, and
the table does not control for any of those. A careful sentence is:
"Education is associated with higher median earnings, and human
capital and signaling are two explanations; the table alone cannot
measure how much each contributes." An incautious sentence is:
"A degree causes $613 a week." The unit's argument task grades the
difference.

## 5. Guided practice

1. A worker's extra output is 30 units a day at $5 each. What is her
   VMP? If her wage is $130 a day, should the firm hire her? Why?
2. Demand for a firm's product doubles. Predict the direction of the
   wage and employment in that occupation, and name the curve that
   shifted.
3. From the BLS table, how much more is the advanced-degree median
   than the high school median, per week? Express it as a difference
   and as a ratio (advanced ÷ high school).
4. Name two factors *other than education* that could contribute to
   the earnings gaps in the table.

## 6. Independent practice

5. A training program graduates many new electricians in one town.
   Predict wage and employment effects, and state which curve shifted.
6. **Error analysis:** A learner writes, "The BLS table proves that
   staying in school causes higher pay, so everyone should get an
   advanced degree." Identify what the table cannot show, and rewrite
   the claim carefully.
7. A hospital's nurses become more productive with new equipment.
   Predict wage and employment effects for nurses, and explain the
   mechanism in one sentence (VMP language).
8. Why does labor supply slope upward? Give a reason in terms of a
   worker's choice, not just "the curve says so."

## 7. Application

Pick one wage difference you have noticed (between two occupations, or
between two places for the same occupation). Write a paragraph that
offers at least two explanations from this lesson (demand, supply,
human capital, productivity, working conditions, or other
compensating differences) and says what evidence would help you tell
them apart. Do not settle the question with one cause.

## 8. Exit check

In two sentences: what is VMP, and why does a firm stop hiring when
the next worker's VMP equals the wage?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** Translate every shock in §2 into the sentence
  frame: "___ shifted, so the wage ___ and employment ___ because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** Answer guided practice with a
  labor-market graph (wage on the vertical axis) instead of sentences,
  labeling the shift and the new equilibrium.
- **Extension:** The BLS table also reports medians by sex (for
  example, in 2024, bachelor's-only medians were $1,770 for men and
  $1,344 for women). With the adult, discuss why a raw group gap is
  not by itself evidence of a single cause — occupation mix, hours,
  experience, and other factors must be examined before any causal
  claim — and why the unit does not ask you to explain the whole gap.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: VMP confused with total output; correlation in
the BLS table treated as proof of causation; wage treated as set by
employers alone or by workers alone, rather than by supply and demand
together. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).
