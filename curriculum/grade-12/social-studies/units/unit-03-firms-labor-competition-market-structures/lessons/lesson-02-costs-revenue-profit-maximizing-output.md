# Lesson 2 — Costs, Revenue, and the Profit-Maximizing Output

**Goal:** Compute total revenue, total cost, and profit from a
schedule; find the profit-maximizing output with the marginal rule;
and apply the short-run shutdown rule.
**Time:** ~50 minutes (plus the W11S3 investigation) · **Materials:**
this lesson, a calculator, graph paper (optional).
**Readiness check:** If a shop sells 10 items at $5 each and its costs
are $30, what is its profit? (Answer: revenue $50 − cost $30 = $20.
If you got that, you have the whole profit idea; this lesson adds the
question "how *many* items should it sell?")

## 1. The firm's arithmetic

- **Total revenue (TR)** = price × quantity sold: TR = P × Q.
- **Total cost (TC)** = **fixed cost (FC)** + **variable cost (VC)**.
  Fixed costs do not change with output in the short run: rent,
  insurance, a loan payment. Variable costs rise with output: flour,
  packaging, hourly wages for extra shifts.
- **Profit** = TR − TC. A negative profit is a loss.
- **Average total cost (ATC)** = TC ÷ Q. **Average variable cost
  (AVC)** = VC ÷ Q.
- **Marginal cost (MC)** = the extra cost of one more unit (or, in a
  table with steps, the extra cost of the next batch ÷ the batch size).
- **Marginal revenue (MR)** = the extra revenue from one more unit
  sold. For a **price taker** — a firm too small to move the market
  price — MR equals the price, because each extra unit sells at the
  same market price.

Two profit concepts must not be confused. **Accounting profit** is
revenue minus the explicit, paid-out costs (rent, wages, materials).
**Economic profit** subtracts opportunity cost as well: what the
owner's time and money could have earned elsewhere. A firm can show an
accounting profit and still be earning zero economic profit — that is
the **normal profit** case, where the owner is earning exactly what
those resources could earn in their next-best use, so there is no
reason to leave, and no reason for a new firm to enter. This lesson
computes accounting profit from the schedule (the schedule's costs are
explicit costs); Lesson 2's extension and the teacher guide flag where
opportunity cost would change the verdict.

## 2. The Harbor Bakery schedule (fictional practice data)

The Harbor Bakery sells loaves at a market price of $9. Its weekly
costs are:

| Loaves per week (Q) | FC | VC | TC | MC per loaf | TR at $9 | Profit |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | $100 | $0 | $100 | — | $0 | −$100 |
| 10 | $100 | $50 | $150 | $5.00 | $90 | −$60 |
| 20 | $100 | $90 | $190 | $4.00 | $180 | −$10 |
| 30 | $100 | $120 | $220 | $3.00 | $270 | $50 |
| 40 | $100 | $160 | $260 | $4.00 | $360 | $100 |
| 50 | $100 | $220 | $320 | $6.00 | $450 | $130 |
| 60 | $100 | $300 | $400 | $8.00 | $540 | $140 |
| 70 | $100 | $400 | $500 | $10.00 | $630 | $130 |

Read one row slowly: at 60 loaves, TC = $100 + $300 = $400; TR =
60 × $9 = $540; profit = $540 − $400 = $140. Marginal cost for the
step from 50 to 60 loaves is ($400 − $320) ÷ 10 = $8.00 per loaf.
Notice MC first falls ($5, $4, $3) — the bakery gets better at using
its oven — then rises ($4, $6, $8, $10) as crowding and overtime set
in. That U-shape is the usual pattern, and it is what makes the
profit-maximizing rule work.

## 3. Worked examples

**Example A — Find the profit-maximizing output two ways.** *By the
profit column:* the largest profit is $140, at 60 loaves. *By the
marginal rule:* expand while MR > MC, stop when MR = MC, and never
expand where MC > MR. Here MR = $9 (the price). The steps: MC $5 < $9
(expand), $4 < $9 (expand), $3 < $9 (expand), $4 < $9 (expand),
$6 < $9 (expand), $8 < $9 (expand), $10 > $9 (stop — the 61st through
70th loaves each cost $10 and earn $9, losing $1 apiece, or $10 over
the batch). Both methods agree: 60 loaves. The marginal rule is the
more powerful method, because it tells you *why* 60 is best — every
step up to 60 added profit, and the next step would subtract it — and
it works even when you cannot see the whole profit column.

**Example B — The shutdown decision.** Suppose the market price falls
to $3, with the same costs. At 20 loaves: TR = $60, TC = $190, profit
= −$130. At 10 loaves: TR = $30, TC = $150, profit = −$120. At zero
output the bakery still pays its $100 fixed cost, so shutting down
loses $100 — *less* than any operating loss. The rule: in the short
run, operate only if price covers average variable cost (P ≥ AVC);
otherwise shut down and lose only the fixed cost. Check at 30 loaves:
AVC = $120 ÷ 30 = $4.00, which is above the $3 price, so every loaf
sold fails to cover even its variable cost — shutting down is right.
Contrast the $4 price case: at 30 loaves, AVC = $4.00 and price =
$4.00, so operating loses exactly $100, the same as shutting down;
the firm is indifferent in the short run (and in the long run, when
fixed costs can be avoided, it would exit unless the price recovers).
A common error is to shut down whenever profit is negative. That is
wrong in the short run: if price covers variable cost, operating loses
*less* than closing, because revenue helps pay the fixed cost that
must be paid anyway.

## 4. Guided practice

Use the Harbor Bakery schedule above.

1. At 40 loaves, what are TR, TC, and profit? Show each step.
2. Compute MC per loaf for the step from 30 to 40 loaves. Is it below
   or above MR ($9)? Should the bakery expand past 40? Why?
3. At 60 loaves, compute ATC and AVC. Why is price ($9) above both a
   good sign for this firm?
4. If the price fell to $6, would the profit-maximizing output rise,
   fall, or stay at 60? Justify with one MC comparison (do not
   recompute the whole table unless you want to check yourself).

## 5. Independent practice

5. A second fictional firm has FC $50, and its VC is $0, $30, $70,
   $120, $180 for Q = 0, 10, 20, 30, 40. Its price is $6. Build its TC,
   TR, and profit columns and find its profit-maximizing output.
6. Explain in two sentences why MC typically falls first and then
   rises. (Hint: specialization, then crowding.)
7. **Error analysis:** A learner writes, "The bakery should produce 70
   loaves because that gives the most revenue ($630)." Explain why
   maximizing revenue is not maximizing profit, using the bakery's
   60-loaf and 70-loaf profits.
8. A firm is losing money but its price ($8) exceeds its AVC ($5).
   Should it shut down this week? Explain with the fixed-cost logic.

## 6. Application

The bakery's owner considers buying a second oven (a fixed cost in the
short run once bought, but a choice now). In a paragraph, explain what
she must compare before buying: which marginal cost would change, what
would happen to profit at 60 loaves if the oven only lowered MC for
outputs above 60, and what price assumption her whole calculation
depends on. (A price fall would undo the case; the teacher guide
credits any answer that names the MC change, the price assumption, and
the profit comparison.)

## 7. Exit check

Without looking at the table: the marginal rule in one sentence, and
the shutdown rule in one sentence. Then check yourself against §3.

## Supports and extensions

- **Numbers alternative:** Every question above can be answered from
  the table alone; graphing the curves is optional, never required.
- **Reading support:** Cover the profit column, decide from MC vs. MR
  alone, then uncover it to check — the marginal rule first, the
  answer column second.
- **Extension:** Recompute the bakery's profit-maximizing output at a
  price of $12. (Answer: still 60 loaves by the batch steps — MC $10
  for the 70-loaf step is still below $12, so check carefully: at $12,
  expanding to 70 adds MR $12 > MC $10 per loaf, so profit at 70 is
  $840 − $500 = $340, above 60-loaf profit $720 − $400 = $320. The
  profit-maximizing output *rises* to 70 loaves. If you first answered
  60, rework the last step — that is exactly the error this extension
  is designed to catch. The teacher guide works it in full.)
