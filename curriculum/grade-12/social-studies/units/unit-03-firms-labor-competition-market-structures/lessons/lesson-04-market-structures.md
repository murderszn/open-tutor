# Lesson 4 — Market Structures: Four Ways a Market Can Be Organized

**Goal:** Classify a market as perfect competition, monopolistic
competition, oligopoly, or monopoly from evidence about sellers,
product differences, and barriers to entry; and explain what each
structure does to price, output, variety, and innovation.
**Time:** ~50 minutes (plus the W13S3 investigation) · **Materials:**
this lesson, the image in `assets/market-structures-street.png`.
**Readiness check:** Name a market where you can easily switch sellers
if one raises its price, and a market where you cannot. What makes the
difference? (Hold that thought — the three tests below formalize it.)

## 1. Three tests for any market

Classify by evidence, never by a firm's size alone or by its name.
Ask, in order:

1. **How many sellers are there, and how large is each one's share?**
   Many tiny sellers, many small sellers, a few large sellers, or one?
2. **Are the products identical or differentiated?** Identical means
   a buyer sees no difference between sellers' products.
   **Differentiated** means sellers compete on features, quality,
   location, brand, or service, not just price.
3. **How high are the barriers to entry?** A **barrier to entry** is
   anything that makes it hard for a new firm to enter and compete:
   huge start-up costs, control of a key resource, a patent, a
   license, or network effects (a service more valuable because many
   people already use it).

A market is a *structure*, not a verdict: real markets sit near these
models, not exactly on them, and the honest classification often comes
with a "mostly" and a reason.

## 2. The four structures

### Perfect competition

Many sellers, identical products, free entry, and full information.
Each firm is a **price taker**: it sells at the market price, because
any buyer can buy the identical product next door. If one firm raises
its price, it sells nothing. Long-run economic profit tends toward
zero: profits attract entrants, entry expands supply, and price falls
until only normal profit remains. Textbook example: farmers selling
identical wheat (an approximation — real farms differ, but the model
captures the discipline of many identical sellers). Consequence:
price is pushed toward marginal cost (P = MC), output is high, and
there is little room for product variety — variety is not what this
market sells.

### Monopolistic competition

Many sellers, **differentiated** products, easy entry. Coffee shops,
restaurants, clothing brands: each has some control over its price
because its product is not identical to the neighbor's — but only a
little, because close substitutes are everywhere. Short-run profits
attract entrants, which steal customers and push each firm's demand
down, so long-run economic profit again tends toward zero. The
distinctive benefit is variety and choice; the distinctive cost is
excess capacity and advertising — firms spend to differentiate rather
than producing at the lowest possible cost.

### Oligopoly

A few large sellers dominate, with high barriers to entry.
**Interdependence** is the defining feature: each firm's decision
depends on what it expects rivals to do. If one carrier cuts prices,
rivals may match, so the cutter gains few customers and all earn less
— which is why oligopolists often compete on features, advertising,
or service instead of price, and why they watch each other closely.
Examples used in textbooks: mobile phone service and airlines on a
single route (in each case, a small number of large firms serve most
of the market). Consequences are mixed: large firms can fund research
and offer wide networks, but they can also sustain prices above
marginal cost and, where they collude (illegally, in most systems),
behave like a shared monopoly. Collusion is treated here as a legal
and institutional fact to note, not as a strategy to admire.

### Monopoly

One seller, with barriers so high that no close rival exists. The
monopolist is a **price maker**: it chooses a price (or, equivalently,
an output) along the market demand curve. Because it must lower its
price to sell more, its marginal revenue is below its price — and it
maximizes profit where MR = MC, at a lower output and a higher price
than a competitive market would produce. The gap between what buyers
would have paid and what the restricted output delivers is called
**deadweight loss**: trades that would have benefited both buyer and
seller do not happen. A **natural monopoly** is the exception that
proves the logic: where fixed costs are enormous and marginal cost
low (a city's water pipes — duplicating the pipe network would waste
resources), one firm can serve the market more cheaply than several,
so the usual policy response is regulation of its prices rather than
breaking it into competitors (Unit 04 returns to this).

## 3. Worked examples

**Example A — Classify from evidence, not from size.** Evidence: "In
this town, twelve bakeries sell bread; each claims its own recipe;
a new bakery can open with a rented oven and a permit." Number of
sellers: many. Products: differentiated (own recipes). Barriers: low.
Classification: monopolistic competition — even though each bakery is
small. The reasoning step is the three tests in order; "small" alone
would have misled you toward perfect competition, but identical
products are missing.

**Example B — The monopoly restriction, in numbers (fictional
illustration, not real prices).** Suppose a competitive market would
sell 100 units at $10 (P = MC = $10). A monopolist facing the same
demand and costs restricts output to 70 units and charges $14. Price
is above marginal cost, output is lower, and the 30 missing trades
(71st through 100th units, which buyers valued above $10 and the firm
could have produced at $10) are deadweight loss. The numbers are
invented to show the mechanism; no real drug, utility, or product is
priced at $14 in this example. The takeaway is directional and
general: monopoly → higher price, lower output, lost trades.

**Text-only alternative (same question, same evidence):** Four scenes
in words — (1) many stalls selling identical red apples under matching
awnings; (2) several shops, each a different color and style, selling
food and drink with outdoor tables; (3) two tall communication towers
behind a fence; (4) one large water plant with a single water tower
serving the town. Classify each scene (1–4) as perfect competition,
monopolistic competition, oligopoly, or monopoly, and give one
evidence phrase for each: the number of sellers, the product
difference (or sameness), or the barrier you see.

Expected reasoning (the teacher guide accepts defensible variants with
evidence): (1) perfect competition — many sellers, identical apples;
(2) monopolistic competition — many sellers, differentiated shops;
(3) oligopoly — a few large sellers, high infrastructure barriers
(the two towers suggest a small number of large networks, not a
single seller); (4) monopoly — one seller of an essential network
service, where duplicating the system would be wasteful (natural
monopoly). If you classified (3) as a duopoly, say so: a duopoly is
an oligopoly with two sellers, and the evidence (two towers) supports
it.

## 5. Guided practice

1. A market has three firms, high start-up costs, and nearly identical
   products. Classify it, and name the feature that most distinguishes
   it from a monopoly.
2. Why does a monopolistically competitive firm have *some* power over
   its price, while a perfectly competitive firm has none? Answer with
   the word "substitutes."
3. A patented medicine has one seller for the patented drug. Classify
   the market for *that drug*, and explain why the classification is
   not "the whole pharmaceutical market."
4. What is deadweight loss, in one sentence, using the Example B
   numbers?

## 6. Independent practice

5. Classify each: (a) wheat farming, (b) a town's restaurants,
   (c) mobile phone service, (d) the town water supply. Give one
   evidence phrase per classification.
6. **Error analysis:** A learner writes, "That firm is huge, so it
   must be a monopoly." Explain the error and give a counterexample
   (a huge firm in a competitive market).
7. Why might an oligopolist prefer to compete on advertising rather
   than on price? Use the word "interdependence."
8. A natural monopoly is regulated instead of broken up. Explain why,
   using the water-pipes reasoning.

## 7. Application

Pick one real market you know (adult-approved). Write a paragraph
classifying it with the three tests, citing one observable fact per
test (a count, a product difference or sameness, a barrier). End with
one sentence: what would have to change for your classification to
change?

## 8. Exit check

Without notes: the three tests, the four structures in order from
most to least competition, and one consequence of monopoly for price
and output.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** Keep the three tests as a checklist beside
  every classification task; do not classify from memory of the
  market's name.
- **Alternate response mode:** Classify by sorting evidence cards
  (seller count / product / barrier) into four piles instead of
  writing paragraphs.
- **Extension:** Compare two oligopolies: one where firms compete
  fiercely on price and one where they do not. What evidence would
  distinguish them — prices, advertising, or entry — and what would
  each pattern mean for consumers?

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "big = monopoly"; "monopoly = illegal" (many
monopolies are legal — patents, natural monopolies — and the policy
question is what to do about them, in Unit 04); "more firms always
means lower prices" (true only if products are substitutes and entry
is real). See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md).
