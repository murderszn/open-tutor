## Lesson 1 — Business organizations

**Guided practice.** 1. Corporation: limited liability protects her
house, and selling shares raises $200,000 from outside investors.
2. Sole proprietorship; its main danger is unlimited liability (plus
limited ability to raise capital). 3. Because in a general partnership
each partner can bind the firm and shares unlimited liability, so one
partner's decision can create the other's debt — hence the need for a
written agreement.

**Independent practice.** 4. At most $5,000, the value of her shares:
shareholders have limited liability. 5. Advantage: it can sell shares
to many investors; disadvantage: separation of ownership from control
and heavier formalities/regulation (either disadvantage accepted).
6. From the corporation: limited liability (personal-asset shield);
from the partnership: pass-through taxation (profit taxed on owners'
returns, generally no second corporate-level tax). 7. Errors:
(a) owners *can* lose money — up to their investment; limited
liability caps loss, it does not eliminate it; (b) "always best" is
false — corporations carry formality, cost, and control tradeoffs,
and a small firm may rationally choose a simpler form.

**Application.** Either a sole proprietorship (simplest, full
control; risk: unlimited liability, mitigated only partly by insurance
and cautious borrowing) or an LLC (liability shield with pass-through
taxation; risk: filing costs/rules and still-personal liability for
personal wrongdoing or personally guaranteed debts) earns full marks
if the liability reasoning is correct and one remaining risk is named.

**Exit check.** Limited liability protects an owner's personal assets
beyond the amount invested; it does not prevent the firm from failing
and does not shield fraud or personal wrongdoing.

## Lesson 2 — Costs, revenue, profit-maximizing output

**Guided practice.** 1. At 40 loaves: TR = 40 × $9 = $360; TC = $260;
profit = $360 − $260 = $100. 2. MC from 30 to 40 loaves =
($260 − $220) ÷ 10 = $4.00 per loaf, below MR ($9), so expand past
40: that step adds $9 − $4 = $5 per loaf of profit. 3. At 60 loaves:
ATC = $400 ÷ 60 = $6.67; AVC = $300 ÷ 60 = $5.00. Price above both
means revenue covers all costs per loaf with $2.33 per loaf to spare
over ATC. 4. Fall: at a lower price, fewer MC steps satisfy MR > MC,
so the profit-maximizing output falls (the exact new output requires
recomputing, but the direction follows from the marginal rule).

**Independent practice.** 5. Second firm (price $6): Q 0/10/20/30/40;
TC $50/$80/$120/$170/$230; TR $0/$60/$120/$180/$240; profit
−$50/−$20/$0/$10/$10. Profit-maximizing outputs within this table:
30 and 40 tie at $10. The step from 30 to 40 has MC =
($230 − $170) ÷ 10 = $6.00 per unit = MR ($6), so it adds zero profit
and the profit stays at $10. Accept 30 or 40 with that reasoning;
accept "30 or 40 (tie)" as the full answer. 6. MC falls first from
specialization and better use of fixed equipment, then rises as
crowding, overtime, and diminishing returns set in. 7. Revenue at 70
loaves ($630) exceeds revenue at 60 ($540), but cost also rises
($500 vs. $400); profit falls from $140 to $130, so maximizing revenue
does not maximize profit. 8. No: price $8 > AVC $5, so operating loses
less than shutting down — revenue covers variable costs and
contributes toward fixed costs.

**Application.** Full marks for naming: the new oven changes MC
(especially at higher outputs); if it only lowers MC above 60 loaves,
profit at 60 is unchanged and the case depends on higher outputs and
on the $9 price holding; a price fall could undo the gain. The fixed
cost of the oven itself must be weighed against the extra profit it
creates.

**Lesson 2 extension (price $12, within the table).** TR at 60 =
$720, profit $320; TR at 70 = $840, profit $340. The 70-loaf step has
MC $10 < MR $12, so profit-maximizing output within the table rises
to 70 loaves. The table ends at 70, so no claim is made beyond it.

## Lesson 3 — Labor markets

**Guided practice.** 1. VMP = 30 × $5 = $150 a day > wage $130, so
hire her. 2. Product demand rises → labor demand rises → wage rises,
employment rises; the demand curve shifted. 3. Advanced minus high
school: $1,901 − $930 = $971 a week; ratio $1,901 ÷ $930 = 2.04.
4. Any two of: occupation mix, hours worked, experience/age,
location, industry, family background, or unmeasured ability
(accept any two with a sentence each).

**Independent practice.** 5. Labor supply rises → wage falls,
employment rises. 6. The table cannot show causation or control for
occupation, hours, experience, location, or background; a careful
rewrite: "Median earnings are higher among more-educated full-time
workers age 25+ in 2024; education is associated with higher earnings
through human capital and signaling, among other factors." The claim
that "everyone should get an advanced degree" also ignores cost,
fit, and that medians describe groups, not individual outcomes.
7. Productivity rises → VMP rises → labor demand rises → wage rises,
employment rises. Mechanism: each nurse produces more value, so the
firm can profitably pay more and hire more. 8. At a higher wage, the
job beats more alternatives (other jobs, schooling, leisure), so more
people offer their labor in that occupation.

**Application.** Full marks for two explanations from the lesson,
applied to the chosen difference, plus a statement of what evidence
would separate them (for example, comparing same-occupation wages
across places, or same-education wages across occupations).

**Exit check.** VMP is the extra revenue from one more worker's extra
output. The firm stops hiring when the next worker's VMP equals the
wage because the next worker after that would add less revenue than
the wage costs (diminishing marginal product).

## Lesson 4 — Market structures

**Guided practice.** 1. Oligopoly: few sellers and high barriers
distinguish it from monopoly (which has one seller). 2. A
monopolistically competitive firm's product is differentiated, so
buyers do not treat rivals' products as perfect substitutes and some
will stay despite a small price rise; a perfectly competitive firm's
product is identical, so any price rise loses all buyers. 3. Monopoly
for that drug (one seller, patent barrier); it is not the whole
pharmaceutical market, which contains many drugs and sellers.
4. Deadweight loss is the value of mutually beneficial trades that do
not happen because the monopolist restricts output (in Example B, the
30 units from 71 to 100).

**Independent practice.** 5. (a) Perfect competition — many sellers,
identical wheat, free entry. (b) Monopolistic competition — many
sellers, differentiated menus. (c) Oligopoly — few large sellers,
high costs. (d) Monopoly (natural monopoly) — one seller, one network.
6. Error: size is not a test; the tests are seller count, product
differences, and barriers. Counterexample: a huge wheat-farming
corporation still faces many sellers of an identical product, or a
huge retailer facing many close substitutes and easy entry. 7. In an
oligopoly, a price cut is likely to be matched by rivals (interdependence),
so it gains few customers and lowers everyone's revenue; advertising
or features can win customers without triggering a price war.
8. Duplicating the pipe network would waste resources (enormous fixed
cost, low marginal cost), so one firm serves the market most cheaply;
regulation controls its price instead of competition disciplining it.

**Image activity.** (1) Perfect competition — many identical apple
stalls. (2) Monopolistic competition — many differentiated shops.
(3) Oligopoly (duopoly accepted) — two large networks, high
infrastructure barriers. (4) Monopoly / natural monopoly — one water
system serving the town. Each classification needs its evidence
phrase; a classification without evidence loses the mark for that
scene.

**Application.** Full marks for a three-test classification with one
observable fact per test and a stated classification-changing fact.

**Exit check.** Tests: sellers, product differences, barriers.
Order: perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly,
monopoly. Monopoly consequence: higher price, lower output than
competition.

## Lesson 5 — Innovation and institutions

**Guided practice.** 1. Any original example with a displaced product,
firm, or occupation named (streaming/video-rental excluded by the
question). 2. Contract enforcement: a ten-year delay makes the
contract practically unenforceable, so parties cannot rely on it.
3. With weak property rights, long-horizon investments may be seized
or disputed before they pay back, so founders choose projects that
return money quickly. 4. No: the test is a *new* combination under
risk, not mere firm formation.

**Independent practice.** 5. Any two of: lenders refuse or demand
more collateral (credit shrinks); founders invest less or only
short-term; buyers/sellers demand cash in advance (trade shrinks);
disputed plots cannot be sold or improved. 6. Missing: the displaced
— workers, firms, and towns harmed by the change. Rewrite: "Innovation
creates new value and displaces old products and jobs; whether it is
good overall, and for whom, depends on the gains, the losses, and how
adjustment costs are shared." 7. Property rights (the patent) and
contract enforcement (the license): without them, copiers could use
the device freely and the inventor's incentive to invent and disclose
would fall. 8. The resource-poor, strong-institution country is
predicted to attract more long-term investment, because enforceable
contracts and secure property lower the risk of lending, building,
and signing long agreements.

**Application / research lab.** Full marks for a source note that
answers the four questions from the source, or honestly reports which
question the source does not answer. Invented facts earn no marks for
the affected element.

**Exit check.** An entrepreneur combines resources in a new way under
uncertainty and risk, rather than running a known process. Weak
contract enforcement makes agreements unreliable, so firms lend less,
demand cash, and invest only short-term — lowering investment.

## Unit assessment (80 points)

**A.** 1. TR: $0/$200/$400/$600/$800/$1,000. Profit:
−$120/−$20/$100/$240/$360/$460. MC per chair: $10.00/$8.00/$6.00/
$8.00/$10.00 (for example, 10→20 chairs: ($300 − $220) ÷ 10 = $8.00).
2. Profit-maximizing output within this table: 50 chairs, profit
$460. Profit column: $460 is the largest profit shown. Marginal rule:
MR = $20 exceeds MC at every step ($10, $8, $6, $8, $10), so each
expansion to 50 added profit; no step within the table has MC > MR.
(The table ends at 50, so no claim is made beyond it.) 3. (a) At 20
chairs and $9: TR = $180; profit = $180 − $300 = −$120; AVC =
$180 ÷ 20 = $9.00. (b) Indifferent in the short run: price ($9) equals
AVC ($9), so operating loses exactly $120 — the same as the $120
fixed-cost loss from shutting down. (Any answer of "shut down" or
"operate" without the equality reasoning loses the justification marks;
"indifferent" with the comparison earns full marks.)

**B.** 4. VMP = 25 × $8 = $200 > wage $180, so hire. 5. Labor supply
rises → wage falls, employment rises; mechanism: more workers compete
for the same jobs, so the equilibrium wage falls while more workers
are employed. 6. (a) $1,901 ÷ $930 = 2.04. (b) Full marks for a
paragraph stating the gaps are group medians for full-time workers
age 25+ in 2024, naming at least two non-education factors, and
stating the correlation-causation caution.

**C.** 7. (a) Perfect competition — 200 sellers, identical wheat,
free entry. (b) Monopolistic competition — many sellers,
differentiated menus. (c) Oligopoly — two sellers, huge costs.
(d) Monopoly (natural monopoly) — one company, one network.
8. (a) Price higher ($15 vs. $10), output lower (60 vs. 100).
(b) The 61st–100th units are lost: buyers valued them above the $10
marginal cost, and the firm could have produced them at $10, so both
sides would have gained from those trades; the monopolist does not
make them because selling more would require lowering the price on
all units.

**D.** 9. Creative destruction: new combinations displace old
products, firms, and jobs; full marks for an original example with a
gaining group and a losing group named. 10. Full marks for the chain:
unreliable property records mean the workshop's collateral cannot be
trusted; rarely enforced contracts mean the loan agreement cannot be
relied on; so the lender refuses or demands more security, the
workshop cannot buy equipment, and its growth (output, hiring) is
limited — not because its product is unviable, but because its
agreements are unenforceable.

**E.** 11. Full marks (10) require: a natural-monopoly reason (one
network is cheapest; duplication wastes fixed costs), a
pro-competition reason (price discipline, service, cost revelation),
and a stated information need (cost structure, regulated price vs.
cost, service quality, or entry costs). One-sided verdicts cap at 6;
a verdict with no information need caps at 8.
