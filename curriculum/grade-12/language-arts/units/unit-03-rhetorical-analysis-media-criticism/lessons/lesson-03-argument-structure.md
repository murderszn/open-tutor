# Lesson 03 — Argument Structure: Claims, Evidence, Warrants, Counterclaims

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can map an argument's claims,
evidence, warrants, and counterclaims, and judge whether its structure
makes its points clear, convincing, and engaging.
**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Readiness check (2 minutes):** "The library should stay open later
because people use it." What is missing from this argument? List two
gaps before reading on.

## 1. The four parts of an argument

- **Claim:** what the author wants you to accept or do.
- **Evidence:** the facts, examples, quotations, or data offered in
  support. Evidence must be checkable, or at least checkable in
  principle: a number with a source, a quotation you can find, an
  example you can inspect.
- **Warrant:** the unstated link between evidence and claim — the
  reason this evidence should count for this claim. Most weak
  arguments fail here, not in their facts: the facts are true, but
  they do not actually lead where the author wants them to go.
- **Counterclaim, concession, rebuttal:** the strongest opposing
  view, fairly stated; any point the author admits; and the author's
  answer. An argument that never names its opposition has not been
  tested, and a careful reader tests it herself.

A useful test for any paragraph: cover the claim and ask, "What would
I need to believe for this evidence to prove that?" The answer is the
warrant. If the warrant is shaky, the argument is shaky even if every
fact is true.

## 2. Structure is also an argument

The order in which parts appear changes how they land:

- **Problem → evidence → proposal** builds urgency, then earns a
  solution.
- **Concession first** ("The cost is real, and…") buys credibility
  before disagreement.
- **Strongest evidence last** ends on the most memorable point; but
  a reader who stops early never reaches it.
- **Refutation buried at the end** can look like an afterthought,
  even when it is strong.

When you evaluate structure (RI.11-12.5), ask three questions: Is it
clear — can a reader follow the chain without re-reading? Is it
convincing — does each link hold? Is it engaging — does the order
keep the reader's attention on the decision, not on the author's
cleverness?

## 3. Worked example 1 — mapping Speech B (fictional)

The following address is original, written for this unit. Speaker:
Sam Whitfield, fictional Riverside resident, speaking to the same
fictional town meeting, against extended hours as currently proposed.

> "I want the library to thrive, and I voted for its last renovation.
> But the proposal before us spends evening money before we have
> repaired the roof, and a library with a leaking roof will not thrive
> for long. Last winter, staff moved shelving twice to avoid drips —
> that is not a rumour, that is what the maintenance log recorded.
> If we phase the hours — two evenings a week this year, and revisit
> the question after the roof is fixed — we protect both the readers
> and the building. A phased plan is not a refusal. It is how a town
> keeps a promise instead of making one it cannot keep."

**Modelled mapping:**

| Part | Text | Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | Phase the hours: two evenings a week this year, revisit after the roof is fixed. | A compromise claim, not a flat refusal. That matters: it is harder to dismiss than "never." |
| Evidence | Staff moved shelving twice last winter to avoid drips; maintenance log recorded it. | Concrete and sourced (to a log the audience could in principle check). Note the limit: two moves show a leak exists, not its repair cost. |
| Warrant | A building must be kept sound before its hours are extended; otherwise the extension is wasted. | Reasonable, but unstated and debatable: a reader could accept the leak and still argue the two projects can run together. The argument depends on this link, so it should be defended, not assumed. |
| Counterclaim handled | "A phased plan is not a refusal" answers the charge that opponents simply do not care about readers. | Fair framing of the opposition's likely charge, answered rather than mocked. |
| Concession | "I want the library to thrive, and I voted for its last renovation." | Concedes shared values and past support, which buys a hearing for the disagreement. |

Structure judgment: clear (problem → evidence → compromise proposal),
convincing in part (the leak is evidenced; the "before" ordering is
asserted more than proved), engaging (the final sentence reframes a
delay as keeping a promise — a memorable close).

## 4. Worked example 2 — a structure that obscures

> "Everyone knows our town values reading. The library is the heart
> of Riverside. Last year thousands of people walked through its
> doors. Therefore we must extend evening hours immediately, before
> it is too late, because towns that do not invest in reading fall
> behind."

**Modelled reasoning:**

- *Claim:* extend evening hours immediately. *Evidence offered:*
  "thousands of people walked through its doors" last year.
- *Warrant gap:* total annual visits do not show evening demand, nor
  that immediate extension is the right response. The evidence could
  support "the library is popular" without supporting "extend hours
  now." The word "therefore" pretends the link is proved.
- *Vagueness as structure:* "everyone knows," "the heart of
  Riverside," "before it is too late," "fall behind" — each phrase
  feels weighty and checks nothing. A reader cannot test a claim that
  never says how much, by when, or compared with what.
- *Missing counterclaim:* no cost, no roof, no alternative is named.
  The argument has not been tested against its strongest objection,
  so a careful reader supplies that objection herself.
- *Revision direction (not a full rewrite):* replace the annual
  total with the evening sample count (242 visits in a sample week),
  name the cost question, and argue why evening demand, not total
  popularity, justifies evening hours. Same conclusion, stronger
  structure — which proves that structure, not sincerity, was the
  problem.

## 5. Guided practice

1. Map Speech B's final two sentences: which is claim, which is
   warrant, and what work does the final sentence do that the
   proposal sentence alone does not?
2. In Worked Example 2, replace "thousands of people" with a precise,
   checkable sentence using the unit's practice data. Does the
   replacement fix the warrant gap? Explain in two sentences.
3. Write a one-sentence counterclaim to Speech A (Lesson 02) that
   Speech A itself does not answer, and a one-sentence rebuttal
   Ortiz could give. Both must be fair — no caricature.

## 6. Independent practice

1. Map an argument of your own (four sentences, on any
   school-appropriate topic) into claim, evidence, warrant, and
   counterclaim, labelling each part.
2. Take the paragraph in Worked Example 2 and restructure it:
   concession first, evidence second, proposal last. Write the
   restructured paragraph (about 80 words) and one sentence judging
   whether your version is clearer, more convincing, or more
   engaging — and why.
3. Explain in three sentences why a true fact can still be weak
   evidence, using one example from this lesson.
4. Find the warrant in this sentence: "The town should fund the
   garden because 200 residents signed a petition." State the
   warrant, then state one reason a reader might reject it even
   while accepting that 200 people signed.

## 7. Applied task

Choose Speech A or Speech B. Write a 200-word structure evaluation:
is its structure clear, convincing, and engaging? Give one strength
and one weakness for each of the three tests, each with a quotation.
End with one concrete revision — a sentence to add, move, or cut —
and the reason it would improve the structure.

## 8. Exit check

In two sentences, define a warrant and explain why finding it is the
most useful step in testing an argument.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners confuse evidence with warrant ("the
number is the warrant"). Reteach with the cover-the-claim test in §1.
