# Lesson 06 — Evaluating a Speaker and Leading a Seminar

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can evaluate a speaker's
point of view, reasoning, evidence, and rhetoric, and present a
reasoned position in a learner-led seminar.
**Time:** about 50 minutes, plus the seminar session (Session 16).
**Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, an adult-selected speech or
presentation (live, recorded, or read aloud).
**Readiness check (2 minutes):** What is the difference between
"the speaker was convincing" and "the speaker was right"? Write one
sentence for each.

## 1. A speaker evaluation in five parts

When you evaluate a speaker (SL.11-12.3), judge five things
separately. A speaker can be strong in one and weak in another, and
the separation is the analysis:

1. **Stance:** what position does the speaker take, stated plainly?
2. **Premises:** what must be true for the argument to work? Are
   those premises defended, assumed, or smuggled in?
3. **Links among ideas:** does each point follow from the last, or
   does the speech jump, repeat, or change the subject when pressed?
4. **Evidence and rhetoric:** is the evidence checkable and
   relevant? Do word choice, emphasis, and tone serve the argument
   or substitute for it?
5. **Handling of opposition:** is the strongest counterclaim stated
   fairly and answered, dismissed, or ignored?

Score each part separately before giving an overall judgment. A
speech with vivid rhetoric and no checkable evidence is not "good
overall" — it is strong in rhetoric and weak in evidence, and the
evaluation should say exactly that.

## 2. Worked example 1 — evaluating a fictional speaker

**Speech (original, written for this unit; fictional speaker, a
Riverside resident addressing a town meeting):**

> "I am a neighbour, not an expert, and I will not pretend otherwise.
> Here is what I know: my daughter finishes her homework at this
> library three nights a week because our house is too crowded to
> study in. I do not know what the roof will cost. I do not know what
> two extra hours will cost. What I do know is that a decision made
> without asking the readers who use the room is a decision made in
> the dark. Ask them. Then decide."

**Modelled evaluation:**

- *Stance:* extend the decision-making, not necessarily the hours —
  the speaker's proposal is consultation first. Naming that
  precisely matters: crediting the speaker with "wants extended
  hours" would misstate the stance.
- *Premises:* (a) readers' experience is relevant evidence for this
  decision; (b) the council has not yet asked readers. Premise (a) is
  defensible; premise (b) is asserted, not evidenced — a fair
  evaluation flags it rather than assuming it.
- *Links:* the speech moves from personal experience to admitted
  ignorance to a procedural proposal. The links hold: admitting what
  is unknown makes the call to ask readers more, not less, credible.
- *Evidence and rhetoric:* the evidence is one family's experience —
  vivid, but a sample of one. The speaker does not pretend otherwise
  ("I am a neighbour, not an expert"), which is an ethos move: honesty
  about limits becomes credibility. Tone is calm and direct; emphasis
  falls on "ask them," a concrete next step rather than a slogan.
- *Handling of opposition:* the speaker does not address the roof or
  cost objections at all. That is a real gap — but it is a gap of
  scope (the speech argues about process), not a fallacy. A fair
  evaluation says so, instead of marking the speaker down for not
  answering a question the speech never took up.
- *Overall judgment:* strong on stance clarity, ethos, and tone;
  limited on evidence (one case) and scope (cost and roof unaddressed).
  The speech supports "consult readers before deciding" better than
  it supports any particular hours decision.

## 3. Worked example 2 — the same speaker, judged badly

> "The speaker was amazing and totally convincing. She clearly loves
> the library and everyone should listen to her."

**Modelled reasoning — why this evaluation fails:**

- It judges the speaker, not the speech: "amazing," "loves the
  library" are impressions, not evidence.
- It states no stance, no premise, no piece of evidence, and no
  handling of opposition. A reader of this evaluation learns nothing
  about what was argued.
- It confuses being moved with being convinced, the exact confusion
  the readiness check named. A good evaluation could still conclude
  the speech was persuasive — but it would have to show its work,
  part by part, as in §2.

## 4. Running a learner-led seminar

A seminar is not a debate to be won. It is a discussion the learner
leads, in which the group tests a question together. The leader's
jobs are:

1. **Open with a precise question,** not a slogan: "Should Riverside
   phase evening hours, extend them now, or keep them as they are —
   and what evidence would change your mind?"
2. **Ground every turn in evidence:** quote or cite before
   commenting. "Speech B says…" before "I think…".
3. **Invite the strongest objection:** "What is the best argument
   against my position, and how would I answer it fairly?"
4. **Synthesise:** after two opposing turns, restate what both sides
   agree on, what they still dispute, and what evidence would settle
   the dispute.
5. **Close with a judgment that names its limits:** what is
   supported, what is unverified, and what you still do not know.

Discussion norms (adult-enforced): address arguments, not people;
no interrupting a speaker's evidence turn; disagreement is graded on
evidence and fairness, never on which side is taken.

## 5. Guided practice

1. Using the five parts in §1, evaluate Speech A (Lesson 02) in five
   short paragraphs, one per part. Give Speech A one overall judgment
   in a final sentence.
2. Rewrite the bad evaluation in §3 as a good one (about 120 words),
   using at least two quotations from the speech in §2.
3. Write three seminar questions for the library question: one that
   opens discussion, one that invites an objection, and one that
   asks what evidence would settle a dispute.

## 6. Independent practice

1. Listen to (or read) an adult-selected speech or presentation.
   Evaluate it in the five parts, with at least one quotation per
   part, and an overall judgment that separates strengths from
   weaknesses.
2. Prepare a two-minute seminar opening on an approved question:
   state the question, your position, your two strongest pieces of
   evidence, and the strongest objection to your position, fairly
   stated.
3. Write a 100-word synthesis of a disagreement you have seen or
   taken part in (school-appropriate, no real names needed): what
   both sides agreed on, what they disputed, and what evidence would
   have settled it.
4. Explain in three sentences why a seminar leader who only calls on
   people who agree with her is failing at the leader's job, even if
   the discussion sounds lively.

## 7. Applied task — seminar rehearsal

With your adult (or a small group, if your adult arranges one),
rehearse your seminar opening from Independent Practice 2. After the
rehearsal, your adult (or a peer) raises the objection you predicted.
Answer it with evidence, concede any point that is genuinely strong,
and restate your position with the concession included. Then write
three sentences: what changed in your position after the objection,
and what did not — and why?

## 8. Exit check

In two sentences, explain why evaluating a speaker part by part is
more useful than giving one overall impression.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "fairness means agreeing with both sides."
Reteach: fairness is stating the opposition's best version accurately,
then answering it — agreement is not required. Answer-key reference:
`../answer-key.md`, Lesson 06.
