# Lesson 05 — Media Criticism: Many Formats, One Question

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can integrate and evaluate
multiple sources in different media and formats on one question, and
use lateral reading and the SIFT routine to judge credibility,
corroboration, and discrepancies.
**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Readiness check (2 minutes):** If a news report, an opinion column,
and a video all agree, does that settle a question? When might
agreement still mislead? Note one case before reading on.

## 1. Why one format is never enough

A question answered in only one format is a question answered from
only one angle. News reports select facts; opinion columns select
arguments; videos select images, music, and pace; charts select a
scale and a time window. None of these selections is automatically
dishonest — but each one hides as much as it shows. Media criticism
is the discipline of asking, for each format, what it can show, what
it cannot show, and what a second format would add.

Two distinctions carry the whole lesson:

- **Fact vs. framing:** two sources can report the same fact and
  frame it oppositely ("242 visits in a sample week" as "strong
  demand" or as "fewer than 50 a night"). Agreement on facts is not
  agreement on meaning.
- **Corroboration vs. repetition:** two sources that both copy one
  original report are not two independent confirmations. Ask whether
  each source did its own checking, or merely repeated the first.

## 2. Sourcing before believing

Before you weigh a source's claims, source it:

1. **Who made it, and when?** A named author with a checkable record
   is easier to evaluate than an anonymous page.
2. **For whom, and with what interest?** An outlet that sells
   advertising to a business it covers, or an author who benefits
   from one outcome, may still be accurate — but the interest must
   be named, not discovered later.
3. **What kind of text is it?** News, opinion, satire, advertising,
   and advocacy follow different rules. Judging an opinion column as
   if it were a news report (or vice versa) is a category error.
4. **What does it link to?** A claim that links to a primary document
   can be checked; a claim that links only to other posts cannot.

## 3. Lateral reading and SIFT

Professional fact-checkers, research summarised by the American
Library Association shows, do not stay on a suspicious page and study
it harder. They leave it — they read *laterally* — and check the
source elsewhere. Mike Caulfield's SIFT routine gives that habit four
steps:

- **S — Stop.** Notice your own reaction. If a post makes you angry,
  delighted, or certain within seconds, pause: strong feeling is
  often the delivery mechanism, not evidence.
- **I — Investigate the source.** Search the author or outlet
  elsewhere. What do other reputable sources say about it? Does it
  have an "About" page with real names and a corrections policy?
- **F — Find better coverage.** Look for the same claim from a
  source with a record of independent checking. If only one obscure
  outlet reports it, treat it as unverified.
- **T — Trace to the original context.** Follow a quotation, image,
  or statistic back to its origin. A real photograph from another
  year, a quotation cut mid-sentence, or a statistic from a
  different question are all common ways a true fragment misleads.

SIFT is a routine, not a verdict machine. It tells you how much
checking a claim still needs; it does not, by itself, prove a claim
true or false.

## 4. Worked example 1 — the media set on one question

All four items below are original, fictional, written for this unit,
on the question: *Should Riverside extend library evening hours?*

**A. News report (*Riverside Gazette*, fictional).** "Council heard
testimony Tuesday on a proposal to extend library evening hours by
two hours, four evenings a week. Staff reported 242 evening visits
in a sample week in February. A maintenance report, also presented,
noted roof leaks that required shelving to be moved twice last
winter. No vote was taken."

**B. Opinion column (fictional).** "Riverside has a choice between a
library that welcomes people and a library that watches the clock.
Two hundred and forty-two visits in a single week is not a rounding
error. It is a line of neighbours at the door, and a town that turns
them away has chosen its priorities."

**C. Short-video transcript (fictional).** "[Upbeat music. Quick cuts
of a bright reading room.] Narrator: 'Riverside reads! Hundreds of
readers every single week! Tell council: keep the lights on!' [Text
on screen: 'Hundreds every week.']"

**D. Practice chart (fictional).** Evening visits, one sample week:
Monday 38, Tuesday 51, Wednesday 44, Thursday 62, Friday 47. Total
242; mean 48.4 visits per evening.

**Modelled integration:**

- *What agrees:* A, B, and D agree on the count 242 — but B repeats
  A's figure, so B is not independent corroboration of it. D is the
  underlying count itself (in this fictional corpus, the source of
  the figure), so the agreement is really one count, reported three
  ways.
- *What differs:* A alone mentions the roof leaks; B omits them.
  C's "hundreds every single week" stretches a one-week sample into
  a standing claim about every week — the hasty generalisation from
  Lesson 04, now in video form. C's music and quick cuts add energy
  but no evidence.
- *Discrepancy to note:* D's highest night is Thursday (62), not
  Tuesday (51, the night Speech A described). Speech A's Tuesday
  story is consistent with D, but a reader who assumed Tuesday was
  the busiest night would be wrong — a small reminder to check a
  vivid example against the full set.
- *Judgment:* on this set alone, evening demand in the sample week
  is supported, the roof problem is supported by A, and the
  cost question is answered by none of the four. A responsible
  conclusion says so, and names the missing evidence, instead of
  picking the format that agrees with a prior opinion.

## 5. Worked example 2 — SIFT on an unfamiliar post (modelled)

**Post (fictional practice post):** "BREAKING: Riverside library
turns away 500 readers a night!! Share before council deletes this!"

**Modelled SIFT:**

- *Stop:* the capital letters, double exclamation, and "share before
  … deletes" are urgency signals. Pause before sharing.
- *Investigate:* the account is unnamed, has no "About" page in the
  practice set, and no other outlet is named. That does not prove
  the claim false, but it lowers its standing until something else
  checks it.
- *Find better coverage:* the fictional *Gazette* report (A) gives
  242 visits in a *week*, not 500 a night. No other item in the set
  supports 500 a night.
- *Trace:* the post links to nothing checkable. There is no original
  count, photograph, or document to trace to.
- *Verdict language:* "Unverified, and inconsistent with the only
  counted figure in the set." Note the discipline: the verdict is
  about the *claim's support*, not about the poster's motives, which
  cannot be known from the post alone.

## 6. Guided practice

1. In the media set, which item is opinion and which is news? Quote
   one sentence from each that proves the classification.
2. Item C says "hundreds every single week." Using item D, explain
   in two sentences why that wording overstates what D shows.
3. Run SIFT, in note form, on a post your adult selects (or on the
   fictional post in §5, if no live post is used). Record one line
   per letter, and end with a verdict sentence in the form "Supported
   / Unverified / Contradicted, because …".

## 7. Independent practice

1. Build a source table for the library question with one row per
   item A–D: format, claim, evidence offered, what it omits, and
   credibility judgment with a reason.
2. Write a 150-word integrated answer to the library question that
   uses at least two formats, names one discrepancy, and names one
   piece of missing evidence. Do not claim more than the set supports.
3. Explain in three sentences the difference between corroboration
   and repetition, using items A and B as your example.
4. A friend says, "I saw it in a video, so it must be true — videos
   show real things." Answer in three sentences, using one point
   about editing or selection from this lesson.

## 8. Applied task

Take one question approved by your adult (it may be the library
question or a new school-appropriate civic question). Find — with
adult supervision — three sources in three different formats that
address it, or use three adult-supplied sources. For each, record
sourcing notes, one SIFT move, and one discrepancy or agreement with
the other sources. Finish with a 100-word judgment that states what
is supported, what is unverified, and what evidence is still missing.

## 9. Exit check

In two sentences, explain lateral reading and why it is more useful
than studying a single suspicious page for longer.
