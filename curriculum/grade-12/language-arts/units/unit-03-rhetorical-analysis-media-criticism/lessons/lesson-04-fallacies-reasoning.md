# Lesson 04 — Fallacies and Evaluating Reasoning in Public Advocacy

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can name common fallacies,
explain where reasoning breaks down, and repair a fallacious argument
without changing its conclusion.
**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Readiness check (2 minutes):** "If we don't extend the hours, the
library will die." What is wrong with this sentence even if you
support extended hours? Answer before reading on.

## 1. What a fallacy is — and is not

A **fallacy** is a pattern of reasoning that does not support its
conclusion, however forcefully it is said. Three cautions keep the
label honest:

1. Naming a fallacy does not prove the conclusion false. "Your
   argument commits a fallacy" means "this reasoning does not prove
   your point," not "your point is wrong." The point might be true
   for other reasons.
2. A fallacy is a pattern, not a tone. An angry argument can be
   valid; a calm argument can be fallacious. Judge the reasoning,
   not the volume.
3. Context matters. A joke, a slogan, and a policy proposal are not
   held to the same standard — but a slogan offered *as* the reason
   for a policy is.

When you evaluate reasoning in public advocacy (RI.11-12.8), you
delineate first — claim, premises, evidence — and only then judge.
A fallacy label without that delineation is just a fancy way of
saying "I disagree."

## 2. Six fallacies, with the break in each

- **Straw man:** attacking a weakened version of the opponent's
  view instead of the view itself. *Break:* the refutation, even if
  successful, refutes something nobody claimed.
- **Ad hominem:** attacking the person instead of the argument.
  *Break:* a person's character does not settle whether their
  evidence is correct. (Note: questioning a source's *relevant*
  expertise or interest is sourcing, not ad hominem — see §3.)
- **False dilemma:** presenting two options as the only options.
  *Break:* a third option may exist and may be better than both.
- **Hasty generalisation:** drawing a broad conclusion from too
  small or unrepresentative a sample. *Break:* the sample does not
  support the scope of the claim.
- **Slippery slope:** claiming one step must lead, without evidence
  for each step, to an extreme outcome. *Break:* each link in the
  chain needs its own support; possibility is not inevitability.
- **Faulty appeal to authority:** citing someone whose expertise
  does not cover the question, or quoting them out of context.
  *Break:* authority in one field does not transfer to another, and
  a quotation without context may reverse its meaning.

## 3. Worked example 1 — six sentences, six diagnoses

Each sentence below is original, written for this unit, about the
fictional Riverside library question. For each, the modelled
reasoning names the fallacy, quotes the breaking point, and states
what would repair it.

1. "Opponents of extended hours just want the library to fail."
   *Straw man.* No opponent in this unit's corpus says that; Speech
   B argues for phasing, not failure. Repair: state the phased
   proposal fairly, then argue against phasing.
2. "You can't trust Whitfield's roof argument — he once ran late
   to a meeting." *Ad hominem.* Lateness has no bearing on whether
   the roof leaks. Repair: check the maintenance log instead.
3. "Either we extend the hours or we abandon evening readers
   entirely." *False dilemma.* Phasing (two evenings a week) is a
   third option, named in Speech B itself.
4. "One Tuesday was busy, so every evening all year will be busy."
   *Hasty generalisation.* One night — even a counted one, 51 visits
   — cannot carry a claim about every evening all year. Repair:
   narrow the claim to that Tuesday, or gather more nights.
5. "If we extend hours by two, next it will be all night, and then
   the library will never close and staff will burn out completely."
   *Slippery slope.* No evidence links a two-hour extension to
   all-night opening. Repair: argue about the two hours actually
   proposed, and about staffing, on their own evidence.
6. "A famous novelist said libraries matter, so extended hours must
   be the right policy." *Faulty appeal to authority.* A novelist's
   love of libraries is not evidence about Riverside's staffing
   costs or evening demand. Repair: cite the evening counts and the
   staffing figures instead.

## 4. Worked example 2 — a longer passage, delineated first

> "The council should reject the phased plan. People who support
> phasing are the same people who opposed the renovation years ago,
> and they have always stood in the way of progress. Besides, if we
> delay now, we will delay forever, and the library's evening readers
> will simply give up and never return."

**Modelled reasoning:**

- *Delineation:* Claim — reject the phased plan. Premises offered —
  (a) phasing supporters opposed a past renovation; (b) delay now
  means delay forever; (c) delayed readers will never return.
- *Diagnosis:* (a) is ad hominem by association: past opposition to
  a different proposal does not show the phased plan is wrong, and
  "stood in the way of progress" is a label, not evidence. (b) and
  (c) are a slippery slope: "delay forever" and "never return" are
  absolute predictions offered without any step-by-step support.
- *What survives:* nothing in the passage actually engages Speech B's
  reason for phasing — the roof. A repaired version would have to
  argue either that the roof can wait, that both projects can run
  together, or that the cost of delay to readers outweighs the roof
  risk — with evidence for whichever claim it makes.
- *Judgment:* the passage is forceful and, as reasoning, empty. That
  combination is exactly what this lesson trains you to notice:
  force is not a reason.

## 5. Guided practice

1. For each sentence in §3, state in your own words what evidence
   would be needed to make the same conclusion without the fallacy.
2. "The librarian says evening demand is high, so we should extend
   the hours." Is this a faulty appeal to authority? Explain why or
   why not, using the distinction in §1 between expertise and
   interest.
3. Rewrite sentence 3 of §3 ("Either we extend…") so it is no longer
   a false dilemma, keeping the speaker's support for extended hours.

## 6. Independent practice

1. Label each fallacy (one per sentence) and quote the breaking
   point: (a) "Three students I asked love late hours, so all
   students do." (b) "If we fund the garden, soon we'll fund every
   hobby in town." (c) "Her argument fails because she failed a
   test once." (d) "We must choose: books or computers — not both."
2. Write two original fallacious sentences of your own (school
   topics, no real people), label each, and repair each without
   changing its conclusion.
3. Explain in three sentences why "that argument is fallacious, so
   its conclusion is false" is itself a reasoning error.
4. Take Worked Example 2 and write a repaired 80-word version that
   argues the same conclusion (reject phasing) with no fallacy.
   State which premise you replaced and with what evidence.

## 7. Applied task

Find (or, if your adult prefers, invent and label as practice) one
short persuasive paragraph — an advertisement, a post, or a speech
excerpt selected by your adult. Delineate its claim and premises,
name any fallacy you find (or state, with reasons, that you find
none), and repair one weak link. If you find no fallacy, explain what
makes the reasoning hold: which premise is best supported, and which
is most open to challenge?

## 8. Exit check

In two sentences, explain the difference between "this argument is
fallacious" and "this conclusion is false."

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners label any insult or exaggeration a
fallacy. Reteach: a fallacy is a broken *link* between premise and
conclusion, not a rude tone. Answer-key reference:
