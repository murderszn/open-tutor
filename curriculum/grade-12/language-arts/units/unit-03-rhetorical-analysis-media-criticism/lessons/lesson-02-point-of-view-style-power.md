# Lesson 02 — Point of View, Style, and Power

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can determine an author's
point of view or purpose in a highly rhetorical text and analyse how
word choice, syntax, and framing contribute to its power.
**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.
**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Rewrite "The library closed at six"
in two ways: one that makes the closing sound like a loss, one that
makes it sound like a sensible decision. Keep both versions.

## 1. Point of view is a position, not just a person

A text's **point of view** is the stance its author takes toward the
question, plus the interests and values behind that stance. Purpose
follows from stance: the same facts can serve to persuade, to warn,
to reassure, or to recruit — and the author's word choices will
quietly serve that purpose even before any argument is stated.

Three questions unlock most rhetorical texts:

1. What does the author want the audience to do or believe?
2. What does the author treat as already agreed, so it never needs
   defending?
3. Whose voice is missing — who is affected but never quoted?

## 2. Style does argumentative work

Style is not decoration on top of an argument; it *is* part of the
argument. Watch for:

- **Word choice (diction):** "investment" vs. "spending," "readers"
  vs. "crowd," "protect" vs. "restrict." Each pair points the same
  fact in a different direction.
- **Syntax:** short sentences punch ("The doors close. The readers
  leave."); long, balanced sentences sound considered and authoritative.
  A list of three builds momentum; a question invites the audience to
  answer it themselves.
- **Framing:** what leads, what is repeated, and what is omitted.
  Framing a decision as "library hours vs. roof repairs" is already
  an argument: it presents two goods as rivals and hides a third
  option (both, phased, or funded differently) unless the reader
  notices the frame.
- **Tone:** the attitude carried by the language — urgent, calm,
  indignant, reasonable. Tone tells the audience how to feel about
  the facts before the facts are weighed.

## 3. Worked example 1 — Speech A (fictional, in full)

The following address is original, written for this unit. Speaker:
Dana Ortiz, fictional Riverside council member, speaking to a
fictional town meeting in favour of extended library evening hours.

> "Good evening. I am not here to sell you a building. I am here to
> ask you to notice who is already using it. On a cold Tuesday in
> February, I counted with the evening staff — not guessed,
> counted — fifty-one readers after six o'clock: students finishing
> homework, a nurse studying between shifts, a grandfather reading
> to two grandchildren who had nowhere warmer to wait. These are
> not strangers. They are our neighbours, and the door we close at
> six closes on them.
>
> Some will say the cost is the question. The cost is part of the
> question. But a town that can find money for a festival stage can
> find two more hours for the one room in this town where nobody has
> to buy anything to belong. Extend the hours. Let the readers stay."

**Modelled reasoning:**

- *Point of view and purpose:* Ortiz speaks as an insider-witness
  ("I counted with the evening staff") to a town-meeting audience,
  with the purpose of turning an abstract budget line into a decision
  about named neighbours. The stance is: extended hours are a
  question of belonging, not only of cost.
- *Style as power:* the opening denial — "I am not here to sell you
  a building" — reframes the speaker as an observer, not a
  salesperson, which buys credibility for everything after it.
  Concrete nouns ("a nurse studying between shifts," "a grandfather
  reading") do the emotional work that an adjective like "important"
  never could: the audience pictures people, not a policy.
- *Syntax:* short sentences ("Extend the hours. Let the readers
  stay.") land as commands after longer, detailed sentences. The
  contrast is the persuasion: detail first, decision second.
- *Framing to notice:* "the one room in this town where nobody has
  to buy anything to belong" frames the library as unique and
  irreplaceable, which makes the cost argument sound small by
  comparison. A fair reader notes the frame and asks whether the
  claim of uniqueness is true — other free public rooms may exist —
  rather than simply feeling its force.
- *What is left uncertain:* the speech gives one Tuesday's count
  (51, matching the fictional sample week's Tuesday figure) but no
  cost figure and no answer to the roof-repair rival claim from
  Lesson 01. Power is not proof: the speech is persuasive and still
  incomplete, and both judgments belong in an analysis.

## 4. Worked example 2 — the same facts, a colder frame

> "Evening use at the library averaged about forty-eight visits per
> sample night last week. Staffing two additional hours, four
> evenings a week, would require additional wages, lighting, and
> heating, and would reduce the maintenance budget available this
> year. Council will weigh these figures against competing needs."

**Modelled reasoning:**

- *Point of view:* an administrative, neutral-sounding stance. The
  speaker is not named, no person appears, and no decision is urged
  — the purpose is to present the decision as a balancing of figures.
- *Style as power:* the average ("about forty-eight") is arithmetically
  fair — 242 ÷ 5 = 48.4 — but the rounding and the passive
  constructions ("would require," "will weigh") remove every human
  actor. Readers who met the nurse and the grandfather in Speech A
  now meet "visits" and "figures." Nothing stated is false, yet the
  text feels entirely different, because style has removed the people
  from the decision.
- *Framing to notice:* "reduce the maintenance budget" presents
  extended hours as taking money from maintenance, just as Speech A
  presented maintenance as taking room from readers. Both frames are
  choices. A strong analysis names the frame in each text and then
  asks which facts each frame hides: Speech A hides costs; this
  version hides readers.
- The lesson of the pair: to analyse rhetoric, compare what each
  text makes vivid and what each text makes invisible. That
  comparison is more useful than deciding which text you "liked."

## 5. Guided practice

1. In Speech A, find one word-choice decision and one syntax decision.
   For each, quote the words, name the effect, and state whose
   interests the effect serves.
2. Rewrite the sentence "These are not strangers. They are our
   neighbours" in a flat, administrative tone. What is lost, and what
   (if anything) is gained?
3. Speech A says "a town that can find money for a festival stage."
   What does this sentence assume the audience already believes?
   Is that assumption defended anywhere in the speech? Explain.

## 6. Independent practice

1. Write a 150-word style analysis of Speech A: one paragraph on
   word choice, one on syntax, one on framing. Quote at least twice.
2. Summarise Speech A objectively in three sentences — no praise, no
   blame, no borrowed vivid language. Then, in one sentence, state
   what your summary lost.
3. Take Worked Example 2 and add one human detail (invented only as
   clearly labelled practice detail, or drawn from Speech A with
   attribution). Explain in two sentences how the addition changes
   the text's power without changing its facts.
4. Explain in three sentences the difference between "the author is
   biased" and "the author has a point of view," using one example
   from this lesson.

## 7. Applied task

Choose one paragraph from Speech A or Worked Example 2. Annotate it
line by line: for each sentence, write (a) what it literally says,
(b) what it invites the audience to feel or assume, and (c) one
question a sceptical reader should ask it. Finish with a one-sentence
judgment: what is this paragraph's purpose, and how does its style
serve that purpose?

## 8. Exit check

In two sentences, explain how style can make a text persuasive even
when it adds no new fact.
