# Lesson 01 — The Rhetorical Situation and the Three Appeals

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can name a text's rhetorical
situation and identify one ethos, one pathos, and one logos appeal,
explaining how each works on its intended audience.
**Time:** about 50 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook,
the unit image in `../assets/rhetoric-media-criticism-scene.png`.
**Readiness check (2 minutes):** In one sentence, why might the same
argument convince one audience and bore or anger another? Keep your
answer; you will test it against the examples below.

## 1. What a rhetorical situation is

Every persuasive text is written by someone, for someone, for a
reason, about something, at a particular moment. Together those five
parts are the **rhetorical situation**:

- **Speaker (or writer):** who is speaking, and what standing do they
  have with this audience?
- **Audience:** who is meant to hear this, and what do they already
  believe, need, or fear?
- **Purpose:** what should the audience think, feel, or do afterwards?
- **Topic:** what is actually being decided or discussed?
- **Context (including exigence):** what problem or occasion prompted
  this text, right now?

Change any one part and the same words can land differently. A plea
for library funding sounds different coming from a librarian to
parents at a school meeting than from a stranger to commuters at a bus
stop — not because the words changed, but because the situation did.

**Vocabulary in context:** *exigence* is the itch the text scratches:
the problem that makes someone speak now rather than later. If you
cannot name the exigence, you have probably not yet understood the
text's purpose.

## 2. The three appeals

Aristotle's three appeals are still the fastest way to sort *how* a
text tries to move its audience. A strong text usually uses all three;
a weak one leans on one and hopes you will not notice the others are
missing.

- **Ethos** — an appeal to credibility. "Trust me, because I know
  this, I have done this, or I share your values." Signals: relevant
  experience, honest handling of counter-evidence, careful language.
- **Pathos** — an appeal to emotion. "Feel this, because the stakes
  are human." Signals: vivid concrete detail, a named person affected,
  language that quickens or softens feeling.
- **Logos** — an appeal to reason and evidence. "Follow this, because
  the reasons connect." Signals: checkable facts, numbers with a
  source, comparisons, and reasoning you can trace step by step.

A label is not an analysis. "This is pathos" earns nothing by itself.
Analysis says *which* words, *what* feeling or reason they aim at,
and *why* that move should work on *this* audience.

## 3. Worked example 1 — sorting appeals in a paragraph

Read this original practice paragraph, written for this unit. The
speaker is fictional; the library is fictional.

> "I have worked the library's evening desk for eleven years, and I
> have watched the same pattern every winter: by six o'clock the
> study tables are full, students stand in the aisles with their
> coats still on, and the last bus leaves before they finish. If we
> keep the doors open two hours longer, four evenings a week, the
> building can hold the readers it already has — 242 evening visits
> in one sample week, counted at the door — instead of turning them
> away into the cold."

**Modelled reasoning:**

- *Ethos:* "I have worked the library's evening desk for eleven
  years" — the speaker claims first-hand, long experience of exactly
  the thing being decided. That should matter to an audience that
  values people who have seen the problem nightly, not just read
  about it.
- *Pathos:* "students stand in the aisles with their coats still
  on" and "turning them away into the cold" — concrete, bodily detail
  that asks the audience to picture discomfort and to feel that a
  small change would relieve it.
- *Logos:* "242 evening visits in one sample week, counted at the
  door" — a checkable count (and, in the unit corpus, a fictional
  practice total: 38 + 51 + 44 + 62 + 47 = 242) offered as evidence
  that demand already exists. Note the limit, which a careful reader
  should keep: one sample week is not a whole winter, so the number
  supports "there is evening demand" better than it supports any
  claim about every week of the year.

Notice the order of the analysis: quotation first, appeal second,
audience effect third, limit fourth. Copy that order in your own work.

## 4. Worked example 2 — the same topic, a different situation

> "Riverside, we have a choice, and it is not complicated. Every
> dollar we spend lighting an empty room at nine at night is a
> dollar we cannot spend fixing the roof that leaks onto the
> children's shelves. I love this library. That is why I will not
> watch us spend its future on a gesture."

**Modelled reasoning:**

- The rhetorical situation here is different: the speaker presents
  as a fellow lover of the library, not an opponent of it, speaking
  to an audience worried about money and maintenance. The purpose is
  not to close the library but to redirect the same money.
- *Ethos:* "I love this library" claims shared values with the
  audience before disagreeing with them — a credibility move that
  makes the disagreement easier to hear.
- *Pathos:* "a roof that leaks onto the children's shelves" — a
  vivid image of harm to something the audience protects.
- *Logos — and its gap:* the argument assumes the evening room is
  "empty," but offers no count. A careful reader asks: empty
  compared with what, and counted when? The unit's practice count
  (242 visits in a sample week) would, if it applied here, push
  against that assumption — which is why checking an assumption
  against evidence is part of analysis, not an optional extra.
- The two examples together show the unit's central habit: never
  analyse a paragraph without naming who is speaking, to whom, and
  about what decision.

## 5. Guided practice

Work with a partner or aloud with your adult. Use the
quotation → appeal → audience effect → limit order.

1. In Worked Example 1, which appeal does the sentence about the
   last bus mainly serve, and what does it add that the visit count
   alone does not?
2. A speaker says, "As a parent of three children who use this
   library every week, I am asking you to keep it open later." Name
   the appeal, quote the words that carry it, and name one audience
   for whom it would work well and one for whom it would work less
   well.
3. Rewrite one sentence of Worked Example 2 so its logos appeal is
   stronger, without changing the speaker's conclusion. State what
   evidence you added and why it helps.

## 6. Independent practice

1. Write a four-sentence paragraph arguing that a school should (or
   should not) start classes later. Include one clear ethos appeal,
   one pathos appeal, and one logos appeal, and label each in the
   margin.
2. Read your paragraph back and underline the one appeal you think
   is weakest. In two sentences, explain what would strengthen it.
3. Name a text you have seen this week (an advertisement, a post, a
   school announcement — no link or quotation needed) and state its
   rhetorical situation in five parts: speaker, audience, purpose,
   topic, context.
4. Explain in three sentences why "this text uses pathos" is not yet
   an analysis, using one example from this lesson to show what is
   missing.

## 8. Exit check

In two sentences, define the rhetorical situation and explain why
naming the audience matters before judging whether an appeal works.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners label every emotional sentence
"pathos" and stop. The teacher guide gives a reteaching move (ask
"pathos *for whom*, and to what action?"). Answer-key reference:
