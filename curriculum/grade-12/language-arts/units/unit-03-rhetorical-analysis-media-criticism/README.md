# Unit 03 — Rhetorical Analysis: Argument and Media Criticism

**Grade 12 · Language Arts · Advanced literature, writing, and communication pathway · Weeks 10–13 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-28 for issue #56; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

A speech can be beautifully said and badly reasoned. A news report, an
opinion column, and a short video can all address the same question and
still leave a reader with three different pictures of it. This unit
teaches the move that separates being moved by a text from being
convinced by it: name the rhetorical situation, trace the argument's
structure, test its evidence and reasoning, and then check the same
question across formats before deciding what you believe.

The unit runs in four moves, following the track scope-and-sequence
(weeks 10–13):

1. **The rhetorical situation** — speaker, audience, purpose, context,
   and the appeals of ethos, pathos, and logos (Lesson 01).
2. **Point of view, style, and power** — how word choice, syntax, and
   framing make a text persuasive, and where persuasion becomes
   manipulation (Lesson 02).
3. **Argument structure and reasoning** — claims, evidence, warrants,
   and counterclaims; structure that clarifies or obscures; fallacies
   that break reasoning (Lessons 03–04).
4. **Media criticism** — integrating news, opinion, visual, and
   digital sources on one question; lateral reading and the SIFT
   routine; evaluating a live speaker and leading a seminar debate
   (Lessons 05–06).

## The practice corpus — read this first

Every worked example, practice item, quiz question, and assessment task
in this unit uses **original, fictional practice texts written for this
unit**. No real speech, article, post, or dataset is reproduced, and no
real person is quoted.

- **The question:** Should the fictional town of Riverside extend its
  public library's evening hours?
- **Speech A (Lesson 02):** a fictional address by Riverside council
  member Dana Ortiz in favour of extended hours.
- **Speech B (Lesson 03):** a fictional address by Riverside resident
  Sam Whitfield against extended hours, arguing for a different use of
  the same money.
- **The media set (Lesson 05 and the project):** a fictional news
  report from the *Riverside Gazette*, a fictional opinion column, a
  fictional short-video transcript, and a fictional practice chart of
  evening visits.
- **Practice data (fictional):** evening visits recorded in one sample
  week at the fictional Riverside library — Monday 38, Tuesday 51,
  Wednesday 44, Thursday 62, Friday 47. These numbers are invented for
  practice; they are not observations from any real library, and no
  conclusion in this unit depends on treating them as real.

Contemporary speeches and media beyond this corpus are **adult-selected,
previewed, and supervised** only. The guiding adult previews every
external link for advertising, comments, and suitability before the
learner opens it, and contemporary media is never assigned unsupervised.
The learner's own seminar topic (Lesson 06 and the project) must be a
school-appropriate civic question approved by the adult in advance.

## Objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Analyse a text's rhetorical situation (speaker, audience, purpose,
   context) and identify ethos, pathos, and logos appeals, explaining
   how each appeal works on its intended audience.
2. Determine an author's point of view or purpose in a highly
   rhetorical text, analysing how word choice, syntax, and framing
   contribute to its power and persuasiveness.
3. Delineate an argument's claims, evidence, warrants, and
   counterclaims; evaluate whether its structure makes its points
   clear, convincing, and engaging; and revise a weak structure.
4. Identify common fallacies (straw man, ad hominem, false dilemma,
   hasty generalisation, slippery slope, faulty appeal to authority)
   and explain, with evidence, where reasoning breaks down.
5. Integrate and evaluate multiple sources in different media and
   formats on one question, using lateral reading and the SIFT routine
   to judge credibility, corroboration, and discrepancies.
6. Evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, evidence, and use of
   rhetoric in a live or recorded presentation, and present a
   reasoned, evidence-based position in a learner-led seminar.

Objectives are adult-assessed against the rubric and assessments in
this unit. "Evaluate" here means a supported judgment with textual
evidence, not a grade for agreeing with any position in the corpus.

## Prerequisites

- U01 close-reading routines: quoting accurately, distinguishing what
  a text says from what it implies, and supporting a claim with
  evidence.
- U02 comparative method: comparing how two texts treat one theme,
  and naming where interpretations differ.
- Sentence-level reading: recognising a claim, a reason, and an
  example, and paraphrasing without copying wording.
- No prior knowledge of Riverside, libraries, or media studies is
  assumed; every fact needed is supplied in the corpus.

## Vocabulary

- **Rhetorical situation:** speaker, audience, purpose, topic, and
  context together — the circumstances that make a text persuasive
  (or not) for a particular audience.
- **Exigence:** the problem or occasion that prompts a text.
- **Ethos / pathos / logos:** appeals to credibility, to emotion,
  and to reason and evidence.
- **Claim / evidence / warrant:** what is asserted; the support
  offered; the unstated link that connects support to claim.
- **Counterclaim / concession / rebuttal:** an opposing view fairly
  stated; admitting a point; answering it.
- **Framing:** choosing which facts, words, and images lead, and
  which are left out, so the same facts point in different directions.
- **Fallacy:** a pattern of reasoning that looks persuasive but does
  not support its conclusion.
- **Sourcing:** asking who made a text, when, for whom, and with what
  interest, before trusting it.
- **Lateral reading:** leaving a page to check its source elsewhere,
  rather than judging it only by how it looks.
- **SIFT:** Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace
  to the original context.
- **Corroboration:** independent sources agreeing on the same checkable
  fact.
- **Stance / premises / emphasis / tone:** a speaker's position; the
  assumptions an argument depends on; what is stressed; the attitude
  the language carries.

## Standards and source notes

Standards codes and descriptions are paraphrases of the Common Core
State Standards for English Language Arts, grades 11–12 band, verified
against the official standards pages at
<https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/> on 2026-10-28
(Reading: Informational Text and Speaking & Listening pages). This is
a reference alignment for planning; it is not a claim of state
adoption, accreditation, or standards certification, and no state or
district requirements were specified.

- **RI.11-12.1** — cite strong, thorough textual evidence for explicit
  and inferential claims, including where a text leaves matters
  uncertain. Used in every lesson's evidence routines.
- **RI.11-12.2** — determine two or more central ideas and analyse
  their development and interaction; provide an objective summary.
  Used in Lessons 02–03 summaries.
- **RI.11-12.5** — analyse and evaluate the effectiveness of the
  structure an author uses in exposition or argument, including
  whether it makes points clear, convincing, and engaging. Lesson 03.
- **RI.11-12.6** — determine an author's point of view or purpose in
  a text in which the rhetoric is particularly effective, analysing
  how style and content contribute to its power, persuasiveness, or
  beauty. Lesson 02.
- **RI.11-12.7** — integrate and evaluate multiple sources in
  different media or formats, in words and otherwise, to address a
  question or solve a problem. Lesson 05 and the project.
- **RI.11-12.8** — delineate and evaluate reasoning in works of
  public advocacy, including premises, purposes, and arguments.
  Lesson 04.
- **SL.11-12.2** — integrate multiple sources in diverse formats and
  media to make informed decisions, evaluating credibility and
  accuracy and noting discrepancies. Lesson 05.
- **SL.11-12.3** — evaluate a speaker's point of view, reasoning, and
  use of evidence and rhetoric, assessing stance, premises, links
  among ideas, word choice, points of emphasis, and tone. Lesson 06.
- **SL.11-12.1.d** — respond thoughtfully to diverse perspectives,
  synthesise comments and evidence on all sides, and resolve
  contradictions where possible. Lesson 06 seminar.
- **W.11-12.1** — write arguments on substantive topics with valid
  reasoning and relevant, sufficient evidence, precise claims
  distinguished from counterclaims, and a conclusion that follows
  from the argument. Project essay and unit assessment.
- **W.11-12.9** — draw evidence from literary and informational texts
  to support analysis and reflection. Project and assessment.

Media-literacy method notes: lateral reading and the SIFT routine are
summarised from the American Library Association's "Keeping Up With …
Misinformation and News Literacy" overview (checked 2026-10-28), which
describes professional fact-checkers reading laterally and Caulfield's
SIFT moves (Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace
to the original context). The rhetorical-situation framework follows
the Purdue Online Writing Lab's rhetorical-situation pages (checked
2026-10-28). Full links and check notes are in `resource-pack.md`.

## Pacing — 20 sessions, about 50 minutes each

| Session | Work | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lesson 01 | Learner names the rhetorical situation and one appeal in a new paragraph, with a quotation. |
| 2 | Lesson 01 workshop + image task | Learner completes the image analysis and revises one appeal label with evidence. |
| 3 | Lesson 02 | Learner traces point of view and one style choice to an effect on the audience. |
| 4 | Lesson 02 workshop | Learner writes a 150-word style analysis with two quotations. |
| 5 | Reading + Practice Set A | Learner scores at least 4 of 6 on Set A (adult checks with the key). |
| 6 | Lesson 03 | Learner maps claim, evidence, warrant, and counterclaim in Speech B. |
| 7 | Lesson 03 workshop | Learner restructures one weak paragraph and explains the improvement. |
| 8 | Lesson 04 | Learner names and explains three fallacies in new sentences. |
| 9 | Lesson 04 workshop | Learner repairs two fallacious arguments without changing their conclusions. |
| 10 | Practice Set B + project launch | Learner chooses an approved seminar/project question and lists three source formats to seek. |
| 11 | Lesson 05 | Learner completes a three-format source table on the library question. |
| 12 | Lesson 05 workshop: SIFT lab | Learner runs SIFT on one unfamiliar source and records each move. |
| 13 | Lesson 06 | Learner evaluates a speaker using stance, premises, evidence, and tone. |
| 14 | Seminar preparation | Learner prepares an opening position, two quotations, and one anticipated objection. |
| 15 | Project drafting | Learner drafts the project essay's claim, counterclaim, and evidence map. |
| 16 | Learner-led seminar debate | Learner presents, responds to an objection with evidence, and synthesises one opposing point fairly. |
| 17 | Project workshop + adult conference | Learner revises one paragraph for warrant and one for counterclaim handling. |
| 18 | Week-04 quiz + review | Learner completes the quiz; adult reviews missed items the same day. |
| 19 | Unit assessment | Learner completes the assessment independently in the session. |
| 20 | Feedback, revision, portfolio assembly | Learner revises one assessment response and files the project with its rubric. |

Reading, practice, project, and review sessions are named above; the
six lessons do not by themselves fill the unit. Sessions 5, 10, 12,
14–15, and 17 are practice, lab, preparation, drafting, and conference
sessions, not repeated lesson labels.

## Supports, extensions, and adult guidance

- **Reading access:** all corpus texts are short and chunked; the
  adult may read a speech aloud while the learner follows, or pair
  reading with a learner-written summary after each paragraph.
- **Language access:** vocabulary is defined in context in each
  lesson; the learner may answer exit checks orally with the adult
  scribing, and may annotate with marginal notes, voice memos, or a
  two-column quotation/claim notebook.
- **Alternate response modes:** seminar contributions may be spoken,
  signed, or submitted as a written dialogue; the project essay may be
  dictated and then revised by the learner in print.
- **Extensions:** analyse an adult-selected real speech or editorial
  with the same routines; compare two real news outlets' coverage of
  one event (adult-selected, previewed); write a counter-speech to
  Speech A or B that concedes its strongest point and still disagrees.
- **Sensitive content:** the corpus avoids real political figures,
  parties, elections, and contested real-world policies. If the adult
  selects a real contemporary text, it must be age-appropriate,
  previewed in full, and discussed as rhetoric to analyse — not as a
  position the learner must adopt. Disagreement in the seminar is
  graded on evidence and fairness, never on which side the learner
  takes.
- **Misconceptions to watch:** "persuasive = true"; "a fallacy means
  the conclusion is false" (it means the reasoning does not support
  it); "bias means lying" (selection and emphasis can mislead without
  any invented fact); "one source that agrees with me is
  corroboration" (corroboration needs independence).
