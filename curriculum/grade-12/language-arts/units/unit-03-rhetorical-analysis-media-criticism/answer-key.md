# Answer Key — Unit 03: Rhetorical Analysis and Media Criticism

This key is for the guiding adult. It is separate from student
questions; do not place it beside a student question. Sample answers
show acceptable reasoning; wording may vary if the evidence and the
reasoning match. Where a task is open (a paragraph, a seminar
opening), the key states what must be present, not a single correct
text.

## Lesson 01

**Guided 1.** The last-bus sentence is mainly pathos, with a logos
edge: it turns an abstract closing time into a concrete human cost
(readers stranded before finishing). The count alone shows demand;
the bus sentence shows *consequence* — what happens to those readers
when the doors close. Accept: pathos primary, logos secondary, if the
reasoning is given.
**Guided 2.** Ethos: "As a parent of three children who use this
library every week" — first-hand, relevant experience. Works well
for an audience of parents or a school board (shared stake); less
well for an audience deciding on staffing costs, for whom parental
experience does not answer the cost question. Accept other audience
pairs if reasoned.
**Guided 3.** Accept any added checkable evidence that bears on
"empty": e.g., "In the sample week, evening visits totalled 242
(38, 51, 44, 62, 47)." It helps because it replaces an assumed
premise with a count the audience can check, turning assertion into
logos.
**Independent 1.** Must contain one of each appeal, labelled in the
margin; check labels against the wording, not the learner's intent.
A common error: labelling any emotional word as pathos without an
audience effect — ask for the effect.
**Independent 2.** Accept any honest weakness with a concrete
strengthening move (add a count, a source, a concession).
**Independent 3.** Must list all five parts; "audience" may be
inferred if marked as inferred. No credit for a list that omits
context or purpose.
**Independent 4.** "This text uses pathos" names a category but not
the mechanism: which words, what feeling, for which audience, to
what action — and with what limit. Example from the lesson: "coats
still on" is pathos only once you say it pictures discomfort for an
audience that values sheltering readers, aiming at extended hours.
**Applied 1.** Speaker: a person at a podium, gesturing to screens,
facing a seated audience — the podium and orientation are the
evidence. Audience: seated listeners with notebooks and phones.
Topic: cannot be determined from the image (screens carry no words);
any invented topic is wrong.
**Applied 2.** Notebooks suggest note-taking, study, or preparation
to respond; phones suggest recording, live note-taking, distraction,
or sharing. Each reading could be wrong: a notebook may be doodling;
a phone may be taking notes. Credit requires the qualification, not
only the reading.
**Applied 3.** Accept either judgment if argued: blank screens hurt
logos (no chart, quotation, or figure is shown) unless the speaker
supplies numbers and sources in words; or they help by forcing
attention onto the speaker's words. The required point: the speaker
must supply checkable evidence verbally to compensate.
**Applied 4.** Accept any one element with a matching part, e.g., a
sign naming the meeting's question clarifies topic; a programme
naming the speaker clarifies speaker; a handout with figures
clarifies evidence (a sub-part of the situation, not a sixth part).
**Exit.** Rhetorical situation = speaker, audience, purpose, topic,
context. Audience matters because an appeal's force depends on what
that audience values, knows, and fears; the same words move different
audiences differently.

## Lesson 02

**Guided 1.** Accept, e.g.: word choice — "our neighbours" (belonging,
shared identity) serves readers' interest in community; syntax —
"Extend the hours. Let the readers stay." (short commands after long
detail) serves urgency and memorability. Each needs quotation +
effect + whose interest.
**Guided 2.** Flat version, e.g.: "These are people who live nearby."
Lost: belonging, warmth, the implied obligation to neighbours.
Gained (if anything): neutrality and brevity; a reader who distrusts
emotional language may find it more credible.
**Guided 3.** Assumes the audience already believes festival spending
is comparable to, or less worthy than, library spending — and that
the town did in fact fund a festival stage. It is not defended in the
speech; no figure or record is given. A sceptical reader should ask
for the comparison's basis.
**Independent 1.** Three paragraphs, two quotations minimum; check
that each paragraph analyses (mechanism + effect), not only
summarises.
**Independent 2.** Objective summary must drop praise/blame and
vivid borrowing, e.g.: "Ortiz argues for extending evening hours,
citing a counted Tuesday total and describing evening readers; she
contrasts library funding with festival funding and urges the town
to extend hours." Lost: tone, urgency, and the human detail that
carried the persuasion.
**Independent 3.** Adding a human detail (e.g., the nurse from Speech
A, attributed) increases pathos and vividness without changing the
figures; it changes power because the audience now pictures a person
affected, not only a budget line. It does not change the facts, and
the learner must say so.
**Independent 4.** "Biased" usually implies distortion or unfairness;
"point of view" means a stance and interests that shape selection
and emphasis, which every text has — including fair ones. Speech A
has a point of view (pro-extension, reader-centred); that alone does
not make it biased in the sense of dishonest.
**Applied.** Each sentence needs (a) literal content, (b) invited
feeling/assumption, (c) a sceptical question. Final judgment must
name a purpose and a style mechanism serving it. Accept variation in
sentence grouping if all sentences are covered.
**Exit.** Style adds no new fact but changes what the audience
notices, feels, and remembers: word choice frames the fact, syntax
controls emphasis and pace, and framing decides which facts lead.

## Lesson 03

**Guided 1.** Final sentences: "A phased plan is not a refusal" is
the claim's defence (a rebuttal of the anticipated charge); "It is
how a town keeps a promise…" is the warrant in slogan form — phasing
= promise-keeping, not abandonment. The final sentence does work the
proposal sentence alone does not: it reframes delay as fidelity.
**Guided 2.** Precise replacement, e.g.: "In one sample week,
evening visits totalled 242 (38, 51, 44, 62, 47)." It improves the
evidence but does *not* fully fix the warrant gap: evening demand
still needs a link to "extend now" (cost, staffing, alternatives).
Credit requires that qualification.
**Guided 3.** Fair counterclaim to Speech A, e.g.: "Extended hours
cost money the roof repair also needs." Fair rebuttal Ortiz could
give, e.g.: "Phase the hours and fund the roof in the same budget,
rather than treating them as rivals." Caricature ("Ortiz hates
roofs") earns no credit.
**Independent 1.** Four parts labelled; warrant must be the link,
not a second piece of evidence. Check with the cover-the-claim test.
**Independent 2.** Restructured paragraph (~80 words) must open with
a concession, give evidence second, and close with a proposal.
Judgment must pick clear, convincing, or engaging and defend the
pick with a mechanism (order, credibility, memorability).
**Independent 3.** A true fact is weak evidence when its warrant
fails: e.g., total annual visits are true but do not show evening
demand, so they do not support an evening-hours claim. The fact is
true; the link is missing.
**Independent 4.** Warrant: the number of signatures shows the
strength or legitimacy of public support, so support should decide
funding. A reader might reject it while accepting the count because
signatures show preference, not need, cost, or merit — 200 signatures
do not show the garden is the best use of the money.
**Applied.** 200-word evaluation: one strength and one weakness for
clear, convincing, and engaging, each quoted; plus one concrete
revision (add, move, or cut a sentence) with a reason tied to the
weakness it fixes.
**Exit.** A warrant is the unstated link between evidence and claim.
Finding it is the most useful test because most weak arguments have
true facts and a broken link; without the warrant you cannot see
where the argument actually fails.

## Lesson 04

**Guided 1.** For each §3 sentence, the needed evidence is the
missing link: (1) a fair statement of opponents' actual position;
(2) the maintenance log, not lateness; (3) a third option or its
absence, argued; (4) more nights, or a narrowed claim; (5) evidence
for each step of the chain; (6) evening counts and staffing figures.
**Guided 2.** No — not faulty, if the librarian's expertise is
relevant (evening use is part of the librarian's work) and the claim
is about use, not about costs outside that expertise. It *would* be
faulty if the librarian were cited for a question outside their
knowledge (e.g., roof engineering costs) or quoted out of context.
Sourcing asks about relevant expertise and interest; ad hominem
attacks the person instead of the argument.
**Guided 3.** Accept, e.g.: "We can extend the hours, phase them, or
keep them as they are; I argue for extension because…" — the third
option is named and the speaker's preference is argued, not assumed
as the only alternative.
**Independent 1.** (a) Hasty generalisation — "Three students I
asked" cannot carry "all students." (b) Slippery slope — no evidence
links one food truck to "nothing but food trucks." (c) Ad hominem —
a failed test does not settle the argument. (d) False dilemma —
"books or computers, not both" excludes funding or phasing both.
Each needs the quoted breaking point.
**Independent 2.** Two original sentences, labelled, repaired without
changing conclusions. Check repairs genuinely remove the fallacy
(narrowing the claim counts; merely rewording the same leap does not).
**Independent 3.** "Fallacious, so false" is itself an error (the
fallacy fallacy): a fallacy shows the reasoning does not prove the
conclusion, not that the conclusion is false. The conclusion might
be true for other, better reasons. The correct response is to ask for
better reasoning or evidence.
**Independent 4.** Repaired ~80-word version must argue "reject
phasing" without ad hominem or slippery slope, e.g., by arguing the
roof can wait, both projects can run together, or delay costs
readers more than roof risk — with a stated premise and its support.
Must name the replaced premise (past opposition / "delay forever" /
"never return") and the replacement evidence.
**Applied.** Delineation (claim + premises) first; fallacy named
with a breaking point, or a reasoned "none found" with the best-
supported premise and the most challengeable premise named; one weak
link repaired. "None found" without the two premise judgments is
incomplete.
**Exit.** "This argument is fallacious" = this reasoning does not
support its conclusion. "This conclusion is false" = the claim itself
is untrue. The first does not entail the second.

## Lesson 05

**Guided 1.** News: item A — "Council heard testimony Tuesday… No
vote was taken" (reporting, no urged position). Opinion: item B —
"a town that turns them away has chosen its priorities" (judgment
and urging, not neutral reporting). Either quoted sentence pair that
proves the kinds earns credit.
**Guided 2.** Item D covers one sample week (38, 51, 44, 62, 47;
total 242). "Hundreds every single week" claims (i) more than the
sample shows in any single week read as a standing pattern, and
(ii) a regularity across weeks that one week cannot establish. It
overstates scope, not just rounding.
**Guided 3.** SIFT notes must have one line per letter and end:
"Supported / Unverified / Contradicted, because …" For the §5 post:
Unverified, and inconsistent with the only counted figure (242 in a
week, not 500 a night). Do not accept a verdict about the poster's
motives.
**Independent 1.** Table rows A–D: A news, claim = proposal heard,
no vote; evidence = testimony + counts + roof report; omits cost
figures; credible as reporting, limited to what was presented.
B opinion, claim = extend; evidence = the 242 figure (repeated from
A, not independent); omits roof; persuasive, not corroborating.
C video transcript, claim = extend ("keep the lights on"); evidence
= music, images, "hundreds" slogan; omits roof, costs, and any
count source; weakest evidential standing. D chart, claim = evening
demand in the sample week; evidence = nightly counts totalling 242,
mean 48.4; omits costs and other weeks; strongest for the count
itself, silent on policy.
**Independent 2.** ~150 words, two formats used, one discrepancy
(e.g., roof present in A, absent in B; Thursday busiest, not Tuesday)
and one missing piece (cost, staffing, other weeks) named. Must not
claim more than the set supports (e.g., must not claim every week
is like the sample week).
**Independent 3.** Corroboration = independent sources agreeing after
their own checking. Repetition = one source restating another's
figure. A and B: B repeats A's 242, so A + B is one count reported
twice, not two confirmations.
**Independent 4.** Videos show edited selections: framing, cuts,
music, and pace choose what the viewer sees and feels; a real image
can be placed in a misleading context (wrong date, cropped, or cut
mid-sentence). "It shows real things" confuses a real fragment with
a true claim about the whole.
**Applied.** Three formats, sourcing notes, one SIFT move, one
discrepancy or agreement, and a ~100-word judgment stating what is
supported, unverified, and missing. Adult-supplied sources only;
unverified live sources without adult preview are not acceptable.
**Exit.** Lateral reading = leaving a page to check its source
elsewhere (other outlets, "About" pages, original documents). It
beats studying one page longer because a page's design, polish, and
internal consistency can all be faked, while independent outside
checking tests the source itself.

## Lesson 06

**Guided 1.** Five paragraphs on Speech A: stance (extend hours,
reader-centred belonging); premises (evening readers exist in
number; belonging is a proper ground for funding); links (denial →
witness detail → cost reframe → command close); evidence/rhetoric
(one Tuesday count, vivid named readers, short closing commands);
opposition (cost named as "part of the question" but not answered
with figures). Overall judgment must separate strengths from gaps.
**Guided 2.** ~120-word rewrite must quote the §2 speech at least
twice, name a stance, a premise, evidence, and a gap — not merely
praise the speaker.
**Guided 3.** Three questions: opening (precise, e.g., the
phase/extend/keep question with "what evidence would change your
mind?"); objection-inviting ("What is the best argument against my
position?"); evidence-settling ("What figure or document would
settle this dispute?").
**Independent 1.** Five parts, one quotation per part, overall
judgment separating strengths from weaknesses. A single overall
impression without parts earns no more than partial credit.
**Independent 2.** Two-minute opening must contain: question,
position, two evidence items, and the strongest objection fairly
stated. Missing the fair objection is the most common gap.
**Independent 3.** ~100-word synthesis must contain agreement,
dispute, and evidence that would settle it. A synthesis that only
restates one side is incomplete.
**Independent 4.** A leader who calls only on agreers fails because a
seminar's job is testing, not rallying: without objections the group
never learns whether its position survives its strongest challenge,
and synthesis becomes impossible (there is nothing to synthesise).
Liveliness is not the standard; testing is.
**Applied.** Rehearsal credit requires: objection answered with
evidence, a genuine concession if the objection is strong, and a
restated position that includes the concession. Three closing
sentences must state what changed, what did not, and why.
**Exit.** Part-by-part evaluation separates stance, premises, links,
evidence/rhetoric, and opposition-handling, so a speech strong in
one part and weak in another is judged accurately instead of being
reduced to a single impression ("convincing" or "unconvincing").

## Practice

**Set A.** A1: speaker = Saturday volunteer (first-hand, three
years); audience = inferred, e.g., library board or town meeting
(marked as inferred); purpose = direct attention/resources to the
children's corner; topic = where the library's life/use lies;
context = a weekend-morning observation and a meeting-room contrast.
A2: ethos — "volunteer who shelved books every Saturday for three
years"; pathos — "busiest room," children and parents before noon
(concrete, human); logos — "forty-one children and parents… before
noon, while the meeting room… sat empty" (counted contrast).
A3: flat version, e.g., "The children's corner had 41 visitors before
noon; the meeting room had none." Lost: vividness, implication,
urgency. Gained: neutrality, checkability.
A4: ethos (experience/identity). Weak alone because residence does
not establish knowledge of library use, costs, or staffing; the
appeal claims standing without relevant evidence for this decision.
A5: (i) invites "strong demand"; (ii) invites "small numbers, not
worth the cost." Neither changes the underlying count (242 in a
sample week, mean 48.4) or the missing cost evidence.
A6: check underlined words actually shift tone (e.g., "urgent
crisis" → "a question to consider"); tone must change without
changing the facts stated.
**Set B.** B1: claim = extend hours; evidence = 242 visits in one
sample week; warrant = evening visit numbers show evening demand
that justifies evening hours (and, implicitly, that demand outweighs
cost — unstated and debatable).
B2: fair counterclaim, e.g., "Costs and roof repairs compete for the
same money." Fair rebuttal, e.g., "Phase the hours and fund the roof
in stages." Caricature earns no credit.
B3: (a) Straw man — "just hate green spaces" attacks a motive nobody
stated. (b) False dilemma — "or no nature at all" excludes partial,
phased, or other funding. (c) Hasty generalisation — two neighbours
cannot carry "the whole street." (d) Slippery slope — no evidence
links one truck to "nothing but food trucks."
B4: repair must narrow or evidence the claim, e.g.: "One food truck
would add traffic and litter on this street; the council should set
a limit and review it after three months." State the change (narrowed
scope / added mechanism / review condition).
B5: conceding a strong point (Speech B concedes shared love of the
library and past support) buys credibility: the audience can trust a
speaker who admits what is true for the other side, so the remaining
disagreement sounds considered rather than partisan.
B6: the student's response commits the fallacy fallacy: a fallacy
shows the reasoning fails, not that the conclusion is false. Correct
response: ask for better reasoning or evidence for the conclusion.
**Set C.** C1: agreed fact — the sample-week evening count is 242
(A, B, D; C's "hundreds" is a looser version of the same figure, not
a separate fact). Reported by one item only — the roof leaks (A
only). Claimed by one item, unsupported by others — "hundreds every
single week" as a standing pattern (C only).
C2: SIFT notes — Stop: urgency/caps/"no outlet will report" signals;
Investigate: unnamed "insiders," no outlet, no about page in the
set; Find: no corroboration in the set (the Gazette report says no
such closure); Trace: no document or link to trace. Verdict:
Unverified (and, against item A, inconsistent with any reported
closure plan).
C3: five sentences, one per Lesson 06 part; check each sentence
actually addresses its part (stance ≠ praise; premises ≠ summary).
C4: U02 comparison compares how two texts treat one theme (meaning
across texts); this unit's comparison compares how formats present
one question (evidence, selection, and credibility across media).
The objects differ (texts' themes vs. formats' evidence), even
though both use comparison.
C5: "Leaves uncertain" is a finding because it marks the boundary of
what the text supports: a careful reader reports uncertainty rather
than filling it with assumption, and can then name what evidence
would resolve it.
C6: ~100 words; position + one evidence item + one underlined
objection, fairly stated. Missing the objection, or caricaturing it,
is incomplete.

## Week-04 quiz (20 pts, 2 pts each)

Q1. Speaker, audience, purpose, topic, context (all five; 2 pts only
if all five named).
Q2. Ethos primary: "I have shelved books in this library for nine
years" (relevant first-hand experience). Accept logos as secondary
only if the Tuesday count is the quoted carrier — the sentence's
main work is credibility, so ethos is the expected answer; a logos
answer must explain why the count, not the experience, is primary.
Q3. Warrant = the unstated link between evidence and claim. For the
given argument: evening visit numbers show evening demand that
justifies evening hours (accept the cost implication as an optional
second clause).
Q4. False dilemma: "extend or abandon" excludes phasing, keeping
hours, or other options; the break is the missing third option.
Q5. Hasty generalisation: one busy Tuesday cannot support "every
evening all year"; the break is sample scope vs. claim scope.
Q6. Corroboration = independent sources agreeing after their own
checking; repetition = restating another source's figure. A and B:
B repeats A's 242, so together they are one count, not two
confirmations.
Q7. Lateral reading = leaving a page to check its source elsewhere.
It most closely matches SIFT's I (Investigate the source); accept
F if argued as "finding coverage elsewhere," but I is expected.
Q8. No. Item D totals 242 in one sample week (mean 48.4 per evening);
"hundreds every single week" as a standing claim goes beyond one
week's sample and misstates the scale (242 is hundreds in total for
the week, but the video's wording claims it for every week).
Q9. Any two of: stance, premises, links among ideas,
evidence/rhetoric, handling of opposition — each with a genuine
evaluative question (e.g., for premises: "What must be true for this
to work, and is it defended?").
Q10. No. A fallacy shows the reasoning does not prove the conclusion;
it does not show the conclusion is false.

## Unit assessment (40 pts, 10 pts per task)

**Task 1 (10).** (a) Situation (2 pts): speaker Jo Park, long-time
neighbour of the library; audience = town meeting / council (the
address form); purpose = persuade council to act this month;
topic = extending library evening hours; context = a budget decision
imminent ("before the budget is set"). (b) Appeals (5 pts): ethos —
"I have lived on the same street… for twenty years" (long local
standing); pathos — "children grow up walking to it," "not a seat
free" (belonging, crowding, human stakes); logos — "on the three
evenings I visited, the reading room was full each time" (personal
observation count, limited to three evenings). Each needs quotation
+ audience effect; a label without quotation earns at most half.
(c) Limit (3 pts): three evenings is a small, self-selected sample;
"full" is not counted; no cost, staffing, or roof evidence; the
address therefore supports "some evening demand on visited nights,"
not a general or costed case.
**Task 2 (10).** (a) Map (4 pts): claim = council should extend hours
this month, before the budget is set; evidence = twenty years'
residence, three full evenings, children walking to the library;
warrant = observed fullness shows demand that justifies immediate
extension (and that waiting = refusing); counterclaim handling =
none fairly stated — "anyone who says evening readers do not exist"
is a straw man, not the actual phased or cost objection, so the
strongest counterclaim (cost/roof/phasing) is missing.
(b) Fallacy (3 pts): accept either — slippery slope: "If we do not
extend… we will lose a generation of readers, and a town that loses
its readers loses its future" (no step-by-step support from hours to
generational loss); or straw man: "anyone who says evening readers
do not exist" (no opponent in the corpus denies readers exist; the
real dispute is cost and phasing). One correctly diagnosed fallacy
with quotation and break earns full marks; naming both earns no
extra, but naming one wrongly and one rightly still earns credit for
the right one.
(c) Repair (3 pts): rewrite must remove the fallacy without changing
the conclusion (extend this month), e.g.: "If we do not extend the
hours now, the readers I saw on three full evenings will keep
finding the doors closed, and the budget decision will lock that in
for a year." Must state the change (narrowed scope / removed
absolute prediction / replaced straw man with the real objection).
**Task 3 (10).** ~150 words, two quotations minimum. Strong answers
note: long residence and personal witness (ethos through duration);
short, absolute sentences and repetition ("loses… loses") building
urgency; framing waiting as refusal ("waiting is the same as
refusing") which removes the phased middle option from view.
Judgment must go beyond "sounds good": e.g., style strengthens
urgency and memorability for a town-meeting audience but weakens
persuasiveness for a cost-focused audience, because urgency
substitutes for the missing cost and count evidence. Mark: quotations
(3), framing named (2), style mechanisms analysed (3), judgment with
a reason beyond taste (2).
**Task 4 (10).** ~200 words. Must use two formats (e.g., Gazette
report + opinion column, or chart + video transcript); name a
discrepancy or omission (roof in A only; Thursday busiest, not
Tuesday; C's "hundreds every week" vs. D's one sample week); name a
missing piece (cost/staffing figures, other weeks, roof repair cost);
state a position with limits (e.g., "phase now, on this evidence,
because demand in the sample week is supported but costs are not
yet known — this does not prove extension is affordable"). Mark:
formats integrated, not listed (3); discrepancy/omission (2);
missing evidence (2); position with stated limits (3). A position
without limits, or a claim beyond the set (e.g., "every week is
busy"), loses the limits marks.
