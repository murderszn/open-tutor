# Answer Key — Unit 01: Critical Lenses and Interpretation

**For the guiding adult only.** Never place beside student questions.
Open responses accept defensible variation — the key gives the
reasoning a strong answer shows and the errors it avoids. All
quotations are from the U.S. public-domain editions named in the unit
README (Garnett's Chekhov, Gutenberg texts verified 2026-10-15).

---

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice** (four-move cycle on "schooled by bitter experience"):

1. *Notice:* "schooled" makes serial unfaithfulness sound like an
   education — a credential. The oddness: experience is supposed to
   teach; here it licenses contempt.
2. *Question (acceptable):* "What does Gurov's 'bitter experience'
   conceal — who was hurt by the schooling, and why does the sentence
   hide them?"
3. *Connect:* the earlier list ("unintelligent, narrow, inelegant")
   shows the same move — Gurov converts his own failures (a bad
   marriage he chose young) into verdicts on women. Pattern: blame
   flows outward, never inward.
4. *Claim (sample, ≤30 words):* "Gurov treats his unfaithfulness as
   tuition: 'schooled by bitter experience' turns his own betrayals
   into expertise that justifies calling women 'the lower race.'"
   Quotation: "schooled by bitter experience" + "the lower race."

**Independent practice:** check that (1) six notices span word choice
/ presentation / omission; (2) three are genuine questions (not
answerable by plot); (3) the claim is ≤40 words, arguable, and quoted.
Common strong claims: the opening withholds Anna's name to mark her
as seen-not-known; the wife's "phonetic spelling" detail exists to
make Gurov's contempt look petty.

**Applied task (sample paragraph):** "The opening wants a mixed
response: curiosity about the lady, and judgment of Gurov held in
reserve. Curiosity is built by the withheld name — 'no one knew who
she was' — and judgment is seeded by the gap between the wife's
'staid and dignified' and Gurov's 'unintelligent, narrow, inelegant.'
The narrator reports Gurov's contempt flatly, without endorsing it,
which keeps the reader watching *him* rather than joining him.
Uncertainty I will watch: whether the story ever lets Anna speak for
herself, or keeps her inside Gurov's gaze."

**Exit check:** 1. notice → question → connect → argue from evidence.
2. Any genuine interpretive question (not plot curiosity). 3. A notice
is an observation; evidence is a notice *connected to a claim and
quoted*.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice** ("on that account"):

1. The phrase assumes a natural law: being hurt by women (his
   "bitter experience") *explains and excuses* speaking ill of them —
   cause (women) → effect (his contempt), with his own agency erased.
2. The flatness *exposes* rather than confirms: "schooled" is Gurov's
   self-flattering vocabulary, and the narrator quotes his verdicts
   ("the lower race") in a way that lets their cruelty hang
   unadorned. A confirming narrator would not preserve the ugly
   phrase in quotation marks — the marks are distance.
3. Sample: "The sentence assumes Gurov's contempt is a *lesson learned*:
   'schooled by bitter experience' makes his unfaithfulness the
   tuition and women the teachers — so 'the lower race' arrives as a
   conclusion, not a prejudice. The flat reporting exposes the logic
   by refusing to soften it."

**Independent practice:** 1. Two outside-descriptions (e.g., the
*béret*/dog catalog; "she was walking alone") + one speech/thought of
Anna's from part II. 2. Paragraph should note: we learn her guilt,
her fear, her past (married at twenty, calls husband a flunkey) — but
always *as reported*, often tearful; her desire remains mostly
inferred. Withheld: whether she loves Gurov or loves being loved;
what she wants beyond escape. 3. Error analysis must distinguish
*text's construction* (filters women through male judgment) from
*author's attitude* (separate claim needing separate evidence, e.g.,
the ironic distance of the narration).

**Applied task (illustration):** acceptable claim sentences: "The image
argues that a lens does not add information to the text but *changes
the text the reader sees* — the same page under amber vs. violet is a
different reading." / "It claims interpretation is active and plural:
one book yields several readings, and the reader chooses the lens."
Qualifying sentence (any defensible): "But the image may overstate
the reader's control — a strong lens is also *constrained* by the
words; you cannot tint a passage into saying its opposite." Require
both parts; description alone earns partial credit.

**Exit check:** 1. The four questions (own wording): who speaks /
who is spoken about; who has agency; what is assumed natural about
gender; how women are described. 2. "Gurov is sexist" judges the man;
"the narrative filters women through Gurov's judgment" describes the
text's construction — the second is arguable from evidence, the first
needs no text at all. 3. Any passage + what changed.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice** (Gurov's Moscow life):

1. The story *questions* his wealthy life: the bank, the club, and
   dinners are rendered as hollow routine ("he was bored"), while the
   servants remain background. So the lens finds a *limit*: the story
   critiques bourgeois emptiness but does not center labor — the
   critique stays inside Gurov's consciousness.
2. Sample: "Detail: Gurov's Moscow house with its routines that bore
   him. Lens question: whose labor sustains this life, and does the
   text notice? Reading: the story shows the comforts but never the
   servants — wealth's infrastructure is invisible even in a story
   about wealth's emptiness."

**Independent practice:** 1. Chart rows — Row 1 (AGREE): feminist sees
male entitlement; Marxist sees consumer entitlement; same sentence,
same power. Row 2 (COLLIDE): feminist centers Anna's guilt as
patriarchal vocabulary; Marxist centers the economics of secrecy
(who can afford a Yalta affair). Row 3 (AGREE or COLLIDE with
reasoning — either verdict is defensible if argued; COLLIDE is
stronger: the passage is *about* class, gender is secondary there).
2. Paragraph must state a criterion (e.g., "explains more of the
passage's specific language") and apply it; either winner is
acceptable if the criterion is applied honestly.

**Applied task:** check the structure — both readings fairly stated
with best quotations, verdict with criterion, and the "what the loser
still sees" sentence. The final third-lens question should be genuine
(e.g., "What would the new-historicist ask about 1922 America here?").

**Exit check:** 1. Four questions (own wording): economic positions;
visible/invisible labor; how class shapes desire; what is naturalized
about wealth. 2. AGREE: "it wouldn't be amiss to make her acquaintance"
(entitlement, both lenses). COLLIDE: valley of ashes (class-centered
vs. gender-centered). 3. "Lenses stack": two lenses can operate on the
same passage without canceling, each revealing a different axis of
power.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice** (feminist lens on Kipling — fit judgment):

1. Women appear essentially not at all: the poem's actors are "ye,"
   "your sons," "serf and sweeper" (male-coded labor), "your peers."
   The imperial project is an all-male drama.
2. The implied "ye" is the American (male) imperial reader — "Go bind
   *your sons* to exile." Women are excluded from both the mission
   and the address.
3. Sample verdict: "The lens fits *thinly but revealingly*: it finds
   almost no women to analyze, and that absence *is* the finding —
   the poem imagines empire as men's work done upon voiceless
   peoples, with women erased from both sides. 'Thin' because there
   is little to quote; 'revealing' because the erasure is total."

**Independent practice:** 1. Two stanzas analyzed for we/they,
characterizing phrase + assumption, question-or-justify verdict with
quotation. 2. Three *verifiable, cited* 1899 facts (e.g., Chekhov
writing in Yalta for his health; restrictive imperial divorce law;
publication in the liberal journal *Russkaya Mysl*), each with a
one-sentence interpretive payoff. Reject uncited "facts." 3. Fit
paragraph: criterion stated; both lenses given best evidence;
verdict defended. (New-historicist is the stronger fit for Chekhov
on most criteria; accept postcolonial if the Crimea-as-periphery or
service-labor argument is genuinely evidenced.)

**Applied task:** must (a) grant the objection's truth (no colony is
the story's subject), (b) test against a specific passage, (c) end
with one of the three verdict words defended. *Forced* is acceptable
if argued ("the passage evidence is thinner than the lens's claims").

**Exit check:** 1. Postcolonial: who speaks / how colonized are
characterized / whose perspective is normal / question or justify.
New-historicist: what first readers knew / what the text takes for
granted / how the text circulates power. 2. Fit = the judgment of
which tool suits which text; it matters because a forced lens
produces thin readings and false confidence. 3. Postcolonial;
quotation: "Half devil and half child" or "The silent, sullen peoples."

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice** (Anna's confession scene):

1. Feminist (sample): "Anna's guilt speaks in the world's vocabulary,
   not her own: she calls herself 'low' for desiring, while Gurov —
   the experienced adulterer — feels no equivalent shame. The double
   standard is audible in *who* confesses."
2. Reader-response (sample): "I felt impatient with Anna's tears —
   the feeling started at the repetition of her self-blame; a reader
   who has been shamed might instead feel recognition, which the
   scene's emotional intensity allows."
3. Verdict with criterion (either winner acceptable if the criterion
   — e.g., "accounts for the asymmetry of shame" — is applied).

**Independent practice:** 1. Journal must have all four parts (a–d);
check (d) especially — the pull-back line. 2. Full staging: two
readings ≥6 sentences each with ≥2 quotations each, verdict paragraph
with criterion, winner, and the loser's surviving insight. This is
essay rehearsal — hold it to essay standard.

**Applied task (seminar prep):** check the sheet has all four
elements; the two probe questions must target a *competing* reading
("How does your reading handle the weeping in Daisy's line?"); the
stall-breaker must be counter-evidence against the learner's own
claim.

**Exit check:** 1. Four questions (own wording): honest response +
where it started; how a different reader responds; what the text does
to you; where response needs the text vs. goes beyond. 2. "I felt X"
becomes criticism when tied to the textual cause: "I felt X *because*
the text does Y" — the *because* with a quotation is the whole
difference. 3. Any genuine criterion (e.g., "which reading explains
more of the passage's language").

## Practice Sets

**Set A.** 1. Marxist. 2. New-historicist. 3. Feminist. 4.
Reader-response. 5. Accept any genuine new question (e.g., Marxist:
"Who profits from this character's suffering?"; postcolonial: "What
language does the text use for the 'other,' and who taught it that
language?"). 6. The error: new historicism is not background trivia —
it asks what the text and its moment do to *each other*; background
without an interpretive payoff is just context, not a lens.

**Set B.** Check the two-column format; the "deliberate choice"
annotation must name *why this lens* (fit reasoning); the expanded
annotation must be a real Move-4 argument (claim + quotation +
reasoning), not a longer note.

**Set C.** Row 1: AGREE (both find male judgment; feminist: gendered
contempt; Marxist: the leisure to judge). Row 2: COLLIDE (feminist:
the resort as male hunting ground; Marxist: the resort as class
space; different centers). Row 3: AGREE (both see constrained
choices — patriarchal marriage *and* economic dependence). Row 4:
COLLIDE or AGREE with reasoning (strong answers note the secrecy's
gendered risk vs. its price tag). Either verdict is acceptable *if*
the reasoning cites the passage.

**Set D.** 1. Stated: the banker's biography (unfaithful, contemptuous).
Meant: the narrator's flat catalog *exposes* Gurov — the quotation
marks around "the lower race" are distance, not endorsement. 2.
Stated: the mission serves others' profit. Meant: the speaker admits
self-interest — "To cloak your weariness" and "The blame of those ye
better" show the mission's cost is the *ruler's* burden too; the poem
knows more than it admits. 3. Surface: Daisy wants a stupid daughter.
Ironic: "wept" + "in this world" mark grief at the patriarchal
bargain — the line indicts the world, not the girl. "Wept" supports
the ironic reading. 4. Any genuine stated/meant gap with quotation
(e.g., Gurov "schooled by bitter experience").

**Set E.** 1. Chekhov: feminist—strong; Marxist—strong; postcolonial—
thin; new-historicist—strong; reader-response—strong. 2. Kipling:
feminist—thin; Marxist—strong (empire as extraction: "To seek
another's profit, / And work another's gain"); postcolonial—strong;
new-historicist—strong; reader-response—strong. 3–4. Graded on
argument quality, not the ratings.

**Set F.** 1. Star the thesis that stages disagreement (e.g., "The
feminist and Marxist lenses agree that Gurov's opening reflection is
about power, but they collide on *whose* power matters most — and the
text supports the feminist reading better *here* because…").
2–3. Check lens questions are in own words; check every quotation
*works* (plot-only quotations flagged). 4–5. The rival paragraph must
be steelmanned; if the learner cannot answer it, the thesis genuinely
needs revision — do not let them paper over it.

**Set G.** 1–4. Memory check against the unit. 5. Accept any
defensible limit (e.g., "a lens cannot supply evidence the text does
not contain"; "every lens has a center and therefore a periphery —
what it does not ask").

## Week-04 Quiz

**Part 1** (2 points each — 1 for an accurate own-words definition,
1 for the core question):

1. Feminist: studies how texts construct gender/power; Q: who speaks,
   who has agency, what is assumed natural about gender.
2. Marxist: studies class and economic power; Q: whose labor, whose
   profit, what does money allow.
3. Postcolonial: studies empire and the colonizer/colonized relation;
   Q: who speaks, who is silenced, who is "other."
4. New-historicist: studies text and historical moment shaping each
   other; Q: what did first readers know, what does the text take for
   granted.
5. Reader-response: studies the reader's role in making meaning; Q:
   what is my honest response, where did it start, what earns it.

**Part 2** (1 point each): 6. Marxist. 7. New-historicist. 8.
Reader-response. 9. Postcolonial. 10. Feminist.

**Part 3:**

11. (5 points) Strong answers note: the sentence introduces the
    central woman *without a name*, as "a new person" who "appeared"
    — she is a sighting, not a person; "simply the lady with the dog"
    (from the next paragraph) confirms the labeling. Must quote at
    least one phrase (e.g., "a new person had appeared," "simply the
    lady with the dog").
12. (5 points) Strong answers note: the sea-front, the pavilion, the
    idle watching imply a leisure class at a resort — the sentence
    assumes a world where men of means observe arrivals as
    entertainment. Must quote at least one phrase (e.g., "on the
    sea-front," "had begun to take an interest in new arrivals").
13. (5 points) Either verdict is acceptable with a stated criterion
    (e.g., "the feminist reading is stronger because the sentence's
    main work is introducing the woman-as-sight; the economic
    reading needs the later 'fortnight at Yalta' detail to stand").

## Unit Assessment ("The Bet" passage)

**1. Close reading (10).** Two genuine questions (e.g., "Why does the
narrator call the bet 'wild, senseless' *after* describing it in
detail — whose judgment is that?" / "What does 'spoilt and frivolous'
conceal about where the millions came from?"). Claim ≤40 words with
quoted phrase (e.g., "The narrator's 'wild, senseless' judges the bet
before the reader can admire it — the adjectives do the moral work
the story will later unpack.").

**2. Marxist (10).** Strong answers: the banker occupies ownership
(throws away millions as "a trifle"); the lawyer occupies wit without
capital (stakes "my freedom"); money lets the banker convert boredom
into another man's fifteen years; the passage naturalizes that a rich
man's whim can purchase a poor man's life — "I'll bet you two millions
you wouldn't stay" treats the transaction as sport. ≥2 quotations
(e.g., "To me two millions are a trifle"; "You stake your millions and
I stake my freedom!").

**3. Choice lens (10).** Examples: *Reader-response* — the passage
recruits amusement at the banker's excess, then the "unhappy man"
pity complicates it; limit: the passage gives little interiority to
respond *to*. *New-historicist* — 1889 readers knew the real
millionaire class and the era's penal debates; limit: the passage is
a fable-like setup, thin on period specificity. *Feminist* — thin
fit: no women present; the finding is the all-male homosocial wager
("Gentlemen, I stake two millions!"). Grade the fit paragraph on
honesty, not on the lens chosen.

**4. Competing readings (10).** Must stage both readings' answers to
*one* question with best quotations, state a criterion, name a
winner, and credit the loser. (E.g., Q: "What is the bet really
about?" Marxist: money converting into power over life — "I stake my
freedom." Reader-response: the thrill of the wager as entertainment —
"delighted at the bet." Verdict by criterion "which reading the later
story supports" → Marxist wins; the loser still sees the *play*
element the economic reading underplays.)

**5. Stated vs. meant (5).** Stated: two millions mean nothing to him.
Meant: the line *performs* wealth — it is said "at supper," before
an audience, as part of "making fun"; the trifle-talk is dominance
display. Supporting phrase: "At supper he made fun of the young man"
or "delighted at the bet."

**6. Reflection (5).** Graded on specificity: names the default lens,
says what it reveals about the reader, and cites one concrete
unit moment when a lens changed what they saw. Vague answers ("lenses
are useful") earn partial credit.

## Project (planning deliverables)

- **Planning sheet:** anchor + two lenses named; lens questions in
  own words; three candidate theses with the disagreement-staging one
  starred; six quotations that *work* (flag plot-only picks).
- **Studio 1 draft:** introduction + thesis + first lens section;
  spot-check the no-claim-without-quotation rule.
- **Studio 2 draft:** second lens + competing reading; run the
  evidence-check colors; the rival paragraph must pass the steelman
  test.
- **Revision:** the 3-sentence revision note must name real changes
  (not "fixed grammar"); verify the checklist boxes are honestly
  earned before portfolio filing.
