# Project — The Multi-Lens Interpretation Essay

**Deliverable:** a revised analytical essay (900–1,200 words) that
advances a claim about one anchor text, developed through **at least
two critical lenses**, with a fairly staged and answered competing
reading. **Sessions:** 14–17 (planning, two drafting studios, revision
studio with adult conference). Filed afterward in the learner's private
portfolio as the seed of the U08 capstone.

## What the essay must do

1. **Claim through lenses.** The thesis advances an interpretation
   developed through at least two named lenses (e.g., feminist +
   Marxist on Chekhov; postcolonial + new-historicist on Kipling).
   Listing two lenses is not enough — the essay must show what each
   lens *reveals*.
2. **Argue from the text.** Every interpretive claim is supported by
   quoted evidence (aim for 6–10 well-chosen quotations, each glossed —
   never dropped in unexplained).
3. **Stage a competing reading.** At least one substantial paragraph
   presents the strongest case *against* the thesis (a different
   lens's reading, or a different verdict from the same lens), fairly
   stated with its best evidence — then answers it.
4. **Mind stated vs. meant.** At least one passage analysis
   distinguishes what the text states from what it means (irony,
   understatement, or the narrator's distance).
5. **Acknowledge uncertainty.** At least one place where the text
   leaves a matter uncertain is named and its effect on the argument
   discussed (RL.11-12.1).

**Choose one anchor:** Chekhov's "The Lady with the Dog" *or*
Kipling's "The White Man's Burden" (the *Gatsby* passages may appear
as supporting evidence but cannot anchor the essay — they were studied
as rereads, not in full).

## Steps and milestones

**Session 14 — Planning (thesis workshop).**

- Choose the anchor text and the two lenses; write the lens questions
  you will actually use.
- Draft three candidate theses (see Practice Set F); the adult helps
  you star the one that stages a disagreement.
- Deliverable: planning sheet (anchor, lenses, questions, starred
  thesis, six candidate quotations).

**Session 15 — Drafting studio 1.**

- Write the introduction (with thesis) and the first lens section.
- Rule: no claim without a quotation; no quotation without a gloss.
- Deliverable: ~400–500 words.

**Session 16 — Drafting studio 2 + peer evidence-check.**

- Write the second lens section and the competing-reading paragraph
  (state the rival case *first*, at its strongest, then answer it).
- Evidence-check protocol (adult or self): highlight every claim
  (yellow), every quotation (green), every gloss (blue). Any yellow
  without adjacent green+blue gets a quotation or gets cut.
- Deliverable: full draft, 900–1,200 words.

**Session 17 — Revision studio (adult conference).**

- Conference agenda (15 min): (1) read the thesis aloud — does the
  draft prove *this* thesis? (2) the competing reading — is it
  steelmanned? (3) one paragraph the adult found unconvincing — revise
  it together.
- Revision checklist: ☐ thesis matches what the draft proves ☐ every
  claim quoted ☐ competing reading answered, not dismissed ☐ stated/
  meant handled once ☐ uncertainty named once ☐ conclusion follows
  from the argument (no new claims).
- Deliverable: revised essay + 3-sentence revision note (what changed
  and why).

## Rubric

| Criterion | Excellent (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Beginning (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lens application** | Both lenses produce genuine discoveries; lens questions are visible in the analysis, not just named | Both lenses applied with evidence; one lens does more work than the other | Lenses named but analysis is mostly lens-neutral; questions not visible | One or no lens actually used |
| **Evidence** | 6–10 quotations, each glossed; quotations are *analyzed*, not dropped; line-level precision | Quotations support most claims; glosses present but sometimes thin | Few quotations; some claims unquoted; quotations occasionally unexplained | Claims rest on plot memory; quotations absent or decorative |
| **Competing reading** | Rival case stated at its strongest with its best evidence, then answered with reasoning (not dismissal) | Competing reading present and answered, but the rival case is weaker than it could be | Competing reading mentioned but straw-manned or unanswered | No competing reading |
| **Stated vs. meant; uncertainty** | Irony/understatement analyzed with precision; uncertainty named and its effect on the argument discussed | Both present; analysis correct but less developed | One present; the other missing or vague | Neither present |
| **Organization & style** | Thesis governs every paragraph; formal academic style; conclusion follows from the argument | Clear structure; minor drift; style mostly formal | Thesis/draft mismatch; some informal or vague language | No discernible thesis; summary instead of argument |

**Scoring:** 18–20 = excellent; 14–17 = proficient (ready for the
portfolio); 10–13 = revise one criterion with the adult and resubmit;
below 10 = re-draft with the planning sheet rebuilt. The adult records
the score privately; the essay itself stays in private storage.

## Misconception watch (for the adult)

- *Thesis as list:* "This essay will use the feminist and Marxist
  lenses to analyze the story." → Require a *claim*: "Through the
  feminist and Marxist lenses, the story shows ___."
- *Lens as label:* dropping "from a Marxist perspective" before a
  plot summary. → Ask: "What does the *question* reveal that the
  summary doesn't?"
- *Competing reading as concession:* "Some might disagree, but I am
  right." → Require the rival's best quotation first.
- *Word-count padding:* long plot retellings. → Cut anything the
  thesis doesn't need; the evidence-check colors expose it.
