# Practice Sets — Unit 01: Critical Lenses and Interpretation

Additional guided and independent practice for sessions 2, 5, 7, 8,
10, and 12, or for reteaching. Worked solutions and acceptable
variation are in `answer-key.md`. Do all quoting from the U.S.
public-domain editions named in the unit README.

## Set A — Lens-question fluency (after Lesson 02)

*Goal: match questions to lenses without hesitation.*

1. "Who does the hard physical work in this scene, and does the
   narrator notice them?" — which lens? (1 sentence)
2. "What did the author's first readers know that I don't?" — which
   lens?
3. "Whose thoughts am I allowed inside, and whose are closed to me?"
   — which lens?
4. "Where did my confusion start, and what in the text caused it?" —
   which lens?
5. Write one *new* question for the Marxist lens that is not in the
   lesson's bank, and one for the postcolonial lens.
6. **Error analysis:** "The new-historicist lens is just the history
   behind the story." Explain in 3–4 sentences what this gets wrong.

## Set B — Annotation lab (after Lesson 02; use with session 5)

*Goal: produce lens-directed annotations, not plot notes.*

On story part III (Gurov in Moscow), annotate with a two-column
notebook — *quotation (≤2 lines)* | *lens question it answers*:

1. Two annotations through the feminist lens.
2. Two annotations through the Marxist lens.
3. One annotation where you *chose* the lens deliberately: name why
   this lens and not another for this passage.
4. Circle your single strongest annotation and expand it into a
   4-sentence Move-4 argument (claim + quotation + reasoning).

## Set C — Agree/collide drills (after Lesson 03; use with session 7)

*Goal: make the comparison move automatic.*

For each pair, write one feminist claim and one Marxist claim (each
≤25 words, each with a ≤15-word quotation), then mark AGREE or
COLLIDE with one sentence of reasoning:

1. Gurov's wife: "she read a great deal… he secretly considered her
   unintelligent, narrow, inelegant."
2. The Yalta setting itself: a resort town where the affair happens.
3. Anna's words about her husband (she calls him a flunkey; she
   married at twenty and calls it a mistake).
4. The hotel meetings in Moscow (secrecy, travel, money — who pays,
   who risks what).

## Set D — Stated vs. meant (after Lesson 04; RL.11-12.6)

*Goal: practice the stated/meant distinction the unit's standard
requires.*

1. "He had begun being unfaithful to her long ago… used to call them
   'the lower race.'" — What is *stated*? What is *meant* (what does
   the flat reporting imply about the narrator's distance from Gurov)?
2. "Take up the White Man's burden — / … / To seek another's profit,
   / And work another's gain." — What is *stated* (the mission's
   purpose)? What might be *meant* (who actually profits)? Quote the
   stanza that most supports the "meant" reading.
3. "I'm glad it's a girl. And I hope she'll be a fool — that's the
   best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool."
   — Give the *surface* reading (Daisy wants a stupid daughter) and
   the *ironic* reading, then say which the word "wept" supports.
4. Write your own: find one more stated/meant gap in the story or
   the poem, quote ≤2 lines, and explain the gap in 3 sentences.

## Set E — Lens-fit judgments (after Lesson 04; use with session 10)

*Goal: defend fit, not just apply lenses.*

1. Rate the fit of each lens on "The Lady with the Dog" as
   *strong / thin / forced*: feminist, Marxist, postcolonial,
   new-historicist, reader-response. One evidence sentence per rating.
2. Rate the fit of each lens on "The White Man's Burden" the same way.
3. Pick your most controversial rating from 1–2 and defend it in
   6–8 sentences against an imagined challenger.
4. **Reflection:** which lens is becoming your *default* — the one
   you reach for first — and what does that reveal about *you* as a
   reader? (4–5 sentences; this is reader-response turned inward.)

## Set F — Essay rehearsal (after Lesson 05; use with sessions 12–14)

*Goal: rehearse every essay move before drafting.*

1. Write three candidate thesis statements for the multi-lens essay;
   star the one that *stages a disagreement* rather than merely
   listing two lenses.
2. For your starred thesis, list the two lenses and write each lens's
   core question in your own words underneath.
3. Collect six quotations (three per lens) with 1-sentence glosses
   each. Check: does every quotation actually *work* for its lens,
   or are any just plot?
4. Write the competing-reading paragraph in full: the strongest case
   *against* your thesis, fairly stated, with its best quotation.
5. Write your answer to that competing reading in 4–5 sentences.
   If you cannot answer it, your thesis needs revision — say so
   honestly and revise it.

## Set G — Mixed retrieval (session 20 review)

1. From memory: all five lenses and one core question each.
2. From memory: the four close-reading moves.
3. Define in your own words: *agency, ideology, subaltern, stated vs.
   meant, counter-reading.*
4. Without looking: which passage did you use for the agree/collide
   chart, and what was your verdict?
5. One paragraph: what is the single most important thing a lens
   cannot do — a limit every lens shares?
