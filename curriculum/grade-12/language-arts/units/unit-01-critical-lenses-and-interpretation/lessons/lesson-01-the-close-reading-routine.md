# Lesson 01 — The Close-Reading Routine at the High End of the Band

**Goal:** You will run the four-move close-reading routine — notice,
question, connect, argue from evidence — on a demanding literary
opening, and produce annotations that raise real interpretive
questions. **Time:** about 50 minutes, plus the session-2 workshop.
**Materials:** the Chekhov opening passage below (or the full story
text), a notebook or printed page with wide margins, pen.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer in your notebook before reading the lesson:

1. In one sentence, what is the difference between *summarizing* a
   paragraph and *interpreting* it?
2. What is one question you asked about a book you read last year that
   the book never directly answered?

(The adult checks these quickly: summary retells; interpretation claims
what it means. If the learner cannot name an unanswered question, the
adult models one from any familiar story before continuing.)

## Teaching: what changes at grade 12 (15 minutes)

You already know how to close-read. What changes now is the *band*:
the standards expect you, by year's end, to read literature at the high
end of the grades 11–CCR complexity band **independently and
proficiently** (RL.11-12.10). At the high end, texts do three things
that easier texts do not:

1. **They withhold.** Key facts arrive late, or never. You must read
   *around* the gap and say what the gap itself means.
2. **They contradict.** A narrator says one thing and shows another.
   You must hold both and decide what the tension proves.
3. **They assume.** The author assumes you know the world the text
   comes from — and sometimes assumes wrongly about you.

The routine that handles all three has four moves. Memorize the moves;
you will use them every week this year.

**Move 1 — Notice.** Mark what the language *does*, not just what it
says: repetitions, word choices that surprise you, who is described and
how, what is left out. Annotation is thinking in the margin: underline,
circle, and write the thought the mark produced.

**Move 2 — Question.** Turn every strong notice into a genuine
question — one the text does not answer directly. "Why does the
narrator tell us this *here*?" beats "What happens next?"

**Move 3 — Connect.** Link the notice to something else: another
passage, a pattern across the story, a fact about the author's world.
A notice becomes evidence only when it connects to a claim.

**Move 4 — Argue from evidence.** State a claim small enough to prove
— then prove it with *quoted* words, not plot memory. If you cannot
quote it, you do not have it yet.

A lens, which you meet in Lesson 02, is simply a disciplined version of
Move 2: a set of questions you bring on purpose instead of waiting for
them to occur to you.

## The anchor passage

Read this opening twice — once for the story, once with a pen. It is
the opening of Anton Chekhov's "The Lady with the Dog" (1899), in
Constance Garnett's 1917 translation (Project Gutenberg ebook 13415;
public domain in the USA).

> IT was said that a new person had appeared on the sea-front: a lady
> with a little dog. Dmitri Dmitritch Gurov, who had by then been a
> fortnight at Yalta, and so was fairly at home there, had begun to take
> an interest in new arrivals. Sitting in Verney's pavilion, he saw,
> walking on the sea-front, a fair-haired young lady of medium height,
> wearing a *béret*; a white Pomeranian dog was running behind her.
>
> And afterwards he met her in the public gardens and in the square
> several times a day. She was walking alone, always wearing the same
> *béret*, and always with the same white dog; no one knew who she was,
> and every one called her simply the lady with the dog.
>
> "If she is here alone without a husband or friends, it wouldn't be
> amiss to make her acquaintance," Gurov reflected.
>
> He was under forty, but he had a daughter already twelve years old,
> and two sons at school. He had been married young, when he was a
> student in his second year, and by now his wife seemed half as old
> again as he. She was a tall, erect woman with dark eyebrows, staid and
> dignified, and, as she said of herself, intellectual. She read a great
> deal, used phonetic spelling, called her husband, not Dmitri, but
> Dimitri, and he secretly considered her unintelligent, narrow,
> inelegant, was afraid of her, and did not like to be at home. He had
> begun being unfaithful to her long ago — had been unfaithful to her
> often, and, probably on that account, almost always spoke ill of
> women, and when they were talked about in his presence, used to call
> them "the lower race."

## Worked example 1: a full four-move cycle (modeled)

**Move 1 — Notice.** The narrator calls Gurov's wife "staid and
dignified" and "intellectual" — then, in the same breath, reports that
Gurov "secretly considered her unintelligent, narrow, inelegant." I
underline the contradiction and write in the margin: *whose judgment is
this — the narrator's or Gurov's?*

**Move 2 — Question.** If Gurov despises his wife and calls women "the
lower race," why does the narrator give *his* contempt such careful,
almost amused attention? Is the story inviting me to share Gurov's view
of women, or to watch him?

**Move 3 — Connect.** Two paragraphs earlier, the lady is described
only from the outside — "fair-haired," "medium height," "the same
*béret*" — as if she is an object being catalogued. Gurov's wife is
described from the inside of Gurov's contempt. Both women arrive
*through Gurov's eyes*. The pattern: we never get a woman unfiltered.

**Move 4 — Argue from evidence.** *Claim:* the opening trains the
reader to distrust Gurov's view of women even before the affair begins.
*Evidence:* the narrator reports Gurov's verdicts ("unintelligent,
narrow, inelegant," "the lower race") immediately after showing the
wife's actual qualities ("staid and dignified," "read a great deal") —
the gap between the two lists is the story's first irony, and irony
means stated ≠ meant (RL.11-12.6).

Notice what the claim does *not* do: it does not summarize the plot,
and it does not claim Chekhov agrees with Gurov. It is small, quoted,
and arguable — which is exactly what makes it strong.

## Worked example 2: a question that fails, then fixed (modeled)

*Weak:* "Will Gurov talk to the lady?" — the text will answer this;
it is curiosity, not interpretation.

*Stronger:* "Why does the narrator stress that she wears *the same*
*béret* every day?" — now I am asking about a choice. Possible
answers: she has little money; she is in mourning for something; the
sameness marks her as an outsider the town has already labeled ("the
lady with the dog" — a label, not a name). Each answer is a small
interpretation I can test against later passages. The test of a good
Move-2 question: **it gives you something to check.**

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Do one full four-move cycle on this sentence from the passage:

> "It seemed to him that he had been so schooled by bitter experience
> that he might call them what he liked, and yet he could not get on
> for two days together without 'the lower race.'"

Prompts (use as many as you need):

1. **Notice:** Circle the phrase "schooled by bitter experience." What
   is odd about a man calling his own unfaithfulness an *education*?
2. **Question:** Turn that oddness into a question the text does not
   answer directly. (Hint: ask what "bitter experience" conceals.)
3. **Connect:** Link it to the earlier list — "unintelligent, narrow,
   inelegant." What pattern do you see in how Gurov explains himself?
4. **Argue:** Write one claim of ≤30 words, then the shortest quotation
   that proves it.

## Independent practice

On a fresh copy of the four paragraphs (or in your notebook with line
references):

1. Mark at least **six** notices — two about word choice, two about
   how a character is presented, two about what is left out.
2. Turn your three strongest notices into Move-2 questions.
3. Choose one question and run Moves 3–4: connect it to another
   passage detail and write a claim with quoted evidence (≤40 words
   for the claim).

## Applied task

Write a 5–7 sentence paragraph answering: *What does the opening want
the reader to feel about Gurov — sympathy, judgment, curiosity, or
something mixed — and how do you know?* Use at least two quotations.
End with one genuine uncertainty: something the opening leaves open
that you will watch for as you read on (RL.11-12.1: "where the text
leaves matters uncertain").

## Exit check

In your notebook, without looking back at the lesson:

1. Name the four moves in order.
2. Write one Move-2 question about *any* text you read this week
   (it does not have to be Chekhov).
3. One sentence: what is the difference between a notice and evidence?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult may read the passage aloud while you
  follow with a pen; chunk it one paragraph at a time. A free
  LibriVox recording of the Garnett translation is listed in the
  resource pack for listen-while-reading.
- **Language access:** "staid," "inelegant," "fortnight" are glossed
  in the margin of your copy before you begin (staid = serious and
  unadventurous; fortnight = two weeks). Lens terms arrive in
  Lessons 02–05, not here.
- **Alternate response mode:** annotations may be voice memos
  (quotation read aloud, then the thought) or a two-column notebook:
  *quotation* | *what it makes me ask*.
- **Extension:** run the four-move cycle on the *second* paragraph
  alone and argue whether "the lady with the dog" is Gurov's label,
  the town's label, or the narrator's — and why the difference
  matters.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners often annotate *plot* ("Gurov sees a lady") instead of
  *language*. Redirect: "What did Chekhov *choose* here that a
  different writer would not have?"
- Learners treat Move-4 claims as summaries. Require the quotation;
  if the claim can be made without quoting, it is too big.
- Some learners will conclude "Chekhov is sexist" from Gurov's lines.
  Hold that thought for Lesson 02 — the feminist lens will test it
  properly. For now: *who says it* is always part of *what it means*.
