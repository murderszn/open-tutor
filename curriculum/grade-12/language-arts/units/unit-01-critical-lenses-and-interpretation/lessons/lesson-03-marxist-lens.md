# Lesson 03 — The Marxist Lens: Who Has What, and What It Costs

**Goal:** You will state the Marxist lens's core questions, apply them
to Gurov and to the valley-of-ashes passage from *The Great Gatsby*,
and chart where the Marxist and feminist readings agree and collide.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 6–8 include the workshop).
**Materials:** the story text (parts I–III), the *Gatsby* passage
below, notebook, the agree/collide chart template (draw it yourself:
two columns, three rows).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. List everything the story tells you about Gurov's *money and work*:
   his job, his houses, his leisure. (Bank employee in Moscow; a house
   in Moscow and one near town; a fortnight's holiday in Yalta.)
2. List everything it tells you about Anna's husband's *money and
   work*. What is the class difference between the two men?

## Teaching: the Marxist lens (15 minutes)

Where the feminist lens asks about gender and power, the **Marxist
lens** asks about class and economic power: who owns, who labors, whose
leisure depends on whose work, and how money shapes what characters
can want. Its core questions:

1. **What are the characters' economic positions** — and what does
   money let each of them do or prevent them from doing?
2. **Whose labor is visible, and whose is invisible?** Who serves,
   cleans, drives, waits — and does the text notice them?
3. **How does class shape desire?** Are the characters' longings
   (love, freedom, escape) available to everyone, or priced?
4. **What does the text treat as natural about wealth and poverty?**
   Does it question the economic order or take it for granted?

The same two cautions as Lesson 02 apply: the lens is not "find the
rich villain" — it asks how *the economic order* structures the story;
and every claim still needs quoted evidence.

## Worked example 1: Gurov's fortnight (modeled)

*Lens questions 1 and 3 on "The Lady with the Dog."*

Gurov "had by then been a fortnight at Yalta." A fortnight's seaside
holiday, a pavilion, the freedom to "take an interest in new arrivals"
— the affair is *staged* by leisure. Gurov is a Moscow bank employee
with a house and servants implied; Anna's husband is a provincial
official whom Anna herself calls a flunkey. Neither lover works for
their meeting: the resort, the gardens, the square are all spaces of
bourgeois leisure, maintained by labor the story never names.

*Claim:* the affair is economically conditioned before it is
romantic: only people with Gurov's income get a Yalta fortnight in
which to "take an interest in new arrivals." *Evidence:* "who had by
then been a fortnight at Yalta, and so was fairly at home there, had
begun to take an interest in new arrivals."

*The lens turn:* re-read "If she is here alone without a husband or
friends, it wouldn't be amiss to make her acquaintance" — through the
Marxist lens, this is not only male entitlement (the feminist reading)
but *consumer* entitlement: a man of means surveying the resort's
offerings. The two readings do not cancel; they stack. Lenses *agree*
here: both find power operating in the same sentence.

## Worked example 2: where the lenses collide (modeled)

*The valley of ashes — a reread passage from* The Great Gatsby *(1925),
Chapter 2. Public domain in the USA (Project Gutenberg ebook 64317):*

> About halfway between West Egg and New York the motor road hastily
> joins the railroad and runs beside it for a quarter of a mile, so as
> to shrink away from a certain desolate area of land. This is a valley
> of ashes — a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges
> and hills and grotesque gardens; where ashes take the forms of houses
> and chimneys and rising smoke and, finally, with a transcendent
> effort, of ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through
> the powdery air.

*Marxist reading (question 2 — whose labor is visible?):* the "ash-grey
men" are laborers literally *made of* the waste of industry — "already
crumbling." The passage's horror is economic: these men are consumed
by the system that enriches West Egg. The road "shrinks away" — wealth
refuses to look.

*Feminist reading (question 4 — how are women described?):* Myrtle
Wilson, the valley's central woman, is introduced pages later
primarily through her body and her affair with Tom — desired,
purchased (the apartment, the dog), and ultimately killed. The feminist
lens asks whether the novel lets Myrtle be a person or keeps her a
*symptom* of the valley.

*The collision:* the Marxist lens centers the ash-grey men — class is
the passage's subject. The feminist lens notes that when women enter
this economic story, they enter as bodies to be bought. Neither lens
is "wrong"; they *disagree about what matters most* in the passage.
Your job in the essay is not to pick a winner but to stage the
disagreement honestly and judge which reading the *evidence* supports
better — on *this* passage, for *this* claim.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Apply lens question 4 to Gurov's Moscow life (story part III):

1. The story shows Gurov returning to "the bank," his club, dinner
   parties — and finding them all hollow. Does the story *question*
   his wealthy Moscow life, or does it treat his dissatisfaction as
   the tragedy while the servants who run that life stay invisible?
2. Find one detail of Gurov's Moscow routine that the Marxist lens
   makes newly strange. Write the detail, the lens question, and a
   2-sentence reading.

## Independent practice

1. Draw the agree/collide chart. **Row 1:** Gurov's opening reflection
   ("it wouldn't be amiss to make her acquaintance"). **Row 2:** Anna's
   confession scene in Yalta (she calls herself guilty; he kisses her
   "like a man in a hurry"). **Row 3:** the valley-of-ashes passage.
   For each row, write one feminist claim and one Marxist claim with a
   short quotation each — then mark the row **AGREE** (lenses point the
   same way) or **COLLIDE** (lenses emphasize different things) with
   one sentence of reasoning.
2. Write a paragraph (6–8 sentences) answering: *On the
   valley-of-ashes passage, which lens produces the stronger reading,
   and what is your criterion for "stronger"?* (Possible criteria:
   more textual evidence, explains more of the passage's strangeness,
   connects to the novel's larger design.)

## Applied task

Choose **one** row of your chart marked COLLIDE. Write 8–10 sentences
that stage the collision fairly: state the feminist reading with its
best quotation, state the Marxist reading with its best quotation,
then render a verdict — not "both are valid" (too easy), but *which
reading accounts for more of the passage's language, and what the
losing reading still sees that the winner misses*. End by naming one
question neither lens asks that a third lens might.

## Exit check

1. State the Marxist lens's four core questions from memory.
2. In one sentence each: one place the feminist and Marxist readings
   of Chekhov AGREE, and one place they COLLIDE.
3. What does "lenses stack" mean, in your own words?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the *Gatsby* passage is short — read it aloud
  twice before annotating; the adult glosses "transcendent" (beyond
  the ordinary) and "grotesque" (strangely distorted).
- **Language access:** pre-teach *bourgeois* (the middle/upper class
  that owns or manages capital — recognition level), *proletariat*
  (wage laborers — recognition level), *material conditions* (the
  economic facts that shape a life).
- **Alternate response mode:** the chart may be built as a recorded
  spoken comparison (feminist voice, then Marxist voice, then judge).
- **Extension:** apply the Marxist lens to the story's ending — the
  secret Moscow meetings at the hotel. Who pays? Whose travel is
  described? Write the reading in 10 sentences.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners may turn the lens into biography ("Marx would say…") or
  into politics ("this proves capitalism is bad"). Redirect to the
  questions: *the lens is a method, not a verdict.*
- "Agree/collide" tempts false balance. Insist on the verdict with a
  criterion — "stronger *by what measure*?" is the unit's key
  critical move.
- Some learners will moralize Gurov's adultery instead of analyzing
  its conditions. Acknowledge the moral question, then ask: "What
  does the *lens* see that the moral judgment doesn't?"
