# Lesson 04 — The Postcolonial and New-Historicist Lenses: Empire, History, and Fit

**Goal:** You will apply the postcolonial lens to Kipling's "The White
Man's Burden" and the new-historicist lens to Chekhov's 1899 context,
and you will judge — with evidence — which lens fits which text best.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 9–10 include the workshop).
**Materials:** the poem below, the story text, notebook. Adult note:
the poem contains racist imperial language; it is studied here as the
*object* of a critical lens, never as a model. Preview the teaching
note before assigning.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. In one sentence: what is the difference between *history as
   background* ("this happened in 1899") and *history as a lens*
   ("1899 changes what this line means")?
2. Write one question you would ask about *who is not allowed to speak*
   in a text.

## Teaching: two lenses about power and time (15 minutes)

The **postcolonial lens** asks how a text represents empire — the
colonizer and the colonized — and, crucially, *who gets to speak*:

1. **Who speaks, and who is spoken for?** Are colonized peoples
   given voices, or only described?
2. **How are the colonized characterized** — as fully human, or as
   "other": childlike, savage, silent, in need of rule?
3. **Whose perspective does the text treat as normal?** Who is "we"
   and who is "they"?
4. **Does the text question empire, or justify it?** (And: can it do
   both at once?)

The **new-historicist lens** asks what a text and its historical moment
do to *each other*: not "what was happening in 1899" as trivia, but
which specific historical pressures the text absorbs, answers, or
resists:

1. **What was happening** when this was written that its first readers
   would have known without being told?
2. **What does the text take for granted** that its moment took for
   granted — and what does it quietly challenge?
3. **How does the text circulate power** — who publishes, who reads,
   who is flattered or threatened by it?

The lesson's second skill is **lens fit**: not every lens fits every
text equally. Part of critical reading is judging *which tool for which
job* — and saying why, with evidence. A forced lens reading is worse
than no lens at all.

## The poem

Rudyard Kipling, "The White Man's Burden" (1899). Public domain. Text
with scholarly notes at *English Literature: Victorians and Moderns*
(eCampusOntario Pressbooks), chapter 66:
<https://ecampusontario.pressbooks.pub/reduxenglit/chapter/the-white-mans-burden/>
(verified 2026-10-15). Written in 1899 as the United States took
control of the Philippines — the poem addresses an empire directly.

> Take up the White Man's burden —
> Send forth the best ye breed —
> Go bind your sons to exile
> To serve your captives' need;
> To wait in heavy harness,
> On fluttered folk and wild —
> Your new-caught, sullen peoples,
> Half devil and half child.
>
> Take up the White Man's burden —
> In patience to abide,
> To veil the threat of terror
> And check the show of pride;
> By open speech and simple,
> An hundred times made plain
> To seek another's profit,
> And work another's gain.
>
> Take up the White Man's burden —
> The savage wars of peace —
> Fill full the mouth of Famine
> And bid the sickness cease;
> And when your goal is nearest
> The end for others sought,
> Watch Sloth and heathen Folly
> Bring all your hope to nought.
>
> Take up the White Man's burden —
> No tawdry rule of kings,
> But toil of serf and sweeper —
> The tale of common things.
> The ports ye shall not enter,
> The roads ye shall not tread,
> Go make them with your living,
> And mark them with your dead!
>
> Take up the White man's burden —
> And reap his old reward:
> The blame of those ye better,
> The hate of those ye guard —
> The cry of hosts ye humour
> (Ah, slowly!) toward the light: —
> "Why brought ye us from bondage,
> "Our loved Egyptian night?"
>
> Take up the White Man's burden —
> Ye dare not stoop to less —
> Nor call too loud on Freedom
> To cloak your weariness;
> By all ye cry or whisper,
> By all ye leave or do,
> The silent, sullen peoples
> Shall weigh your Gods and you.
>
> Take up the White Man's burden —
> Have done with childish days —
> The lightly proffered laurel,
> The easy, ungrudged praise.
> Comes now, to search your manhood
> Through all the thankless years,
> Cold-edged with dear-bought wisdom,
> The judgment of your peers!

## Worked example 1: who speaks? (modeled — postcolonial)

*Lens questions 1–3 on the poem's first and sixth stanzas.*

"Your new-caught, sullen peoples, / Half devil and half child." The
colonized are *described* — never quoted, never named, never given a
single verb of their own except sullenness. The one direct speech in
the poem — "Why brought ye us from bondage, / Our loved Egyptian
night?" — is *reported* by the imperial speaker, framed as ingratitude.
The final stanza seals it: "The silent, sullen peoples / Shall weigh
your Gods and you" — they are *silent*, yet they judge; the poem needs
their judgment and refuses their voice in the same breath.

*Claim:* the poem performs the colonial relation it describes: the
speaker claims the right to speak *for* empire's subjects while
displaying them as voiceless. *Evidence:* seven stanzas of imperative
address ("Take up," "Send forth," "Go bind") to "ye" — and zero lines
spoken by the "peoples" themselves; the single quoted cry is mediated
by the speaker's "humour."

*The lens's honesty check:* does the poem *know* what it's doing? "Nor
call too loud on Freedom / To cloak your weariness" admits the
mission's self-interest — the poem contains the seed of its own
critique. A strong lens reading reports that too (RL.11-12.6: stated
vs. meant cuts both ways).

## Worked example 2: 1899 as a lens (modeled — new-historicist)

*Lens questions 1–2 on Chekhov.*

Three verifiable facts about 1899 that the story's first readers knew
without being told: (a) Chekhov wrote the story in Yalta, where he
lived for his health — the resort in the story is the author's own
exile; (b) divorce in imperial Russia was difficult and socially
ruinous, especially for women — Anna's terror at being "found out"
is not melodrama but material fact; (c) the story appeared in
*Russkaya Mysl* (Russian Thought), a liberal journal — its first
readers were the educated class Gurov belongs to, reading about
themselves.

*Claim:* the new-historicist lens makes the story's *ending* —
famously unresolved, the lovers with "the most complicated and
difficult part" still ahead — legible as historical, not just
artistic, choice: in 1899 Russia there *was* no available resolution
for two married lovers, so the open ending is the honest one.
*Evidence:* Anna's panic about her husband discovering the affair
paired with the historical fact of restrictive divorce; the story
refuses the consolation its moment could not supply.

*Lens-fit judgment (modeled):* the new-historicist lens fits Chekhov
deeply — the story is saturated with its moment. The postcolonial
lens fits Kipling's poem deeply — empire is its subject. Crossed over,
each lens still finds *something* (a postcolonial reader can ask about
Crimea as imperial periphery in Chekhov; a new-historicist can date
Kipling's imperial occasion), but each is thinner there. Fit is a
judgment you defend, not a fact you discover.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Judge lens fit for this pairing: **the feminist lens on Kipling's
poem.**

1. Apply feminist question 4 (how are women described?): find every
   reference to women in the poem. What do you find?
2. Now apply feminist question 1 (who speaks?): the poem's "ye" and
   "your sons" — who is the implied audience, and who is excluded
   from it?
3. Verdict in 3–4 sentences: does the feminist lens *fit* this poem?
   Distinguish "the lens finds little" from "the lens finds the
   poem's masculinist address itself" — which is it, and what is your
   evidence?

## Independent practice

1. **Postcolonial close read:** choose any two stanzas of the poem.
   For each, write: (a) who is "we" and who is "they"; (b) one
   characterizing phrase for the colonized and what it assumes;
   (c) whether the stanza questions or justifies empire — with a
   quoted phrase.
2. **New-historicist notes:** list three verifiable facts about
   1899 Russia relevant to the story (use the resource pack's
   references; cite each). For each fact, write one sentence on how
   it changes the reading of a specific passage.
3. **Fit paragraph (8–10 sentences):** *Which lens — postcolonial or
   new-historicist — produces the stronger reading of "The Lady with
   the Dog," and why?* State your criterion for "stronger," give each
   lens its best evidence, and name what the weaker lens still sees.

## Applied task

A classmate says: "The postcolonial lens doesn't fit Chekhov at all —
there's no colony in the story." Write a 10–12 sentence response that
(a) grants what is true in the objection, (b) tests it against one
specific passage (consider: Yalta/Crimea as imperial resort space;
Gurov's "Oriental" fantasies; who serves whom in the story's
background), and (c) concludes with a defended fit judgment: *thin
but real*, *forced*, or *surprisingly strong* — with the evidence
that decides it. (This is the unit's hardest move: you are arguing
about the *tool*, not just using it.)

## Exit check

1. State each lens's core questions from memory (postcolonial: 4;
   new-historicist: 3).
2. One sentence: what is "lens fit," and why does it matter?
3. Which lens fits Kipling's poem best, and what single quotation
   proves it?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** read the poem aloud twice — once for sound
  (the hammering imperatives), once for sense. The adult previews
  "heathen," "sullen," "tawdry" before the first read.
- **Language access:** pre-teach *empire/colony*, *subaltern* (a
  person or group denied a voice in the dominant discourse —
  recognition level), *discourse* (the language and assumptions a
  culture uses to talk about something).
- **Adult supervision note:** the poem's imperial racism is the
  *object* of analysis. The adult frames the lesson explicitly: "We
  are studying how this poem *thinks*, not adopting its thinking."
  If the learner has family history touched by empire, the adult
  invites — never requires — that perspective into the discussion.
- **Alternate response mode:** the fit paragraph may be delivered as
  a structured debate with the adult (claim, evidence, rebuttal).
- **Extension:** read the poem's original subtitle ("The United
  States and the Philippine Islands") and research the 1899
  Philippine-American War's first year; write 10 sentences on how
  that occasion sharpens — or complicates — the postcolonial reading.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners may treat the postcolonial lens as "calling Kipling
  racist" and stop. Push past verdict to *mechanism*: "Show me
  *how* the poem silences — which lines, which grammar?"
- New historicism tempts trivia-dumping ("In 1899 X happened").
  Require the *interpretive payoff*: "…and therefore this passage
  means ___."
- The applied task tempts both-sides mush ("every lens fits a
  little"). Require the verdict word — *thin*, *forced*, or
  *strong* — and the evidence that earns it.
