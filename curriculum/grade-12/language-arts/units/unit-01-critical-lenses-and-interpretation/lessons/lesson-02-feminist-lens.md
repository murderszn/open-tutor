# Lesson 02 — The Feminist Lens: Who Gets to Be a Person?

**Goal:** You will state the feminist lens's core questions in your
own words and apply them to "The Lady with the Dog," producing an
evidence-based reading of how the story presents its women.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 4–5 include the workshop).
**Materials:** the story text (parts I–II read), the illustration in
`../assets/critical-lenses-illustration.png`, notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Write down everything the story has told you *about Anna Sergeyevna*
   so far — not what happens to her, but what you know of her inner
   life. (Most learners find this list very short. That shortness is
   the lesson.)
2. Who tells this story — a character, or a narrator outside it? How
   do you know?

## Teaching: what a lens is (15 minutes)

A **critical lens** is a set of questions you bring to a text *on
purpose*. Lesson 01 taught you to notice and ask; a lens tells you
*what kinds of questions are worth asking*. The lens does not replace
evidence — it directs your attention, and then the text still has to
answer.

The **feminist lens** asks how a text constructs gender: who has power,
whose inner life the reader is allowed inside, and what the text
assumes about what men and women are. Its core questions:

1. **Who speaks, and who is spoken about?** Whose thoughts does the
   narrator report? Whose remain closed?
2. **Who has agency** — the power to act and choose — and who mainly
   *is acted upon*?
3. **What does the text assume is natural** about men and women? Does
   it confirm or unsettle those assumptions?
4. **How are women described** — as people with inner lives, or as
   objects to be looked at, judged, labeled?

Two cautions before you apply it. First, the feminist lens is not "find
the sexism and condemn the book." It asks what the text *does* with
gender — a text can *show* a sexist worldview in order to *expose* it.
Second, a lens reading must still be argued from the text (Move 4).
The lens tells you where to look; the quotations do the proving.

## Worked example 1: who speaks? (modeled)

*Lens question 1 applied to the opening.*

Anna appears in the first two paragraphs — but count what we learn:
she is "fair-haired," "of medium height," wears "a *béret*," walks
with "a white Pomeranian dog." Every detail is *visible from the
outside*. Then Gurov "reflected": "If she is here alone without a
husband or friends, it wouldn't be amiss to make her acquaintance."
The verb is his; the plan is his; Anna has no reported thought at all.

*Claim:* in the opening, Anna exists as an object of Gurov's
perception, not as a consciousness. *Evidence:* four paragraphs give
Anna's appearance ("fair-haired young lady of medium height, wearing
a *béret*") and Gurov's interior monologue ("it wouldn't be amiss to
make her acquaintance"), but not one sentence of Anna's inner life.

Notice the discipline: the claim is about *the text's construction*
(who the narrator lets us inside), not about whether Gurov is a bad
person. That is what makes it a lens reading rather than a moral
judgment.

## Worked example 2: agency and the label (modeled)

*Lens questions 2 and 4 applied together.*

"Every one called her simply the lady with the dog." The town gives
her a label built from two possessions: a dog, and her status as *the*
lady — defined by being looked at. She is "walking alone" — the
repetition ("always wearing the same *béret*, and always with the same
white dog") fixes her as a *sight*, the way a landmark is fixed.

*Claim:* the label "the lady with the dog" performs the story's gender
economy in miniature: the woman is named by what accompanies her, in a
sentence whose subject is "every one" — the watching town. *Evidence:*
"no one knew who she was, and every one called her simply the lady
with the dog."

*The turn the lens enables:* now re-read Gurov's wife through the same
questions. She *does* get interior-adjacent detail — "as she said of
herself, intellectual" — but only to be dismissed: "he secretly
considered her unintelligent, narrow, inelegant." The wife speaks; the
text lets Gurov's contempt have the last word. Two women, two
techniques, one pattern: the narrative filters women through male
judgment. Whether Chekhov *endorses* that filtering is a question for
your essay — the lens has done its job by making the pattern visible.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Apply lens question 3 (*what does the text assume is natural?*) to this
sentence:

> "He had begun being unfaithful to her long ago — had been unfaithful
> to her often, and, probably on that account, almost always spoke ill
> of women, and when they were talked about in his presence, used to
> call them 'the lower race.'"

Prompts:

1. What does the phrase "on that account" assume — what cause-and-effect
   about men, women, and guilt is treated as obvious?
2. The narrator reports this flatly, without condemnation. Does the
   flatness *confirm* Gurov's view or *expose* it? What in the wording
   supports your answer? (Hint: compare "schooled by bitter experience"
   from Lesson 01 — whose vocabulary is "schooled"?)
3. Write a 2–3 sentence lens reading with one quotation.

## Independent practice

Read story part II (the affair's beginning in Yalta) with the four
lens questions in the margin. Then:

1. Find **two** passages where Anna is described from the outside and
   **one** passage where she speaks or thinks. Copy all three with
   line references.
2. Write a paragraph (6–8 sentences): *What does the story let the
   reader know about Anna's inner life by the end of part II, and what
   does it withhold?* Use at least two quotations.
3. Error-analysis: a classmate claims "the feminist lens proves Chekhov
   hated women." In 3–4 sentences, explain the error — what the lens
   actually claims, and what evidence would be needed to claim authorial
   hatred.

**Task:** In 3–4 sentences, explain what the image *claims* about

## Exit check

1. Write the feminist lens's four core questions from memory (your own
   wording is fine).
2. One sentence: what is the difference between "Gurov is sexist"
   (a moral judgment) and "the narrative filters women through Gurov's
   judgment" (a lens reading)?
3. Name one passage from today's reading where the lens changed what
   you saw.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** parts I–II may be read across sessions 3–5; the
  adult reads the densest paragraphs aloud first, then the learner
  re-reads silently with the lens questions.
- **Language access:** pre-teach *agency* (power to act/choose),
  *patriarchy* (a social order privileging men — recognition level),
  *objectification* (treating a person as a thing to be looked at).
  Keep the question bank visible during all annotation.
- **Alternate response mode:** the applied task may be answered as a
  recorded 90-second spoken response; annotations may be voice memos.
- **Extension:** research (with the adult) one fact about women's legal
  status in 1890s Russia (marriage, divorce, property) and write a
  paragraph on how it reframes one Anna passage. Cite the source.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners collapse "lens reading" into "the author's opinion." Keep
  returning to: *the lens describes what the text does; the author's
  attitude is a separate claim needing separate evidence.*
- Learners may treat Anna as a real person to rescue rather than a
  construction to analyze. Redirect with question 1: "What does the
  *narrator* let you know?"
- The illustration task tempts pure description. Require the *claim*
  sentence ("the image argues that…") before the qualifying sentence.
