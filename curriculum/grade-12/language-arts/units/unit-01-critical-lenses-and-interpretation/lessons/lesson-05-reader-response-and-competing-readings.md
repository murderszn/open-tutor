# Lesson 05 — Reader-Response and Competing Readings: Your Reading Counts (and Must Be Defended)

**Goal:** You will use reader-response journaling to make your own
response part of the evidence, reread Daisy's speech through two
lenses, and stage two competing readings of one passage with a
defended verdict. **Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 11–13 include
prep and seminar). **Materials:** the story text (complete), the
*Gatsby* passage below, reader-response journal, notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Write 3 sentences: how did the story's ending make you *feel* —
   and is that feeling *evidence* of anything about the story, or
   just about you?
2. What is one reading of "The Lady with the Dog" from Lessons 02–04
   that you *resist* — and can you say why in one sentence?

## Teaching: the reader is part of the circuit (15 minutes)

The **reader-response lens** starts from a simple observation: a text
does nothing until someone reads it, and different readers — bringing
different lives — notice different things. Its core questions:

1. **What is your honest response** — feeling, confusion, resistance,
   recognition — and *where exactly* in the text did it start?
2. **How might a different reader respond differently** — a reader
   from another time, place, or life — and what in the text allows
   both responses?
3. **What does the text *do* to you as you read** — does it recruit
   you, repel you, implicate you? (Chekhov's narrator, for instance,
   keeps making you complicit with Gurov's gaze before you notice.)
4. **Where does your response need the text, and where does it go
   beyond it?** A response is evidence *about the reading*, not a
   free pass: "I felt X *because* the text does Y" is criticism; "I
   felt X" alone is a diary.

The lens's discipline is question 4. Reader-response is not "any
reading goes" — it is *your* reading, *defended*: the text must still
be quoted, and a competing reader must still be answered.

That gives the unit's capstone move: **competing readings**. Take one
passage. Produce the strongest feminist reading and the strongest
Marxist (or new-historicist) reading. Let them disagree. Then judge —
with a stated criterion — which the evidence supports better, and say
what the losing reading still sees. Lessons 03–04 practiced the parts;
now you assemble them.

## The reread passage

Daisy's speech after her daughter's birth — *The Great Gatsby*,
Chapter 1 (1925). Public domain in the USA (Project Gutenberg ebook
64317; passage re-verified 2026-10-15):

> She told me it was a girl, and so I turned my head away and wept.
> "All right," I said, "I'm glad it's a girl. And I hope she'll be a
> fool — that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a
> beautiful little fool."

## Worked example 1: reader-response journal (modeled)

*On the story's unresolved ending.*

**My response:** I felt cheated — then, a page later, relieved. The
cheated feeling started at the final paragraph's refusal to resolve:
no divorce, no plan, just "the most complicated and difficult part"
still ahead. The relief came a breath later, when I realized a tidy
ending would have lied — in 1899, for these two people, there *was*
no tidy ending available.

**Where the text earns it:** the ending withholds exactly what
romance plots promise (resolution) and gives instead what the story
has been building: two people who finally see each other clearly and
have nowhere to go. My "cheated" feeling is evidence *of the story's
design* — it trained me to expect a romance plot, then refused it.

**A different reader:** a reader in 1899, for whom divorce meant ruin,
might feel not cheated but *recognized* — the ending names their
world. A reader who has lived an impossible love might feel the
opposite of cheated: *seen*. The text allows all three because it
refuses to tell you how to feel — the refusal is the technique.

**Where I go beyond the text:** if I conclude "Chekhov believes love
always fails," I have left the evidence. The story claims only this
much: *for these two, now, it is beginning and it is hard.* The
discipline is staying inside what the words support.

## Worked example 2: competing readings, staged (modeled)

*Passage: Daisy's "beautiful little fool."*

**Reading A — feminist lens.** Daisy has just survived childbirth to
learn her child is a girl — in 1922, a girl inherits the world's
constraints. "The best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful
little fool" is Daisy stating the patriarchal bargain plainly:
beauty without thought is the only safe strategy she can imagine for
her daughter. The weeping is grief, and the sentence is indictment —
Daisy *sees* the trap. Evidence: "I turned my head away and wept"
frames the line as sorrow, not celebration; "in *this* world" marks
the world's terms as the problem.

**Reading B — new-historicist lens.** In 1925, the "flapper" era's
public argument was precisely whether the modern woman should be
clever or charming — and Daisy's line lands inside that live debate,
not above it. The novel's first readers would hear a fashionable
cynicism, the era's own voice, and might miss the indictment Reading
A finds. Evidence: the line's epigrammatic polish ("beautiful little
fool" is built to be quoted) matches the era's appetite for
sophisticated despair; Nick reports it without comment, as period
color.

**The staging.** The readings collide on *Daisy's awareness*: A says
she sees the trap; B says she *is* the trap's voice, polished for the
era. **Verdict (criterion: which reading explains more of the
sentence's language):** Reading A is stronger — "wept" and "in this
world" are doing grief-work that B's "period color" cannot absorb;
B explains the polish but not the tears. **What B still sees:** that
Daisy's indictment arrives in the very language of the world it
indicts — she cannot imagine an alternative, only a prettier trap.
The losing reading keeps the winner honest.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Stage a mini-competition on Anna's confession scene (Yalta: she calls
herself guilty and low; he kisses her):

1. Write the strongest **feminist** reading in 3 sentences with one
   quotation. (Hint: who names her guilt — Anna, or the world's
   vocabulary speaking through Anna?)
2. Write the strongest **reader-response** reading in 3 sentences:
   your honest response, where it started, and what a different
   reader might feel.
3. Verdict in 2 sentences with a stated criterion.

## Independent practice

1. **Reader-response journal (20 minutes):** re-read the story's final
   two pages. Write: (a) your honest response in 5+ sentences;
   (b) the exact textual moment each feeling started; (c) how a
   reader from 1899 and a reader your age might respond differently,
   with one textual reason each; (d) one place your response goes
   beyond the evidence — and the line that pulls you back.
2. **Full staging:** choose one passage (the opening, the confession
   scene, or the ending). Write two competing readings (two different
   lenses), 6–8 sentences each with ≥2 quotations each, then a
   verdict paragraph: criterion stated, winner named, what the loser
   still sees. This is the direct rehearsal for the essay.

## Applied task — seminar prep

Tomorrow's seminar question: **"Whose reading wins?"** Prepare a
one-page sheet:

- Your claim about one passage (≤30 words).
- Three quotations that prove it, each with a 1-sentence gloss.
- Two questions that *probe* a competing reading (not "don't you
  agree?" but "how does your reading handle ___?").
- One sentence you will say if the discussion stalls: your strongest
  piece of counter-evidence against your *own* claim.

(SL.11-12.1: come prepared; build on others' ideas; probe reasoning;
synthesize. The adult leads; your sheet is your ticket in.)

## Exit check

1. State the reader-response lens's four core questions from memory.
2. In your own words: why is "I felt X" not yet criticism, and what
   turns it into criticism?
3. Name your verdict criterion from the guided practice — the actual
   words you would use to judge two competing readings.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the final pages are the story's hardest; the
  adult reads them aloud once before the journal work.
- **Language access:** pre-teach *complicit* (involved with others
  in a questionable act — here, the reader's entanglement in Gurov's
  gaze), *recruit* (the text winning the reader to its side).
- **Alternate response mode:** the journal may be a voice memo; the
  seminar may be a written dialogue with the adult.
- **Extension:** stage a *third* reading of your chosen passage with
  the postcolonial lens; write 8 sentences on whether three lenses
  clarify or muddy the passage — and why.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- "Any reading goes" is the classic reader-response failure. Enforce
  question 4 relentlessly: *show me the line that earns the feeling.*
- Learners stage straw men ("the feminist reading says men are bad").
  Require each reading's *strongest* form — steelman before verdict.
- Verdicts drift to "both are valid." That is allowed only as a
  *conclusion argued from evidence* (the readings genuinely
  complement), never as a dodge. Ask: "What would change your mind?"
