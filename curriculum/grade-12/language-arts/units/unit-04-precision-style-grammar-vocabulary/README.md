# Unit 04 — Precision Style: Grammar and Vocabulary Refinement

**Grade 12 · Language Arts · Advanced literature, writing, and communication pathway · Weeks 16–19 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-11-03 for issue #56; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

Good writing is not fancy writing. It is writing in which every
sentence does exactly the job its writer intended — no word doing
two jobs badly, no clause hiding the claim, no vocabulary word
pretending to mean more than it does. This unit teaches the last
revision skill the year's essays need: **precision style**. It
answers three questions a twelfth-grade writer must be able to
answer about any sentence of their own:

1. **Is this usage settled, or contested — and what does a
   reference work actually say?** (Week 16)
2. **What does this sentence's shape do to its meaning?**
   (Week 17)
3. **Does every word here earn its place, and can I prove what
   it means?** (Weeks 18–19)

The unit runs in four moves, following the track
scope-and-sequence (weeks 16–19):

1. **Usage as convention** — contested usage and how reference
   works resolve it (L.11-12.1.a–b).
2. **Syntax for effect** — varying sentence structure
   deliberately, and applying syntax study to complex texts
   (L.11-12.3.a).
3. **Academic and domain-specific vocabulary** — context clues,
   word-change patterns, etymology, and dictionary verification
   (L.11-12.4.a–d, L.11-12.6).
4. **Style revision workshop** — revising the Unit 01 and Unit 02
   essays for precision, and building a personal style sheet the
   learner carries into Units 05–08.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **when
you change a sentence, what exactly changes — the grammar, the
emphasis, the claim, or all three — and how do you know?**

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–03 and the track's entry skills (verified in the
week-1 diagnostic; insecure skills are re-taught in use):

- Cite strong, thorough textual evidence for explicit and
  inferential claims about literature (RL.11-12.1).
- Analyze word choice and syntax for impact on meaning and tone
  (RL.11-12.4, L.11-12.5.a).
- Write organized analytical paragraphs and essays with claims,
  evidence, and counter-readings (W.11-12.1, W.11-12.9.a).
- Know the eight parts of speech and basic sentence structure
  (subject, predicate, phrase, clause, sentence types) — the
  adult-side guides `resources/language_arts_parts_of_speech.md`
  and `resources/language_arts_sentence_structure.md` are the
  reference for this vocabulary; they are not assigned as
  grade-12 instruction.
- Have a Unit 01 lens paragraph and a Unit 02 comparative passage
  available for revision (drafts, not finished essays — the
  unit revises real work, not invented sentences alone).

The unit does **not** assume fluency with: judging contested
usage against a reference work instead of a remembered rule;
describing a sentence's structure and predicting its rhetorical
effect; diagnosing nominalization, redundancy, and weak verbs in
one's own prose; using morphology and etymology to infer a word's
meaning and then verifying it; or keeping a personal style sheet
— those are this unit's new content.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Resolve at least six contested-usage cases (from the unit's
   case bank) by stating the remembered rule, what a reference
   work actually says, and the choice for a stated audience —
   distinguishing settled errors from genuine contests.
   (L.11-12.1.a–b; track objectives 12, 8)
2. Revise a paragraph by varying syntax deliberately: produce at
   least one short sentence used for emphasis, one periodic
   sentence, one parallel structure, and one deliberate passive
   or active choice, each annotated with the effect intended.
   (L.11-12.3.a; track objectives 5, 8)
3. Diagnose and repair at least four sentence-level faults in
   their own writing — nominalization, redundancy, a vague verb,
   and a misplaced modifier or faulty parallel structure —
   naming the fault and showing the repair.
   (L.11-12.1, L.11-12.2, L.11-12.3.a; track objectives 8, 12)
4. Infer the meaning of at least eight unfamiliar academic words
   from context clues, morphology, or etymology, verify each
   meaning in a dictionary, and use each word accurately in an
   original sentence; maintain a personal academic word bank of
   at least 20 entries with part of speech, definition in the
   learner's own words, and an original example.
   (L.11-12.4.a–d, L.11-12.6; track objectives 5, 12)
5. Complete a precision revision of two earlier passages (one
   from Unit 01, one from Unit 02) and a personal style sheet,
   defending at least three revision choices with reference
   evidence or a stated rhetorical effect, in the Precision
   Style Portfolio project. (L.11-12.1–6, W.11-12.5;
   track objectives 8, 10, 12)

## Vocabulary

**Unit terms** (used in lessons and assessed): *contested usage,
reference work, usage note, descriptive vs. prescriptive,
hypercorrection, restrictive vs. nonrestrictive clause, periodic
sentence, loose (cumulative) sentence, parallel structure,
subordination, coordination, end weight, nominalization,
redundancy, active voice, passive voice, misplaced modifier,
dangling modifier, context clue, morphology, prefix, suffix,
root, etymology, connotation, denotation, register, collocation,
academic vocabulary, style sheet.* Recognition terms: *zeugma,
anacoluthon, pleonasm, polysyndeton, asyndeton.*

**The reference-work test, in four steps** (posted on the wall
for the whole unit): (1) What rule do you remember? (2) What does
the reference actually say — and does it call the form standard,
contested, informal, or nonstandard? (3) Who is your audience,
and what does that audience expect? (4) What is your choice, and
what does it cost? A choice without a reason is a guess; a rule
without a source is a rumor.

## Standards notes

Codes and short descriptions verified against the track audit's
source-backed crosswalk (scope-and-sequence §5, verified
2026-10-08 against the official Common Core State Standards for
grades 11–12 at <https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/>).
Descriptions here are paraphrases; this crosswalk is a planning
reference, not a claim of state adoption, accreditation, or
complete alignment.

- **L.11-12.1.a** — apply the understanding that usage is a
  matter of convention, can change over time, and is sometimes
  contested.
- **L.11-12.1.b** — resolve issues of complex or contested
  usage, consulting references as needed.
- **L.11-12.2** — demonstrate command of the conventions of
  standard English capitalization, punctuation, and spelling
  when writing.
- **L.11-12.3.a** — vary syntax for effect, consulting
  references for guidance as needed; apply an understanding of
  syntax to the study of complex texts when reading.
- **L.11-12.4.a** — use context as a clue to the meaning of a
  word or phrase.
- **L.11-12.4.b** — identify and correctly use patterns of
  word changes that indicate different meanings or parts of
  speech (e.g., *conceive, conception, conceivable*).
- **L.11-12.4.c** — consult general and specialized reference
  materials, both print and digital, to find a word's
  pronunciation, precise meaning, part of speech, or etymology.
- **L.11-12.4.d** — verify the preliminary determination of a
  word's meaning by checking the inferred meaning in context or
  in a dictionary.
- **L.11-12.5.a** — interpret figures of speech in context and
  analyze their role in the text (applied here to connotation
  and register choices, not to figurative invention).
- **L.11-12.6** — acquire and use accurately general academic
  and domain-specific words and phrases sufficient for reading,
  writing, speaking, and listening at the college- and
  career-readiness level.
- **W.11-12.5** — develop and strengthen writing as needed by
  planning, revising, editing, and rewriting (the portfolio's
  revision cycle).
- **RL.11-12.4** — determine word and phrase meaning, including
  figurative and connotative senses, and analyze word-choice
  impact on meaning and tone (the reading side of precision:
  Lesson 02's syntax study of complex texts).

## Anchor materials

No literary anchor text is assigned whole in this unit. All
worked sentences are original, written for this unit, so that
every revision can be judged on its language rather than on a
text the learner has not read. The learner's own Unit 01 and
Unit 02 drafts supply the revision material for Lessons 03 and
05 and for the portfolio project. Reference works are consulted,
never reproduced: a learner's dictionary (print or free online),
a usage dictionary, and the adult's style guide of choice (see
the resource pack). No copyrighted text is reproduced in this
unit.

## Differentiation

- **Reading access:** reference-work entries are dense; the
  adult reads the usage note aloud first and the learner
  paraphrases it before judging — never copy the entry as the
  answer. All lesson sentences are short enough to be read
  aloud in one breath.
- **Language access:** the four-step reference test and the
  five-step precision pass are taught as questions, not
  definitions; the learner may answer exit checks orally with
  the adult scribing.
- **Alternate response modes:** revision notes may be marginal
  annotations, voice memos, or a two-column table (before |
  change + reason); the peer style-check may be a written
  exchange.
- **Extensions:** adjudicate a usage dispute in a published
  style guide's archive (adult-selected); trace one word's
  etymology to a primary dictionary entry and report the false
  friends it acquired; revise a paragraph from a public-domain
  essay for syntax variety alone and defend every change.
- **Support:** each lesson names likely misconceptions and the
  teacher guide gives reteaching moves; insecure prerequisite
  skills are re-taught in use, not as a separate unit.
