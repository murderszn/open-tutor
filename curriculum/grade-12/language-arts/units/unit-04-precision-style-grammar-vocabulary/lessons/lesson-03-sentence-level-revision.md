# Lesson 03 — Diagnosing Your Own Sentences

**Goal:** You will diagnose and repair four sentence-level
faults in your own writing — nominalization, redundancy, a vague
verb, and a misplaced modifier or faulty parallel structure —
naming each fault and showing the repair.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 6–7 include the revision clinic).
**Materials:** this lesson, one paragraph from your Unit 01
lens essay (draft, not a finished copy).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Which sentence is clearer, and what exactly did the clearer
version change — a word, a structure, or both?

- A: "The utilization of multiple lenses by the reader makes
  possible the achievement of a deeper understanding of the text."
- B: "A reader who uses several lenses understands the text
  more deeply."

Sentence B changed a structure (nouns turned back into verbs)
and several words, but the real change is one idea: the action
is back in a verb, performed by a person. That is the whole
lesson in miniature.

## Teaching: four faults, four repairs (15 minutes)

**1. Nominalization — a verb hiding inside a noun.**
Nominalization turns an action into a thing: *decide* becomes
*make a decision*, *fail* becomes *experience a failure*,
*understand* becomes *achieve an understanding*. Sometimes the
noun is the right choice (a concept you will discuss for a
page deserves a name). Usually it buries the actor and the
action under extra words. Test: can you rewrite the sentence
with a person doing a verb? If yes, and nothing is lost, do it.

- Faulty: "The implementation of the policy resulted in the
  reduction of complaints."
- Repaired: "When the school implemented the policy,
  complaints fell." (Actor restored, verb restored, six words
  saved — and "fell" is now a claim you could check.)

**2. Redundancy — saying it twice.**
Redundancy is not emphasis; emphasis repeats on purpose and
says so. Redundancy repeats by accident: "due to the fact that"
for *because*, "in the event that" for *if*, "at this point in
time" for *now*, "past history," "completely finished," "the
reason is because" (the *because* repeats *reason*). Test: cut
the phrase. If the sentence means the same thing, the phrase
was padding.

**3. Vague verbs — verbs that refuse to say what happened.**
*Is, are, was, were, has, makes, gives, shows, deals with,
involves, relates to* — these verbs can be correct and still
say almost nothing. "The chapter deals with exile" tells the
reader a topic, not a claim. Replace a vague verb with a
specific one only when you know the specific claim: "The
chapter traces exile from punishment to identity" is not a
synonym for "deals with"; it is a stronger, riskier claim,
and it must be true. Precision is not decoration — a precise
verb commits you. That is why vague verbs feel safe, and why
they weaken an argument.

**4. Misplaced and dangling modifiers; faulty parallelism.**
A modifier belongs next to what it modifies. "Walking home,
the storm broke over the hill" dangles: the storm was not
walking home. Repair by naming the walker: "Walking home, I
watched the storm break over the hill." A misplaced modifier
attaches to the wrong noun: "She served sandwiches to the
children on paper plates" — are the children on the plates?
Repair: "She served the children sandwiches on paper plates."
Faulty parallelism (Lesson 02) belongs here too, because it is
diagnosed the same way: list the items, check their forms
match, fix the odd one out.

**The diagnostic habit.** Read a paragraph backwards,
sentence by sentence, asking four questions in order: (1) Is
the action in a verb? (2) Can I cut a phrase without losing
meaning? (3) Does each verb say what happened? (4) Does every
modifier touch what it modifies, and does every list match in
form? Four questions, one pass, every paragraph you revise in
this unit. The portfolio project (sessions 12–18) uses exactly
this pass.

## Worked example 1: diagnosing a paragraph (modeled)

Original paragraph (written for this lesson):

> "The utilization of irony by the author makes possible the
> communication of criticism without the making of a direct
> statement. Due to the fact that the narrator is unreliable,
> the reader is involved in a process of questioning of the
> stated facts. Walking through the argument, the contradictions
> become visible."

Diagnosis, fault by fault:

1. **Nominalization, twice in sentence 1.** *Utilization* →
   *uses*; *communication* → *communicates*; *making* →
   *makes*. Repaired: "By using irony, the author criticizes
   without stating the criticism directly."
2. **Redundancy in sentence 2.** "Due to the fact that" →
   *because*. "Is involved in a process of questioning of" →
   *questions*. Repaired: "Because the narrator is unreliable,
   the reader questions the stated facts."
3. **Vague verb in sentence 2 (original).** "Is involved in"
   said nothing about what the reader does; *questions* says
   it. Note the repair for fault 2 and fault 3 is the same
   rewrite — faults overlap, and one good verb can cure two.
4. **Dangling modifier in sentence 3.** Who walks through the
   argument? The reader, not the contradictions. Repaired:
   "As the reader works through the argument, the
   contradictions become visible." (Or, stronger: "…the reader
   sees the contradictions.")

Revised paragraph: "By using irony, the author criticizes
without stating the criticism directly. Because the narrator is
unreliable, the reader questions the stated facts. As the
reader works through the argument, the contradictions become
visible." Three sentences, 39 words instead of 55, and every
claim is now checkable against the text.

## Worked example 2: a repair that goes too far (modeled)

Original: "The committee's consideration of the proposal
continued for three weeks, during which time the members
engaged in a review of the budget."

A hasty revision: "The committee considered the proposal for
three weeks and reviewed the budget." That is shorter — and it
loses information: the original says the review happened
*during* the consideration, as part of it, not as a separate
act. A better repair keeps the relation and cuts only the
padding: "The committee considered the proposal for three
weeks, reviewing the budget as it went."

Rule: **a repair must preserve every true claim in the
original.** Cutting words is easy; cutting meaning is a fault
of its own. When a repair changes what the sentence claims,
say so in your revision note and justify it — or restore the
lost claim. The answer key marks repairs that lose meaning as
incomplete, however short they are.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Diagnose and repair this paragraph (original, written for this
lesson). Name at least one fault per sentence; some sentences
have two.

> "The implementation of the new schedule was met with the
> expression of concern by several teachers. At this point in
> time, the reason is because the schedule makes possible the
> scheduling of classes in rooms that are too small. Having
> reviewed the room assignments, the problem was identified as
> a shortage of large rooms."

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. Take your Unit 01 paragraph. Run the four-question pass
   (nominalization, redundancy, vague verb, modifier/parallel
   check) and mark every fault in the margin with its number
   (1–4). Repair at least four faults. Attach a revision note:
   for each repair, the fault, the original phrase, and the
   repaired phrase.
2. Write a sentence that commits each fault on purpose, then
   repair it — a "fault museum" of four sentence pairs. The
   deliberate version proves you can recognize the fault when
   you are not looking for it in your own work.
3. Find one nominalization in your own writing that you will
   *keep*, and defend it in two sentences: what concept does
   the noun name that the verb form would lose? (If you cannot
   defend it, repair it — that is also a correct answer.)

## Applied task

Exchange your repaired Unit 01 paragraph with a partner (or
the adult). The checker may only do two things: (a) flag a
repair that lost or changed a true claim, with the lost claim
named; (b) flag a fault the writer missed, with its number.
The writer then accepts or rebuts each flag in writing, one
sentence each. File the exchanged paragraph and the flags with
the portfolio — the rebuttals are evidence of precision, not
of stubbornness, when they cite the sentence.

## Exit check

In four short lines, one per fault, define nominalization,
redundancy, a vague verb, and a dangling modifier — each
definition followed by a three-to-six-word example of the
fault (not the repair).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** run the pass on one sentence at a time, not a
  whole paragraph; use the four questions as a printed
  checklist. Read the paragraph aloud — nominalizations and
  redundancies are easier to hear than to see.
- **Alternate response mode:** dictate the revision notes;
  mark faults with colored underlines (one color per fault)
  instead of numbers.
- **Extension:** diagnose a paragraph from a public-domain
  essay (adult-selected) and report which faults the author
  commits deliberately for effect — a fault used on purpose,
  with a named effect, is a style choice, not an error.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "shorter is always better." Redirect to
Worked example 2: a repair that loses a true claim is a worse
sentence, however short. Also watch for overcorrection: learners
may convert every noun to a verb and destroy useful concepts
("the argument" becoming "what she argued" everywhere).
