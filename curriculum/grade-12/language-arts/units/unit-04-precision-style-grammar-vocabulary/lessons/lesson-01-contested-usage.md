# Lesson 01 — Contested Usage and the Reference-Work Test

**Goal:** You will resolve contested-usage cases by stating the
rule you remember, what a reference work actually says, and the
choice you make for a stated audience — and you will tell a
genuine contest apart from a settled error.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 1–3 include the workshop and lab).
**Materials:** this lesson, a dictionary with usage notes (print
or free online — see the resource pack), your case-bank notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Someone tells you "10 items or less" on a supermarket sign is
   wrong. What rule are they appealing to? (Fewer for countable
   things, less for amounts — hold that thought; this lesson
   checks it against a reference.)
2. Which of these would you correct without looking anything up,
   and which would you check first: "irregardless," "less
   problems," "between you and I"? Write one sentence for each
   explaining why. There is no penalty for a wrong first guess
   here — the lesson's method exists precisely because first
   guesses about usage are unreliable.

## Teaching: usage is a convention, and conventions are contested (15 minutes)

A **convention** is an agreement: a community of writers agrees,
for now, to do something one way. Conventions change — that is
why a usage your grandparent learned as a law can be a choice
today, and why a form your teacher marked wrong can be standard
in a dictionary's usage note. The grade-12 standard behind this
lesson (L.11-12.1.a) says exactly that: usage is a matter of
convention, it can change over time, and it is sometimes
contested. L.11-12.1.b adds the working rule: resolve complex or
contested usage by consulting references.

That gives us the unit's central tool, **the reference-work
test**:

1. **What rule do you remember?** State it plainly, including
   where you learned it if you know ("my Year 9 teacher," "a
   style blog").
2. **What does the reference actually say?** Open the
   dictionary or usage guide. Does it call the form *standard*,
   *contested*, *informal*, or *nonstandard*? Quote or paraphrase
   the note — do not summarize it from memory.
3. **Who is your audience, and what does that audience expect?**
   A college application essay, a lab report, a text message,
   and a poem do not share one audience. Formal writing usually
   means: follow the stricter choice when a contest is live,
   unless you have a reason not to.
4. **What is your choice, and what does it cost?** Every choice
   has a cost. Choosing the strict form can sound stiff;
   choosing the contested form can distract a reader who holds
   the old rule. Name the cost. A choice with a named cost is a
   decision; a choice without one is a habit.

Three distinctions keep the test honest:

- **Contested** is not the same as **wrong**. A contested form
  is one that careful writers and reference works disagree
  about. A settled error is one that reference works agree is
  nonstandard — "irregardless" in formal writing, for example,
  or "between you and I" as an object (the pronoun after a
  preposition takes the object form, *me*).
- **Descriptive** is not the same as **prescriptive**. A
  descriptive note reports how people actually use a word. A
  prescriptive rule tells you what to do. A good usage
  dictionary does both, and labels which is which. Read the
  label.
- **Hypercorrection** is over-applying a rule where it does not
  belong. "Between you and I" is the classic case: someone
  learned "say 'you and I,' not 'you and me'" (true for
  subjects — "You and I went") and applied it after a
  preposition, where the object form belongs. Knowing a rule
  is not the same as knowing where it applies.

## Worked example 1: less or fewer? (modeled)

*Case: "The survey found less mistakes in the second draft."*

**Step 1 — the remembered rule.** Fewer for things you count,
less for amounts you measure. Mistakes are countable, so the
rule says *fewer mistakes*.

**Step 2 — what the reference says.** Merriam-Webster's usage
guidance on fewer vs. less (checked 2026-11-03; see the resource
pack) reports that the fewer/less distinction is a received rule
originating as one critic's personal preference in 1770, that
*less* has been used with countable nouns for over a thousand
years, and that *less* remains standard for amounts and
measurements — distances, sums of money, units of time and
weight, and statistical counts ("less than three miles," "less
than twenty dollars"). But it also reports that *less* before
ordinary plural count nouns ("less mistakes") is rare in
writing and usually better avoided.

**Step 3 — audience.** A formal essay. The reader may hold the
received rule, and nothing is gained by fighting it here.

**Step 4 — choice and cost.** Revise to "fewer mistakes." Cost:
none worth noting — the strict form is also the clearer one
here. But note what the test did *not* license: it did not
license correcting "less than three miles" to "fewer than three
miles," which would sound wrong to most readers and contradict
the reference. The same test, applied to a different sentence,
gives a different verdict. That is the point.

## Worked example 2: singular they (modeled)

*Case: "Each applicant must submit their portfolio by Friday."*

**Step 1 — the remembered rule.** "Their is plural; an applicant
is singular; therefore use 'his or her portfolio.'" Many writers
learned exactly this.

**Step 2 — what the reference says.** Modern usage references
report singular *they* as standard English for a person whose
gender is unknown or irrelevant, and for a generic singular
antecedent like *each applicant* or *anyone*. It is not a recent
invention: singular *they* appears in English writing centuries
back. What has changed is that major style guides now accept it
explicitly. Check your audience's house style — a particular
publication may still prefer "his or her" — but do not call
singular *they* an error in general formal writing.

**Step 3 — audience.** A school or college document with no
house style stated. The priority is clarity and not excluding
any applicant.

**Step 4 — choice and cost.** Keep "their portfolio." Cost: a
reader holding the old rule may notice it; benefit: the sentence
is shorter and clearer than "his or her portfolio," and it does
not assume the applicant's gender. Where a house style is
stated, follow the house style and note the choice — the test's
step 3 exists for exactly that case.

Notice the difference between the two examples. In the first,
the reference supported the remembered rule for that sentence
but not for every sentence like it. In the second, the reference
overturned the remembered rule for general formal writing. Both
outcomes are normal. The test does not exist to confirm your
memory; it exists to replace memory with evidence where the two
differ.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Apply the four steps to each case. Do not write only the final
choice — show all four steps, in one or two sentences each.

1. "The data is clear: sales rose in every region." (Hint: is
   *data* plural or singular? Check a dictionary entry, not
   your memory. What does the audience — a science report vs.
   a general memo — change?)
2. "Whom should I address the letter to?" (Hint: test the
   pronoun's job in the clause. Who is doing what to whom?
   Then ask whether the formal or the natural choice serves
   this letter better, and what each costs.)
3. "The committee is comprised of six members." (Hint: look up
   *comprise*. What does it mean on its own? What do usage
   guides say about "is comprised of"? What is the safe formal
   replacement?)
4. "To boldly go where no one has gone before." (Hint: is a
   split infinitive an error, or a contested choice? What does
   the unsplit version — "to go boldly" — do to the rhythm?
   When would you unsplit anyway?)

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

Write a case card for each of the following, using the
four-step test. A complete card has: the sentence, the
remembered rule, the reference's verdict (paraphrased, with
the reference named), the audience, and the choice with its
cost.

1. "Hopefully, the funding arrives before spring."
2. "The author impacted a generation of readers."
3. "This policy is different than the one adopted last year."
4. "Between you and I, the proposal needs more work."
5. "She gave the report to Tom and I." (Careful: same trap as
   case 4, different position. Test the pronoun's job.)
6. "There are less than fifty respondents in the sample."
   (Careful: the measurement exception from Worked example 1
   applies to a *count* here — read the reference note again
   before deciding. Statistical enumerations are treated as
   amounts by the usage guidance.)

## Applied task

Take one paragraph of your own writing — any draft, any
subject — and run the reference-work test on every usage
choice you are unsure about, not only the ones you suspect are
wrong. Mark each with a number in the margin and list the
verdicts below the paragraph. Bring at least one case where
the reference overturned your memory to the next session's
workshop: those cases are the unit's real curriculum.

## Exit check

In three sentences, explain the difference between a contested
usage and a settled error, using one example of each from this
lesson. If you cannot name the reference behind your example,
your answer is incomplete — go back and name it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** work the guided practice with the adult reading
  the usage note aloud; paraphrase it together before writing
  the verdict. Use only two cases at a time if four steps for
  four cases is too much in one sitting.
- **Alternate response mode:** dictate the case cards and have
  the adult scribe; the thinking, not the handwriting, is
  assessed.
- **Extension:** find one usage dispute in a published style
  guide's online archive (adult-selected), run the test on it,
  and report where the guide and the dictionary disagree — and
  which you would follow for a college essay, and why.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "the dictionary says it, so it must be
right for every audience." Redirect to step 3: a dictionary
describes usage; a house style or a formal audience can still
require the stricter form, and the learner's job is to choose
knowingly. Answer-key reference: `../answer-key.md`,
Lesson 01. Do not let learners copy usage-note wording as
their verdict — a paraphrase proves they read it.
