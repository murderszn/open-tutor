# Lesson 04 — Vocabulary from Context, Morphology, and Etymology

**Goal:** You will infer unfamiliar academic words from
context clues, word-change patterns, and etymology, verify each
inference in a dictionary, and use each word accurately in an
original sentence — and you will start a personal academic word
bank you can carry into Units 05–08.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 8–9 include the workshop).
**Materials:** this lesson, a dictionary (print or free
online), your word-bank notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Read this sentence and guess the meaning of *obdurate* before
reading on: "Despite three appeals, two warnings, and the
loss of her funding, the director remained obdurate, refusing
every compromise the committee offered."

What clues did you use? The list of pressures (appeals,
warnings, loss) sets up a contrast; "refusing every
compromise" restates the word's meaning in plainer terms.
Those are two different clue types, and this lesson names
them so you can use them on purpose.

## Teaching: four ways in, one verification (15 minutes)

The grade-12 vocabulary standards (L.11-12.4.a–d) describe a
method, not a talent: use context, use word-change patterns,
consult a reference, verify. Four ways in:

**1. Context clues.** A sentence usually leaves fingerprints:

- *Definition clue:* the meaning is stated nearby ("…an
  *obdurate* person, stubborn beyond persuasion…").
- *Synonym clue:* a familiar word sits beside the unfamiliar
  one ("…obdurate, even stubborn, in her refusal…").
- *Antonym clue:* a contrast word frames it ("…not flexible
  but obdurate…").
- *Example clue:* instances illustrate it ("…obdurate: she
  rejected the appeal, the warning, and the compromise…").
- *Inference clue:* the whole situation implies it (the
  readiness-check sentence: pressures + refusals = stubborn
  refusal). Inference clues are the weakest — always verify
  them, because a situation can imply several meanings.

**2. Morphology — word-change patterns.** English builds words
from parts. Knowing a part narrows the meaning before you
check it:

- Prefixes: *un-* (not), *re-* (again), *pre-* (before),
  *dis-* (apart, not), *inter-* (between), *sub-* (under),
  *anti-* (against), *con-/com-* (with, together).
- Suffixes change part of speech and shade meaning: *-tion*
  (noun, act of), *-ity* (noun, state of), *-ous* (adjective,
  full of), *-ize* (verb, make), *-able* (adjective, able to
  be), *-ment* (noun, result of).
- Roots carry the core idea: *dict* (say), *scrib/script*
  (write), *spec* (look), *struct* (build), *vert/vers*
  (turn), *pos* (put, place), *ject* (throw), *port* (carry),
  *cred* (believe), *voc* (call, voice).

Word-change patterns (L.11-12.4.b) are the payoff: *conceive*
(verb) → *conception* (noun) → *conceivable* (adjective) →
*inconceivable* (adjective, negated). If you know one form
well, you can often infer the family — then verify the exact
shade, because families drift (*conceivable* does not mean
exactly "able to be conceived" in every context).

**3. Etymology — a word's history.** A dictionary's etymology
tells you where a word came from and what it once meant. It
explains why *muscle* relates to a little mouse (Latin
*musculus*), and why *sincere* has nothing to do with wax
(that story is a myth — a good example of why etymology must
be checked, not repeated from anecdotes). Two warnings:
etymology is not current meaning (a word's history can mislead
you about its present use — this is the *etymological
fallacy*), and folk etymologies are everywhere. Use etymology
to remember a verified meaning, never to replace verification.

**4. Verification.** L.11-12.4.d is blunt: verify the
preliminary determination in context or in a dictionary. The
method is: infer first (so you learn to read), then check (so
you learn the truth), then use the word in an original
sentence (so you learn whether you really know it). A word
you cannot use in your own sentence is a word you have
recognized, not learned.

**Connotation, denotation, and register.** *Denotation* is what
a word literally means; *connotation* is what it suggests or
feels like (*stubborn* vs. *steadfast* vs. *obdurate* — all
denote refusal to change, but they praise, blame, or
neutrally describe it differently). *Register* is the level of
formality a word belongs to: *ask*, *request*, and *beseech*
do not interchange in a formal essay, a memo, and a poem.
Precision vocabulary is choosing among near-synonyms for the
connotation and register you actually want — not choosing the
longest word available. A long word used imprecisely is worse
than a short word used exactly.

## Worked example 1: infer, then verify (modeled)

Sentence (original): "The committee's *intransigence* on the
budget surprised even its allies, who had expected at least a
token concession."

- **Morphology first:** *in-* (not) + *trans-* (across) +
  *-ence* (noun, state of). Literally, "not going across" —
  not moving to the other side. That suggests refusal to
  compromise, but it is a guess from parts.
- **Context second:** "surprised even its allies, who had
  expected at least a token concession" — the allies expected
  some movement and got none. That supports "refusal to
  compromise."
- **Verification third:** a dictionary entry for
  *intransigence* gives (paraphrased): refusal to change one's
  views or to agree about something; stubbornness. The
  inference holds, and the dictionary adds the shade: it is a
  formal word, stronger than *stubbornness* and more about a
  negotiating position than a personality.
- **Original sentence (the learning test):** "The
  negotiators' intransigence meant the talks ended exactly
  where they began." If that sentence sounds right, the word
  is learned; if it sounds like a word pasted onto a sentence,
  it is not yet.

## Worked example 2: a word-change family (modeled)

Start with *spec* (look): *inspect* (look into), *inspection*
(the act of looking into), *spectator* (one who looks),
*speculation* (a looking — now figurative: forming a theory
without full evidence), *speculative* (based on such a theory).

Notice the drift: *speculation* no longer means "looking" in
any literal sense. Morphology got you to the family; only the
dictionary and the context tell you that *speculative* in an
academic sentence means "not yet proven" — a precise, useful
shade that the root alone would not give you. That is why
step 4 (verification) is not optional, even when steps 1–3
feel certain.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

For each word, (a) name the clue type or the word parts you
used, (b) write your inferred meaning, (c) write the
dictionary-verified meaning in your own words, (d) write an
original sentence using the word accurately.

1. "The report was *ambiguous*: two readers could defend
   opposite conclusions from the same paragraph, and both
   could quote it." (Clue type? Root?)
2. "Her *candid* assessment — blunt, unflattering, and, the
   committee admitted, accurate — ended the debate."
3. "The *proliferation* of small journals made it harder, not
   easier, to judge which findings mattered." (Parts: *pro-* +
   *lifer*? Check the dictionary — the parts may mislead you
   here, which is itself the lesson.)
4. "He offered a *pragmatic* solution: not the ideal one, but
   the one the budget and the calendar would actually allow."

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. Infer and verify these four words, following the same
   four-step format (clue/parts, inference, verified meaning,
   original sentence): *criterion, sustain, derive, constraint*.
   For at least one, use morphology (word-change pattern) as
   your first step and show the family (e.g., *derive →
   derivation → derivative*).
2. Write a "connotation ladder" for refusal to change, from
   most praising to most blaming, using at least five words
   (you may include *steadfast, stubborn, obdurate,
   intransigent, resolute, inflexible*). For each word, write
   one sentence in which only that word on the ladder fits —
   the sentence must break if you swap in its neighbor.
3. Find one word in your own Unit 02 essay that you used
   because it sounded academic, and test it: does your sentence
   survive if you replace it with a plainer word? If yes, the
   academic word was decoration — replace it or defend it in
   one sentence. (Both outcomes are acceptable; an undefended
   decoration is not.)

## Applied task — start the word bank

Begin your personal academic word bank (project part 2's
companion; the style sheet is separate). Each entry has: the
word, its part of speech, a definition in your own words, the
clue or parts that helped you, and an original sentence. Add
at least 10 entries this session, drawn from this lesson, the
guided practice, and words you meet in your own reading.
By session 18 the bank must hold at least 20 entries. A bank
copied from a dictionary is not a bank — the definition in
your own words and the original sentence are what make it
yours.

## Exit check

Choose one word from the guided practice. Without looking at
your notes, write: its part of speech, its meaning in your own
words, one word-part or clue that helps you remember it, and
an original sentence using it. Then check yourself against
your notes and mark any part you missed — that mark is your
study list, not a failure.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** work with a partner's dictionary lookup: one
  learner infers aloud, the other checks, then swap. Use the
  word bank's "clue or parts" column as a memory hook, not as
  extra writing for its own sake.
- **Alternate response mode:** record the original sentences
  as voice memos and have the adult check them against the
  dictionary meanings.
- **Extension:** trace one word's etymology to a dictionary
  entry and report one folk etymology about it (or about a
  word like *sincere*) that the entry contradicts; explain why
  the folk story is tempting and why it is wrong.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "knowing the root means knowing the
word." Redirect to Worked example 2: roots open the family,
context and dictionary fix the shade. Also watch for
thesaurus-swapping: a synonym that fits one sense of a word
often fails in another sense — the original sentence is the
test, and the answer key accepts any sentence that uses the
verified sense correctly, not only the key's sentence.
