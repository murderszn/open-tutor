# Lesson 05 — The Full Precision Pass and the Style Sheet

**Goal:** You will run a complete five-step precision pass on
a paragraph, revise two earlier passages for the portfolio,
and build a personal style sheet of ten rules — each rule
backed by a reference or a stated rhetorical effect.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 11–18 include the studios).
**Materials:** this lesson, your Unit 01 and Unit 02 drafts,
your case-bank notebook, your word bank, the project sheet
(`../project.md`).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Look at a paragraph you revised in Lesson 03. Answer honestly:
did you change anything you could not explain? If yes, that
change was a guess wearing a revision's clothes. This lesson's
pass exists to make every change explainable — to you, to a
peer checker, and to a future reader (including future you, in
Unit 08's capstone portfolio).

## Teaching: the five-step precision pass (15 minutes)

Run the steps in order. Each step has one question and one
kind of evidence. Do not skip to step 5; a paragraph can be
grammatically clean and still say the wrong thing precisely.

**Step 1 — Claim check.** *What does this paragraph claim,
sentence by sentence?* Write each claim in five words or
fewer in the margin. If you cannot state a sentence's claim,
the sentence is decoration, throat-clearing, or two claims
fused together — split it or cut it. This step comes first
because steps 2–5 can polish a sentence that should not exist.

**Step 2 — Usage check.** *Is anything here contested?* Run
the Lesson 01 reference-work test on every doubtful usage.
Mark each verdict (rule, reference, audience, choice) in the
margin. If a usage is settled and correct, leave it alone —
revising correct sentences is how good paragraphs get worse.

**Step 3 — Syntax check.** *Does each sentence's shape serve
its claim?* Use Lesson 02: mark sentence lengths (S/M/L),
find runs of three identical shapes, and check that the main
clause carries the news and the emphasized word sits at the
end. Change one shape per paragraph on purpose, not every
shape at random.

**Step 4 — Sentence-level check.** *Do the four faults survive?*
Run Lesson 03's four questions: action in a verb?
Redundancy? Vague verbs? Modifiers and parallelism? Repair
what you find, preserving every true claim (Lesson 03,
Worked example 2).

**Step 5 — Word check.** *Does every word earn its place?*
Use Lesson 04: check any word you used for sound rather than
sense against the dictionary; check connotation and register
against the paragraph's purpose; replace decoration with
precision or defend it in a note. Add any word you verified
and will reuse to your word bank.

After the pass, write a **revision note**: for each change,
the step number, the original phrase, the repaired phrase, and
the reason in one sentence. A revision note is not bureaucracy;
it is the evidence that the revision was thinking, and it is
what the portfolio project assesses.

**The style sheet.** A style sheet is a personal, one-page
record of decisions you have made and will reuse: ten rules,
each in this form — *Rule, Example, Reason (reference or
effect)*. Example: "Rule: use *fewer* for plural count nouns
in formal writing, *less* for amounts and measurements.
Example: 'fewer errors,' but 'less than three miles.' Reason:
Merriam-Webster usage guidance, checked 2026-11-03; the strict
form costs nothing here and avoids distracting a reader who
holds the received rule." A style sheet is a living document:
when a rule fails a test in a new sentence (session 16), revise
the rule and note the change. A rule you never revise is a rule
you never tested.

## Worked example 1: the full pass on one paragraph (modeled)

Original paragraph (written for this lesson):

> "The utilization of satire by the author makes possible the
> communication of a criticism of society. Due to the fact that
> the target is powerful, the criticism is indirect. The reader
> is involved in a process of recognizing the criticism through
> the gap between what is said and what is meant. This is an
> important point."

**Step 1 — claims.** (a) Satire lets the author criticize
society indirectly. (b) The target's power explains the
indirectness. (c) The reader recognizes criticism via the
said/meant gap. (d) "This is important" — no claim; it is a
label, not a reason. Cut sentence 4 or replace it with a
reason *why* the point matters.

**Step 2 — usage.** No contested usage in this paragraph
("due to the fact that" is redundancy, not a usage contest —
step 4 will catch it). Nothing to mark; that is a legitimate
result.

**Step 3 — syntax.** Lengths: long, medium, long, short. The
short final sentence ("This is an important point.") tries to
land emphasis on a sentence with no content — emphasis
wasted. After step 1's cut, the paragraph ends on sentence 3,
whose end ("what is meant") is a good emphasis point already.

**Step 4 — sentence level.** Nominalizations: *utilization*,
*communication*. Redundancy: "due to the fact that." Vague
verb: "is involved in a process of recognizing" → *recognizes*.
Repaired paragraph: "By using satire, the author criticizes
society indirectly. Because the target is powerful, the
criticism stays indirect. The reader recognizes the criticism
in the gap between what is said and what is meant."

**Step 5 — words.** *Satire, criticism, indirect* are all
used in their verified senses; *gap* is doing precise work
(the said/meant gap is the unit's own term from Unit 02).
Nothing to replace. Add nothing to the bank from this
paragraph — a word already known does not need banking.

**Revision note (as the learner would write it):** "Step 1:
cut 'This is an important point' — it states no claim. Step 4:
'utilization… makes possible the communication of' →
'By using satire, the author criticizes' — action restored to
a verb. Step 4: 'due to the fact that' → 'because' —
redundancy. Step 4: 'is involved in a process of recognizing'
→ 'recognizes' — vague verb. Step 3: no shape change needed;
the paragraph now ends on 'what is meant.'"

## Worked example 2: a style-sheet rule that fails its test (modeled)

A learner's draft rule: "Always replace passive voice with
active voice."

Test sentence (from a lab-style paragraph the learner wrote):
"The samples were tested at 20°C and the results were
recorded hourly." Active rewrite: "We tested the samples at
20°C and recorded the results hourly." The active version is
fine — but in a report whose topic is the samples, not the
testers, the passive version keeps the samples as the subject
of every sentence, which is the paragraph's organizing
principle. The rule, applied blindly, would break the
paragraph's coherence to satisfy a slogan.

Revised rule: "Prefer active voice, except when the actor is
unknown, irrelevant, or the object is the paragraph's topic —
then use passive deliberately and note the choice." The
revision is the learning: a style sheet records *judgments
with conditions*, not slogans. Session 16 tests every rule
this way, and the portfolio rewards a revised rule more than
an untested one.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Run the five-step pass on this paragraph (original, written
for this lesson). Write the margin claims (step 1), mark one
usage question to check (step 2), mark the sentence lengths
(step 3), name the faults (step 4), and flag one word to verify
(step 5). Then write the revision note.

> "The implementation of the argument by the writer is
> achieved through the use of evidence from two texts. There
> are less examples in the second half, which is a problem
> because the claim is bigger there. The reader is given a
> process of weighing the evidence. It is very interesting."

(Hints: step 2 has a genuine usage case hiding in sentence 2
— *less examples* — and step 1 should catch that sentence 4
states no claim. Step 4's nominalizations are *implementation*
and *use*.)

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. Run the full pass on your Unit 02 comparative passage (the
   one the portfolio will use). Write the revision note in
   full. This is portfolio part 1's second half — do not rush
   it to finish the lesson; sessions 12–13 are studios for
   exactly this work.
2. Draft five style-sheet rules now (five more in session 13):
   two usage rules (with references named), two syntax or
   sentence-level rules (with effects stated), and one
   vocabulary rule (with a connotation or register reason).
   Each rule needs an example — a rule without an example is
   a slogan.
3. Take one rule from task 2 and try to break it: write a
   sentence where following the rule makes the sentence worse,
   and revise the rule with a condition (as in Worked
   example 2). If you cannot break it, say why it is
   unconditional — some rules are.

## Applied task

Assemble the portfolio's first draft by session 18's checklist
(see `../project.md`): two revised passages with revision
notes, the ten-rule style sheet, the original precision
paragraph, and the reflection. Then run the peer style-check
(session 14): exchange portfolios, check every usage claim
against a reference, and flag any revision that lost a true
claim. Accept or rebut each flag in writing. The portfolio is
due at session 20, alongside the unit assessment.

## Exit check

Write the five step-names of the precision pass from memory,
in order, each with its one question. Then write one sentence
explaining why step 1 comes first. If you missed a step, your
study list is the pass itself — copy it onto your style
sheet's back page until you own it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** run the pass on one paragraph with the adult
  doing step 1 aloud (margin claims) while you do steps 2–5;
  swap roles for the next paragraph.
- **Alternate response mode:** record the revision note as a
  voice memo, one change per memo, with the step number spoken
  first.
- **Extension:** apply the pass to a paragraph of published
  prose (adult-selected, public domain) and report which steps
  find nothing — a clean pass is a finding, not a failure to
  revise, and the report should say what the author's choices
  teach about the step that found nothing.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "a revision note is extra work on top of
revising." Redirect: the note *is* the revision's evidence,
and it is what lets a peer checker verify the work instead of
trusting it. Also watch for step-skipping under time pressure
— especially step 1. A polished paragraph with no claim is the
unit's most expensive failure. Answer-key reference:
`../answer-key.md`, Lesson 05.
