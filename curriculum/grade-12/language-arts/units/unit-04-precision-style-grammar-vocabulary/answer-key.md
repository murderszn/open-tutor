# Answer Key — Unit 04: Precision Style (Separate Teacher File)

**For the guiding adult only.** Do not place this file beside
student materials. Every question in the lessons, practice
sets, formative quiz, and unit assessment is solved here
independently and reconciled with the lesson text. For open
responses, the key gives a model answer and the range of
acceptable variation — a different correct sentence is still
correct. Reference claims were checked 2026-11-03 (see the
resource pack); dictionary paraphrases are in the key's own
words.

## Lesson 01 — Contested usage

**Guided practice.**

1. *Data.* Contested. *Data* is plural in scientific usage
   ("the data are") and a singular mass noun in general and
   computing usage ("the data is"). Science report: "the data
   are clear." General memo: "the data is clear" is acceptable
   if used consistently. The cost of the plural in a general
   memo is stiffness; the cost of the singular in a science
   report is a distracted specialist reader.
2. *Whom should I address the letter to?* Contested in
   practice, formally correct. *Whom* is the object of
   *address* (test: "I should address it to him," not "he").
   Formal letter: keep *whom*. Informal note: *who* is natural
   and widely accepted; its cost in a formal letter is a
   reader who holds the old rule noticing it.
3. *Comprised of.* Contested. *Comprise* means "include, be
   made up of" (the whole comprises the parts). Usage guides
   report "is comprised of" as widely used but still
   objected to by many formal readers; the safe formal
   replacements are "is composed of" or "consists of."
   Choice for a formal essay: "The committee consists of six
   members." Cost of keeping "comprised of": none in meaning,
   some in reader trust.
4. *Split infinitive.* Settled correct (not an error).
   "To boldly go" is standard; the unsplit "to go boldly"
   changes rhythm and, in famous cases, meaning emphasis.
   Unsplit anyway only if a stated house style requires it,
   or if the split creates ambiguity — neither applies here.

**Independent practice (case cards, summarized).**

1. *Hopefully* (sentence adverb). Contested. Modern usage
   references accept "Hopefully, the funding arrives" (meaning
   "it is hoped that"); some formal readers still object.
   Formal essay with no house style: acceptable, but "It is
   hoped that the funding arrives" costs nothing and avoids
   the objection — the key accepts either with a stated
   audience reason.
2. *Impacted* (verb). Contested, leaning informal. Accepted in
   modern usage, but often vague: "impacted a generation"
   could mean affected, influenced, or transformed. Formal
   choice: name the effect — "influenced a generation of
   readers." Cost of keeping *impacted*: vagueness, not error.
3. *Different than.* Contested by position. "Different from"
   is the standard formal choice before a noun phrase ("the
   one adopted last year"); "different than" is common before
   a clause ("different than we expected"). Choice: "different
   from the one adopted last year."
4. *Between you and I.* Settled error. After a preposition,
   the object form belongs: "Between you and me." The "you and
   I" rule applies to subjects ("You and I went"), not to
   objects — this is hypercorrection.
5. *To Tom and I.* Settled error, same trap. "Gave" takes an
   indirect object: "to Tom and me." Test: "gave it to me,"
   not "to I."
6. *Less than fifty respondents.* Settled correct (acceptable)
   under the measurement/statistical exception. Usage guidance
   treats statistical enumerations as amounts: "less than
   fifty respondents" is standard, as are "less than three
   miles" and "less than twenty dollars." "Fewer than fifty"
   is also acceptable; the key does not mark *less* wrong here
   — that distinction is the lesson's point.

## Lesson 02 — Syntax for effect

**Guided practice.**

1. Periodic: "Only in the final chapter, when its contents
   reverse the reader's judgment of the hero, does the
   novel's narrator reveal the letter." Effect: the revelation
   arrives late and feels earned. Three short sentences: "The
   narrator withholds the letter. The novel runs its course.
   The final chapter reverses everything." Effect: hammer
   blows; the reversal, not the withholding, is the news.
   Accept any pair that correctly uses the shapes with a
   stated effect.
2. (a) "The job requires patience, careful listening, and
   accuracy." Unequal: "to listen carefully" (infinitive
   phrase) against nouns. (b) "She likes hiking, swimming,
   and biking." Unequal: infinitive against gerunds.
   (c) "The report is thorough, well-organized, and
   comprehensive." Unequal: a clause ("it covers…") against
   adjectives.
3. Accept either main-clause choice with a stated reason.
   Recommended: (a) main = "The experiment failed" (the result
   is the news; the miscalibration is the reason, subordinate:
   "because the equipment was miscalibrated"). (b) main =
   "The treaty was signed" (the event is the news; the
   concessions are background: "after both sides had conceded
   the border"). A learner who reverses either with a reason
   tied to their paragraph's topic is correct.
4. (a) Lab report: passive justified — the tester is
   irrelevant, the samples are the topic. "The samples were
   tested at 20°C." (b) Essay about suppression: active
   required — passive hides responsibility. "The committee
   suppressed the report." Any passive here must be defended
   as deliberate, not default.

**Independent practice.** Open responses; assess by the
stated criteria: (1) the rewritten sentence must actually
break the run of three identical lengths, with a stated
effect; (2) both versions must be correctly shaped, with an
effect per version; (3) each moved-emphasis version must end
on the named word (on "debate": "The proposal was decided
not by the debate but by the funding formula" — ends on
formula; acceptable variants must end on the named word: for
"debate," e.g., "It was the debate, not the funding formula,
that decided nothing" is wrong — it ends on "nothing";
correct example: "The funding formula decided the proposal,
not the debate." For "proposal": "The debate did not decide
the proposal" is weak; better: "What the funding formula
decided, after all the debate, was the proposal."); (4) the
passive judgment must name the test (actor unknown /
irrelevant / object as topic) and follow it.

**Applied task (illustration).** Acceptable moves: (1)
reordering sentences or clauses to change emphasis; (2)
splitting one long sentence into two (or combining two short
ones) to change pace; (3) moving a clause to the front or end
to change what is emphasized. The blank-strip qualification:
the image shows the method, not any sentence — a learner who
claims the image "proves" a revision is correct has missed
the point; the rebuilt paragraph is the evidence.

## Lesson 03 — Sentence-level diagnosis

**Guided practice paragraph — diagnosis and repair.**

Faults: (1) nominalization — *implementation, expression*;
(2) redundancy — "at this point in time" (→ *now*), "the
reason is because" (*because* repeats *reason*); (3) vague
verb + nominalization — "makes possible the scheduling of"
(→ *schedules*); (4) dangling modifier — "Having reviewed
the room assignments, the problem was identified…" (the
problem did not review anything).

Repaired paragraph (model; accept any repair preserving all
true claims): "Several teachers objected to the new schedule.
The reason is that it schedules classes in rooms that are too
small. A review of the room assignments identified the
problem as a shortage of large rooms." Note: "A review…
identified…" repairs the dangle by making the review the
subject; naming a reviewer ("After reviewing…, the committee
identified…") is also correct if the learner notes the agent
was unspecified in the original.

**Independent practice.** (1) Assessed against the learner's
own paragraph: at least four faults repaired, each with fault
number, original phrase, repaired phrase. (2) Fault museum:
each deliberate fault must be a genuine instance of its fault
and each repair must preserve meaning. (3) A kept
nominalization is correct only with a two-sentence defense
naming the concept the noun preserves (e.g., "the argument"
as a discussed object, "the revision" as a process under
discussion).

## Lesson 04 — Vocabulary

**Guided practice.**

1. *Ambiguous.* Clue: definition/inference ("two readers
   could defend opposite conclusions"). Root: *ambi-* (both).
   Verified meaning (paraphrased): open to more than one
   interpretation. Original sentence (model): "The
   ambiguous instruction left half the class starting on the
   wrong task."
2. *Candid.* Clue: synonym ("blunt, unflattering").
   Verified meaning: frank, straightforward, saying what one
   thinks. Model sentence: "Her candid review listed the
   essay's three weakest paragraphs by name."
3. *Proliferation.* Parts mislead if read as *pro-* + "life":
   the word comes from Latin *proles* (offspring) + *ferre*
   (to bear) — rapid production of offspring, generalized.
   Verified meaning: rapid increase in number. Model
   sentence: "The proliferation of sources made selection,
   not collection, the hard skill."
4. *Pragmatic.* Clue: antonym/inference ("not the ideal one,
   but the one the budget and calendar would allow").
   Verified meaning: practical, concerned with what works
   rather than with theory or ideals. Model sentence: "The
   pragmatic compromise funded half the program this year
   instead of none of it."

**Independent practice.**

1. *Criterion:* a standard or rule by which something is
   judged (plural *criteria*). *Sustain:* to support, keep
   going, or bear the weight of (a claim, an argument).
   *Derive:* to obtain or deduce from a source or premises
   (family: *derive → derivation → derivative* — note
   *derivative* as an adjective can also mean unoriginal, a
   drift to flag). *Constraint:* a limitation or restriction
   that shapes what is possible. Each requires an original
   sentence in the verified sense; accept any accurate one.
2. Connotation ladder (model, order may vary with stated
   reasons): most praising → *resolute, steadfast* (firm by
   principle) → *inflexible* (neutral-to-blaming) →
   *stubborn, obdurate* (blaming) → *intransigent* (most
   blaming, negotiation context). Each "only this word fits"
   sentence must break on swapping — assess the break, not
   only the order.
3. Open response; a decoration verdict is correct if defended
   ("the plainer word keeps the meaning, so I replace it") or
   if the academic word is defended by a shade the plainer
   word lacks.

## Lesson 05 — Precision pass

**Guided practice paragraph — full pass.**

Margin claims: (a) the writer argues from evidence in two
texts; (b) the second half has fewer examples, a problem
because its claim is larger; (c) the reader weighs the
evidence; (d) "It is very interesting" states no claim — cut
or replace with a reason.

Usage (step 2): *less examples* is the genuine case —
ordinary plural count nouns in formal writing take *fewer*:
"fewer examples." (Contrast Lesson 01's statistical
exception: examples here are not a measurement.)

Syntax (step 3): lengths run long, medium, long, short; the
short final sentence wastes emphasis on a content-free claim
— after the step-1 cut, the paragraph ends on the weighing,
a good emphasis point.

Sentence level (step 4): *implementation, use* nominalized;
"is achieved through the use of" vague; "a process of
weighing" nominalized.

Model repaired paragraph: "The writer argues from evidence in
two texts. The second half has fewer examples, which is a
problem because the claim there is larger. The reader weighs
the evidence." Revision note must name step, original,
repair, reason for each change; accept any note meeting that
form.

**Independent practice.** Assessed against the learner's own
passage and style sheet: five rules in the required
categories, each with an example; one rule tested to breaking
point with a revised rule and condition (or a reasoned case
for unconditionality).

## Practice Set A — Usage

1. *Their notebook.* Contested; acceptable in general formal
   writing (singular *they* for a generic singular); keep.
   Cost: a reader holding the old rule may notice; benefit:
   shorter and gender-neutral vs. "his or her."
2. *Data are.* Settled correct for a science/formal context
   (plural). 3. *Data is.* Acceptable in a general memo or
   computing context (singular mass noun); the two cards must
   differ by audience, not by claiming one form universally
   right.
4. *Whom did the committee elect?* Settled correct in formal
   writing (*whom* is the object of *elect*). 5. *Who did…?*
   Acceptable informally; in a formal essay prefer *whom* —
   the stiffness cost is small and the formality gain is real.
6. *Comprises three stories.* Settled correct (whole
   comprises parts). 7. *Is comprised of.* Contested; formal
   choice: "is composed of" or "consists of."
8. *To fully understand.* Settled correct — a split infinitive
   is not an error; do not "fix" it unless a house style
   requires it.
9. *Between you and me.* Settled correct. 10. *For my sister
   and I.* Settled error → "for my sister and me" (object of
   the preposition *for*).

## Practice Set B — Syntax and repair

1. Sample patterns (accept any correctly built patterns with
   stated effects): short–long–short — "The claim comes
   first. The evidence, drawn from two texts and tested
   against a counterargument, follows in the second
   paragraph. The conclusion restates it." Effect: the long
   middle carries the weight; the short ends frame it.
2. (a) "Good revision requires patience, careful reading, and
   honesty about weak claims." (b) "The essay argues,
   explains, and concludes." (c) "She is a careful reader, a
   sharp critic, and a clear writer."
3. (a) *The error was noticed too late.* Justified if the
   noticer is irrelevant and timing is the point; otherwise
   active ("Someone noticed the error too late" is weak —
   better: name the noticer if known). Accept either with a
   stated reason tied to the paragraph's topic. (b) *Samples
   were weighed.* Justified (lab convention; samples are the
   topic). (c) *Mistakes were made.* Unjustified evasion —
   active with the agent: "The committee made mistakes in
   the budget." (d) *Treaty was signed in 1848.* Justified
   (signatories are not the topic; the date and fact are).
4. Diagnosis: (1) *utilization, achievement*; (2) "in the
   event that" (→ *if*); (3) "is given a process of checking"
   (→ *checks*); (4) dangling modifier — "Having checked the
   evidence, the argument becomes convincing" (the argument
   did not check). Model repair: "By using evidence, the
   writer earns credibility. If the reader checks the
   evidence, that credibility grows. Once the reader has
   checked it, the argument convinces."
5. Periodic: "Only when the funding formula, which no one had
   explained, was finally published did the proposal fail."
   Emphasizes the failure as the earned conclusion. Loose:
   "The proposal failed when the funding formula, which no one
   had explained, was finally published." Emphasizes the
   failure first, the reason as explanation.
6. End on *committee*: "The report was written by the
   committee, not the chair." End on *chair*: "The committee
   wrote the report, not the chair." End on *report*: "What
   the committee, not the chair, wrote was the report."
   Each version stresses its final word.
7–8. Open responses; assess by the stated criteria (no two
adjacent sentences sharing length class and shape; a kept
nominalization with a two-sentence defense).

## Practice Set C — Vocabulary

1. *Verdict:* a formal judgment or decision, especially after
   weighing evidence. Clue: legal/formal context + "evidence
   had pointed one way." Model sentence: "The verdict on the
   proposal was unanimous: the evidence left no room for
   dissent."
2. *Tentative:* provisional, hesitant, not yet final. Clue:
   "three qualifications and an invitation to disagree."
   Model sentence: "She offered a tentative date, warning
   that the archive might close early."
3. *Synthesis:* combining parts into a new whole. Parts:
   *syn-* (together) + *thesis* (placing, proposition).
   Model sentence: "The synthesis of field notes and census
   data produced a map neither source contained."
4. *Undermined:* weakened gradually from below. Metaphor:
   digging beneath a structure's foundations. Model sentence:
   "One retracted citation undermined the chapter's central
   claim without touching its conclusion directly."
5. *Press* family (parts of speech and model sentences):
   *pressure* (noun: force, or stress), *pressing* (adjective:
   urgent; or gerund: the act of pressing), *pressurize*
   (verb: to put under pressure), *depress* (verb: to push
   down, literally or figuratively), *suppress* (verb: to hold
   back, put down), *repress* (verb: to hold back, especially
   feelings or dissent), *express* (verb: to press out — now
   chiefly "to state, convey"). Furthest drift: *express* or
   *depress* in its mood sense — accept either with a stated
   drift reason.
6. Ladder (weakest → strongest commitment; note that
   *concede/admit* are acknowledgments against interest, not
   points on the same scale — a learner who separates them is
   correct): *suggest < claim < assert < maintain*; *admit*
   and *concede* acknowledge something reluctantly or against
   one's position. Each "only this word fits" sentence is
   assessed by whether swapping breaks it.
7. Register: (a) *ask* general, *request* formal — college
   essay: *request* for a formal ask, *ask* elsewhere.
   (b) *kids* informal, *children* neutral/formal,
   *young people* general-formal — policy essay: *children*
   or *young people*, not *kids*. (c) *a lot of* informal,
   *numerous* formal — college essay: *numerous* (or a
   number, if known). (d) *get* general, *obtain* formal —
   college essay: *obtain* for formal acquisition, *get*
   elsewhere.
8. Word bank entries: assessed on own-words definitions and
   original sentences, not on the words chosen.

## Formative Quiz (50 points)

**Part A.** 1. Contested; formal choice "fewer chairs"
(plural count nouns, ordinary case — not a measurement
exception). 2. Contested; acceptable — keep "their" (singular
*they*, generic singular, no house style stated). 3. Settled
error → "by Maya and me" (object of *by*). 4. Settled correct
— "comprises four experiments" (whole comprises parts).

**Part B.** 5. Periodic model: "Only in the final scene,
after withholding it for the whole novel, does the author
reveal the narrator's motive." Emphasis: the revelation as
earned conclusion. 6. Faults: (1) *utilization, achievement*;
(2) "due to the fact that" (→ *because*); (3) "is involved in
a process of verification" (→ *verifies*). Model repair:
"By using evidence, the author earns trust, because the
reader can verify it." 7. "The revision requires cutting
redundancy, strengthening verbs, and clarifying claims."
Unequal: *clarity* (noun) against verb phrases.

**Part C.** 8. *Criterion:* a standard used to judge (from
Greek *kriterion*, a means of judging). Clue: "stated before
any paper was read" — a rule set in advance. Model sentence:
"The only criterion for the prize was originality."
9. *Reluctance:* unwillingness, hesitation. Clue: contrast
("despite three invitations"). Model sentence: "His
reluctance to answer came from uncertainty, not indifference."
10. *Irreconcilable:* unable to be brought into agreement.
Parts: *ir-* (not) + *re-* (again) + *conciliare* (bring
together). Clue: antonym/example ("each denies the central
fact the other asserts"). Model sentence: "The two timelines
are irreconcilable: one requires the meeting to have happened
in March, the other in May."

## Unit Assessment (100 points)

**Part A.** 1. Contested → "fewer responses" (ordinary plural
count nouns; the statistical exception covers amounts and
measurements, and a survey's response count stated this way
is a plain count — accept "less responses" only with a
measurement-exception argument tied to a stated amount
reading, and note the stricter formal choice is *fewer*).
2. Contested; acceptable — keep "their paper" (singular
*they*, generic *anyone*, no house style stated; cost: a
reader holding the old rule may notice). 3. Contested →
"is composed of" / "consists of" (see Lesson 01). 4. Settled
correct — split infinitive is not an error; keep "to boldly
revise." 5. Settled error → "to James and me" (object of
*to*).

**Part B (model revision).** S/M/L marked; periodic and
parallel labeled; revision note names each change.

> "The sources disagree. (S) Only after weighing the
> conflicting evidence, rather than merely collecting it,
> does the writer earn a stronger argument. (L — periodic)
> The weighing tests the claim, sharpens the evidence, and
> exposes the gaps. (M — parallel)"

Revision note (model): "Step 1: cut 'This is important' — it
states no claim. Step 4: 'utilization… makes possible the
achievement of' → 'earn' / verb forms — nominalizations
restored to verbs. Step 4: 'due to the fact that' → 'after'
context / 'because' — redundancy. Step 4: 'is involved in a
process of weighing of' → 'weighing' — vague verb and
nominalization. Step 3: periodic sentence added for the
earned-conclusion effect; parallel structure added for
balance. No true claim lost: the disagreement, the weighing,
and the strengthening are all preserved." Accept any revision
meeting criteria (a)–(d) with a complete note.

**Part C.** 7. *Implication:* something implied; a consequence
that follows without being stated. Model: "The implication of
her silence was clear to everyone in the room." 8. *Evaluation:*
a careful judgment of value or quality, based on evidence.
Model: "His evaluation of the plan weighed cost against
benefit and found it wanting." 9. *Ambiguity:* openness to
more than one interpretation (here deliberate). Model: "The
poem's ambiguity is its point: two readings survive the last
line." 10. *Derived:* obtained by reasoning from premises or
a source (family: *derive → derivation → derivative*).
Model: "She derived the date from two letters and a shipping
record." 11. *Constraint:* a limiting factor. Model: "The
constraint was the deadline, not the budget."

**Part D (rubric).** 30 points: 10 for the pair where the long
sentence wins (correctly built long sentence — periodic or
well-subordinated — with an explanation naming why the
complexity earns its keep: suspense, hierarchy, or balance);
10 for the pair where short sentences win (correctly built
short sentences with an explanation naming why separation
serves emphasis or clarity); 10 for the general principle
stated with unit vocabulary (periodic/loose, subordination,
end weight, parallelism) and applied to both pairs. A pair
that merely asserts a preference without a syntactic reason
scores at most half for that pair.
