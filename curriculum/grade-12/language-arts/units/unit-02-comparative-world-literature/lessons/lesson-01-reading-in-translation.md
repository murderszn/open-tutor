# Lesson 01 — Reading in Translation: Two Odysseys, One Opening

**Goal:** You will compare two public-domain translations of the
*Odyssey* opening, name at least two consequential differences between
them, and explain how each difference changes what the hero or the poem
seems to be.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 1–2 include the workshop).
**Materials:** the two opening passages below, the illustration in
`../assets/comparative-world-literature-illustration.png`, notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. In one sentence, what do you already know about Odysseus before he
   appears? (If your answer is "he is trying to get home," you already
   know the poem's whole plot — which is exactly what the opening has
   to do for a first-time listener.)
2. If a poem was composed centuries before it was written down, and
   then translated 2,800 years later, how many chances has it had to
   change? Name them: performance, writing down, translation.

## Teaching: translation is interpretation (15 minutes)

A translation is not a window. It is a decision, made hundreds of
times per page, about what matters most: the sound, the word order,
the image, or the plain sense. Two translators can both be faithful
and still give you different heroes — because they made different
decisions about the same Greek words.

The *Odyssey* opens with an **invocation**: the poet asks the Muse
(the goddess of memory and song) to sing through him. That is not
decoration. It tells the first audience three things at once: this
story is bigger than one singer; the singer is a vessel, not the
owner; and the tale is already known — the interest is in *how* it is
told, not in surprise. The poem then starts **in medias res** — in the
middle of things: Odysseus is already stranded, already ten years from
home, and the story will circle back to explain how he got there.

The historical-context method's first question is: *what did the first
audience know without being told?* A Greek listener at a festival knew
the Troy story the way you know a famous film: they knew Odysseus
would get home, knew his men would die, and listened for *how* the
singer handled what they already knew. Keep that in mind as you read:
the opening is not trying to surprise you. It is trying to frame a man
you already know, in a particular light.

**Two translations, one opening.** Samuel Butler (1900) rendered the
poem into English prose. S. H. Butcher and A. Lang (1879) also rendered
it into prose — and Butler himself printed their opening in his
preface so readers could compare. Read both slowly, out loud if you can.

> **Butler:** "Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled
> far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy. Many cities
> did he visit, and many were the nations with whose manners and
> customs he was acquainted; moreover he suffered much by sea while
> trying to save his own life and bring his men safely home; but do
> what he might he could not save his men, for they perished through
> their own sheer folly in eating the cattle of the Sun-god Hyperion;
> so the god prevented them from ever reaching home."

> **Butcher and Lang:** "Tell me, Muse, of that man, so ready at need,
> who wandered far and wide, after he had sacked the sacred citadel of
> Troy, and many were the men whose towns he saw and whose mind he
> learnt, yea, and many the woes he suffered in his heart on the deep,
> striving to win his own life and the return of his company."

## Worked example 1: what kind of hero? (modeled)

*Question: what does each translator's first description of Odysseus
make him?*

Butler calls him "that ingenious hero." *Ingenious* is a mind-word:
cleverness, invention, device. A reader meeting Butler's Odysseus
expects tricks — and the poem will deliver them (the Cyclops, the
wooden horse is behind him, the bow contest ahead).

Butcher and Lang call him "that man, so ready at need." *Ready at
need* is a character-word: reliability under pressure, not brilliance.
Their Odysseus is less the inventor and more the man who holds when
things go wrong.

Neither is wrong. The Greek word behind both (the famous *polytropos*,
"of many turns") holds both meanings at once: many-turning in mind,
many-turning in fortune. English forces a choice, and the choice is
the interpretation. **A consequential difference is one that changes
what you expect from the hero** — and this one does.

## Worked example 2: whose story is it? (modeled)

*Question: who is the subject of the suffering?*

Butler: "he suffered much by sea while trying to save his own life and
bring his men safely home; but do what he might he could not save his
men, for they perished through their own sheer folly."

Butcher and Lang: "many the woes he suffered in his heart on the
deep, striving to win his own life and the return of his company."

Three small decisions, three effects:

1. Butler's "by sea" puts the suffering *outside* (place, weather,
   gods). Butcher and Lang's "in his heart" puts it *inside*
   (grief, endurance). Same voyage, different hero: one weathers a
   sea, the other carries a sorrow.
2. Butler's "his men" are people with a fate ("he could not save his
   men"). Butcher and Lang's "his company" is a military unit. The
   first sounds like responsibility for lives; the second like a
   captain's command.
3. Butler adds a verdict — "through their own sheer folly" — that
   judges the men before the story even starts. Butcher and Lang, in
   this excerpt, withhold the verdict longer. A reader of Butler
   starts out blaming the crew; a reader of Butcher and Lang starts
   out wondering.

**Rule for the whole unit:** never write "the translations differ."
Write "Translation A says ___, Translation B says ___, and the effect
is ___." Difference without effect is trivia; effect is interpretation.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

Apply the rule to one more pair inside the passages above:

1. Butler: "sacked the famous town of Troy." Butcher and Lang:
   "sacked the sacred citadel of Troy." What does "famous" emphasize
   that "sacred" does not, and vice versa? (Hint: fame is about
   reputation among people; sacred is about standing before gods.)
2. Butler: "manners and customs he was acquainted" vs. Butcher and
   Lang: "whose mind he learnt." Which Odysseus *learns* more, and
   which merely *visits* more? Quote the words that decide it.
3. Write a 2–3 sentence comparison: which opening would you give to a
   reader who wants an adventure story, and which to a reader who
   wants a story about endurance? Defend each choice with one quoted
   phrase.

## Independent practice

Read Butler's Book I further (the council of the gods through
Telemachus' challenge to the suitors, in excerpt). Then:

1. Find **two epithets** (stock descriptive phrases, e.g., for Athene
   or Odysseus) and explain what each one tells a listener who hears
   it repeatedly — why would an oral poem repeat descriptions instead
   of inventing new ones each time?
2. Find **one** place where the gods debate human blame (Zeus on
   Aegisthus, in the council scene). In 3–4 sentences, state the
   gods' claim about who is responsible for human suffering, quoting
   one line.
3. Context notebook, entry 1: list three facts about the *Odyssey*'s
   first audience (oral performance; festival listeners who knew the
   Troy story; gods as real agents in the world, not symbols) and, for
   each, write one sentence beginning "This changes my reading
   because…" with a quotation.

## Exit check

1. In your own words, what is a consequential translation difference?
2. Give one from today's passages, with both translators' words and
   the effect.
3. Name the four steps of the historical-context method.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the 19th-century prose feels dense, listen to a
  LibriVox recording of the Butler text while following along (see
  resource pack), and annotate only the opening ten lines.
- **Alternate response mode:** the comparison table may be spoken
  aloud and scribed by the adult; the exit check may be answered orally.
- **Extension:** find a third rendering of the opening from the
  learner's library (any modern translation) and add a third column:
  what does *it* choose that neither Butler nor Butcher and Lang chose?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "the older translation is more accurate" or "the
newer one is better." Redirect: accuracy is not a single scale — ask
*accurate to what*: the Greek word, the sentence rhythm, the hero's
character, the listener's experience? A translation can be accurate to
one and loose on another. The answer key gives the acceptable range;
see `teacher-guide.md` for reteaching moves.
