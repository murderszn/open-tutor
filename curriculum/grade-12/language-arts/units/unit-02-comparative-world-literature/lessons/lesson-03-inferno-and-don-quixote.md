# Lesson 03 — Satire and Moral Order: Dante and Cervantes

**Goal:** You will distinguish satire from mere insult, show how
Dante and Cervantes each build a moral order, and compare how two
periods treat the same theme — what a person owes the world, and what
happens when they get it wrong.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 11–12 include the close-reading
workshop).
**Materials:** *Inferno* Canto I (Longfellow), *Don Quixote* Part I,
Chapter VIII (Ormsby), notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. What is the difference between laughing *at* someone and laughing
   *with a purpose*? Give one example of each from something you have
   read or watched.
2. Dante puts real people — popes, politicians, neighbors — into Hell.
   Cervantes makes a man attack windmills thinking they are giants.
   Before you read: which one sounds crueler? Hold that answer.

## Teaching: satire says one thing and means another (15 minutes)

**Satire** is writing that exposes folly or vice by making it look
ridiculous, so that the reader judges it. It is not the same as an
insult. An insult says "you are bad." Satire says "look at this
carefully — *see* what it is." The test is whether the passage gives
you evidence to judge with, rather than just a verdict to repeat.
That is why satire is a reading skill under RL.11-12.6: you must
distinguish what is stated from what is meant.

**Dante's moral order.** The *Inferno* (written in the early 1300s,
while Dante was exiled from Florence after the city's factional
conflict drove him out in 1302) imagines Hell as an ordered place:
sins are sorted, punished by a logic that fits the sin, and guided by
the Roman poet Virgil — reason itself, in the poem's scheme, leading a
lost man. The poem opens with Dante lost:

> Midway upon the journey of our life
> I found myself within a forest dark,
> For the straightforward pathway had been lost.

(Longfellow translation, Canto I, checked 2026-10-21.) The "forest
dark" is not a place on a map. It is a moral state: a life that has
lost its straight path. Three beasts block the way up the mountain —
a leopard, a lion, a she-wolf — and readers have argued for centuries
about exactly what each stands for. That argument is the point: Dante
writes in a world where a beast can *be* a sin and *mean* a faction
and *feel* like a fear, all at once. His context — a Florence torn by
feuding factions, a church and empire fighting over who rules souls
and cities — is not background to the poem. It is the poem's subject,
translated into a journey.

**Cervantes' moral order.** *Don Quixote* (Part I, 1605; Part II,
1615) is a parody — a work that imitates a form in order to expose
it. Spain in Cervantes' day was flooded with chivalric romances:
knights, giants, enchanters, impossible quests. Cervantes gives us a
man, Alonso Quixano, who has read so many romances that he decides to
become a knight-errant himself, renames himself Don Quixote, and rides
out to right wrongs. The joke is not that he is stupid. The joke is
that a whole culture's favorite stories, taken literally, produce a man
who cannot tell a windmill from a giant — and that the culture loves
him for it anyway.

Read the windmill scene (Ormsby translation, Part I, Chapter VIII,
checked 2026-10-21):

> At this point they came in sight of thirty or forty windmills that
> there are on that plain, and as soon as Don Quixote saw them he said
> to his squire, "Fortune is arranging matters for us better than we
> could have shaped our desires ourselves, for look there, friend
> Sancho Panza, where thirty or more monstrous giants present
> themselves, all of whom I mean to engage in battle and slay, and with
> whose spoils we shall begin to make our fortunes; for this is
> righteous warfare, and it is God's good service to sweep so evil a
> breed from off the face of the earth."
>
> "Look, your worship," said Sancho; "what we see there are not giants
> but windmills, and what seem to be their arms are the sails that
> turned by the wind make the millstone go."

Quixote charges, a sail shatters his lance, and horse and rider go
"rolling over on the plain, in a sorry condition." Then comes the
move that makes this satire and not slapstick: Quixote explains the
failure away. The giants were turned into mills by "that same sage
Friston," an enchanter, "in order to rob me of the glory of
vanquishing them." His story cannot fail; it can only be enchanted.
That is the satiric mechanism: a closed system that explains away
every counterexample.

## Worked example 1: stated vs. meant in Dante (modeled)

*Question: what does the dark forest state, and what does it mean?*

Stated: a man is lost in a wood at midlife and cannot find the path.
Meant (on the poem's own terms): a soul that has lost its moral
direction, in a world — Dante's Florence, Dante's church — where the
straight path is blocked by appetites and factions figured as beasts.
The evidence that this is meaning, not just scenery: the poem spends
the rest of its length *walking out* of the forest through an ordered
Hell, as if to say that finding your way requires seeing the order of
wrong first. A forest you merely walk out of would not need nine
circles. The form *is* the argument: moral confusion is cured by
moral classification.

## Worked example 2: stated vs. meant in Cervantes (modeled)

*Question: what does the windmill scene state, and what does it mean?*

Stated: a knight sees giants, his squire sees windmills, the knight
attacks and falls. Meant: a reader who lives inside romances sees
giants everywhere, and no amount of plain evidence ("what seem to be
their arms are the sails") can break the frame, because the frame has
an enchanter ready to explain every failure. The evidence that this
is satire and not just a joke: Sancho is *right* and *loses the
argument anyway* — and the reader, who can see both the mills and the
giants, is put in Sancho's position: seeing clearly, in a world that
rewards the story. Cervantes' target is not one foolish man. It is a
reading habit — taking a genre's fantasies as a guide to life — and
every reader who has ever preferred a good story to a plain fact is
inside the target.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

1. Dante's beasts: the poem never labels them "fraud, violence,
   incontinence" in Canto I. Why is the *absence* of labels part of
   the poem's method? (Hint: if Dante labeled everything, the reader
   would classify instead of journeying.)
2. Quixote calls the fight "righteous warfare" and "God's good
   service." Sancho calls the objects "windmills." Which speaker is
   using religious language, and what work does it do for his claim?
   Quote both.
3. Write 2–3 sentences: is Quixote's enchanter excuse more like a
   lie, a delusion, or a theory? Defend your choice with one quotation
   from after the fall.

## Independent practice

1. Read Longfellow's Canto I to the end (Virgil's arrival and offer
   to guide). Write 3–4 sentences: what does Virgil offer Dante that
   Dante cannot give himself, and why does the poem need a *pagan*
   poet as guide? (Acceptable answers note: reason guiding a
   Christian soul; a poet from before Christ who cannot enter Heaven
   himself.)
2. Context notebook, entry 3: three facts (Dante's exile from
   Florence in 1302 after factional conflict; the *Inferno* as the
   first part of a journey through Hell, Purgatory, Paradise;
   Cervantes writing in a Spain saturated with chivalric romances,
   Part I 1605). For each, write one sentence beginning "This changes
   my reading because…" with a quotation.
3. Comparative chart, first draft: one row per text (Sophocles,
   Dante, Cervantes) under the theme "what a person owes the world."
   Each row needs: the duty as the text states it, the form that
   carries it (tragedy / ordered journey / parody), and one quotation.

## Applied task: the same theme, three centuries

Choose the theme **illusion versus reality**. In 6–8 sentences,
compare how two of the three texts treat it:

- In *Antigone*, who lives in an illusion, and what breaks it?
- In the *Inferno* opening, is the forest an illusion or a reality?
  What tells you?
- In *Don Quixote*, who sees reality, who sees illusion, and why does
  the person who sees reality lose the argument?

Your paragraph must quote at least one text and must end with a
sentence that states what the *comparison* shows that neither text
alone shows. (Example of a comparison sentence: "Both texts make the
clear-sighted character powerless — Sancho and Haemon both speak
truth and both are ignored — which suggests that seeing clearly was
never, in either period, the same as being heard.")

## Exit check

1. In one sentence each: what is satire, and how is it different from
   an insult?
2. Quote one line where Dante states a moral state as a place, and
   one line where Quixote states a mill as a giant.
3. Name one thing Dante's context (exile, factional Florence) changes
   about how you read Canto I.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if Longfellow's verse feels slow, read Canto I aloud
  with the adult and mark only the three beasts and Virgil's entrance
  on the first pass; meaning first, detail second.
- **Alternate response mode:** the comparative chart may be dictated;
  the exit check may be answered orally.
- **Extension:** read the *Inferno* passage where Dante meets a
  contemporary Florentine (from the learner's library selection) and
  ask: is Dante settling a score, teaching a moral, or both? Defend
  with a quotation.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "satire = making fun of people." Redirect to
the evidence test: does the passage give the reader reasons to judge,
or only a verdict to repeat? The second misconception is treating
Quixote as simply mad; the unit's line is that his madness has a
method (the enchanter excuse), and a method can be analyzed even when
the belief cannot be cured. See `teacher-guide.md`.
