# Lesson 04 — A Doll's House: The Door

**Goal:** You will trace how Nora's language of duty changes from
Act I to Act III, explain why the play's ending was a public
controversy, and judge what a staging choice does to meaning.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (session 16; Acts I–II are read in summary
with selected scenes before this lesson).
**Materials:** Ibsen's *A Doll's House* (Sharp translation), notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. In *Antigone*, a woman defies a king's law for a higher law. In
   this play, a woman defies a husband and a marriage. What is the
   same about the two conflicts, and what is different? (Keep your
   answer to two sentences; you will test it against the play.)
2. What does a door mean in a play where almost everything happens in
   one room? List three things a slammed door can communicate that
   words cannot.

## Teaching: duties, again — but in a living room (15 minutes)

*A Doll's House* premiered at the Royal Theatre in Copenhagen on
21 December 1879. It is set, famously, in a single room: the Helmers'
living room, just before Christmas. Nora Helmer appears at first to be
exactly what her husband Torvald calls her — his "little skylark,"
his "squirrel" — a wife who spends, charms, and keeps secrets of the
sweet kind. The secret that matters is not sweet: years earlier, to
save Torvald's life, Nora borrowed money and forged her dying father's
signature on the note. She has been repaying it in secret ever since.
When the lender, Krogstad, threatens exposure, Nora faces a choice
between the law, her marriage, and herself.

The play's method is the opposite of Dante's and Sophocles'. There is
no chorus, no guide, no gods. There is a room, a conversation, and a
woman listening to her own life until she can hear what it actually
says. That is why the play belongs in this unit as the *modern* voice:
its historical context is not gods or factions but law — in 1879
Norway, a married woman could not legally borrow money without her
husband's consent, which is why the forgery was necessary at all —
and a theatre audience that had never seen a wife walk out on stage
and mean it.

The end of the play is a debate, not a speech. Torvald, after first
raving at Nora when the secret comes out and then forgiving her the
moment the danger passes, expects gratitude. Nora answers with a
question that reframes the whole marriage:

> **NORA:** What do you consider my most sacred duties?
>
> **HELMER:** Do I need to tell you that? Are they not your duties to
> your husband and your children?
>
> **NORA:** I have other duties just as sacred.
>
> **HELMER:** That you have not. What duties could those be?
>
> **NORA:** Duties to myself.
>
> **HELMER:** Before all else, you are a wife and a mother.

(Sharp translation, Act III, checked 2026-10-21.) Nora then leaves —
through the door whose slam the play is famous for — to, as she puts
it, educate herself, because she has been treated first as her
father's doll-child and then as her husband's doll-wife, and has never
been a person in her own right. The ending was a scandal: some
theatres demanded, and for a German production Ibsen himself supplied,
an alternative ending in which Nora stays. That fact is itself a
context lesson: a play's meaning is partly decided by what its first
audiences could tolerate seeing.

The historical-context method's third question — *what does this
passage assume about the world?* — lands hard here. Torvald's line
"Before all else, you are a wife and a mother" assumes an order of
duties in which "wife and mother" is not one duty among others but the
definition of the woman. Nora's answer does not deny that she *has*
duties to husband and children. It denies that they are *first*, and
denies that they exhaust her. The whole play turns on that one word:
"other."

## Worked example 1: tracing one word (modeled)

*Question: how does Nora's use of "duty" change?*

In Act I, Nora's duties are things she *performs*: she performs
cheerfulness for Torvald, performs the role of the spendthrift wife
who must be scolded and forgiven, performs secrecy as devotion ("I
saved his life; I will pay for it myself"). The language is
affectionate and diminutive — she is praised for being what Torvald
calls her.

In Act III, "duty" becomes a *claim about order*: "I have other
duties just as sacred." The word has not changed its dictionary
meaning. It has changed its owner. In Act I, duties are assigned to
Nora by others; in Act III, Nora assigns one to herself. A reader who
tracks only the plot ("she leaves") misses the linguistic turn that
makes the leaving intelligible: she does not leave because she stops
loving her children. She leaves because she has discovered a duty
whose subject is herself, and no one in the house has a language for
it yet.

## Worked example 2: staging as interpretation (modeled)

*Question: what does a staging choice do to the final scene?*

Take the last minute. Torvald stands by the table. Nora puts on her
coat. The door is downstage, between her and the audience.

- If Nora pauses at the door, looks back once, and then leaves
  quickly, the staging claims: this is hard, and she does it anyway.
  The pause is the evidence of cost.
- If Nora leaves without looking back, and Torvald is left alone
  holding the coat she did not take, the staging claims: the break is
  complete, and what remains is his, not theirs.
- If the slam is loud and the stage stays lit on the empty room for
  ten seconds, the staging claims: the absence is the final character
  in the play.

None of these is "what Ibsen meant" in a way the others are not. Each
is an interpretation (RL.11-12.7's skill: multiple interpretations of
one work), and each must be defended the way a thesis is defended:
with what the text gives and what the choice adds. A staging that
cannot say what it adds is decoration; a staging that can is
criticism.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

1. Torvald forgives Nora the moment Krogstad's letter is withdrawn.
   In 2–3 sentences, explain what that timing reveals about what
   Torvald actually values — Nora, or his own reputation? Quote one
   line of his relief or his earlier rage (from the Act III scene as
   read in summary).
2. Nora says she has been "a doll" — first her father's, then
   Torvald's. Is "doll" her word or the play's word? Why does it
   matter who owns the metaphor? (Hint: the title is *A Doll's
   House*, not *A Woman's House*.)
3. Write 2–3 sentences: does the alternative ending (Nora stays)
   change the play's meaning, or only its plot? Defend your answer.

## Independent practice

1. Trace one more piece of language across the play: Torvald's pet
   names for Nora ("skylark," "squirrel," and the like) in Act I
   versus his language in Act III. Write 3–4 sentences on what the
   change in *his* language shows about the marriage, quoting once
   from each act.
2. Context notebook, entry 4: three facts (the 1879 Copenhagen
   premiere and the controversy; the legal position of a married
   woman borrowing money in 1879 Norway; the alternative ending
   supplied for a German production). For each, write one sentence
   beginning "This changes my reading because…" with a quotation.
3. Comparative seed: Nora and Antigone both defy a man who claims
   authority over them. Write a thesis seed in one sentence that
   states a *difference* between the two defiance scenes — not a
   similarity — and name the evidence you would use.

## Applied task: defend a staging

Choose one staging decision for the final two minutes of the play
(the pause, the coat, the slam, the lighting — or one of your own).
Write a 6–8 sentence defense: what your staging claims, what textual
evidence supports it, what it hides or risks, and why it is stronger
than the obvious alternative. Your defense must quote the duties
exchange at least once. A defense that only describes the staging
("the light goes down slowly") without claiming anything fails; a
defense that claims without quoting fails differently.

## Exit check

1. In one sentence, what is Nora's discovery in Act III?
2. Quote the line in which she names the duty that changes everything.
3. Name one context fact that changes how a modern reader hears
   Torvald's "Before all else, you are a wife and a mother."

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the play's argument feels abstract, watch one
  scene's blocking with the adult (two chairs, one door, acted
  simply) — the play's ideas live in who stands, who sits, and who
  leaves the room.
- **Alternate response mode:** the staging defense may be presented
  as a spoken pitch with the adult scribing; the exit check may be
  answered orally.
- **Extension:** read one contemporary review of the 1879 premiere
  (adult-selected, from the resource pack's search routes) and ask:
  did the first reviewers argue about the play's morals, its art, or
  both? Quote one line.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "Nora is selfish for leaving her children."
Redirect to the text: she names her duties to husband and children
and then adds "other duties just as sacred" — the play's argument is
about *order*, not about absence of love. The second misconception is
treating the alternative ending as proof the original was wrong; it
is proof the original was *felt*, which is a different and more
interesting fact. See `teacher-guide.md`.
