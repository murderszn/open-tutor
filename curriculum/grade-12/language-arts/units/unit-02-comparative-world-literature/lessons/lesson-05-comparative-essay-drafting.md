# Lesson 05 — The Comparative Essay: Two Texts, One Theme

**Goal:** You will draft a comparative essay that advances a thesis
about one theme across two anchor texts, develops it with quoted
evidence from both, uses historical context as evidence, and answers
a competing reading.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 17–18 include the revision studio).
**Materials:** your context notebook, duty chart, comparative chart,
project plan, notebook.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Write your thesis seed from Lesson 04 (or a new one) at the top of
   the page. Underline the theme, circle the two texts, and put a
   star next to the word that makes the thesis contestable (a thesis
   nobody could disagree with is a summary, not an argument).
2. Write the strongest objection to your thesis in one sentence, in
   the voice of someone who has read both texts. You will answer it in
   the essay; an essay with no objection to answer is a speech, not
   an argument.

## Teaching: what a comparative essay is not (15 minutes)

A comparative essay is not two mini-essays stapled together ("First
Antigone does X. Then Dante does Y."). It is one argument that *needs*
both texts, because the point lives in the comparison: what the two
treatments, set side by side, reveal about the theme that neither
shows alone.

**Structure that works:**

1. **Introduction (1 paragraph):** name the theme as a question, not
   a topic. "Duty" is a topic. "Can a duty be owed to someone who has
   not earned it?" is a question. End with a thesis that answers the
   question and names both texts: "Antigone and *A Doll's House* both
   stage a woman defying a man's law, but where Sophocles makes the
   defiance a clash of two public laws, Ibsen makes it a private
   discovery that no law yet names — which is why Antigone dies
   certain and Nora leaves uncertain."
2. **Body 1 (the first text, on its own terms):** one claim about
   Text A, developed with 2 quotations and one context fact used as
   evidence (not decoration — the fact must do work in the argument).
3. **Body 2 (the second text, on its own terms):** the same, for
   Text B. Do not mention Text A here except to set up the turn.
4. **Body 3 (the comparison):** the turn. Put the two treatments side
   by side and state what the juxtaposition shows. This paragraph is
   the essay: without it, you have two summaries.
5. **Counter-reading (1 paragraph):** state the strongest objection
   fairly (the steelman rule from Unit 01), then answer it with
   evidence — concede what is true in it, and show why your thesis
   survives the concession.
6. **Conclusion (1 paragraph):** restate the thesis as a finding, not
   a repetition. What do we now understand about the theme that the
   introduction only asked about?

**Context as evidence, not decoration.** Compare:

- Decoration: "Dante was exiled in 1302. The *Inferno* is about Hell."
  (The fact sits beside the argument and does nothing.)
- Evidence: "Dante was exiled from Florence in 1302 by the faction he
  opposed, and the *Inferno*'s opening — a man lost in a dark forest
  whose straight path is blocked — reads differently once you know
  the poet himself had lost his city's straight path. The forest is
  not only a symbol; it is an exile's description of what faction
  does to a citizen." (The fact changes a reading, with a quotation.)

## Worked example 1: a thesis, tested (modeled)

*Seed thesis:* "Both Dante and Cervantes use journeys to judge their
worlds."

*Test 1 — contestable?* Barely. Nobody would disagree; it is a
summary. *Revision:* "Dante's journey judges by sorting — every sin
finds its circle — while Cervantes' journey judges by *failing to
sort*: Quixote calls mills giants, and the world refuses to confirm
his categories, which suggests that by 1605 the ordered moral cosmos
Dante trusted had become a story a man tells himself."

*Test 2 — does it need both texts?* Yes: the claim is about the
*difference* between two orderings, and neither text alone can show a
difference. *Test 3 — is it provable in four pages?* The writer would
need: Dante's forest and one circle (sorting in action); Quixote's
windmills and the enchanter excuse (sorting failing); a comparison
paragraph on what changed between 1300 and 1605 (printing, romance
culture, a church no longer the single map). That is a plan, not a
wish. A thesis that passes all three tests is ready to draft.

## Worked example 2: a counter-reading, answered (modeled)

*Objection:* "Quixote is not judging anything — he is mad, and
Cervantes is just laughing at him. Comparing him to Dante dignifies a
joke."

*Fair statement first:* it is true that the windmill scene is funny
and that Quixote is deluded; any reading that forgets the comedy
misreads the book.

*Answer with evidence:* but the comedy has a mechanism — the
enchanter excuse — and mechanisms are arguments. When Quixote says
Friston "turned these giants into mills in order to rob me of the
glory of vanquishing them," he shows a mind that cannot be refuted by
evidence, because every refutation is absorbed as enchantment. That
is not just a joke about one man; it is a portrait of how a beloved
genre teaches its readers to protect the story from the world. Dante's
pilgrim, by contrast, *can* be corrected — Virgil corrects him, scene
after scene. The comparison survives the objection precisely because
the objection names what is different: one pilgrim can learn, one
knight cannot. Concede the comedy; keep the thesis.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

1. Trade thesis seeds with the adult (or read your own aloud). Apply
   the three tests (contestable? needs both texts? provable?). Rewrite
   the thesis in one sentence that passes all three.
2. Write your counter-reading paragraph's first two sentences: the
   fair statement of the objection, and the concession ("It is true
   that ___").
3. Pick one context fact from your notebook and rewrite it twice:
   once as decoration, once as evidence. Label which is which and
   why.

## Independent practice

Draft the essay (800–1,200 words) following the six-part structure
above, using your revised thesis. Requirements, checked in the
revision studio:

- A thesis that names the theme as a question and answers it with
  both texts.
- At least 2 quotations from each text, integrated (not dropped in
  as blocks without commentary).
- At least 1 context fact used as evidence (labeled in the margin:
  "context as evidence").
- A counter-reading paragraph that states the objection fairly and
  answers it.
- A comparison paragraph that states what the juxtaposition shows
  that neither text alone shows.

## Applied task: the thesis defense

After drafting, prepare a 2-minute defense (spoken or written) of
your thesis for the seminar: state the thesis, name your two strongest
quotations and what each proves, name your counter-reading and your
answer to it in one sentence each, and end with what you are least
sure about. The defense is graded on honesty, not confidence: a
defense that names its weakest point is stronger than one that hides
it. (The teacher guide scores this with the essay rubric's
"counter-reading" row.)

## Exit check

1. In one sentence: what makes a comparison paragraph different from
   two summaries?
2. Give one example, from your draft, of context used as evidence
   (fact + quotation + changed reading).
3. What is the strongest objection to your thesis, and in one phrase,
   what is your answer?

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the draft stalls, write Body 3 (the comparison)
  first — it is the essay's engine, and the other paragraphs are
  easier once the engine runs. Sentence frames are in the teacher
  guide.
- **Alternate response mode:** the essay may be dictated and then
  revised in print; the defense may be spoken.
- **Extension:** add a third text to the comparison (a third anchor
  text, or a passage from Unit 01's Chekhov) and rewrite the
  comparison paragraph to handle three treatments — or explain, in a
  paragraph, why the third text *breaks* your thesis, and what that
  break teaches you.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "comparison = similarities." Redirect: the
strongest comparative essays are usually built on a *difference*
inside a similarity (both stage defiance; one ends in certainty, the
other in uncertainty). The second misconception is treating the
counter-reading as a paragraph to "defeat"; the unit's standard is
steelman-then-answer, and a concession that survives is worth more
than a straw man that falls. See `teacher-guide.md` for the rubric
and sentence frames.
