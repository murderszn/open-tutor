# Lesson 02 — Antigone: Whose Law?

**Goal:** You will state Creon's and Antigone's competing claims with
quoted evidence, name the three duties in conflict, and explain why the
play's ending refuses a simple verdict.
**Time:** about 50 minutes (sessions 6–7 include the full-play workshop).
**Materials:** Sophocles' *Antigone* (Storr translation), notebook,
duty chart (below).

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Two brothers have killed each other fighting over a city. One
   died defending it; the other died attacking it. The new king
   forbids burial for the attacker, on pain of death, so the city can
   heal. Their sister buries him anyway. Before you read: who is right?
   Hold that answer — the play is designed to make it uncomfortable.
2. What is the difference between a law you can read on a wall and a
   duty nobody wrote down but everybody knows? Give one example of
   each from ordinary life.

## Teaching: three duties, one stage (15 minutes)

*Antigone* was first performed in Athens around 441 BCE, at a civic
festival, before thousands of citizens, by actors in masks with a
**chorus** — a group that speaks, sings, and comments, sometimes as
the city's voice, sometimes as a voice the city should argue with.
That staging matters: a Greek tragedy is not a private story. It is a
city watching itself argue.

The plot in brief: after a civil war over Thebes, King Creon decrees
that Eteocles, who died defending the city, will be buried with honor,
but Polyneices, who died attacking it, will lie unburied — food for
dogs and birds. Antigone, their sister, defies the decree and buries
Polyneices. She is caught and condemned. Creon's son Haemon, who is
engaged to Antigone, pleads for her; the prophet Teiresias warns that
the gods are angry; Creon relents too late. Antigone, Haemon, and
Creon's wife Eurydice all die. Creon is left alive, alone, saying he
has learned — at a price nobody would call a happy ending.

Three duties collide, and the play's power is that each one is real:

1. **Civic duty** — the ruler's duty to the city: order after civil
   war, loyalty rewarded, treason visibly punished. Creon's claim:
   a city that honors traitors like patriots cannot hold.
2. **Kinship duty** — the family's duty to its dead: a sister must
   bury her brother. Antigone's claim: no decree can cancel what a
   sister owes a brother.
3. **Divine law** — the unwritten laws of the gods, older than any
   king: the dead must receive burial rites, or pollution falls on the
   city. Antigone's second claim, and Teiresias' warning, rest here.

The historical-context method's second question — *what did the form
require?* — matters here. Tragedy does not give you a narrator who
tells you who is right. It gives you speeches, a chorus that wavers,
and an ending. Your job is not to pick a team. It is to state each
claim at its strongest, show where each claim overreaches, and explain
what the ending costs everybody.

Read the confrontation (Storr translation, checked 2026-10-21):

> **CREON:** Now answer this plain question, yes or no, wast thou
> acquainted with the interdict?
>
> **ANTIGONE:** I knew, all knew; how should I fail to know?
>
> **CREON:** And yet wert bold enough to break the law?
>
> **ANTIGONE:** Yea, for these laws were not ordained of Zeus, and she
> who sits enthroned with gods below, Justice, enacted not these human
> laws. Nor did I deem that thou, a mortal man, could'st by a breath
> annul and override the immutable unwritten laws of Heaven. They were
> not born today nor yesterday; they die not; and none knoweth whence
> they sprang.

Later, when Creon accuses her of pride and claims the city is with
him, Antigone answers:

> **ANTIGONE:** These think as I, but bate their breath to thee.
>
> **CREON:** Hast thou no shame to differ from all these?
>
> **ANTIGONE:** To reverence kith and kin can bring no shame.

And at the end of the same exchange, her shortest self-definition:

> **ANTIGONE:** My nature is for mutual love, not hate.

Against that, Creon's counter-claim — that a ruler who lets a woman
defy him loses the city itself:

> **CREON:** No woman shall be the master while I live.

## Worked example 1: steelmanning Creon (modeled)

*Question: what is Creon's strongest argument, stated fairly?*

It is not "he is a tyrant." It is this: Thebes has just survived a
civil war between two brothers. If the king buries the attacker with
the defender, he tells every future attacker that treason costs
nothing. His decree is a public act of civic memory: *this* death was
loyalty, *that* death was betrayal, and the city will remember the
difference. Evidence: he frames the decree as a defense of order
itself — and Haemon later has to argue not that order doesn't matter,
but that a wise ruler listens, which concedes that Creon's concern is
legitimate.

Where Creon overreaches: he turns a civic argument into a personal
one. "No woman shall be the master while I live" is not about the
city's safety; it is about his authority. And he claims the city
agrees with him ("Of all these Thebans none so deems but thou") when
Antigone — and Haemon, reporting what people say in private — say the
city is afraid to speak. A claim that needs fear to look unanimous is
weaker than it sounds.

## Worked example 2: steelmanning Antigone — and her limit (modeled)

*Question: what is Antigone's strongest argument, and where does it
stop?*

Her strongest argument is the hierarchy of laws: a mortal king's
breath cannot "annul and override" laws that "were not born today nor
yesterday." She does not deny knowing the decree — "I knew, all knew"
— she denies its jurisdiction over burial. That is a precise legal
claim, not mere defiance: some duties outrank some decrees.

Her limit: she claims a certainty the play does not fully grant her.
She says the townspeople agree with her but are gagged by terror —
but the play never lets the townspeople speak for themselves; the
chorus wavers and praises both sides at different moments. And her
certainty costs lives she did not intend: Ismene, Haemon, Eurydice.
The play's ending — Creon alive and ruined, Antigone dead and
vindicated in part — refuses to say "she was right, therefore all is
well." Rightness and ruin can arrive together. That is tragedy, not
a courtroom verdict.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

1. Fill one row of the duty chart: choose **one** duty (civic,
   kinship, divine), one scene, one quotation, and one sentence on
   where that duty overreaches.
2. Creon says, "Whome'er the State appoints must be obeyed in
   everything, but small and great, just and unjust alike." Is that
   a claim about *this* decree, or about *all* decrees? Why does the
   difference matter for judging him?
3. The chorus calls Antigone "a stubborn daughter of a stubborn
   sire." Is that praise, blame, or both? Defend your answer with
   what "stubborn" has meant so far in the play.

## Independent practice

1. Read the Haemon scene and the Teiresias scene (in full, in the
   Storr text). For each scene, write 3–4 sentences: what new
   information or argument does this scene add, and does it help
   Creon, Antigone, or neither? Quote once per scene.
2. Context notebook, entry 2: three facts about *Antigone*'s first
   audience (festival performance before citizens; masks and a chorus
   as the city's voice; a city that had itself known civil strife and
   debated the limits of rulers). For each, write one sentence
   beginning "This changes my reading because…" with a quotation.
3. Write the paragraph you will defend in the seminar: "The play's
   ending proves ___ about ___." Your paragraph must quote both
   Antigone and Creon, and must name one thing the ending leaves
   unresolved.

## Applied task: two stagings

Imagine two stagings of the confrontation above:

- **Staging A:** Creon stands on a raised platform above Antigone for
  the whole scene; guards flank her; the chorus watches silently.
- **Staging B:** Creon and Antigone stand at the same level, face to
  face; the chorus turns its heads between them like spectators at a
  contest.

For each staging, write 2–3 sentences: what does this staging claim
about who holds power in the scene, and what does it hide? Then state
which staging you would choose for the unit's seminar and why, in one
sentence with a quotation. (There is no single correct staging — but
there are undefended ones. "It looks cool" is not a defense.)

## Exit check

1. Name the three duties in conflict, in one phrase each.
2. Quote one line for Creon's claim and one for Antigone's.
3. In one sentence: why is "Antigone wins" a bad summary of the ending?

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the verse feels archaic, read the confrontation
  aloud in parts with the adult (adult as Creon, learner as Antigone,
  then swap) — hearing the short exchanges makes the argument's shape
  audible.
- **Alternate response mode:** the duty chart may be a spoken chart
  scribed by the adult; the seminar prep may be a voice memo.
- **Extension:** compare the Storr confrontation with a modern
  translation from the learner's library: does the modern version
  make Antigone sound more or less certain? Quote the difference.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: picking a team and defending it by ignoring the
other side's best argument. Redirect with the steelman rule: you may
not criticize a claim until you have stated it in the form its
defender would accept. The second misconception is treating the chorus
as the author's voice; the chorus changes its mind, and that change is
evidence about the city, not a verdict. See `teacher-guide.md`.
