# Project — Comparative Exhibit: Two Worlds, One Theme

**Deliverable:** a curated comparative exhibit — a display board (or
its paper equivalent) plus a curator's essay — that a visitor could
learn from without you standing beside it. The exhibit proves the
unit's method: translation judged, context used as evidence, and two
texts compared on one theme.
**Time:** sessions 14–15 (planning and first drafting), completed
during week 9 alongside Lesson 05's essay studio; final exhibit due
at session 20.
**Materials:** anchor texts, context notebook, illustration in
`assets/`, display board or large paper, markers or printouts.

## What the exhibit contains (4 parts)

1. **Translation panel (1 page).** One passage — the *Odyssey*
   opening — in the Butler and Butcher–Lang translations, side by
   side, with your judgment: which translation serves which purpose
   (adventure reading? endurance reading? classroom close reading?),
   defended with at least two quoted differences and their effects.
   Label the panel: "Two translations, one opening — and the choice
   is an interpretation."
2. **Comparative analysis (600–900 words).** Your Lesson 05
   comparative essay, revised, on one theme across two anchor texts
   (a third text may be added as an extension). It must contain: a
   thesis that answers the theme as a question; at least 2 quotations
   from each text; at least 1 context fact used as evidence (marked
   in the margin); a counter-reading paragraph that states the
   objection fairly and answers it.
3. **Staging note (1 page).** One scene — the Antigone confrontation
   or the final two minutes of *A Doll's House* — with a staging
   decision (positions, a pause, a prop, lighting) defended in 6–8
   sentences: what the staging claims, what textual evidence supports
   it, what it risks, and why it beats the obvious alternative.
   Include a simple labeled sketch or blocking diagram (positions
   marked; no artistic skill required) — a reproducible diagram, not
   the generated illustration.
4. **Context card (1 page).** For each anchor text in your analysis,
   three context facts, each followed by one sentence beginning
   "This changes my reading because…" with a quotation. Facts must be
   verifiable (the adult checks at least one per text against the
   resource pack); readings must quote the text, not the fact.

The generated illustration (`assets/comparative-world-literature-illustration.png`)
may appear on the exhibit's title panel with its caption and a
one-sentence note: what the image claims about comparative reading,
and one qualification (see Lesson 01's applied task). It is a concept
illustration, not evidence about any text, and must not be labeled as
a historical document.

## Steps

1. **Choose (session 14).** Theme, two texts, translation passage,
   staging scene. Write the thesis seed and pass the three tests
   (Lesson 05). Get the adult's sign-off on the theme and texts.
2. **Gather (session 14).** Quotations with locations (book/chapter,
   act/scene); context facts with sources (resource pack or
   adult-approved reference). No quotation without a location.
3. **Draft (session 15).** Comparative analysis first draft; staging
   note; translation panel. Peer evidence-check: a partner (or the
   adult) checks every quotation against its text and marks any
   context fact used as decoration.
4. **Revise (sessions 17–18).** Revision studio: address the
   evidence-check marks, strengthen the comparison paragraph, answer
   the counter-reading. Three specific changes, named in a revision
   note attached to the exhibit.
5. **Assemble and present (session 20).** Mount the four parts in
   order; present a 2-minute thesis defense (Lesson 05's applied
   task). File the exhibit in the learner's portfolio for the U08
   capstone.

## Rubric (40 points)

| Criterion | 10–9 (strong) | 8–7 (solid) | 6–5 (developing) | 4–0 (needs rework) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Translation judgment (10) | 2+ quoted differences, each with an effect; judgment defended for a stated purpose | 2 differences, effects mostly clear | 1 difference, or differences without effects | Lists differences without judgment, or misquotes |
| Comparative analysis (10) | Contestable thesis needing both texts; 2+ quotations per text, integrated; comparison paragraph states what juxtaposition shows | Thesis and quotations present; comparison paragraph mostly comparative | Thesis is summary-like or one text dominates; quotations dropped in | Two summaries; no comparison paragraph |
| Context as evidence (10) | 3 facts per text, each changing a reading with a quotation; sources checkable | Facts present, most tied to readings | Facts listed beside readings (decoration) | Facts missing, wrong, or invented |
| Staging note + presentation (10) | Staging claims something, defends it with text, names a risk and beats an alternative; defense honest about weakest point | Staging defended with text; defense mostly honest | Staging described, not defended | No staging decision, or defense without quotation |

**Passing:** 28/40 with no criterion below 5. Below that, revise the
weakest criterion with the adult and resubmit; the rubric, not the
adult's impression, decides.

## Adult notes

- Check at least one context fact per text against a reputable
  source before the exhibit is assembled; correct invented facts on
  the spot (a wrong fact with a confident sentence is the exhibit's
  most common failure).
- The staging sketch is a blocking diagram (who stands where, when
  they move). It is not judged on drawing quality.
- Keep the exhibit anonymous: no learner name, photo, or school on
  any panel that could enter a shared repository; names stay in
  private storage.
