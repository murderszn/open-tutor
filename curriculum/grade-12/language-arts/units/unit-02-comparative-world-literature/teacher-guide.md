# Teacher Guide — Unit 02: Comparative World Literature and Historical Context

**For the guiding adult only.** Do not place this file beside student
materials; the answer key (`answer-key.md`) is a separate file. This
guide covers pacing, misconceptions, discussion moves, and reteaching.
All quotations below are from the U.S. public-domain editions named in
the unit README, checked 2026-10-21.

## Pacing and workload

- The 20-session plan assumes ~50-minute sessions, five per week. If
  sessions run 30 minutes, split each lesson across two sessions and
  move the workshops as marked; do not cut the drafting studios
  (sessions 15, 17–18) — the comparative essay and exhibit are the
  unit's primary summative evidence.
- Reading load: the *Odyssey* and *Don Quixote* are assigned in
  excerpt only, never whole. *Antigone* is short and is read in full;
  *A Doll's House* Act III is read in full, Acts I–II in summary with
  selected scenes. If the learner stalls on 19th-century translation
  prose, use the LibriVox listen-while-reading route (resource pack)
  without reducing the passages.
- The seminar (session 9) needs the prep sheet from session 8; never
  run the seminar without it — unprepared discussion teaches that
  opinions need no evidence, the opposite of this unit.
- Check at least one context fact per text against a reputable source
  before the exhibit is assembled. The most common exhibit failure is
  a confident, wrong fact (usually a date) carrying a good argument.

## Misconceptions by lesson (and the redirect)

**Lesson 01.** *"The older translation is more accurate" / "the newer
one is better."* Redirect: "Accurate to what — the Greek word, the
rhythm, the hero's character, the listener's experience? Name your
criterion, then judge." *"Translations differ" as a complete
answer.* Require the rule: difference + quoted words + effect.

**Lesson 02.** *Picking a team.* Enforce the steelman rule: you may
not criticize a claim until you have stated it in the form its
defender would accept. *Treating the chorus as the author's voice.*
Redirect: the chorus changes its mind across the play; track the
change and ask what it shows about the city.

**Lesson 03.** *"Satire = making fun of people."* Redirect to the
evidence test: does the passage give the reader reasons to judge, or
only a verdict to repeat? *"Quixote is just mad."* Redirect: his
madness has a method (the enchanter excuse); analyze the method.

**Lesson 04.** *"Nora is selfish for leaving her children."*
Redirect to the duties exchange: she names duties to husband and
children and adds "other duties just as sacred" — the argument is
about order, not absence of love. *Treating the alternative ending as
proof the original was wrong.* Redirect: it is proof the original was
felt, which is a different and more interesting fact.

**Lesson 05.** *"Comparison = similarities."* Redirect: build on a
difference inside a similarity (both stage defiance; one ends in
certainty, the other in uncertainty). *"Counter-reading = a paragraph
to defeat."* Redirect: steelman, concede what is true, then answer;
a concession that survives is worth more than a straw man that falls.

## Seminar moves (session 9)

- Open with the learner's prep-sheet claim; ask for the *strongest*
  counter-evidence against it before anyone agrees.
- When the learner agrees too fast, play the rival: "Creon would say
  your Antigone reading forgets the city just survived a civil war —
  answer him."
- When the learner stalls, return to a quotation: "Read the line
  aloud. What word is doing the work?"
- Close by asking the learner to state one way their reading changed
  during the discussion, and what changed it.

## Thesis-defense moves (session 17/20)

- Ask for the two strongest quotations first; if a quotation cannot
  be tied to the thesis in one sentence, it does not belong yet.
- Ask: "What would make you abandon this thesis?" A thesis with no
  abandonment condition is usually a summary.
- Score the defense with the essay rubric's counter-reading row:
  full marks require a fairly stated objection and an answer, not
  confidence.

## Sentence frames (for learners who stall)

- Translation: "Butler says ___, while Butcher–Lang say ___. The
  effect is ___ because ___."
- Context as evidence: "Knowing that ___, this passage reads
  differently: ___ (quotation). Without that fact, I would have read
  it as ___."
- Comparison: "Both texts treat ___, but where Text A ___, Text B
  ___. The comparison shows ___."
- Counter-reading: "One might object that ___. It is true that ___.
  Even so, ___ because ___."

## Reteaching moves

- **Translation comparison weak:** return to Lesson 01's worked
  examples and do one new pair together, out loud, with the adult
  modeling the "difference + effect" sentence before the learner
  attempts one.
- **Context as decoration:** take one of the learner's decorated
  facts and rewrite it together into the evidence frame above; then
  require the learner to rewrite a second one alone.
- **Satire confusion:** use Practice Set D item 3 (Sancho's sentence)
  as the diagnostic: if the learner can say what Sancho proves and
  why it changes nothing, satire is understood; if not, re-teach
  from the enchanter excuse.
- **Essay as two summaries:** require the learner to write Body 3
  (the comparison paragraph) before revising anything else; grade
  Body 3 alone against the rubric's comparison row before the full
  essay is resubmitted.
- **Quiz below 20:** reteach the missed method only (not the whole
  unit), using the practice set named in the quiz scoring bands, then
  re-administer the missed part.

## What readiness looks like (for U03)

The learner is ready for rhetorical analysis when they can: state a
thesis that needs two texts; quote accurately with locations; use one
context fact as evidence without prompting; and state a counter-reading
fairly. If the counter-reading is still a straw man, carry that one
skill into U03's first week explicitly — it is the hinge of the whole
year's argument writing.
