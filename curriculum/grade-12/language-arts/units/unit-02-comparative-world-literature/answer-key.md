# Answer Key — Unit 02: Comparative World Literature and Historical Context

**For the guiding adult only.** Keep separate from student materials.
Sample answers are acceptable-response models, not the only correct
answers: accept any answer that quotes accurately, reasons from the
quotation, and meets the item's stated requirement. Where a range is
given, the range is the grading rule.

## Lesson 01 — Reading in translation

**Readiness check.** 1. Acceptable: "Odysseus is trying to get home
after Troy" (or any accurate plot kernel). 2. Acceptable list:
performance/composition, writing down, translation (also: copying,
editing). Any three genuine stages earn full marks.

**Guided practice.** 1. "Famous" emphasizes reputation among people
(what others say of Troy); "sacred" emphasizes standing before the
gods (what Troy is in a divine order). Either direction argued with
the words earns credit. 2. Butcher–Lang's Odysseus *learns* more:
"whose mind he learnt" makes the journey an education; Butler's
"manners and customs he was acquainted" makes it acquaintance/visiting.
Accept the reverse only if argued from "acquainted" as deeper knowledge
— it is a weaker reading; note it and discuss. 3. Adventure reader:
Butler ("ingenious hero," "travelled far and wide," "famous town") —
cleverness and fame promise exploits. Endurance reader: Butcher–Lang
("so ready at need," "woes he suffered in his heart") — steadfastness
and inward suffering promise endurance. Any pairing defended with a
quoted phrase earns credit.

**Independent practice.** 1. Epithets: accept any two genuine stock
phrases from Book I (e.g., "grey-eyed Athene," "godlike Odysseus,"
"father of men and gods" for Zeus). Explanation must note repetition
as a memory aid for oral performance and a signal of identity/role,
not decoration. 2. Gods' claim: humans blame gods for evils that come
from their own folly — Zeus on Aegisthus: "how men lay blame upon us
gods for what is after all nothing but their own folly." Accept any
accurate paraphrase with that quotation or its substance. 3. Context
notebook: three facts (oral performance; festival audience knowing
the Troy story; gods as real agents). Each "This changes my reading
because…" sentence must tie the fact to a quotation; a fact listed
without a changed reading earns half marks.

**Applied task (illustration).** 1. Scroll → ancient; illuminated
manuscript → medieval; plainer bound books → early modern/modern;
globe + compass → exploration/comparison (accept: navigation across
cultures). Each match needs a visible reason (material, decoration,
binding). 2. Claim: different centuries can sit side by side and be
read against each other; comparison is possible across time. 3.
Qualification (any one): the table hides that the books were never
in one room, their readers never met, and shared display can make
different worlds look alike. 4. Not evidence because it is a
2026-generated concept illustration depicting no real manuscript and
proving nothing about any text.

**Exit check.** 1. A translation difference that changes what the
reader understands (hero, theme, expectation), not a mere wording
variant. 2. Any pair from the lesson with both translators' words and
an effect (e.g., "ingenious hero" vs. "so ready at need" → cleverness
vs. reliability). 3. Four steps: (1) what did the first audience
know? (2) what did the form require/forbid? (3) what does the passage
assume about the world? (4) what does the context change about your
first reading, in one sentence with one quotation?

## Lesson 02 — Antigone

**Readiness check.** 1. Any held answer is acceptable; the point is
to revisit it after reading. 2. Wall law: e.g., a posted rule or
statute. Unwritten duty: e.g., burying family, keeping promises.
Accept any genuine pair.

**Guided practice.** 1. Any duty row completed with scene, quotation,
and a genuine overreach earns credit (models: civic — Creon's decree
scene, "Whome'er the State appoints must be obeyed in everything"
overreaches into "just and unjust alike"; kinship — Antigone's burial
claim overreaches if it denies the city's interest entirely; divine —
Teiresias' warning is authoritative yet the gods never speak
directly). 2. It is a claim about *all* decrees ("in everything…
just and unjust alike"), which is why it is stronger — and more
dangerous — than a defense of this one decree: it removes the
distinction Antigone insists on. 3. Both: "stubborn" praises resolve
and blames refusal to listen; the chorus's line ("a stubborn daughter
of a stubborn sire") carries both, and either one-sided answer
without the other earns half marks.

**Independent practice.** 1. Haemon scene: adds the voice of the
city in private (people agree with Antigone but fear to speak) and
the son's counsel to listen — helps Antigone's case on public opinion
while conceding Creon's concern for order. Teiresias scene: adds
divine confirmation that the city is polluted (unburied dead, altars
defiled) — helps Antigone decisively on divine law, yet comes too
late to save anyone. Each needs one quotation. 2. Context notebook:
three facts (festival performance; masks/chorus; a city familiar with
civil strife and ruler-limits debates), each with a "changes my
reading" sentence + quotation. 3. Seminar paragraph: must quote both
Antigone and Creon and name one unresolved thing (e.g., whether the
city learned, whether Antigone's certainty was justified, whether
Creon learned in time for anyone). A paragraph declaring a clean
winner without an unresolved point earns at most half marks.

**Applied task (two stagings).** Staging A claims: power sits with
Creon (height, guards, silent chorus as audience to authority); it
hides Antigone's moral standing by making her small. Staging B claims:
the scene is a contest between equals; it hides the real asymmetry
(guard, decree, death sentence) by making the stage level. Choice:
any staging defended with a quotation earns credit; "it looks cool"
without a claim earns none.

**Exit check.** 1. Civic duty (city/order), kinship duty (family/
burial), divine law (unwritten laws of the gods). 2. Creon: e.g.,
"No woman shall be the master while I live" or "Whome'er the State
appoints must be obeyed in everything." Antigone: e.g., "The
immutable unwritten laws of Heaven" or "My nature is for mutual love,
not hate." 3. "Antigone wins" is bad because she dies, Haemon and
Eurydice die, and Creon survives ruined — vindication and ruin arrive
together; the ending is tragic, not a verdict.

## Lesson 03 — Dante and Cervantes

**Readiness check.** 1. Any genuine pair (laughing at a person's
misfortune vs. laughing to expose a folly so it can be judged).
2. Any held answer; revisit after reading — the lesson's point is
that Dante's sorting can be crueler in effect than Cervantes' joke,
or vice versa, depending on the evidence the learner cites.

**Guided practice.** 1. The absence of labels is the method: the
reader must journey and judge rather than classify at a glance; a
labeled Hell would be a diagram, not a pilgrimage. Accept any version
of this argument. 2. Quixote uses religious language ("righteous
warfare," "God's good service") to turn a mistaken attack into a holy
duty; Sancho uses plain observation ("not giants but windmills").
The religious language does the work of justifying what the eyes deny.
3. Best answer: a theory (a system that absorbs counterevidence via
the enchanter excuse) — accept "delusion" if defended from the text,
but note the lesson's line: delusion describes the belief, theory
describes the method that protects it. Quotation required (Friston
passage).

**Independent practice.** 1. Virgil offers guidance reason can give
(a path through Hell toward understanding) that Dante cannot give
himself while lost; a pagan poet is needed because the guide must
embody reason without claiming Christian salvation — he can lead
through Hell but not into Paradise. Accept any version with a
quotation or close paraphrase of Virgil's offer. 2. Context notebook:
three facts (exile 1302; three-part journey; chivalric-romance Spain,
1605), each with a changed-reading sentence + quotation. 3. Chart:
one row per text (duty as stated, form, quotation). Accept any
accurate duty statement (e.g., Sophocles: burial/law; Dante: moral
order/sin; Cervantes: knightly duty as romance defines it).

**Applied task (illusion vs. reality).** Accept any 6–8 sentence
comparison of two texts that: names who sees what, quotes at least
one text, and ends with a comparison sentence stating what the
juxtaposition shows. Model comparison sentence: "Both texts make the
clear-sighted character powerless — Sancho and Haemon both speak
truth and both are ignored — which suggests that seeing clearly was
never, in either period, the same as being heard." A paragraph that
only summarizes two texts without a comparison sentence earns at most
half marks.

**Exit check.** 1. Satire: writing that exposes folly/vice by making
it ridiculous so the reader judges it. Insult: a verdict without
reasons to judge. 2. Dante: "Midway upon the journey of our life / I
found myself within a forest dark, / For the straightforward pathway
had been lost." Quixote: "where thirty or more monstrous giants
present themselves" (with Sancho's "not giants but windmills" as the
correction). 3. Any genuine effect (e.g., the forest reads as an
exile's lost city as well as a soul's state; the beasts read as
factions as well as sins).

## Lesson 04 — A Doll's House

**Readiness check.** 1. Same: a woman defies a man's claimed
authority over her. Different: Antigone defies a public king's law
for divine law; Nora defies a private husband's order for a duty to
herself that no law yet names. Accept any accurate same/different
pair. 2. Any three genuine door-meanings (leaving, exclusion, a
decision made audible, finality, freedom, abandonment).

**Guided practice.** 1. The timing reveals Torvald values reputation
over Nora: rage while exposure threatens, forgiveness the moment
danger passes. Any quotation of his relief (after Krogstad's letter
is withdrawn) or earlier rage earns credit. 2. "Doll" is the play's
word (the title) that Nora takes up as her own diagnosis — it matters
because she reclaims the metaphor to indict the house that made her
one; a metaphor owned by the victim accuses differently than one
imposed by the critic. 3. Either answer defended earns credit, but
the stronger answer is: it changes meaning, not only plot — an ending
in which Nora stays endorses Torvald's order of duties, while the
original endorses her reordering. A "plot only" answer that ignores
the duties argument earns half marks.

**Independent practice.** 1. Pet names (Act I: "skylark,"
"squirrel," and similar diminutives) vs. Act III language (accusation,
then possessive forgiveness). The change shows the marriage's
affection was conditional on Nora playing the role; when she steps
out of it, the language turns to ownership. One quotation per act
required. 2. Context notebook: three facts (1879 Copenhagen premiere
and controversy; married women's borrowing law; alternative ending
for a German production), each with a changed-reading sentence +
quotation. 3. Thesis seed: must state a *difference* (e.g.,
Antigone's defiance appeals to a higher public law, Nora's to a
private duty no law names) with named evidence. A similarity-only
seed earns half marks.

**Applied task (staging defense).** Full marks: claims something,
quotes the duties exchange, names a risk, beats an alternative.
Description without claim, or claim without quotation, earns at most
half marks.

**Exit check.** 1. Nora discovers a duty to herself that is "just as
sacred" as duties to husband and children, and that she has lived as
someone else's doll. 2. "I have other duties just as sacred" (or
"Duties to myself."). 3. Any genuine fact (1879 law on married
women's borrowing; the premiere controversy; the alternative ending)
tied to hearing Torvald's line as a legal/moral order, not just a
personal opinion.

## Lesson 05 — Comparative essay

**Readiness check.** 1. Thesis seed with theme underlined, texts
circled, contestable word starred — check mechanically; a seed with
no contestable word must be revised before drafting. 2. Objection in
the opponent's voice, one sentence — any genuine objection earns
credit; "nobody would disagree" is not an objection.

**Guided practice.** 1. Revised thesis passing all three tests
(contestable, needs both texts, provable). 2. Counter-reading
opening: fair statement + concession ("It is true that ___").
3. Decoration vs. evidence pair, labeled with a reason (decoration:
fact sits beside argument; evidence: fact changes a reading with a
quotation).

**Independent practice (draft).** Grade against the assessment essay
descriptors below (thesis 6, evidence 8, comparison 6,
counter-reading 5, context 5). A draft missing any required element
(2 quotations per text, margin-marked context fact, counter-reading
paragraph, comparison paragraph) is returned for that element before
scoring.

**Applied task (defense).** Full marks: thesis, two quotations with
what each proves, counter-reading + answer in one sentence each, and
an honestly named weakest point. A defense that hides its weakest
point earns at most half marks on the counter-reading row.

**Exit check.** 1. A comparison paragraph states what juxtaposition
shows; two summaries only report each text. 2. Any genuine example
from the draft (fact + quotation + changed reading). 3. Strongest
objection + one-phrase answer; an objection the learner cannot
answer is acceptable if named honestly — note it for the revision
studio.

## Practice sets

**Set A.** 1. Either hero defensible with both phrases quoted;
"ingenious" → cleverness/tricks, "ready at need" → reliability under
pressure. 2. Both: "by sea" locates suffering outside, "in his heart"
locates it inside; the cause (gods, voyage, loss) is shared, the
place of suffering changes — accept "place" or "both" with both
quotations; "cause only" earns half marks. 3. "Sing" asks the Muse to
perform through the poet (song, inspiration, a voice not the poet's
own); "tell" asks for report/information. Accept any version of this
distinction. 4. Two faults: age is not a criterion of accuracy; and
"better" without "better for what" is empty (also: Butler 1900 is not
"older" than Butcher–Lang 1879 in the relevant sense — Butler is
later; a learner who catches the chronology earns a bonus note, not
extra marks).

**Set B.** 1. Confusion: the fact (oral performance) does not entail
the reading (Odysseus as sea-symbol). Rewrite: oral performance means
epithets and repetition aid memory — that is the warranted inference.
2. Reading supported by fact: acceptable as stated (festival
audience → civic hearing), provided the learner notes it as an
inference, not a certainty. 3. Confusion: exile does not make the
forest *mean* exile; it makes exile one warranted lens. Rewrite with
"reads as" language + quotation. 4. Confusion: publication date does
not entail funniness; the humor comes from parody mechanism. 5. Fact
+ warranted reading: acceptable as stated — the law explains why
forgery was the route, within the play's world.

**Set C.** Civic row: scene (decree/confrontation), quotation (e.g.,
"Whome'er the State appoints must be obeyed in everything"),
overreach (extends obedience to "just and unjust alike"). Kinship
row: scene (burial act / Antigone's defense), quotation (e.g., "To
reverence kith and kin can bring no shame"), overreach (may deny the
city's interest in distinguishing traitor from patriot). Teiresias
row: quotation (any genuine Teiresias warning, e.g., on pollution of
altars), overreach (divine warning arrives too late to save anyone;
authority without remedy). Final question: any judgment defended with
the ending's outcome (Antigone, Haemon, Eurydice dead; Creon alive
and ruined) and the both-true point earns credit.

**Set D.** 1. Said: lost in a dark wood, path lost. Meant: a soul
that lost moral direction. Satire? No — it is allegory, not satire:
it gives the reader an image to interpret, not a folly to judge.
(Accept "allegorical, not satiric" or "not satire by the evidence
test.") 2. Said: giants to fight. Meant: Quixote reads the world
through romance. Satire: yes — it gives the reader Sancho's plain
evidence alongside Quixote's frame, so the reader judges. 3. Said:
windmills and sails. Meant: plain fact against romance. Satire hinge:
yes — it proves the fact and proves fact alone does not persuade,
which is the scene's argument. 4. Said: an enchanter changed giants
to mills. Meant: a closed system that absorbs refutation. Satire:
yes — it exposes the mechanism, giving the reader the pattern to
recognize elsewhere. 5. Original paragraph: any 3-sentence satiric
paragraph earns credit if its final sentence names evidence the
reader can judge with (a quoted headline vs. article fact, a
contradiction, a mechanism). A paragraph that only insults its target
earns half marks.

**Set E.** 1. Any three genuine diminutives (e.g., "skylark,"
"squirrel," "little" + pet forms). Implication: Nora exists in
Torvald's language as something small, charming, and kept — a pet,
not a partner. 2. "Other" does the work: it does not cancel duties to
husband and children; it adds a duty of equal sacredness and thereby
demotes the first duties from definitions to items in a list. Any
version of this argument earns credit. 3. Any rewrite (e.g., "Before
all else, you are a wife and a mother — and a person") with a
2–3 sentence explanation of what the addition/reordering changes
(order of identity, who defines her). 4. Contradicting line: "I have
other duties just as sacred" (or "Duties to myself") — it
contradicts "doesn't love" because it *adds* a duty rather than
denying the existing ones; the contradiction matters because it
reframes leaving as reordering, not abandonment.

**Set F.** 1. Fails tests 1 and 2 (not contestable; does not need two
texts — bravery alone is true of either). Rewrite example: "Antigone's
bravery is public and fatal, Nora's is private and open-ended — and
that difference is the difference between tragedy and modern drama."
2. Passes all three (contestable, needs both, provable with Dante's
circles + Quixote's mills + enchanter excuse). 3. Fails test 1 (true
but trivial) and test 2 (Greekness alone is not an argument). Rewrite
must add a theme and a difference. 4. Passes all three. 5. Own seed:
grade by the three tests explicitly; a seed failing any test must be
rewritten in the answer, not merely diagnosed.

**Set G.** 1. Three faults (any three): summary instead of thesis; no
quotations; juxtaposition without comparison; "important" is not
contestable; no context; no counter-reading. 2. Rewrite: 5–7 sentences
with a thesis sentence, one quotation per text, and a comparison
sentence — grade as the comparison paragraph (see below). 3.
Counter-reading sentence + one-sentence answer; a straw-man objection
earns half marks.

## Formative quiz (30 points)

**Part 1 (2 points each).** 1. Translation as interpretation: every
translation chooses among sound, sense, image, character — and the
choice changes meaning. Example required (e.g., "ingenious hero" vs.
"ready at need"). 2. Historical-context method: the four steps (see
Lesson 01 exit check). Example required. 3. Consequential difference:
a difference that changes what the reader understands (hero, theme,
expectation). Example required. 4. Satire: exposes folly/vice with
evidence so the reader judges; insult gives only a verdict. Example
required (windmills/enchanter excuse). 5. Comparative thesis: a
contestable claim about one theme that needs two texts to prove.
Example required. Definitions without examples earn 1 point.

**Part 2 (4 points each).** 6. "Ingenious" → clever, trick-solving
hero; "ready at need" → reliable, enduring hero; the tricks hero is
Butler's. Both phrases quoted. 7. Context fact (e.g., Athenian law
vs. divine/unwritten law; festival civic audience; burial duty as
religious obligation). Claim: Creon's decree, a mortal man's breath,
cannot override laws "not born today nor yesterday." Line quoted.
8. Sancho proves the objects are mills; it changes nothing because
Quixote's system absorbs refutation (enchanter excuse) — the scene's
argument is that evidence alone does not break a beloved story. Line
quoted.

**Part 3 (4 points each).** 9. Antigone: duty as competing public
laws (city vs. divine/kinship), quoted (e.g., "The immutable unwritten
laws of Heaven"). Nora: duty as a reordering ("I have other duties
just as sacred" / "Duties to myself"). Comparison must note the
difference (public clash vs. private discovery), not only that both
concern duty. 10. Any genuine theme + difference + form (e.g., moral
order: Dante sorts sins into circles — ordered journey; Cervantes
refuses sorting — parody in which categories collapse). One quotation
required.

## Unit assessment (60 points)

**Part 1 (5 points each).** 1. Two effects: character (clever vs.
reliable) and expectation (tricks vs. endurance), each with both
phrases quoted. One effect only: 3 points. 2. Fact + changed reading
+ quotation + without-the-fact reading; missing any element loses
1–2 points. 3. Both claims quoted; one sentence on why the ending
refuses a simple winner (death + survival, vindication + ruin).
4. Stated vs. meant + judging evidence + quotation + one sentence on
satire-vs.-insult (evidence test). Missing the evidence-test sentence:
lose 1 point.

**Part 2 — Essay (30 points).** Thesis (6): 6 = contestable, needs
both texts, answers the theme as a question; 4–5 = contestable but
one text dominates or the question is vague; 2–3 = summary-like;
0–1 = no thesis. Evidence and quotation (8): 8 = 2+ integrated
quotations per text, each doing argumentative work; 5–7 = quotations
present but some dropped in or misused; 3–4 = few quotations or
misquotations; 0–2 = no usable evidence. Comparison (6): 6 = a
comparison paragraph stating what juxtaposition shows; 3–5 = comparison
attempted but mostly juxtaposition; 0–2 = two summaries. Counter-
reading (5): 5 = fairly stated + answered with a concession that
survives; 3–4 = stated but straw-manned or unanswered; 0–2 = absent.
Context as evidence (5): 5 = at least 1 fact, margin-marked, changing
a reading with a quotation; 3–4 = fact present but decorative;
0–2 = absent or wrong.

**Part 3 — Exhibit check (5 points each).** 1. Translation judgment:
purpose + one quoted difference + effect. 2. Staging decision: claim
+ textual evidence + risk. Missing any element loses 2 points.
