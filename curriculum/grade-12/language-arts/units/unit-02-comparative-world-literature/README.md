# Unit 02 — Comparative World Literature and Historical Context

**Grade 12 · Language Arts · Advanced literature, writing, and communication pathway · Weeks 6–9 of the course year**
(draft, delivered 2026-10-21 for issue #56; not educator-reviewed)

## Overview

A story does not arrive alone. It arrives in a language that is not
its own, from a century that is not yours, written for readers who
already knew things you have to learn. This unit teaches the
**historical-context method**: before you judge a text, you reconstruct
what its first readers knew, what its form expected, and what its
words could and could not mean — and then you let that reconstruction
change your reading, in public, with evidence.

The unit runs in four moves, following the track scope-and-sequence
(weeks 6–9):

1. **Reading in translation** — the *Odyssey* opening in two
   public-domain English translations (Butler; Butcher and Lang), and
   the epic conventions that make an opening do so much work.
2. **Greek tragedy** — Sophocles' *Antigone*: civic conflict and
   competing duties, and why the play refuses to declare a simple
   winner.
3. **Medieval to early modern** — Dante's *Inferno* (excerpt) and
   Cervantes' *Don Quixote* (excerpt): satire and moral order across
   cultures, and how similar themes are treated in different periods.
4. **Modern world voices** — Ibsen's *A Doll's House*: a play whose
   ending was a public controversy, and what a staging choice does to
   meaning. The unit closes with a comparative essay (two texts, one
   theme) and the comparative exhibit project.

The essential question running through all four weeks is: **what does
a text mean once you know when it was written, for whom, and in what
form — and what happens when you compare two texts that answer the
same human question centuries apart?**

## Prerequisites

From Unit 01 and the track's entry skills (verified in the week-1
diagnostic; insecure skills are re-taught in use):

- Cite strong, thorough textual evidence for explicit and inferential
  claims about literature (RL.11-12.1).
- Determine two or more themes, analyze their development, and write
  an objective summary (RL.11-12.2).
- Analyze an author's choices — structure, point of view, word choice
  — and their contribution to meaning and tone (RL.11-12.3,
  RL.11-12.4, RL.11-12.6).
- Write organized analytical paragraphs and short essays with claims
  and evidence (W.11-12.1, W.11-12.9.a); use the Unit 01 lens
  vocabulary (theme, irony, point of view) without re-teaching it.

The unit does **not** assume fluency with: reading literature in
translation and judging translations against each other; the
historical-context method (context facts as evidence, not decoration);
Greek tragic form (chorus, masks, festival performance); satire as a
mode that can praise, blame, or do both at once; or comparing two
texts' treatments of one theme across periods — those are this unit's
new content.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Compare two public-domain translations of one passage (the
   *Odyssey* opening), naming at least two consequential differences
   between them and explaining, with quoted words, how each difference
   changes what the hero or the poem seems to be. (RL.11-12.4,
   RL.11-12.10; track objectives 1, 4, 5)
2. Apply the historical-context method to each anchor text: state at
   least three verifiable context facts per text and show, for each
   fact, how it changes a specific reading of a specific passage —
   rather than listing facts beside the text. (RL.11-12.9; track
   objectives 4, 10)
3. Analyze the competing duties in *Antigone* (city law, kinship and
   burial duty, divine law), evaluating Creon's and Antigone's claims
   with quoted evidence and explaining why the play's ending refuses a
   simple verdict. (RL.11-12.1, RL.11-12.3; track objectives 1, 3)
4. Compare how two texts from different periods treat one shared theme
   (moral order, duty, or illusion versus reality), showing how each
   period's form — epic, tragedy, satire, modern drama — shapes the
   treatment, including at least one case where satire says one thing
   and means another. (RL.11-12.9, RL.11-12.6; track objectives 3, 4)
5. Write a comparative essay (drafted, peer-checked, and revised) that
   advances a thesis about one theme across two anchor texts, develops
   it with quoted evidence from both, uses historical context as
   evidence, and acknowledges and answers a competing reading.
   (W.11-12.1, W.11-12.9.a; track objectives 4, 8, 10)

## Vocabulary

**Unit terms** (used in lessons and assessed): *translation,
translator's choice, literal vs. literary translation, epic, invocation
to the Muse, in medias res, epithet, chorus, tragic conflict, civic
duty, kinship duty, divine law, satire, parody, moral order, historical
context, anachronism, staging, interpretation, comparative thesis,
counter-reading.* Recognition terms: *catharsis, hubris, chivalric
romance, naturalism (drama).*

**The historical-context method, in four steps** (posted on the wall
for the whole unit): (1) What did the first audience know without being
told? (2) What did the form require or forbid? (3) What does this
passage assume about the world? (4) What does the context change about
your first reading — in one sentence, with one quotation?

## Standards notes

Codes and short descriptions verified 2026-10-08 against the official
Common Core State Standards for grades 11–12 at
<https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/> (the track audit's
source-backed crosswalk, scope-and-sequence §5). Descriptions here are
paraphrases; this crosswalk is a planning reference, not a claim of
state adoption, accreditation, or complete alignment.

- **RL.11-12.1** — cite strong, thorough evidence for analysis and
  inferences, including where the text leaves matters uncertain.
- **RL.11-12.2** — determine two or more themes, analyze how they
  interact and build to a complex account; write an objective summary.
- **RL.11-12.3** — analyze the impact of the author's choices in
  developing story elements (used for Antigone's and Nora's choices).
- **RL.11-12.4** — determine word/phrase meaning including figurative
  and connotative senses; analyze word-choice impact on meaning and
  tone (the translation-comparison standard).
- **RL.11-12.6** — analyze point of view requiring distinguishing what
  is stated from what is meant (satire, sarcasm, irony, understatement)
  — the *Inferno*/*Quixote* standard.
- **RL.11-12.7** — analyze multiple interpretations of a work across
  versions, including a Shakespeare play and a play by an American
  dramatist; applied here as the skill — comparing stagings and
  translations of one work — not as a claim about the standard's text
  list.
- **RL.11-12.9** — analyze how two or more texts from the same period
  treat similar themes or topics (and, in the standard's letter,
  foundational American works of the 18th–early-20th centuries). This
  unit claims the *comparison skill* for world texts across periods;
  it does not claim the standard's text list covers these works.
- **RL.11-12.10** — read literature in the 11–CCR complexity band
  proficiently, independently at the high end by year's end.
- **L.11-12.5.a** — interpret figures of speech in context; analyze
  their role in the text.
- **W.11-12.1.a–e** — write arguments with precise claims, fair
  counterclaims, cohesion, formal style, and a supported conclusion
  (the comparative essay).
- **W.11-12.9.a** — draw evidence from literary texts to support
  analysis and reflection.
- **W.11-12.10** — write routinely over extended and shorter time
  frames (context notebooks, drafting studios).
- **SL.11-12.1.a–d** — prepared, civil, evidence-based discussion
  (the Antigone seminar and the comparative thesis defense).

## Anchor texts and editions

All anchor texts are public domain in the USA; all quotations in
lessons were checked against these editions on 2026-10-21. A guiding
adult selects any licensed editions through the learner's library; no
copyrighted text is reproduced in this unit.

- Homer, *The Odyssey*, translated by Samuel Butler (1900), Project
  Gutenberg ebook 1727:
  <https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1727/1727-h/1727-h.htm> — Book I
  opening and the Telemachus episodes, read in excerpt. The second
  translation for comparison is S. H. Butcher and A. Lang's prose
  translation, Project Gutenberg ebook 1728:
  <https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1728/1728-h/1728-h.htm> (quoted in
  Butler's own preface, which this unit uses as its comparison text).
- Sophocles, *Antigone*, translated by F. Storr (Loeb, 1912), in *The
  Oedipus Trilogy*, Project Gutenberg ebook 31:
  <http://www.gutenberg.org/files/31/31-h/31-h.htm> — read in full
  (short play); the confrontation scene is printed in Lesson 02.
- Dante Alighieri, *Divine Comedy, Longfellow's Translation, Hell*
  (*Inferno*), translated by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, Project
  Gutenberg ebook 1001: <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1001> —
  Canto I and selected later cantos, read in excerpt.
- Miguel de Cervantes, *Don Quixote*, translated by John Ormsby,
  Project Gutenberg ebook 996:
  <https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/996/pg996-images.html> — Part
  I, Chapter VIII (the windmills) and the prologue discussion of
  chivalric romances, read in excerpt.
- Henrik Ibsen, *A Doll's House*, translated by R. Farquharson Sharp,
  Project Gutenberg ebook 2542:
  <https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2542> — Act III, read in full;
  Acts I–II in summary with selected scenes.

## Differentiation

- **Reading access:** the older translations (Butler, Storr,
  Longfellow, Ormsby) are 19th-century-inflected; the adult may read
  the anchor openings aloud while the learner follows, or pair reading
  with free LibriVox recordings (see resource pack). Excerpts are
  chunked to the session-sized sections named above; the *Odyssey*
  and *Don Quixote* are never assigned whole.
- **Language access:** the four-step context method is taught as
  questions, not definitions; the learner may answer exit checks
  orally with the adult scribing.
- **Alternate response modes:** annotations may be marginal notes,
  voice memos, or a two-column notebook (quotation | context
  question); the seminar may be a written dialogue.
- **Extensions:** compare a third translation of the *Odyssey*
  opening from the learner's library; research one context fact to a
  primary source (e.g., Dante's exile documents in summary, or
  contemporary reviews of *A Doll's House*); stage the Antigone
  confrontation two ways and defend the stronger staging.
- **Support:** each lesson names likely misconceptions and the
  teacher guide gives reteaching moves; insecure prerequisite skills
  are re-taught in use, not as a separate unit.
