# Lesson 02 — Scale Areas and a Different Scale

**Goal:** You will compute actual areas from a scale drawing and
reproduce a scale drawing at a different scale.
**Time:** 45 minutes · **Materials:** paper, pencil, ruler
**Unit:** [Unit 05](../README.md) · **Objective 1** · **Standard 7.G.A.1**

## Readiness check (3 min)

1. A rectangle is drawn 5 cm by 3 cm. What is its drawn area?
   (15 cm².)
2. The scale is 1 cm = 4 m. A line drawn 5 cm long is really how
   long? (20 m.)
3. 4² = ? (16.) Why might a square matter when areas are scaled?

## Teaching

Lesson 01 scaled **lengths**. Areas scale differently, and the
difference is the whole lesson: when every length is multiplied by
the scale factor, the area is multiplied by the **square** of the
scale factor.

> If the scale is 1 cm = k (actual units), then
> actual length = drawn length × k, and
> actual area = drawn area × k².

Why? A rectangle drawn a cm by b cm has drawn area a × b cm². Its
real sides are (a × k) and (b × k), so its real area is
(a × k) × (b × k) = (a × b) × k². The k² is not a trick — it is what
happens when two lengths are each scaled. The same rule works in
reverse: to reproduce a drawing at a **different scale**, first find
the actual lengths (Lesson 01), then divide by the new scale factor.

**Worked example 1.** A room is drawn 5 cm by 3 cm. The scale is
1 cm = 4 m. What is the room's actual area?

Actual length: 5 × 4 = 20 m. Actual width: 3 × 4 = 12 m.
Actual area: 20 × 12 = **240 m²**.
Check with the square: drawn area = 15 cm²; k² = 4² = 16;
15 × 16 = 240 m². ✓ Both routes agree.

**Worked example 2.** The garden from Lesson 01 is really 24 m long
and 16 m wide (drawn 12 cm × 8 cm at 1 cm = 2 m). Redraw it at the
new scale 1 cm = 4 m. How big is the new drawing? How big is one bed
drawn?

World → page, divide by the new factor 4. Garden: 24 ÷ 4 = **6 cm**
long, 16 ÷ 4 = **4 cm** wide. One bed is really 18 m by 4 m, so it is
drawn 18 ÷ 4 = **4.5 cm** by 4 ÷ 4 = **1 cm**. The shed (4 m by 4 m)
is drawn **1 cm by 1 cm**. The new drawing is smaller than the old
one because each centimeter now stands for more meters.

**Worked example 3 (error analysis).** Mia finds the garden's drawn
area: 12 × 8 = 96 cm². The scale is 1 cm = 2 m. She computes the
actual area as 96 × 2 = 192 m². What is her error, and what is the
correct area?

Mia scaled the area by the *linear* factor 2, but area needs the
squared factor: 2² = 4. Correct area: 96 × 4 = **384 m²**.
Check by lengths: the real garden is 24 m by 16 m, and
24 × 16 = 384 m². ✓ Her answer is exactly half the truth — the
missing factor of 2 is the second dimension she forgot to scale.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** A patio is drawn 4 cm by 6 cm. The scale is 1 cm = 3 m. What
is its actual area?
*Hint: scale each side first (12 m by 18 m), then multiply.*

**G2.** A square is drawn with side 5 cm. The scale is 1 cm = 2 m.
What is its actual area?
*Hint: actual side 10 m; or drawn area 25 cm² × 2².*

**G3.** A poster is drawn 9 cm by 6 cm at 1 cm = 2 m. Reproduce it at
1 cm = 3 m. What are the new drawn dimensions?
*Hint: actual size first (18 m by 12 m), then divide by 3.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** A field is drawn 7 cm by 2 cm. The scale is 1 cm = 5 m. What
is its actual area?
**I2.** A drawn shape has area 20 cm². The scale is 1 cm = 3 m. What
is its actual area?
**I3.** A room is really 15 m long and 9 m wide. At a scale of
1 cm = 3 m, how large is it drawn?
**I4.** A garden bed is really 18 m by 4 m (Lesson 01). At a scale of
1 cm = 4 m, how large is it drawn, and what is its actual area?

## Applied task

A banner is drawn 10 cm by 6 cm at a scale of 1 cm = 2 m. (a) What
is the banner's actual area? (b) You redraw it at 1 cm = 5 m. What
are the new drawn dimensions, and why is the new drawing smaller?
(c) A classmate says the banner's actual area is now 240 ÷ 5 = 48 m².
Explain the error in one or two sentences.

## Exit check

A rectangle is drawn 3 cm by 2 cm. The scale is 1 cm = 6 m. What is
its actual area? Show both the side lengths and the area.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: scaling area by the linear factor instead of
its square (Worked example 3 is the antidote — always verify an area
answer by scaling the sides first). A second confusion: thinking a
re-drawn figure at a larger scale factor changes the real object — it
does not; only the drawing changes. Answers: see the
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
