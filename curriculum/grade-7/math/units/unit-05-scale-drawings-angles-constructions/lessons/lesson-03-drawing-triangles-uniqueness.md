# Lesson 03 — Drawing Triangles: One, Many, or None

**Goal:** You will draw triangles from given conditions and decide
whether three measures determine a unique triangle, more than one
triangle, or no triangle.
**Time:** 45 minutes · **Materials:** paper, pencil, ruler, protractor,
compass (adult supervises), graph paper
**Unit:** [Unit 05](../README.md) · **Objective 2** · **Standard 7.G.A.2**

## Readiness check (3 min)

1. The angles of any triangle add to how many degrees? (180°.)
2. A triangle has sides 5 cm, 5 cm, and 8 cm. Is it equilateral,
   isosceles, or scalene? (Isosceles — two equal sides.)
3. Draw a right angle with a protractor. How many degrees is it? (90°.)

## Warm-up: triangle spotting (10 min)

Before constructing anything, look for triangles in the real world
— this rebuilds the legacy triangle-spotting field lab with explicit
directions (the original pointed to a "Shared spec" that does not
exist, so use this list instead). Adult-accompanied, outdoors or
around the home:

1. Find at least 4 real-world triangles (roof lines, shelf brackets,
   folded paper, road signs, bridges — no photographs of people,
   addresses, or license plates).
2. Sketch each one and estimate or measure its side lengths.
3. Classify each by side length (equilateral, isosceles, scalene)
   and by angle (acute, right, obtuse), and write one sentence of
   evidence for each classification ("two sides measured equal, so
   isosceles").
4. Pick one spotted triangle and copy it to scale on graph paper
   (choose a scale, for example 1 cm = 10 cm of real length, and
   record it).

Keep the sketches — Lesson 06 returns to them.

## Teaching

A triangle is fixed by **three measures** — but not every set of
three measures fixes it the same way.

- **Three sides (SSS):** if the sides can form a triangle at all, the
  triangle is **unique**. There is only one shape with those three
  side lengths.
- **Two sides and the angle between them (SAS):** **unique**. The
  included angle locks the third side in place.
- **Two angles and a side (ASA or AAS):** **unique**. The third angle
  is forced (angles sum to 180°), and the side sets the size.
- **Two sides and an angle that is *not* between them (SSA):**
  **more than one** triangle can result. The swinging side can meet
  the base in two different places — picture a compass arc crossing
  the base line twice. This is the one ambiguous case.
- **Three angles only (AAA):** **many** triangles — all the same shape
  (similar) but any size. Angles fix shape, not size.
- **No triangle:** when the measures cannot close into a triangle at
  all. The test is the **triangle inequality**: a triangle exists
  only when the sum of *any* two side lengths is **greater than**
  the third. If the two shorter sides cannot reach across the
  longest side, there is no triangle — the sides lie flat or fall
  short.

**Worked example 1.** Can sides 5 cm, 6 cm, and 7 cm form a triangle?
If so, is it unique?

Check the inequality three times: 5 + 6 = 11 > 7 ✓; 5 + 7 = 12 > 6 ✓;
6 + 7 = 13 > 5 ✓. All three pass, so the triangle exists. Three sides
given means SSS, so it is **unique** — only one triangle has sides
5, 6, and 7 cm. (Try it with a ruler and compass: the arcs meet in
exactly one place above the base.)

**Worked example 2.** Can sides 3 cm, 4 cm, and 8 cm form a triangle?

Check: 3 + 4 = 7, and 7 is **not** greater than 8. The two short sides
together are shorter than the long side — they cannot reach across
it. **No triangle.** (If you try to build it, the 3 cm and 4 cm sides
lie flat along the 8 cm side and never meet.)

**Worked example 3.** Draw a triangle with angles 50° and 60° and
the side between them 5 cm. Is it unique?

Two angles and the included side is ASA, so **unique**. The third
angle is forced: 180° − 50° − 60° = **70°**. Any triangle drawn with
these measures is the same triangle (flipped or rotated, but the
same size and shape).

**Worked example 4.** Two sides are 5 cm and 7 cm, and the angle
opposite the 5 cm side is 30°. Is the triangle unique?

This is SSA — the angle is *not* between the two given sides. Fix
the 7 cm side as the base and the 30° angle at one end; the 5 cm side
swings on a compass arc from the other end. The arc can cross the
base line in **two** places, giving two different triangles with the
same three measures. **More than one triangle.** At this grade you
do not need to construct both — you need to recognize the SSA
pattern and say "ambiguous."

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Sides 4 cm, 9 cm, 12 cm. Does a triangle exist? Is it unique?
*Hint: check the smallest two sides first: 4 + 9 = 13. Is 13 > 12?*

**G2.** Two angles are 30° and 90°, and the side between them is
4 cm. Is the triangle unique? What is the third angle?
*Hint: ASA — and 180° − 30° − 90° = ?*

**G3.** Sides 2 cm, 5 cm, 9 cm. Does a triangle exist?
*Hint: 2 + 5 = 7. Compare with 9.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Sides 6 cm, 8 cm, 10 cm. Does a triangle exist? Is it unique?
(Extra: check 6² + 8² against 10².)
**I2.** Three angles are given: 40°, 60°, 80°. No side lengths. How
many triangles fit? Explain in one sentence.
**I3.** Two sides are 8 cm and 8 cm, and the angle between them is
40°. Is the triangle unique? What kind of triangle is it by sides?
**I4.** Sides 10 cm, 4 cm, 5 cm. Does a triangle exist? Show the
failing check.

## Applied task

A builder has two boards, 3 m and 4 m long, and wants a triangular
brace. (a) Would a third board of 2 m work? Of 7 m? Of 8 m? Test each
with the triangle inequality. (b) In one or two sentences, explain
why the third board cannot be 7 m or longer.

## Exit check

Sides 9 cm, 12 cm, 15 cm. Does a triangle exist, and is it unique?
Show the inequality check for the two shorter sides.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "three measures always means one triangle" —
the SSA and AAA cases break that rule, and the triangle inequality
breaks it again. Have the learner *try* the failing construction
(Worked example 2) with strips of paper: the gap is visible and
memorable. For SSA, a compass arc crossing a line twice is the
picture; do not demand a formal two-triangle proof. Answers: see the
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
