# Lesson 06 — Multi-Step Real-World Problems

**Goal (student-readable):** I can solve multi-step problems using all four
operations on rational numbers, and I can check my answers with estimation
and a number-line sketch.
**Time:** 45 minutes · **Materials:** paper, pencil, calculator for
checking only.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Estimate, then compute exactly: (−48.6) + 51.2 − (−3.8).
(Estimate: −50 + 50 + 4 = 4. Exact: −48.6 + 51.2 = 2.6; 2.6 + 3.8 = 6.4.)
If your estimate and exact answer disagreed wildly, which one would you
trust — and what would you recheck? (Recheck the exact work; estimation
only flags, never proves.)

## Teaching

**The problem-solving routine.** (1) Name what each number means and its
sign — units included. (2) Write one expression before computing; this
forces you to plan the operations. (3) Estimate first: round to friendly
numbers and predict the sign. (4) Compute step by step, showing each
operation. (5) Reason-check: does the sign match the estimate? Is the
size plausible? Does a number-line sketch agree?

**Estimation is a guardrail, not an answer.** Round −7.8 × 3.1 to
−8 × 3 = −24, then compute exactly: −24.18. The estimate says "about −24";
if you had gotten +24.18, the estimate would catch the sign error even
though it can't give the exact digits.

**Complex fractions in the wild.** A rate like "2/3 of a tank per 4/5 of
an hour" is the complex fraction (2/3)/(4/5) = (2/3) × (5/4) = 10/12 =
5/6 of a tank per hour. Whenever a rate's numerator and denominator are
both fractions, rewrite the long bar as ÷ and flip.

**Mean with signed data.** The mean of signed numbers works exactly like
grade 6: sum, then divide by the count. The sign of the mean tells you
which side of zero the data leans. Temperature changes of +2, −5, −1, +4
(°C) sum to 0 — the mean change is 0°C: rises and falls balanced out.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A hiker starts at −30 m (below the trailhead datum), climbs
85 m, descends 120 m, then climbs 50 m. Final elevation?

Plan: −30 + 85 + (−120) + 50. Estimate: −30 + 90 − 120 + 50 = −10.
Compute: −30 + 85 = 55; 55 + (−120) = −65; −65 + 50 = −15.
**Final elevation: −15 m.** Reason check: sign negative ✓ (estimate −10,
close); size plausible ✓. Number-line sketch: start −30, hop right 85,
hop left 120, hop right 50, land −15. ✓

**Example 2.** A small business account: starts at $0, withdraws $450 for
supplies (−450), deposits 2/3 of that withdrawal back from a refund, then
pays a $75 fee. Ending balance? As a complex-fraction check, what fraction
of the withdrawal was the final balance's magnitude?

Expression: 0 + (−450) + (2/3)(450) + (−75) = −450 + 300 − 75 = −225.
**Ending balance: −$225** (overdrawn $225). Estimate: −450 + 300 − 75 ≈
−225 ✓.
Fraction check: |−225| / 450 = 225/450 = 1/2 — the final overdraft is
exactly half the original withdrawal. (As a complex fraction:
(−225)/(−450) = 225/450 = 1/2.) ✓

**Example 3.** Over five days a stock's daily changes ($) are
−1.25, +2.50, −0.75, −1.50, +0.25. (a) Total change? (b) Mean daily
change? (c) If the stock started at $40, what is it worth now?

(a) Sum: −1.25 + 2.50 = 1.25; 1.25 − 0.75 = 0.50; 0.50 − 1.50 = −1.00;
−1.00 + 0.25 = −0.75. **Total change: −$0.75.**
(b) Mean: −0.75 ÷ 5 = **−$0.15 per day.**
(c) 40 + (−0.75) = **$39.25.**
Estimate check: positives (2.75) roughly cancel negatives (3.50) → small
negative total ✓; mean must be total ÷ 5 = −0.15 ✓ (a common error is
dividing by 4).

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. A submarine at −150 m rises 60 m, dives 95 m, then rises 40 m. Write
   the expression, estimate, and compute the final depth.
   *(Hint: "rises" = positive, "dives" = negative.)*
2. Compute the mean of: −8, 3, −2, 7, −5, −1. *(Hint: sum first; watch
   the count — six numbers.)*
3. A tank drains at 2/3 liter per minute for 4.5 minutes, then 15 liters
   are added. Write one expression for the net change in liters and
   evaluate it. *(Hint: 4.5 = 9/2.)*
4. Simplify (5/6)/(−10/3) and explain what the negative sign in the
   answer means if this were "tank fraction per hour."
5. Estimate (−19.8) ÷ (4.1) − (−3.2), then compute exactly. Does the
   estimate catch a sign error if you make one?

## Independent practice

6. Morning temperature −4.5°C; it rises 7.25°C by noon, falls 9.5°C by
   midnight, then rises 2.75°C by the next dawn. Final temperature?
   Show estimate + exact.
7. A diver's depth changes (m): −12, +5, −8, −3, +10, −6 (relative to
   start at the surface). (a) Final depth? (b) Total distance traveled
   vertically? *(Hint: (b) needs absolute values — distance, not
   displacement.)*
8. You have $120. You spend 1/4 of it, then earn $35 babysitting, then
   spend 2/3 of what remains. How much is left? Write the full expression
   first.
9. **Error analysis.** Kim computes the mean of −6, −2, 4 as (−6 − 2 + 4)
   ÷ 2 = −2. Find two errors (count and one arithmetic slip) and give the
   correct mean with a number-line reason for its sign.
10. A recipe's ingredient ratio is (3/4 cup flour) per (1/2 cup sugar).
    Write the complex fraction, simplify it, and state the unit rate with
    units. Then scale: how much flour for 2.5 cups of sugar?

## Application

**Five-day forecast audit.** Using any free no-account weather source (or
the adult's phone), record your town's actual high temperatures for five
consecutive days, including any below-freezing days as negatives in °F or
°C (pick one scale and stick to it). Compute (a) the mean high,
(b) the largest single-day swing |high − low| if lows are available,
(c) the total five-day change from day 1's high to day 5's high.
Write a three-sentence weather summary using the words *mean*, *swing*,
and *change* correctly. If no weather source is available, use the
fictional dataset in [project.md](../project.md#dataset-b-fictional-harbor-town-week).

## Exit check

1. Why do we estimate *before* computing, not after?
2. In problem 7(b) above, why can't you just add the signed numbers?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** underline the verb in each sentence of a
  word problem (rises, dives, withdraws) and write its sign above it
  before building the expression.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the expression with signed number
  cards on the desk, then compute left to right on the number line.
- **Extension:** A "round-trip" problem: start at 0, apply a sequence of
  four signed operations that returns exactly to 0, where no two
  consecutive operations have the same sign. Prove yours works and argue
  whether the *order* of your four operations matters for the final result
  (it doesn't for addition — why?).

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners compute left-to-right without writing the planned expression,
  then lose track of which operation applied to which number. Require the
  expression first — it is the plan, and plans are gradable.
- Mean problems: dividing by the wrong count, or forgetting that the mean
  of signed data can be negative. The number-line reason ("more weight on
  the negative side") fixes the sign intuition.
- In multi-step contexts learners drop units mid-problem. Keep units in
  every line of the worked solution; a unit mismatch is often the first
  sign of a setup error.
