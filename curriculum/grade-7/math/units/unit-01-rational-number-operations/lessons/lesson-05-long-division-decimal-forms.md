# Lesson 05 — Long Division and Decimal Forms

**Goal (student-readable):** I can convert any quotient of integers to a
decimal by long division, and I can tell whether the decimal terminates or
repeats.
**Time:** 45 minutes · **Materials:** paper, pencil, calculator for
checking only.

## Readiness check (5 min)

By hand, divide: (a) 1 ÷ 2; (b) 1 ÷ 4; (c) 1 ÷ 3 — carry (c) to four
decimal places. (Answers: 0.5; 0.25; 0.3333….) The first two stopped; the
third didn't. Today's question: does it ever stop, and how do you know?

## Teaching

**Every quotient of integers is a rational number** — that is what
"rational" means: expressible as p/q with integers p, q and q ≠ 0.
Conversely, every rational number has a decimal form, and long division
finds it.

**Terminating decimals.** 3/8: divide 3.000 by 8. 8 goes into 30 three
times (24), remainder 6; bring down 0 → 60; 8 goes seven times (56),
remainder 4; bring down 0 → 40; 8 goes five times exactly. **3/8 = 0.375.**
The division ended because the remainder hit 0.

**Repeating decimals.** 5/6: 5.000 ÷ 6. 6 goes into 50 eight times (48),
remainder 2; bring down 0 → 20; 6 goes three times (18), remainder 2 —
the *same remainder as before*. From here the digits repeat forever:
**5/6 = 0.8333… = 0.8̄3̄** (the bar marks the repeating block).
Key insight: **a remainder that repeats forces the digits to repeat.**

**Why only these two outcomes?** When dividing by q, every remainder is
less than q, so there are only q possible remainders (0 through q−1). If
0 appears, the decimal terminates. Otherwise, within at most q steps a
remainder must repeat (pigeonhole principle — only q−1 nonzero options),
and from that repeat the whole digit block cycles. So every rational
number's decimal **terminates or eventually repeats** — there is no third
option.

**Signs.** The sign rules from Lesson 04 apply: −(p/q) = (−p)/q = p/(−q).
So −3/8 = −0.375 and (−5)/6 = −0.8̄3̄. Divide the absolute values, then set
the sign.

**Spotting termination in advance.** A fraction in lowest terms terminates
exactly when its denominator's prime factors are only 2s and 5s (the prime
factors of 10). 3/8 = 3/2³ → terminates. 7/20 = 7/(2²×5) → terminates
(0.35). 5/6 = 5/(2×3) → the 3 forces repetition. This is a prediction
tool, not a replacement for dividing — verify by dividing.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Convert −7/11 to a decimal.

Ignore the sign; divide 7 by 11: 7.000… ÷ 11. 11 into 70 → 6 (66),
remainder 4; bring down 0 → 40; 11 into 40 → 3 (33), remainder 7 — the
original dividend's remainder. The cycle is 63: **7/11 = 0.6̄3̄**, so
**−7/11 = −0.6̄3̄**. Prediction check: 11 has prime factor 11 (not 2 or 5)
→ repeats. ✓

**Example 2.** Convert 9/16 and decide termination first.

16 = 2⁴, only 2s → terminates. Divide: 9.0000 ÷ 16. 16 into 90 → 5 (80),
remainder 10 → 100; 16 into 100 → 6 (96), remainder 4 → 40; 16 into 40 →
2 (32), remainder 8 → 80; 16 into 80 → 5 exactly. **9/16 = 0.5625.** ✓

**Example 3.** Which is larger, −0.6̄ or −0.65?

Write digits: −0.666… vs −0.650. On the number line, −0.65 is right of
−0.666…, so **−0.65 > −0.6̄**. Trap check: for negatives, the number
closer to zero is larger — "more digits" does not mean "bigger."

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Predict terminating or repeating, then divide to verify: (a) 5/8;
   (b) 4/9; (c) 11/25. *(Hint: factor the denominator after reducing.)*
2. Convert: (a) −2/3; (b) (−7)/20; (c) 13/6. Write repeating blocks with
   bar notation.
3. Order from least to greatest: 2/3, 0.6, 0.6̄, 3/5.
   *(Hint: write each as a decimal first.)*
4. A recipe calls for 5/6 cup of flour per batch and you make 4 batches.
   How many cups is that as a decimal? Is an exact decimal possible?
5. Explain in your own words why 1/7 must repeat, without dividing it out.

## Independent practice

6. Convert to decimals: (a) 7/40; (b) −11/12; (c) 5/11; (d) −9/25.
7. True or false, with a reason: (a) 0.1̄4̄ = 0.141414…; (b) Every
   terminating decimal can be written as a fraction with denominator a
   power of 10; (c) 22/7 = π.
8. Between 0.3̄ and 0.34 there are infinitely many rationals. Name three
   and write each as a fraction.
9. **Error analysis.** Alex divides 5 by 6, gets 0.83, and stops. Explain
   what Alex missed and why stopping early changes the value.
   *(0.83 vs 0.833… — how big is the error after 4 batches of the
   recipe in #4?)*
10. Write −4/15 as a decimal. Then find |−4/15 − 0.2̄6̄| as a decimal —
    what does the result tell you?

## Application

**Measurement lab.** A ruler is marked in sixteenths of an inch. Measure
three small objects to the nearest sixteenth, write each length as a
fraction, convert to a decimal by long division, and confirm each
terminates (why must it?). Then measure one object with a centimeter ruler
marked in millimeters: write the length as a fraction of a centimeter
(e.g., 37/10 cm) and convert. Which measuring system gives terminating
decimals more naturally, and why?

## Exit check

1. Convert −5/12 to a decimal with bar notation. Show the repeating
   remainder.
2. A classmate claims 2/13 terminates "because 13 is prime." Agree or
   disagree, with the denominator-factor reason.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** keep a long-division template posted
  (divide → multiply → subtract → bring down → repeat) and narrate each
  step aloud while writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** use graph paper — one digit per square —
  to keep place values aligned during long division.
- **Extension:** Find the repetend length of 1/17 by dividing (it is 16
  digits — the maximum possible for denominator 17). Then explain why the
  repetend of 1/q can never be longer than q − 1 digits.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners write 0.333… = 1/3 correctly but then treat 0.9̄ as "less than
  1." Note honestly that 0.9̄ = 1 (every 9 added halves the remaining gap;
  the infinite sum is exactly 1) — state it as a fact to revisit in
  grade 8, not a proof to demand now.
- 22/7 ≈ 3.142857… is a repeating rational approximation of π, not π
  itself — π is irrational and never repeats. Learners often conflate
  them; the bar notation only ever marks rationals.
- When comparing negative repeating decimals, learners compare digit
  strings as if positive. Always reroute through the number line.
