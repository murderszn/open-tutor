# Lesson 01 — Integers on the Number Line

**Goal (student-readable):** I can place integers on a number line, name
opposites, use absolute value as distance from 0, and order rational
numbers.
**Time:** 45 minutes · **Materials:** number line −20 to 20 (print or draw),
paper, pencil, ruler.

## Readiness check (5 min)

Point to each of these on your number line and say them aloud: 7, −7, 0,
−3, 4. Then answer: which two are the same distance from 0? (Answer: 7 and
−7; both are 7 units from 0.) If pointing is easier than saying, point and
have the adult confirm.

## Teaching

**Integers** are the whole numbers … −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, … — positives,
negatives, and zero. On a horizontal number line, numbers increase to the
right. That one fact powers the whole unit: *moving right means larger,
moving left means smaller.*

**Opposites (additive inverses).** Two numbers are opposites if they are
the same distance from 0 but on different sides. The opposite of 5 is −5;
the opposite of −8 is 8. The opposite of 0 is 0 itself — it is its own
opposite because it sits exactly at zero distance from 0.

**Absolute value** |n| is the distance of n from 0 on the number line.
Distance is never negative, so absolute value is never negative: |−9| = 9
and |9| = 9. Think of it as "how far from zero," ignoring the sign.

**Ordering.** To compare two rational numbers, place both on the number
line: the one farther right is larger. So −4 < −1, because −4 sits left of
−1 — even though 4 > 1, the negative signs flip the order. A common trap:
"the bigger the number part, the bigger the number" fails for negatives.
−9 < −2 because −9 is farther left.

Look at the illustration
[assets/elevation-number-line-scene.png](../assets/elevation-number-line-scene.png):
the ocean surface is the zero line. The mountain peak rising above the
surface is at a **positive** elevation; the submarine and the fish below
the surface are at **negative** elevations (depths). Elevation above sea
level and depth below it are opposite quantities — the same idea as numbers
above and below zero.

> **Text alternative (use instead of the image if you prefer):** Imagine a
> sea-level line marked 0. A mountain peak stands 800 meters above it
> (elevation +800). A submarine cruises 200 meters below the surface
> (elevation −200). A fish swims 50 meters below the surface (elevation
> −50). Positive numbers measure above the line; negative numbers measure
> below it.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Name the opposite of −6 and find |−6|.

Opposite: the number the same distance from 0 on the other side → **6**.
Absolute value: distance from 0 → **|−6| = 6**. Notice both answers are 6,
but they answer different questions: "which number is opposite?" vs. "how
far from zero?"

**Example 2.** Order −3, 5, −8, 0, 2 from least to greatest.

Place each on the number line. Leftmost first: −8 is left of −3, which is
left of 0, then 2, then 5. So **−8 < −3 < 0 < 2 < 5**.
Reason check: every negative is less than 0 and less than every positive;
among negatives, the one with the larger number part is smaller.

**Example 3.** A submarine is at −120 meters and a fish is at −35 meters.
Which is deeper? What is each one's distance from the surface?

Deeper means farther below the surface → the more negative number: **the
submarine (−120 m) is deeper**. Distances from the surface: |−120| = 120 m
and |−35| = 35 m.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. On your number line, plot −10, −4, 3, −1, 0. *(Hint: mark 0 first, then
   count left for negatives.)*
2. Write the opposite of each: 12, −5, 0, −23.
3. Find each absolute value: |14|, |−14|, |0|, |−3.5|.
   *(Hint: absolute value only asks "how far from 0?")*
4. Insert <, >, or =: (a) −7 __ −2; (b) −1.5 __ 0; (c) |−6| __ |4|;
   (d) −(3) __ −3.
5. A weather report says −8°C overnight and −2°C in the morning. Which
   temperature is colder? How many degrees colder?

## Independent practice

6. Order from greatest to least: −6, 4, −11, 0, −2, 7.
7. True or false, with a one-sentence reason each: (a) |−5| > 4;
   (b) −(−9) = 9; (c) The opposite of −0.5 is 0.5; (d) −12 < −12.
8. List three integers between −4 and 3, then three rational numbers (not
   integers) between −4 and 3.
9. A scuba diver descends to −18 feet. Her buddy stays at −9 feet. Who is
   deeper, and by how many feet? (Use absolute values.)
10. Draw a vertical number line from −10 (bottom) to 10 (top). Plot the
    freezing point of water (0°C), a freezer temperature (−18°C), and room
    temperature (20°C won't fit — mark it with an arrow). Label each.

## Application

**Elevation journal.** Using the illustration (or the text alternative),
write each as a signed integer and order them: the mountain peak (+800 m),
the submarine (−200 m), a fish (−50 m), sea level itself (0), and a cloud
at +1500 m. Then answer: what is the vertical distance between the
submarine and the fish? (Hint: distance = |(−200) − (−50)| = |−150| = 150 m.)

## Exit check

1. What is |−13|, and what does it mean in words?
2. Which is greater, −6 or −4? Draw a quick number-line sketch showing why.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** act out the number line — tape one to the
  floor, walk right for positive, left for negative; "opposite" = "turn
  around to face the same distance the other way."
- **Alternate response mode:** for ordering tasks, cut out number cards and
  arrange them physically before writing.
- **Extension:** Between −3 and −2 there are infinitely many rational
  numbers (e.g., −2.5, −2.75). Name five, and argue why you can always find
  one more between any two you name.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners often read −7 as "bigger" than −3 because 7 > 3. Anchor every
  comparison to the number line, not to digit size, until the habit forms.
- Some learners think |−5| = −5 "because the negative stays." Stress that
  absolute value is a distance and distances are never negative.
- Zero is neither positive nor negative; its opposite is itself. Say it
  explicitly — many learners hesitate on 0.
