# Lesson 03 — Testing Proportionality with Tables

**Goal:** You will decide whether two quantities are proportional by
testing for equivalent ratios in a table — and explain your verdict.
**Time:** 45 minutes · **Materials:** paper, pencil, calculator for checking only
**Unit:** [Unit 02](../README.md) · **Objective 2** · **Standard 7.RP.A.2.a**

## Readiness check (3 min)

1. Are 2/3 and 8/12 equivalent? How do you know?
2. A table shows (2, 6), (3, 9), (5, 15). What do you notice?
3. If 4 notebooks cost $10, and the price is "fair" (same price each),
   how much do 10 notebooks cost?

## Teaching

Two quantities are in a **proportional relationship** when every pair in
the table forms an **equivalent ratio** — the ratios all simplify to the
same value. "Same taste no matter the batch size" means the ratio never
changes.

**The test:** for each row, compute y ÷ x (the second quantity divided by
the first). If every row gives the *same* quotient, the relationship is
proportional. If any row differs, it is not — one counterexample is
enough.

**Worked example 1.** A lemonade recipe table:

| Cups of lemon juice (x) | Cups of water (y) |
|---|---|
| 2 | 6 |
| 3 | 9 |
| 5 | 15 |

Test: 6 ÷ 2 = 3; 9 ÷ 3 = 3; 15 ÷ 5 = 3. Every row gives 3, so the
relationship **is proportional** — every batch uses 3 cups of water per
1 cup of lemon juice. Equivalent ratios: 2:6 = 3:9 = 5:15 (all simplify
to 1:3).

**Worked example 2.** A taxi charges a $3 pickup fee plus $2 per mile:

| Miles (x) | Cost in dollars (y) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 5 |
| 2 | 7 |
| 3 | 9 |

Test: 5 ÷ 1 = 5; 7 ÷ 2 = 3.5; 9 ÷ 3 = 3. The quotients differ (5 ≠ 3.5),
so this is **not proportional**. Reason: the $3 flat fee means the first
mile "costs" more per mile than later miles — doubling the miles does
not double the cost (2 miles cost $7, not $10).

**Worked example 3.** Quarters and dimes (same value):

| Quarters (x) | Dimes (y) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 2.5 |
| 4 | 10 |
| 6 | 15 |

Test: 2.5 ÷ 1 = 2.5; 10 ÷ 4 = 2.5; 15 ÷ 6 = 2.5. **Proportional** —
each quarter is worth 2.5 dimes. Note the table can include a decimal
(half a dime is fine as a value even if you cannot hand someone half a
dime — the *math* relationship is what we test).

Key idea: proportional means "scales perfectly." If doubling x always
doubles y, and tripling x always triples y, the ratios are equivalent
and the relationship is proportional. A flat fee, a starting bonus, or a
changing rate breaks it.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Table: (3, 12), (5, 20), (8, 32). Proportional? Show the test.
*Hint: divide the second number by the first in each row.*
**G2.** Table: (2, 7), (4, 12), (6, 17). Proportional? Explain what breaks it.
*Hint: one differing quotient is enough for "not proportional."*
**G3.** A paint mixture uses 3 cups red for every 7 cups white (a pink
shade). The table shows (7, 3), (14, 6), (21, 9) for (white, red).
Proportional? What is each quotient, and what does it mean?

## Independent practice

**I1.** Table: (4, 10), (6, 15), (10, 25). Verdict + test.
**I2.** Table: (1, 4), (2, 9), (3, 14). Verdict + test. What pattern do
you see in the second column?
**I3.** A gym charges a $20 signup fee plus $30 per month. Table for
1, 2, 3 months: (1, 50), (2, 80), (3, 110). Proportional? Explain with
the test and in words.
**I4.** Table: (2, 5), (6, 15), (10, 25). Verdict + test.
**I5.** A cookie recipe: 2 cups flour → 24 cookies; 3 cups → 36; 5 cups
→ 60. Write the table, test it, and state the unit rate in cookies per
cup of flour.
**I6.** **Error analysis.** Sam tests the table (2, 8), (3, 12), (5, 18)
and says "8 ÷ 2 = 4 and 12 ÷ 3 = 4, so it's proportional — I don't need
the third row." Is he right? Finish the test and give the correct
verdict.

## Application

A phone plan offers two options. Plan A: $15 per GB, no monthly fee —
table (1, 15), (2, 30), (4, 60). Plan B: $10 monthly fee plus $10 per GB
— table (1, 20), (2, 30), (4, 50). Test both tables. Which plan is
proportional, and for a 4-GB month, which plan costs less? Explain both
answers.

## Exit check

A babysitter charges $12 per hour. A second charges $30 for the first
hour and $10 for each extra hour. Make a small table for 1, 2, and 3
hours for each sitter, test both, and say which relationship is
proportional. Which sitter is cheaper for 3 hours?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading access:** rewrite each table row as a fraction (y over x)
  and simplify — equivalent fractions are easier to see than quotients
  for some learners.
- **Alternate response:** use two-color counters — build each row as a
  stack and check visually whether all stacks have the same shape
  (same ratio).
- **Extension:** invent a table that is proportional *except* for one
  row, and explain what real-world story could produce that single
  off-row (e.g., a bulk discount on the largest size — this previews
  the project).

## Teacher note

The deep misconception: "the numbers go up together, so it's
proportional." Lessons 03–04 exist to replace that with the two tests
(equivalent ratios; line through the origin). I2's pattern (4, 9, 14 —
adding 5 each time) is the classic additive-vs-multiplicative trap; name
it explicitly. I6 shows why *every* row must be tested. Answer key:
[../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
