# Lesson 01 — The Photosynthesis Equation

**Goal (student-readable):** I can write the photosynthesis equation
in words and in symbols, name what goes in and what comes out, and
explain the jobs of chlorophyll and chloroplasts.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, scissors,
paper cards for the equation-builder (see Teaching).

## Readiness check (5 min)

A plant sits in sunlight all day. It takes in water through its
roots and a gas from the air. By evening it has grown a tiny bit.
Write one hypothesis: *what* did the plant add to itself, and *where*
did that new material come from? (You will test this hypothesis
against evidence in Lesson 02.)

## Teaching

**Photosynthesis** is the process by which green plants (and algae
and some microbes) capture light energy and use it to build sugar
(food) out of two simple raw materials: **carbon dioxide** (a gas
from the air) and **water** (from the soil through the roots). The
green pigment that captures the light is **chlorophyll**; it lives
inside organelles called **chloroplasts**, mostly in leaf cells.
Chlorophyll looks green because it reflects green light and absorbs
mostly red and blue light — the light it absorbs is the energy the
plant gets to spend.

In words, the equation is:

> carbon dioxide + water + light energy → sugar (glucose) + oxygen

In symbols, the balanced equation is:

> 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Read it piece by piece. The things on the left of the arrow are the
**reactants** — what goes in. The things on the right are the
**products** — what comes out. The big numbers in front (the 6s) say
*how many* of each molecule take part; the small lowered numbers
(subscripts) say how many atoms of each element are inside *one*
molecule. So 6CO₂ means six carbon dioxide molecules, and each CO₂
has one carbon atom (C, no subscript means one) and two oxygen
atoms (O₂). C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose, a sugar) has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen,
and 6 oxygen atoms in a single molecule.

The equation is **balanced**: the same number of each kind of atom
appears on both sides, because atoms are not created or destroyed —
they are rearranged. Count them: carbon, 6 on the left (6 × 1) and 6
on the right (in C₆H₁₂O₆). Hydrogen, 12 on the left (6 × 2) and 12
on the right. Oxygen, 18 on the left (6 × 2 from CO₂ plus 6 × 1 from
H₂O) and 18 on the right (6 in glucose plus 6 × 2 in 6O₂). Balanced.

The most important idea in the equation is the energy story. Light
energy goes in; it does not appear as a product. That is because the
energy is now *stored* in the sugar — sugar is chemical energy in
solid form. When a plant (or an animal that eats the plant) later
needs energy, it breaks the sugar apart again (Lesson 03). And the
oxygen? It is a by-product: the plant releases it through tiny leaf
openings, and it is the oxygen animals breathe.

### Paper equation-builder (15 min, replaces the legacy interactive)

Cut out or write these cards: `carbon dioxide`, `water`, `light
energy`, `sugar (glucose)`, `oxygen`, and number cards `6`, `6`,
`1`, `6`. Arrange them into the word equation, then flip the word
cards to their symbols (CO₂, H₂O, C₆H₁₂O₆, O₂) and place the number
cards to build 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂. Check
your build by counting atoms on both sides, as above. Keep the cards:
you will rebuild this equation in reverse in Lesson 03.

### Worked example 1 — Reading the symbols

*Question:* In 6H₂O, how many hydrogen atoms and oxygen atoms are
there in total?

*Reasoning:* H₂O is one water molecule: 2 hydrogen atoms, 1 oxygen
atom. The big 6 in front means six such molecules. Hydrogen: 6 × 2
= 12. Oxygen: 6 × 1 = 6. So 6H₂O holds 12 hydrogen atoms and 6 oxygen
atoms in total. (A common error is to read 6H₂O as "6 hydrogen, 6
oxygen" — the big number multiplies the *whole molecule*, including
its subscript.)

### Worked example 2 — What is missing?

*Question:* A learner writes: CO₂ + H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ +
O₂. Is this equation balanced? If not, what is wrong?

*Reasoning:* Count carbon: left 1, right 6 — not balanced. Count
hydrogen: left 2, right 12 — not balanced. Count oxygen: left 3,
right 8 — not balanced. The learner has the right *ingredients* but
the wrong *amounts*. The fix is the big numbers: 6 in front of CO₂,
6 in front of H₂O, and 6 in front of O₂. Ingredients alone do not
make an equation; the amounts must balance because atoms are
conserved.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Copy the word equation, then write the symbol equation
underneath it. Label each side "reactants" and "products" and
circle the energy input. (Hint: energy is written above the arrow
or as a reactant — it goes in, it is not a product.)

**G2.** Count the atoms in 6O₂: how many oxygen atoms in total?
Then count all the oxygen atoms on the product side of the full
equation and show they equal 18. (Hint: products are C₆H₁₂O₆ and
6O₂.)

**G3.** A plant is kept in a sealed glass jar in sunlight with water
but no fresh air. Predict: will it keep photosynthesizing for a
week? Explain using the reactants. (Hint: which reactant comes
from the air, and does the jar supply more of it?)

## Independent practice

**I1.** A learner says, "Plants make food from soil and sunlight."
Using the equation, correct the two mistakes in that sentence.

**I2.** Chlorophyll is green. Explain, in your own words, why a leaf
looks green — and why "green light is reflected" does not mean the
plant is wasting its favorite light. (Hint: absorbed colors are the
ones the plant uses.)

**I3.** Write one sentence for each product of photosynthesis
telling where it goes: one for the sugar, one for the oxygen.

## Application

Build the equation from memory with your cards, then turn all the
cards face down and rebuild it again. Time yourself on the second
build. Then answer: if a plant made *two* glucose molecules, how
many CO₂ molecules would it need? Show the counting. (Answer in
the teacher key: 12.)

## Exit check

Write the symbol equation from memory, label reactants and
products, and finish this sentence: "Photosynthesis stores light
energy as ______ energy in ______." Then name the pigment and the
organelle where the capturing happens.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** pair each symbol with its word card
  throughout the lesson; say "C-O-2" aloud as "carbon dioxide"
  until the symbols are familiar.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may build and photograph or
  sketch the card equation instead of writing the symbols, as long
  as they can count the atoms aloud.
- **Extension:** research (adult-approved source) one organism
  besides a green plant that photosynthesizes — algae, kelp, or
  cyanobacteria — and write two sentences about where it lives and
  what it releases.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "plants eat soil" — held for Lesson 02's
evidence; do not merely correct it here, let the equation's
reactants do the work; (2) light as a *product* rather than an
input; (3) big numbers and subscripts confused — re-teach with
