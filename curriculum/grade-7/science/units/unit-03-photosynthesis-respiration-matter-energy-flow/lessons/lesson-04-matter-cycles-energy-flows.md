# Lesson 04 — Matter Cycles, Energy Flows

**Goal (student-readable):** I can build a food web, trace one
carbon atom through it, and explain why matter cycles but energy
flows one way.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil,
colored pencils (optional), the matter–energy cycle diagram
[`assets/matter-energy-cycle.svg`](../assets/matter-energy-cycle.svg)
(deterministic diagram; text alternative below).

## Readiness check (5 min)

A rabbit eats grass, a fox eats the rabbit, and the fox dies. Write
one hypothesis for each question: where does the fox's body's matter
go after death, and where does the energy the fox once had go?
(You will check both hypotheses in this lesson.)

## Teaching

Zoom out from one cell to one ecosystem — say, a meadow. Three
roles organize everything that eats and is eaten:

- **Producers** make their own food from nonliving raw materials.
  Green plants, algae, and some microbes photosynthesize: they are
  the only members of most ecosystems that capture new energy from
  sunlight and fix carbon from the air.
- **Consumers** eat other living things: grass → rabbit → fox, and
  also rabbits eating grass, foxes eating rabbits, and humans eating
  both plants and animals.
- **Decomposers** (fungi, bacteria, worms) break down dead
  organisms and wastes, returning their matter to soil, water, and
  air as reusable raw materials — carbon dioxide, water, minerals.

A **food chain** is one feeding path (grass → rabbit → fox). A
**food web** is the honest version: many chains woven together,
because most organisms eat — and are eaten by — more than one
other organism. Arrows in a food web point in the direction energy
and matter *move*: from the food toward the eater (grass → rabbit
means the rabbit gets matter and energy from the grass).

Now the two great rules, and they are different on purpose:

1. **Matter cycles.** Atoms are conserved. A carbon atom in a blade
   of grass may become rabbit muscle, then fox muscle, then — after
   the fox dies and decomposers work — carbon dioxide in the air
   again, then a new blade of grass. Nothing is created or
   destroyed; it just changes address. That is why the carbon cycle
   is a *cycle*.
2. **Energy flows one way.** Energy enters as sunlight, is stored
   as chemical energy in producers, and passes up the web — but at
   every step most of it leaves the web as heat (from respiration,
   movement, and the general business of staying alive) and is not
   recovered. Energy is not recycled. That is why food webs are
   short (rarely more than four or five levels): by the top, little
   usable energy remains.

A useful rule of thumb, kept here as **optional enrichment** (the
full treatment belongs to Unit 06): roughly a tenth of the energy
at one level shows up in the next level's new growth — the "10%
rule." It is approximate, not a law, and it explains why there are
always far more plants than foxes: each level can only pass on a
fraction of what it received.

### The cycle diagram

The deterministic diagram shows the whole system in one picture: a
plant box (photosynthesis: CO₂ + water + light → sugar + O₂), an
animal box (respiration: sugar + O₂ → CO₂ + water + energy), and
arrows cycling carbon dioxide, water, and oxygen between them,
with a sunlight arrow entering and a heat arrow leaving. Study it
before the worked examples.

> **Text alternative for the diagram** (same information, no image
> needed): Two boxes. Left box, "Plant — photosynthesis": inputs
> carbon dioxide, water, sunlight; outputs sugar, oxygen. Right
> box, "Animal — respiration": inputs sugar, oxygen; outputs carbon
> dioxide, water, released energy (work + heat). Arrows: oxygen
> flows plant → animal; carbon dioxide flows animal → plant; sugar
> flows plant → animal (by eating); sunlight enters the plant box
> from outside; heat leaves the animal box to outside. The system
> boundary is the meadow: sunlight crosses in, heat crosses out,
> and carbon, water, and oxygen cycle inside.

### Worked example 1 — One carbon atom's journey

*Question:* Trace one carbon atom from the air through a meadow
food web and back to the air, naming four stops.

*Reasoning:* (1) Air: the atom is in a CO₂ molecule. (2) Grass:
photosynthesis fixes it into sugar, then into cellulose in a leaf.
(3) Rabbit: the rabbit eats the leaf; respiration rearranges some
of it, and some becomes rabbit muscle. (4) Fox: the fox eats the
rabbit; later the fox respires, releasing the atom as CO₂ back to
the air — or decomposers release it after the fox dies. Same atom,
four addresses, never created or destroyed.

### Worked example 2 — Why so few foxes?

*Question:* A meadow grows 10,000 kilograms of grass in a year. Why
can it not support 10,000 kilograms of foxes?

*Reasoning:* Energy flows one way and most of it leaves as heat at
each step. Grass → rabbit keeps only a fraction (roughly a tenth
as new rabbit growth, enrichment rule); rabbit → fox keeps a
fraction again. By the fox level, only a small sliver of the
grass's original energy remains available, so the meadow can
support far fewer kilograms of fox than of grass. Matter, by
contrast, is not lost this way — it cycles.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Draw a food web with five organisms from a meadow (grass,
rabbit, mouse, fox, hawk) plus decomposers. Use at least six arrows
and check: does every arrow point from food toward eater?
(Hint: the hawk eats both rabbit and mouse; the mouse eats grass.)

**G2.** In your web, trace one carbon atom from air to grass to
rabbit to decomposer to air, naming what process moves it at each
step (photosynthesis, eating, respiration, decomposition).
(Hint: eating moves matter without changing it; respiration
rearranges it.)

**G3.** A learner draws energy arrows in a circle: fox → grass →
rabbit → fox. Explain the error in two sentences. (Hint: can the
fox's waste heat become sunlight again?)

## Independent practice

**I1.** Decomposers are sometimes called "nature's recyclers."
Explain the title using both rules: what do they recycle (matter),
and what can they *not* recycle (energy)?

**I2.** A lake's algae die off after a hot summer. Predict two
consequences for the lake's food web, one for a consumer and one
for a decomposer, and justify each with an arrow from your web
logic.

**I3.** Define the system boundary for a meadow food web: what
crosses *into* the system from outside, what crosses *out*, and
what stays cycling inside? (Three short lists.)

## Application

A farmer removes all the foxes from a meadow to protect chickens.
Predict what happens to the rabbit population in the short term
and to the grass in the longer term, using your web. Then name one
way the farmer's choice could backfire. (Three to five sentences.)

## Exit check

Write one sentence for each rule: "Matter ______." "Energy ______."
Then label each organism producer, consumer, or decomposer:
oak tree, deer, mushroom, wolf, algae.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the words "cycle" and "flow" are
  contrasted on the board before any web is drawn; learners may
  build webs with organism cards before drawing arrows.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may narrate the carbon
  atom's journey aloud as a story instead of writing it, as long
  as the four stops and processes are named.
- **Extension:** add humans to your web twice — once as consumers
  of plants, once as consumers of animals — and write two sentences
  about which position gives a population more usable energy, and
  why (use the 10% enrichment rule).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) arrows pointing from eater to food
(predator → prey) — re-teach "arrow follows the food"; (2) energy
recycled like matter — G3 targets it; (3) decomposers "eating
waste" confused with "destroying matter" — matter is returned, not
destroyed. The full keystone-removal and 10%-rule treatment is
banked for Unit 06; here the rule stays enrichment. Keys:
