# Lesson 03 — Cellular Respiration

**Goal (student-readable):** I can describe cellular respiration in
words and symbols, explain how food molecules are rearranged into
new molecules with energy released, and explain why plants respire
too.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, the
paper equation-builder cards from Lesson 01.

## Readiness check (5 min)

You run up a flight of stairs and feel warm and out of breath.
Write one hypothesis: what did your body just *use up*, what did it
*take in* faster, and what did it *give off*? (You will check this
hypothesis against the respiration equation.)

## Teaching

**Cellular respiration** is the process that releases usable energy
from food. It happens in the cells of animals, plants, fungi, and
many microbes — in the **mitochondria** above all, the organelles
Unit 02 named as the cell's energy-releasing structures. In words:

> food (sugar) + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + released energy

In symbols, using glucose as the food:

> C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy

Compare this with Lesson 01's photosynthesis equation and the
pattern jumps out: respiration is photosynthesis *in reverse*.
Photosynthesis builds sugar out of carbon dioxide and water using
light energy; respiration breaks sugar apart with oxygen, returning
carbon dioxide and water and releasing the stored energy for the
cell's work. The same atoms, rearranged. Count to check: carbon 6 =
6, hydrogen 12 = 12, oxygen 18 = 18. Balanced again — because atoms
are conserved, never created or destroyed.

But respiration is more than "burning sugar for energy." As food
molecules are broken apart, their pieces are also **rearranged into
new molecules**: some carbon pieces become new cell material, some
become carbon dioxide that leaves the cell, and the released energy
powers everything from muscle contraction to building proteins to
keeping a root cell absorbing minerals. That is the heart of
MS-LS1-7: food is rearranged through chemical reactions into new
molecules that support growth, release energy, or both, as matter
moves through an organism.

Two facts surprise most learners. First, **plants respire too** —
day and night. In daylight a leaf both photosynthesizes (building
sugar) and respires (spending sugar); at night, with no light, it
only respires, living off its stored starch. A plant that only
photosynthesized and never respired could never *use* its sugar.
Second, **breathing is not respiration**. Breathing moves gases in
and out of the body; cellular respiration is the chemical
rearrangement inside cells that releases energy. Breathing supplies
the oxygen and removes the carbon dioxide, but the energy release
happens cell by cell.

### Rebuild the equation in reverse (10 min)

Take your Lesson 01 cards and rebuild the equation backwards: start
from sugar + oxygen and end at carbon dioxide + water + energy.
Then answer: which side of each equation holds the stored energy —
the sugar side or the carbon-dioxide-and-water side? (The sugar
side: sugar is stored chemical energy; carbon dioxide and water
are the low-energy leftovers.)

### Worked example 1 — The runner's breath

*Question:* A runner breathes faster and feels warm during a run.
Explain both observations with the respiration equation.

*Reasoning:* Faster breathing takes in more oxygen (a reactant)
and removes more carbon dioxide (a product) — the runner's muscle
cells are respiring faster to release more energy for movement.
Feeling warm: some released energy becomes heat, a by-product of
the rearrangement. Both observations are the equation running
faster, not a new process.

### Worked example 2 — The plant at night

*Question:* A bean plant in a dark room for a night loses a tiny
amount of dry mass by morning, although nothing was taken from it.
Explain.

*Reasoning:* At night there is no light, so no photosynthesis and
no new sugar. The plant still respires: it breaks apart stored
starch/sugar, releases energy for its living cells, and gives off
carbon dioxide and water. The carbon that leaves as CO₂ is mass
leaving the plant — hence the tiny loss. Plants, like animals, pay
a nightly energy bill.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Write both equations side by side (photosynthesis,
respiration) and draw one arrow between them labeled with what
flows each way: sugar and oxygen one way, carbon dioxide and water
the other. (Hint: photosynthesis *makes* sugar and oxygen;
respiration *uses* them.)

**G2.** A learner says, "Respiration is just breathing." Correct
the sentence in two sentences: one defining breathing, one defining
cellular respiration. (Hint: where does each happen — lungs/body
surface, or inside cells?)

**G3.** A sealed jar holds a green plant in sunlight. Predict: over
a day, does the jar's oxygen level rise, fall, or stay the same?
Explain using *both* equations. (Hint: in sunlight, which process
runs faster — and does the other one stop?)

## Independent practice

**I1.** Yeast in bread dough makes the dough rise and gives bread
its smell. Yeast cells respire (and ferment) sugar, releasing
carbon dioxide. Explain what the rising dough proves about yeast
cells, and name the gas doing the lifting.

**I2.** A muscle cell and a leaf cell are both given glucose and
oxygen in the dark. Which cell releases energy from the glucose?
Explain why *both* do — and why the leaf cell's answer surprises
people.

**I3.** Write the respiration equation from memory, then check it
by counting atoms. If your count fails, find the error before
moving on: which element is off, and by how much?

## Application

A hospital patient breathes pure oxygen for a while. A learner
claims, "More oxygen means the patient's cells release unlimited
energy." Evaluate the claim: what else limits respiration, and what
happens to the extra oxygen the cells cannot use? (Two to four
sentences; answer in the teacher key.)

## Exit check

Write the respiration equation in words, name the organelle where
most of it happens, and finish this sentence: "Photosynthesis
______ energy into sugar; respiration ______ energy out of sugar."
Then answer true or false with one reason: "Plants respire only at
night."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** keep both equations visible side by
  side for the whole lesson; "reverse" is taught as "the same
  cards, walked backwards," not as a new vocabulary word.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer G1 by physically
  rearranging the cards and narrating the two directions instead of
  drawing the arrows.
- **Extension:** research (adult-approved source) one organism that
  respires without oxygen (an anaerobe) and write two sentences
  about where it lives; note that this unit's equation describes
  the oxygen-using form.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) respiration = breathing — G2 targets it
directly; (2) plants photosynthesize *instead of* respiring — G3
and Worked example 2; (3) energy is "created" by respiration —
re-teach conservation: energy is *released from* sugar, and atoms
are conserved (count them). Keys:
