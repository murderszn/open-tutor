# Lesson 02 — Where Does a Plant's Mass Come From?

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain, with evidence, that
most of a plant's mass comes from carbon dioxide in the air — not
from the soil — and describe what a plant does with the sugar it
makes.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, the
generated illustration

(AI illustration — used in the CER activity; text alternative
below), colored pencils (optional).

## Teaching

A growing tree can gain hundreds of pounds, yet the soil around it
barely drops. How? In the 1600s a scientist named Jan Baptist van
Helmont tested exactly this. He planted a small willow tree in a pot
with a weighed amount of dried soil, watered it for five years, and
then weighed tree and soil again. The tree had gained about 164
pounds. The soil had lost only about 2 ounces. Van Helmont
concluded the tree's new mass came mostly from the water — he did
not know about gases from the air. With the photosynthesis equation
we can do better: the tree's mass came mostly from **carbon dioxide
in the air**. Each CO₂ molecule donates its carbon atom, and carbon
is the backbone of sugar, wood, leaves, and roots. The water matters
too (it supplies hydrogen and some oxygen), but the soil's job is
mostly to supply water and small amounts of minerals — not the bulk
of the mass. Soil is not plant food; air and water, plus light
energy, are the raw materials.

So what does the plant *do* with the sugar it makes? Three fates:

1. **Used now.** Some sugar is broken apart right away to release
   energy for the plant's own work — growing roots, opening leaf
   pores, repairing damage. (That breaking-apart is cellular
   respiration, Lesson 03 — plants do it too.)
2. **Stored for later.** Extra sugar is linked into long chains
   called **starch** and stored, often in roots, seeds, or tubers.
   A potato is mostly stored starch — a pantry the plant fills in
   summer and spends in spring.
3. **Built into new molecules for growth.** Sugar carbons are
   rearranged, with other elements from water and soil minerals
   (nitrogen, for example), into **cellulose** for strong cell
   walls, proteins for new cells, and other plant molecules. This is
   how a plant literally grows new leaves out of thin air.

### The seedlings illustration — CER activity

Look at the illustration: two potted bean seedlings, same pots,
same soil. The left plant stands in warm sunlight: short, sturdy,
broad green leaves. The right plant stands in dark shadow: tall,
thin, pale yellow, drooping leaves, leaning as if reaching for light
that is not there.

> **Text alternative for the illustration** (same information, no
> image needed): Left — a bean seedling in yellow sunlight: short
> upright green stem, many broad green leaves, terracotta pot with
> brown soil. Right — a bean seedling in dark grey-blue shadow: one
> tall thin pale-yellow stem bending sideways, small pale drooping
> leaves, identical terracotta pot and soil. Use these descriptions
> to complete the CER activity without the image.

Write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph answering: *which
seedling is photosynthesizing, and how do you know?* Your claim
names one seedling. Your evidence cites at least two visible
differences (color, height/sturdiness, leaf size). Your reasoning
connects the evidence to the equation: green leaves mean chlorophyll
is present to capture light; without light, no light energy enters
the equation, so little sugar is made, so the plant cannot build
sturdy green growth — it stretches pale and thin instead, spending
its seed's stored food. Note what the illustration cannot prove:
it is an AI illustration of a typical outcome, not a photograph of
an experiment you ran — real seedlings vary, and a careful learner
would repeat the comparison with real plants (see the Session 4
observation).

### Worked example 1 — The mass question, answered with numbers

*Question:* A potted plant gains 500 grams of dry mass in a season.
The soil loses 10 grams. A learner says, "The other 490 grams came
from the water I poured in." Use van Helmont's result to evaluate
this claim.

*Reasoning:* Van Helmont's soil lost almost nothing while his tree
gained enormously, so "from the soil" fails. But "from the water"
fails too as the *main* source: water supplies hydrogen and oxygen,
yet the dry mass of a plant is mostly carbon-based molecules
(cellulose, starch, proteins) whose carbon skeletons must come from
somewhere — and the only carbon input in the equation is CO₂ from
the air. Water is necessary, but it is not the bulk source of the
plant's carbon mass. Claim: mostly from air (CO₂), with water and
soil minerals contributing smaller parts.

### Worked example 2 — Three fates, one plant

*Question:* In autumn a bean plant's leaves yellow and drop, yet
its roots grow fatter. Explain using the three fates of sugar.

*Reasoning:* In summer the leaves photosynthesized and stored extra
sugar as starch (fate 2). In autumn, with leaves failing, the plant
moves stored starch to the roots (still fate 2, now being spent)
and uses some sugar now for root growth (fate 1) and to build new
root cells (fate 3). The plant is not "dying and doing nothing" —
it is relocating its pantry underground for next spring.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Van Helmont's soil lost 2 ounces while his tree gained 164
pounds. Write the claim this evidence *rules out*, and the claim it
leaves standing. (Hint: "rules out" = cannot be true given the
numbers.)

**G2.** Name the three fates of sugar and give one example of each
from a plant you know (a tree, a potato, a bean seedling).
(Hint: potato = storage is already given; find your own others.)

**G3.** The dark-grown seedling is taller than the sunlit one. A
learner concludes, "Darkness makes plants grow more." Explain the
error using the illustration and the equation. (Hint: taller is
not the same as more mass or healthier growth — what is the pale
plant missing?)

## Independent practice

**I1.** A gardener adds a bag of fertilizer to a potted plant and
the plant grows faster. Does this prove the plant's mass comes from
the fertilizer? Explain, using the equation and the van Helmont
numbers.

**I2.** Write a CER paragraph: "Most of a tree's wood comes from
the air." Use van Helmont's numbers as evidence and the equation
as reasoning. (Three to five sentences.)

**I3.** A seed planted in a dark cupboard sprouts and grows a pale
shoot several centimeters tall. Where did *that* growth's mass come
from, if the plant cannot photosynthesize yet? (Hint: think about
what is inside a seed.)

## Exit check

Finish these two sentences: "Most of a plant's dry mass comes from
______, not from ______." "A plant stores extra sugar as ______,
and builds cell walls out of ______." Then name the three fates of
sugar in order: used now, ______, ______.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the words "mass," "dry mass," and
  "fate" are defined in context before they are used in questions;
  learners may answer I2 as a labeled diagram plus two sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the CER activity
  orally to the adult, who scribes the claim-evidence-reasoning
  labels.
- **Extension:** research (adult-approved source) how much of a
  tree's mass is water versus dry mass, and why scientists dry
  samples before weighing them — write three sentences.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "plants eat soil" — the van Helmont
numbers are the cure, but learners often replace soil with water
as the mass source; Worked example 1 blocks that move; (2) "dark
seedling grew more, so darkness helps" — height vs. health; (3)
seeds "growing from nothing" — I3's stored-food answer. Keys:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
