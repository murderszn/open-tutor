# Lesson 05 — One System, Two Flows

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain photosynthesis and
cellular respiration as one linked system, judge a model's strengths
and limits, and write an evidence-based explanation of
photosynthesis's role in matter cycling and energy flow.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, the
paper equation-builder cards, the matter–energy cycle diagram
[`assets/matter-energy-cycle.svg`](../assets/matter-energy-cycle.svg).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Write both equations from memory (photosynthesis, respiration).
Then circle the two substances that appear as products in one
equation and reactants in the other. What does that pairing tell
you about how the two processes are linked?

## Teaching

Put the two equations together and a system appears. The plant
makes sugar and oxygen; the animal (and the plant itself, at
night) uses sugar and oxygen and returns carbon dioxide and water;
the plant takes carbon dioxide and water up again. Matter cycles
between them. Energy does not cycle: it enters once, as sunlight,
is stored in sugar, is spent step by step, and leaves as heat. One
system, two flows — a cycle and a one-way street running through
the same boxes.

Models help us see this system, and every model has strengths and
limits. The cycle diagram's strengths: it shows both flows, both
equations, and the system boundary (sunlight in, heat out) in one
picture. Its limits: it hides *where* each process happens
(chloroplasts vs. mitochondria), it makes the flows look instant
and perfectly balanced (real ecosystems wobble), and it leaves out
decomposers, who are the return crew for most dead matter. A good
scientist names a model's limits before trusting it — your project
model will be judged partly on whether you do.

### The closing CER explanation (20 min)

Write the unit's closing explanation, answering the essential
question: *what is the role of photosynthesis in the cycling of
matter and the flow of energy into and out of organisms?* Use the
CER frame:

- **Claim (one sentence):** Photosynthesis is the process that
  brings new matter and new energy into nearly every ecosystem.
- **Evidence (two or three pieces):** the balanced equation (CO₂
  + water + light → sugar + O₂); van Helmont's numbers (tree +164
  lb, soil −2 oz); the seedlings illustration (sunlit plant green
  and sturdy, dark plant pale and spindly); the food-web rule that
  producers sit at the base of every chain.
- **Reasoning (two or three sentences):** because photosynthesis
  fixes carbon from the air into sugar and stores light energy as
  chemical energy, every other organism's matter and energy either
  is that sugar or comes from eating something that was. Matter
  then cycles (sugar → eater → CO₂ → plant again) while energy
  flows one way (sunlight → sugar → work + heat → out). Without
  photosynthesis, the cycle would have no entry point and the flow
  would have no source.

Draft the paragraph now; you will revise it with the teacher-guide
checklist in Session 14 and submit the revised version with the
unit assessment.

### Worked example 1 — A strong CER, annotated

*Question:* Read this paragraph and name its claim, its evidence,
and its reasoning: "Photosynthesis feeds almost every ecosystem.
The equation shows plants turning carbon dioxide, water, and light
into sugar and oxygen, and van Helmont's tree gained 164 pounds
while its soil lost only 2 ounces. So the tree's new matter came
from the air, not the soil — and because animals eat plants or
eat plant-eaters, the sugar plants make is the starting point of
nearly every food web."

*Reasoning:* Claim = "Photosynthesis feeds almost every
ecosystem." Evidence = the equation's inputs/outputs and the
van Helmont numbers. Reasoning = "so the tree's new matter came
from the air" (links numbers to the claim) and "because animals
eat plants…" (links the equation to food webs). Note the honest
hedge: "almost every" — deep-sea vent ecosystems run on chemical
energy, not light, and a careful claim leaves room for them.

### Worked example 2 — Judging a model

*Question:* A learner's model shows a plant and a rabbit with a
single two-headed arrow labeled "energy" between them. Name one
strength and two limits of this model.

*Reasoning:* Strength: it shows the plant and rabbit are linked.
Limits: (1) a two-headed arrow suggests energy flows *back* to the
plant, but energy flows one way — the rabbit never returns usable
energy to the plant; (2) it hides matter entirely — the carbon
that actually cycles (CO₂, sugar, waste, decomposition) is
missing, so the model cannot explain where the rabbit's body
comes from or goes.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** List three strengths and three limits of the cycle
diagram. (Hint: strengths = what it shows clearly; limits = what
it hides, speeds up, or leaves out — check decomposers.)

**G2.** A model shows sunlight entering a meadow and heat leaving
it, but no arrows for carbon dioxide. What can this model explain
well, and what can it not explain at all? (Hint: energy story vs.
matter story.)

**G3.** Improve Worked example 2's model in words: describe two
changes (one arrow fix, one added box) that would make it honest.
(Hint: one-headed arrows; add decomposers.)

## Independent practice

**I1.** Write your CER paragraph (see Teaching). Underline your
claim once, circle each piece of evidence, and bracket your
reasoning. Trade with a partner if an adult is present, or check
it yourself against the frame: claim + 2–3 evidences + reasoning?

**I2.** A town builds a sealed glass greenhouse with plants, soil,
and water but no animals, and seals it for a year. Predict: do the
plants survive? Use both equations and the system boundary in your
answer. (Hint: what crosses the boundary — light? gases?)

**I3.** Name one ecosystem where photosynthesis is *not* the
energy source, and explain in one sentence why the CER claim says
"almost every" rather than "every."

## Application

Choose one model from this unit (the equation-builder cards, the
cycle diagram, or the seedlings illustration) and write a
"model card" for it: what it shows, what it hides, and one question
it cannot answer. Your project rubric rewards exactly this honesty.

## Exit check

Without looking: write both equations, name the system boundary
crossings (in: sunlight; out: heat), and finish this sentence:
"Photosynthesis brings ______ and ______ into an ecosystem;
respiration spends the ______ and returns the ______."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the CER frame is posted as
  claim/evidence/reasoning with sentence starters ("My claim is…",
  "The evidence is…", "This matters because…").
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may dictate the CER
  paragraph to the adult, who scribes it, as long as the learner
  names the claim, evidence, and reasoning labels.
- **Extension:** research (adult-approved source) one
  chemosynthetic ecosystem (deep-sea hydrothermal vents) and write
  three sentences comparing its energy source with a meadow's.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "energy cycles back" — the two-headed
arrow error; (2) CER paragraphs that list facts without reasoning —
Worked example 1's annotation is the fix; (3) "all ecosystems run
on photosynthesis" — I3's hedge. The Session 14 revision checklist
is in [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md#session-14-cer-revision-checklist).
