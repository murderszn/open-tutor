# Unit 04 — Reproduction, Inheritance, and Genetic Variation

**Grade:** 7 · **Subject:** Science · **Track weeks:** 15–18
**Backlog:** [issue #35](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/35), section U04
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Unit 03 traced matter and energy through a plant: air and water become
sugar, sugar becomes growth. This unit asks where the *instructions*
for that growth come from, and why offspring resemble their parents
without being copies of them: *how do living things make new living
things, what exactly gets passed on, why does sexual reproduction
create variation while asexual reproduction does not, and why do the
same genes sometimes grow very different organisms in different
conditions?* The learner compares reproduction in plants and animals
at an age-appropriate level — no human reproductive anatomy or sexual
behavior is taught here — models chromosome halving with pea-plant
numbers, reads and builds Punnett squares as probability models, and
runs a two-variety, two-condition plant-growth investigation that
separates genetic from environmental effects.

## Essential question

Why do offspring look like their parents, why are they never exact
copies (unless reproduction is asexual), and how much of the
difference comes from genes and how much from the environment?

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–03 and the diagnostic weeks:

- Cell parts and jobs (nucleus, chloroplasts, mitochondria); cells
  divide as organisms grow and repair (Unit 02)
- Matter tracing and the photosynthesis/respiration equations
  (Unit 03); growth as building new molecules from food
- Claim-evidence-reasoning paragraphs; labeled data tables; finding
  a mean; reading a simple bar comparison
- Fractions and percentages at a grade-7 level (1/4 = 25%);
  probability as "what fraction of equally likely outcomes"
- No prior knowledge of chromosomes, genes, meiosis, Punnett
  squares, or mutations is assumed — all are taught here from zero

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Compare asexual and sexual reproduction: state how many parents
   are involved, whether genetic information is copied or combined,
   and why asexual offspring carry (barring mutation) identical
   genetic information while sexual offspring vary — with one plant
   and one animal example of each.
2. Compare mitosis and meiosis with a pea-plant model (body cells
   14 chromosomes, gametes 7): state each process's purpose, number
   of divisions, chromosome number in the resulting cells, and
   whether the resulting cells are genetically identical or varied.
3. Explain the gene → protein → trait chain in plain language:
   genes are sections of DNA on chromosomes; a gene chiefly controls
   the production of a specific protein; proteins affect structures
   and functions, hence traits. Model why a structural change to a
   gene (a mutation) may change a protein and may therefore be
   harmful, beneficial, or neutral — and why the effect can depend
   on the environment.
4. Use Punnett squares and coin-toss simulations to predict genotype
   and phenotype ratios for a single-gene pea trait (seed shape:
   R = round, dominant; r = wrinkled, recessive), including
   Rr × Rr (3 round : 1 wrinkled) and Rr × rr (1 round : 1 wrinkled),
   and explain that a Punnett square gives probabilities for a
   group, not a guarantee for any single offspring.
5. Construct an evidence-based explanation for how both genetic and
   environmental factors influence growth, using the unit's
   two-variety plant-growth investigation (or its labeled practice
   dataset): separate the variety effect from the light effect with
   means, and explain why height alone does not equal health.
6. Use evidence and reasoning to explain how a characteristic animal
   behavior or a specialized plant structure affects the probability
   of successful reproduction, and to distinguish an inherited trait
   from an acquired trait with two correct examples of each.

**Teaching notes on scope and sensitivity.** All reproduction
examples use plants and non-human animals. Human examples are
limited to general trait language (for example, that children
resemble but do not copy their parents) and to the chromosome-count
pattern (body cells 46, gametes 23) stated once in Lesson 02 for
comparison; no human anatomy, sexual behavior, or personal or family
trait data is requested. Learners never report their own or their
family's traits. The pea seed-shape model is a simplified
single-gene model: most real traits are influenced by many genes
plus the environment, and the unit says so in Lesson 04. Mutation
examples in Lesson 03 are simplified teaching models, labeled as
such — not documented field observations.

  and allele cards (Lesson 04: R and r cards, or two coins)
- For the adult-supervised investigation (Sessions 4, 12, 14):
  two varieties of fast-growing seeds (for example, two bean
  varieties), small pots or cups with drainage, potting soil, water,
  a ruler, and a notebook for dated measurements. One set grows in
  bright indirect light, the other in low light — never in total
  darkness for two weeks, and never with chemicals, lamps left

  dataset in [project.md](project.md) teaches the same objectives
  without plants.
- Notebook, pencils, colored pencils (optional), scissors, glue/tape
