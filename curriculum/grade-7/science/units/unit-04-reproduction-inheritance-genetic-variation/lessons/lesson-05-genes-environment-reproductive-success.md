# Lesson 05 — Genes, Environment, and Reproductive Success

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain how genetic and
environmental factors together influence growth, tell an inherited
trait from an acquired trait, and explain how a behavior or a plant
structure changes the probability of successful reproduction.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, the
growth-investigation log (Sessions 4, 12, 14).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Two seedlings from the same seed packet grow side by side. One gets
plenty of light and water; the other gets little light. After two
weeks one is tall and pale, the other short and green. Which one is
"healthier" — and can you tell from height alone? Write your answer
with one reason. (This lesson returns to that question with data.)

## Teaching

A trait is **inherited** when it is passed through genetic
information from parents to offspring: a pea plant's seed shape, a
bird's beak shape, a flower's color. A trait is **acquired** when it
is gained during an organism's life through experience, use, injury,
or environment — and it is **not** passed to offspring through
genes: a scar, a muscle built by exercise, a plant stem bent toward
a window, a bird's learned song dialect. The test is simple: could
the offspring have it without living through the same experience?
If no, it is acquired. Cutting a dog's tail does not give its
puppies short tails; a gardener's tanned skin does not give her
children tanned skin at birth.

Growth shows both factors working together. **Genetic factors**
(variety, breed) set the range: different seed varieties grow at
different rates even in identical conditions. **Environmental
factors** (light, water, space, food) move an organism within that
range: the same variety grows differently in bright light than in
low light. The unit's investigation tests both at once — two
varieties × two light conditions — so the learner can separate
them: if variety A outgrows variety B in *both* light conditions,
that is a variety (genetic) effect; if *both* varieties grow taller
in low light than in bright light, that is a light (environmental)
effect. When both patterns appear, both factors matter — the honest
answer, and the one the data in the project actually show.

A warning from the investigation: in low light, seedlings often
grow *taller* but paler and weaker — they stretch toward the light
(etiolation). Height alone therefore does not equal health: a tall
pale seedling and a short green one must be judged on color,
sturdiness, and leaf size as well. Learners who report only height
have measured one variable and mistaken it for the whole plant.

Finally, reproduction itself is not left to chance alone. A
**characteristic animal behavior** or a **specialized plant
structure** can raise the *probability* of successful reproduction
— probability, not certainty. Examples from the standard's own
clarifications: birds building nests protect their young from cold;
animals herding protect their young from predators; colorful
plumage and birdsong attract mates; bright flowers and nectar
attract insects that transfer pollen; hard shells let squirrels
bury nuts that later germinate. In each case the behavior or
structure does not guarantee offspring — it changes the odds, and
the learner's explanation must say *how* (protection, attraction,
transport, or a better germination site), not merely that "it
helps."

### Worked example 1 — Separating two effects with means

*Question:* In the project's labeled practice dataset, five plants
per group were measured on day 14 (heights in cm). Variety A, full
light: 18, 20, 19, 21, 17. Variety A, low light: 24, 26, 25, 27, 23.
Variety B, full light: 14, 15, 13, 16, 14. Variety B, low light: 19,
20, 18, 21, 19. Which factor — variety or light — affects height,
and how do you know?

*Reasoning:* Find each group's mean. A full light: (18 + 20 + 19 +
21 + 17) ÷ 5 = 95 ÷ 5 = 19.0 cm. A low light: 125 ÷ 5 = 25.0 cm.
B full light: 72 ÷ 5 = 14.4 cm. B low light: 97 ÷ 5 = 19.4 cm.
Now compare. Variety effect: in full light, A (19.0) beats B (14.4)
by 4.6 cm; in low light, A (25.0) beats B (19.4) by 5.6 cm — A is
taller in *both* conditions, so variety matters. Light effect: A
rises from 19.0 to 25.0 in low light (+6.0 cm) and B rises from 14.4
to 19.4 (+5.0 cm) — both varieties grow taller in low light, so
light matters too. Conclusion: both factors influence growth. And
the health warning applies: the low-light plants are taller, but
the investigation log also records them as paler and thinner — so
"taller" here means "stretching for light," not "healthier."

### Worked example 2 — Inherited or acquired? Behavior or structure?

*Question:* Sort each case, with a reason: (a) a scar a horse got
from a fence; (b) a foal's coat color; (c) a bird building a nest
before laying eggs; (d) a sunflower's bright yellow petals.

*Reasoning:* (a) Acquired: gained during life, not carried in genes
— the foal will not be born with that scar. (b) Inherited: passed
through genetic information from its parents. (c) A behavior that
raises reproductive success: the nest protects eggs and chicks from
cold and predators, raising the probability that offspring survive
— it does not guarantee it. (d) A specialized plant structure that
raises reproductive success: bright petals attract pollinators
that transfer pollen, raising the probability of seeds forming.
The sorting test is always the same: "passed in genes, or gained in
life?" for traits; "how does it change the odds?" for behaviors
and structures.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Sort these traits: seed shape of a pea plant; a scar on a
   gardener's hand; a dog's learned trick; a rose's flower color.
   Give the test (genes vs. life experience) for each sorting.
2. Using the Worked example 1 means, write two sentences: one
   stating the variety effect with numbers, one stating the light
   effect with numbers.
3. A learner concludes from the dataset: "Low light is better for
   plants because they grow taller." Correct the conclusion using
   the health warning (color and sturdiness), in two sentences.
4. Choose one animal behavior and one plant structure from the
   teaching above and explain, for each, *how* it raises the
   probability of successful reproduction — not just that it does.

## Independent practice

5. Write a CER paragraph answering: "Do genetic factors, environmental
   factors, or both influence the growth of organisms?" Use the
   project's practice dataset means (19.0, 25.0, 14.4, 19.4 cm) as
   evidence and name both effects.
6. A plant grown in a windy spot develops a thick, short stem.
   Its seeds are planted in a calm greenhouse. Will the offspring
   have thick, short stems because their parent did? Explain in
   three sentences, naming inherited vs. acquired correctly.

## Applied task

Take one measurement from your own investigation log (or, if you
used the paper alternative, one group from the practice dataset)
and write a four-sentence observation report: what you measured,
the number, what it suggests about genes or environment, and one
thing you would change to test your suggestion further.

## Exit check

(a) Define inherited trait and acquired trait in one sentence each.
(b) Name one genetic factor and one environmental factor that
influence growth. (c) Name one behavior or structure that raises
the probability of successful reproduction, and state how.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the sorting test ("passed in genes,
  or gained in life?") is printed on the practice page; learners
  may sort by pointing to the two columns.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer the CER paragraph orally in
  three labeled sentences (claim, evidence, reasoning) instead of
  writing.
- **Extension:** design a follow-up test that separates "taller
  because stretching" from "taller because healthier": name the
  extra measurements (stem thickness, leaf color, leaf count) and
  predict what each would show in low light vs. full light.

## Teacher note

Watch for "acquired traits are inherited" (the classic Lamarckian
error), "taller = healthier," and explanations of behaviors that
never state the mechanism ("it helps reproduction" without how).
Full guidance and keys are in the teacher guide and answer key.
