# Lesson 02 — Mitosis and Meiosis

**Goal (student-readable):** I can compare mitosis and meiosis by
purpose, number of divisions, chromosome number, and whether the
resulting cells are identical or varied — using pea-plant numbers.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, paper
chromosome strips (14 strips in 7 matching pairs, two colors).

## Readiness check (5 min)

A pea plant's body cell has 14 chromosomes. If that cell divides
once and each new cell must also run the whole plant, how many
chromosomes should each new cell get? If instead the plant is making
a sex cell (gamete) that will later combine with another gamete,
how many chromosomes should the gamete get — and why would the
full 14 be a problem? Write both numbers with one reason each.

## Teaching

A **chromosome** is a packaged bundle of genetic information (DNA)
inside a cell's nucleus. A body cell's chromosome number is written
**2n** ("two sets"); a gamete's is **n** ("one set"). In garden
peas, 2n = 14 and n = 7: a pea body cell carries 14 chromosomes in
7 pairs, and a pea gamete carries 7, one from each pair. For
comparison only, in people 2n = 46 and n = 23. The pattern — body
cells double, gametes single — is the same in both.

**Mitosis** is cell division for growth and repair. One body cell
divides **once** into **two** cells, and each new cell gets a full
copy of all the chromosomes: 14 → 14 and 14. Mitosis cells are
**genetically identical** to the parent cell (barring mutation).
When you grow, heal a cut, or when a strawberry runner grows a new
plantlet, mitosis is doing the copying.

**Meiosis** is cell division for making gametes. One cell divides
**twice** into **four** cells, and each new cell gets only half the
chromosomes: 14 → 7, 7, 7, and 7. Meiosis cells are **genetically
varied**: each gamete gets a different subset — one chromosome from
each pair, and which member of each pair it gets is a matter of
chance. When two gametes later combine at fertilization, the full
number is restored (7 + 7 = 14), but the combination is new: that
is why sexual offspring vary.

Model it with strips. Lay out 7 pairs (one strip of each color per
pair) for a pea body cell: 14 strips. For mitosis, copy every strip
and split the copies into two piles of 14 — two identical cells.
For meiosis, do not copy: split the pairs so each of the four piles
gets one strip from each pair — 7 strips each — but shuffle which
color goes to which pile. The four piles differ from each other,
and none of them matches the parent cell. That difference — copied
vs. shuffled-and-halved — is the whole lesson.

### Worked example 1 — Counting through mitosis

*Question:* A pea body cell (14 chromosomes) divides by mitosis.
How many cells result, how many chromosomes does each have, and are
they identical to the parent cell?

*Reasoning:* Mitosis is one division into two cells, and each cell
gets a full copy. So: 2 cells, 14 chromosomes each, genetically
identical to the parent cell. Check: 14 copied, then split evenly,
gives 14 and 14 — the number is conserved because the chromosomes
were copied before the split. If a learner answers "7 and 7," they
have described meiosis, not mitosis: halving is the meiosis move.

### Worked example 2 — Counting through meiosis and fertilization

*Question:* A pea cell (14 chromosomes) divides by meiosis. How
many cells result, how many chromosomes does each have, and what
happens to the number when two of those cells combine?

*Reasoning:* Meiosis is two divisions into four cells, each with
half the chromosomes: 4 cells, 7 chromosomes each, genetically
varied (different subsets of the 7 pairs). At fertilization, two
gametes combine: 7 + 7 = 14, restoring the body-cell number in the
offspring — but with a new combination, so the offspring varies
from both parents. Check the arithmetic both ways: halving must give
7 (14 ÷ 2), and restoring must give 14 (7 + 7). A gamete with the
full 14 would be a problem because fertilization would then give
28, then 56 — the number would double every generation.

A common error is to say meiosis "loses" genetic information.
Nothing is lost from the species: each gamete carries a complete
*set* (one from each pair), just not both members of each pair.
The information is shuffled and halved for transport, then
recombined — not thrown away.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Build both models with the strips: a mitosis split (14 → 14, 14)
   and a meiosis split (14 → 7, 7, 7, 7). Label each pile with its
   chromosome number and "identical" or "varied."
2. Complete the comparison: for mitosis and for meiosis, state
   purpose, number of divisions, number of resulting cells,
   chromosomes per resulting cell, and identical vs. varied.
   (This is the revised legacy reproduction-assignment comparison
   task; the keyed model is in the answer key.)
3. A learner writes: "Meiosis makes two identical cells with 14
   chromosomes each." Name the two errors and correct each with a
   number.
4. Explain in two sentences why a strawberry plantlet (Lesson 01)
   can be made by mitosis alone, while a pea seed needs meiosis
   plus fertilization.

## Independent practice

5. In 6–8 sentences, compare mitosis and meiosis covering purpose,
   number of divisions, chromosome number, and whether the cells
   are identical or varied — the legacy comparison task in full.
6. A pea gamete has 7 chromosomes. A gardener claims a pea plant
   grown from that gamete alone (without fertilization) would be a
   normal pea plant. Explain, using chromosome numbers, why that
   plant would not have the normal body-cell number.

## Applied task

Draw two labeled diagrams (boxes and numbers are enough): one cell
dividing by mitosis and one dividing by meiosis, both starting from
a pea body cell with 14 chromosomes. Each diagram must show the
number of divisions, the number of resulting cells, and the
chromosome number in each resulting cell.

## Exit check

Fill in, from memory: Mitosis — divisions: ___; cells made: ___;
chromosomes each (pea): ___; identical or varied? ___. Meiosis —
divisions: ___; cells made: ___; chromosomes each (pea): ___;
identical or varied? ___.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** keep the strip model on the table
  while answering; a learner may point to piles and count aloud
  instead of writing numbers.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer the exit check by building
  the two strip models and labeling the piles, rather than writing.
- **Extension:** explain why the same halving pattern (46 → 23 in
  people) matters for keeping the chromosome number stable across
  generations, in three sentences.

## Teacher note

Watch for the two classic swaps: "mitosis halves" and "meiosis
copies." The strip model, not re-reading, fixes both — make the
learner build, not just recite. Full guidance and keys are in the
teacher guide and answer key.
