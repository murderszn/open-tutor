# Lesson 03 — Genes, Proteins, and Mutations

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain the gene → protein →
trait chain and judge whether a mutation is harmful, beneficial, or
neutral — and explain why the answer can depend on the environment.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil.

## Readiness check (5 min)

A recipe book has one recipe for a cake. If someone changes one
word in that recipe, what are three possible outcomes for the cake?
(Think: ruined, better, or no difference at all.) Keep your three
outcomes in mind: genes work like recipes, and this lesson tests
whether that comparison holds.

## Teaching

A **gene** is a section of **DNA** — the instruction molecule inside
chromosomes — and each chromosome carries many genes. The chain that
turns instructions into traits runs in three steps:

**Gene → protein → trait.** A gene chiefly controls the production
of a specific **protein** (a working molecule that does a job in the
cell: building a structure, carrying something, or speeding up a
chemical change). Proteins, working together, affect the structures
and functions of the organism — and those structures and functions
are what we see as **traits**: flower color, seed shape, height,
leaf shape. Change the instruction, and you may change the protein,
and changing the protein may change the trait. That "may" matters:
not every instruction change changes the protein, and not every
protein change changes a visible trait.

A **mutation** is a structural change to a gene — a change in the
DNA instructions on a chromosome. Mutations happen by chance: they
are copying errors or damage, not changes an organism chooses or
"needs." Because a mutation may change a protein, it may result in
an effect on the organism that is:

- **Harmful** — the changed protein works worse or not at all, and
  the organism is worse off in its environment;
- **Beneficial** — the changed protein works better for that
  organism in that environment, or opens a new possibility;
- **Neutral** — the changed protein works about the same, or the
  trait does not change in any way that matters, so the organism is
  neither helped nor harmed.

The same mutation can be beneficial in one environment and harmful
in another. That is why the judgment always names the environment:
"beneficial" is never a property of the mutation alone.

All three examples below are **simplified teaching models**, not
documented field observations. They are built to show the logic of
the chain, and the unit never asks learners to treat them as real
case data.

### Worked example 1 — Tracing the chain, step by step

*Question:* A simplified model: a flower-color gene controls a
protein that makes purple pigment. A mutation changes that gene so
the protein makes no pigment, and the flower grows white instead of
purple. Trace the chain and judge the effect — twice: once in a
meadow where the local pollinators visit purple flowers far more
often than white ones, and once in a meadow where pollinators visit
both colors equally.

*Reasoning:* Step 1, gene: the pigment gene's instructions changed.
Step 2, protein: the protein no longer makes purple pigment. Step 3,
trait: the flower is white instead of purple. Judgment: in the first
meadow the white flower is visited less, so it is less likely to be
pollinated and make seeds — the mutation is **harmful there**,
because it lowers the probability of successful reproduction. In
the second meadow pollinators visit both colors equally, so seed
production is unchanged — the mutation is **neutral there**. Same
gene change, same protein change, different verdict: the environment
is part of the judgment.

### Worked example 2 — A mutation that breaks something essential

*Question:* A simplified model: a seedling inherits a mutation in a
gene whose protein builds chlorophyll, the pigment that captures
light for photosynthesis (Unit 03). The seedling grows pale yellow
and stays tiny while its green siblings grow tall. Judge the effect
and explain each step of the chain.

*Reasoning:* Gene changed → chlorophyll-building protein changed
(works poorly or not at all) → trait: pale leaves with little
chlorophyll → the seedling captures little light energy, makes
little sugar, and grows poorly. In any sunlit environment where
growth depends on photosynthesis, this mutation is **harmful**: it
damages a structure the organism depends on. Contrast this with a
neutral case: a mutation that changes a gene but leaves the protein
working exactly as before gives no trait change at all — nothing to
judge harmful or beneficial, because nothing the organism does has
changed. A common error is to call every mutation harmful; the
neutral case disproves that in one step.

A second common error is to say a mutation happens "because the
organism needs it." Mutations are random changes. What is *not*
random is what happens next: organisms with a helpful variant in a
given environment tend to leave more offspring — but that sorting
process is Unit 05's topic, not this lesson's. Here, keep the two
ideas apart: mutation creates variation by chance; the environment
judges it.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Draw the chain gene → protein → trait for the pale-seedling
   model, labeling what changed at each step.
2. A mutation changes a gene, but the protein it controls works
   exactly as before. What kind of effect is this, and why? Answer
   in two sentences.
3. Judge this modeled mutation twice: a mutation makes a plant's
   seed coat thicker. In a dry meadow where seeds must survive a
   long dry season, is it harmful, beneficial, or neutral? In a wet
   meadow where thick coats delay germination past the best growing
   weeks, what is it? Give one reason for each judgment.
4. Correct this claim: "Mutations are always bad, and plants mutate
   on purpose when they need a new trait." Name the two errors.

## Independent practice

5. Write a paragraph tracing a mutation of your own invention
   (plant or animal) through gene → protein → trait, then judge it
   harmful, beneficial, or neutral in a named environment, with one
   reason. Label your example "teaching model" so it is never
   mistaken for a real observation.
6. Explain in three sentences why two learners can judge the same
   mutation differently and both be right.

## Applied task

Choose one of the two worked examples and re-tell it as a
three-panel strip (words or drawings): panel 1, the gene change;
panel 2, the protein change; panel 3, the trait and the judgment
with its environment named. A strip that omits the environment is
incomplete — add it before you finish.

## Exit check

(a) State the chain in order, using the words gene, protein, and
trait. (b) Name the three possible effects of a mutation.
(c) In one sentence, explain why a mutation's effect can depend on
the environment.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the recipe comparison from the
  readiness check may be reused throughout: recipe = gene, cake =
  protein's work, and the finished cake's taste = trait.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer the applied task orally,
  panel by panel, instead of drawing.
- **Extension:** explain why a mutation in a body cell that is not
  passed to offspring (for example, in a leaf cell) cannot change
  the traits of the next generation, while a mutation in a gamete
  can — in three sentences. (Gamete vs. body-cell inheritance is
  developed further in the teacher guide; do not guess beyond the
  chain taught here.)

## Teacher note

Watch for "mutation = always harmful," "mutation = on purpose," and
"gene = trait" (skipping the protein step). Insist on the full
chain and a named environment in every judgment. Full guidance and
keys are in the teacher guide and answer key.
