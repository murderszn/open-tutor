# Answer Key — Unit 04: Reproduction, Inheritance, and Genetic Variation

**Adult only.** Every student item in the lessons, practice bank,
quiz, assessment, and project has a solution or scoring note here.
Open responses accept any answer that makes the same scientific
point in the learner's own words; the model answers below show the
required elements, not the only acceptable wording.

## Lesson 01

- **Readiness check:** the cutting is more likely genetically
  identical: it grows by copying one parent's information
  (asexual). The seedling combines two parents' information
  (sexual), so it varies.
- **Guided 1:** Left panel — one parent, identical information,
  asexual. Right panel — two parents' information combined via
  flower and seed, varied information, sexual.
- **Guided 2:** Potato "eyes" — asexual (one parent, copied
  information). Rose cross seeds — sexual (two parents, combined
  information).
- **Guided 3:** Runner plantlets grow directly from the parent, so
  they carry its copied information. Pea seeds each carry a
  different combination from two parents, so they differ.
- **Guided 4:** Correction: sexual reproduction gives variation,
  not necessarily more offspring; asexual reproduction is the
  faster route to many offspring, because one parent copies itself
  without finding a mate.
- **Independent 5 (model):** "In asexual reproduction one parent
  copies its genetic information into offspring that carry
  identical information, as when a strawberry runner roots into a
  new plantlet. In sexual reproduction two parents each contribute
  genetic information, and each offspring receives a different
  combination, as when pea seeds form in a pod — so sexual
  offspring vary from their parents and from each other."
- **Independent 6 (model):** "If a new disease attacks a crop, a
  field of genetically identical plants may all be vulnerable at
  once, while a varied field is more likely to contain some plants
  whose combination resists it — variation gives a population
  more chances to survive a change."
- **Applied task:** caption for the right panel, for example:
  "A flowering pea plant visited by a bee, with a pod of seeds
  forming. This is sexual reproduction: the seeds carry combined
  genetic information from two parents, so they will vary."
- **Exit check:** (a) Asexual: one parent copies its genetic
  information into offspring. (b) Sexual: two parents combine
  genetic information in offspring. (c) Sexual produces variation,
  because each offspring gets a different combination.

## Lesson 02

- **Readiness check:** Growth/repair division: 14 chromosomes each
  (full copy). Gamete: 7 (half), because two gametes must combine
  back to 14 — a full 14 in each gamete would double the number at
  fertilization.
- **Guided 1:** Mitosis piles: 14 and 14, "identical." Meiosis
  piles: 7, 7, 7, and 7, "varied."
- **Guided 2:** Mitosis — purpose growth/repair; 1 division;
  2 cells; 14 chromosomes each (pea); identical. Meiosis — purpose
  making gametes; 2 divisions; 4 cells; 7 chromosomes each (pea);
  varied.
- **Guided 3:** Errors: meiosis makes four cells, not two; and
  each has 7 chromosomes, not 14.
- **Guided 4:** The cutting needs mitosis only: its cells divide
  by copying (14 → 14). A pea seed needs meiosis (to make gametes
  with 7) plus fertilization (7 + 7 = 14) to form.
- **Independent 5 (model):** "Mitosis divides a body cell once into
  two cells, each with the full chromosome number — in peas 14 —
  and the cells are genetically identical, which serves growth and
  repair. Meiosis divides a cell twice into four cells, each with
  half the number — in peas 7 — and the cells are genetically
  varied, because each receives a different subset of chromosome
  pairs. Meiosis serves sexual reproduction: its gametes combine
  at fertilization to restore the full number in a new combination."
- **Independent 6:** A plant grown from one gamete alone would
  have body cells with 7 chromosomes, not the normal 14 — it would
  be missing one full set, so it would not be a normal pea plant.
- **Applied task:** Mitosis diagram: 14 → (1 division) → 14, 14.
  Meiosis diagram: 14 → (2 divisions) → 7, 7, 7, 7. Both diagrams
  must label divisions, cell counts, and chromosome numbers.
- **Exit check:** Mitosis — 1 division; 2 cells; 14; identical.
  Meiosis — 2 divisions; 4 cells; 7; varied.

## Lesson 03

- **Readiness check:** Three outcomes: the cake is ruined
  (harmful), better (beneficial), or unchanged (neutral) — the
  same three outcomes as a mutation.
- **Guided 1:** Panel chain for the pale seedling: gene for the
  chlorophyll protein changed → protein works poorly or not at
  all → trait: pale leaves, little chlorophyll, poor growth.
- **Guided 2:** Neutral: the protein is unchanged, so no trait
  changes and the organism is neither helped nor harmed.
- **Guided 3:** Dry meadow — beneficial (thicker coat helps seeds
  survive the dry season, raising the chance of germination later).
  Wet meadow — harmful (the thick coat delays germination past the
  best growing weeks, lowering the chance of successful growth).
  Both judgments are for the modeled case, with the environment
  named.
- **Guided 4:** Errors: (1) not all mutations are bad — neutral
  and beneficial cases exist; (2) mutations are random changes,
  not purposeful responses to need.
- **Independent 5:** Accept any coherent modeled chain that names
  gene → protein → trait, judges harmful/beneficial/neutral, names
  an environment, and is labeled "teaching model."
- **Independent 6:** The same mutation changes the same protein
  and trait in both learners' cases, but the trait's value depends
  on the environment (for example, pollinator visits or water),
  so the verdict can differ and both can be right.
- **Applied task:** Three panels — gene change, protein change,
  trait plus judgment with environment named. Missing environment
  = incomplete.
- **Exit check:** (a) Gene → protein → trait. (b) Harmful,
  beneficial, neutral. (c) A trait's value depends on the
  environment, so the same protein/trait change can help in one
  environment and harm (or not matter) in another.

## Lesson 04

- **Readiness check:** Coin counts vary; exactly 5 heads in 10 and
  exactly 5 mixed pairs in 10 are possible but not guaranteed —
  probability describes the group, not each toss.
- **Guided 1:** Rr × Rr boxes: RR, Rr, Rr, rr. Genotype ratio
  1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr. Phenotype ratio 3 round : 1 wrinkled.
- **Guided 2:** Rr × rr boxes: Rr, Rr, rr, rr. Genotype ratio
  1 Rr : 1 rr. Phenotype ratio 1 round : 1 wrinkled = 1/2 round,
  1/2 wrinkled = 50% each.
- **Guided 3:** Error: the learner counted the two Rr boxes as
  wrinkled. Rr is round (one dominant R is enough). Correct ratio:
  3 round : 1 wrinkled (RR, Rr, Rr round; rr wrinkled).
- **Guided 4:** Expected wrinkled in 80: 80 × 1/4 = 20. The real
  count may be 17, 23, or another nearby number because each
  offspring is an independent chance event.
- **Independent 5:** RR × Rr boxes: RR, RR, Rr, Rr. Genotype ratio
  2 RR : 2 Rr = 1 RR : 1 Rr. Phenotype ratio 4 round : 0 wrinkled
  (all round).
- **Independent 6:** The round parent must be Rr. The wrinkled
  offspring is rr, so it received r from each parent; the round
  parent therefore carries r, and since it looks round it must
  also carry R — genotype Rr.
- **Applied task:** Simulation counts will vary (for example, 28
  round / 12 wrinkled, or 32/8). Compare with the 30/10 expectation
  and explain the difference as chance variation across 40
  independent pairs, not as an error in the square.
- **Exit check:** (a) Rr is round, because one dominant R allele
  is enough for round seeds. (b) 3 round : 1 wrinkled.
  (c) A Punnett square gives the probabilities across a group of
  offspring, not a guarantee for any single seed.

## Lesson 05

- **Readiness check:** Height alone cannot settle it: the tall
  pale seedling may be stretching for light (etiolation), while
  the short green one may be sturdier and healthier. Judge with
  color and sturdiness too.
- **Guided 1:** Inherited: pea seed shape, rose flower color
  (passed in genes). Acquired: scar, learned trick (gained in life,
  not passed in genes).
- **Guided 2:** Variety effect: "Variety A is taller than B in
  both lights — 19.0 vs. 14.4 cm in full light and 25.0 vs. 19.4 cm
  in low light." Light effect: "Both varieties grow taller in low
  light — A from 19.0 to 25.0 cm (+6.0) and B from 14.4 to 19.4 cm
  (+5.0)."
- **Guided 3:** Correction: low-light plants are taller but paler
  and thinner — they are stretching toward light, not growing
  more healthily. Taller does not mean healthier.
- **Guided 4:** Accept any correct pair, for example: nest building
  protects eggs/chicks from cold and predators, raising survival
  odds; bright petals attract pollinators that transfer pollen,
  raising seed-formation odds. The mechanism must be stated.
- **Independent 5 (model CER):** Claim: both genetic and
  environmental factors influence growth. Evidence: A beats B by
  4.6 cm in full light and 5.6 cm in low light; both varieties rise
  in low light (A +6.0, B +5.0 cm). Reasoning: the variety pattern
  repeats across lights and the light pattern repeats across
  varieties, so neither factor alone explains the data.
- **Independent 6:** No. The thick, short stem is an acquired
  response to wind during the parent's life, not a change in its
  genes, so its seeds carry the parent's genes — not the wind
  response — and grow normal stems in calm conditions.
- **Applied task:** Accept any honest four-sentence report with a
  measurement, a genes-or-environment suggestion, and a follow-up
  test idea.
- **Exit check:** (a) Inherited: passed through genes from
  parents. Acquired: gained during life, not passed through genes.
  (b) Genetic factor: variety/breed. Environmental factor: light,
  water, space, or food. (c) Any correct behavior/structure with
  its mechanism (see Guided 4).

## Practice bank

**P1.** Strawberry runner — 1 parent, identical information,
asexual. Pea seeds — 2 parents' information combined, varied
information, sexual.
**P2.** Accept any correct pair (for example, bacterium dividing =
asexual; dogs having puppies = sexual). Benefit of variation:
in a changing environment, a varied population is more likely to
contain individuals whose combination survives the change (for
example, resists a new disease).
**P3.** Bacterium dividing — asexual; two bacteria with identical
information. Coral eggs + sperm — sexual; offspring with combined,
varied information.
**P4.** Correction: asexual reproduction has exactly one parent,
not none; and its offspring are not necessarily weaker — copying
is fast and successful in stable conditions, though identical
offspring share vulnerabilities.
**P5.** Each seedling received a different combination of genetic
information from the two rose parents, so the seedlings vary from
both parents and from each other.
**P6.** Model paragraph: see Lesson 02 Independent 5 above
(mitosis: 1 division, 2 cells, 14 each, identical, growth/repair;
meiosis: 2 divisions, 4 cells, 7 each, varied, gametes).
**P7.** Meiosis: 4 cells, 7 chromosomes each. (Two divisions;
14 ÷ 2 = 7.)
**P8.** Errors: mitosis makes two cells, not four; each has 14
chromosomes, not 7. (Four cells with 7 each describes meiosis.)
**P9.** 7 + 7 = 14. The number does not double every generation
because meiosis halves it (14 → 7) before fertilization restores
it (7 + 7 = 14).
**P10.** No meiosis is needed for the cutting: the new plant's
cells are made by mitosis, copying the parent's 14 chromosomes
into identical cells.
**P11.** Order: gene → protein → trait. A gene controls a
protein's production; proteins affect structures and functions,
which appear as traits.
**P12.** Neutral: the protein works exactly as before, so no trait
changes and the organism is neither helped nor harmed.
**P13.** Pollinator-preferring-purple meadow: harmful (white
flowers are visited less, so fewer seeds). Equal-visit meadow:
neutral (seed production is unchanged). Each judgment names its
environment.
**P14.** Correction: mutations are random changes, not purposeful
responses to need; and they are not all damaging — neutral and
beneficial outcomes exist.
**P15.** RR × Rr boxes: RR, RR, Rr, Rr. Genotypes 1 RR : 1 Rr
(2:2 simplified). Phenotypes 4 round : 0 wrinkled (all round).
**P16.** Rr × Rr genotypes: 1 RR : 2 Rr : 1 rr = 1/4, 1/2, 1/4.
Phenotypes: 3 round : 1 wrinkled = 75% round, 25% wrinkled.
**P17.** Expected wrinkled in 200: 200 × 1/4 = 50. The real count
could differ because each offspring is an independent chance event.
**P18.** The round parent is Rr. Half the offspring are wrinkled
(rr), so each wrinkled offspring received r from the round parent;
the parent looks round, so it must also carry R.
**P19.** Error: the square gives group probabilities, not a
guarantee for four particular seeds. Four seeds could be 4 round,
2 round / 2 wrinkled, or any other combination — 3:1 is the
expectation across many offspring.
**P20.** Inherited: pea seed shape, dog coat color (passed in
genes). Acquired: scar, learned song (gained in life, not passed
in genes). Test: "passed in genes, or gained in life?"
**P21.** Variety: "A beats B in both lights (19.0 vs. 14.4 cm full;
25.0 vs. 19.4 cm low)." Light: "Both varieties are taller in low
light (A +6.0 cm, B +5.0 cm)."
**P22.** Correction: low-light plants are taller but paler and
thinner — they stretch toward light. Height alone does not show
health; color and sturdiness must be judged too.
**P23.** The thorny bush protects eggs and chicks from predators
(and the nest from disturbance), raising the probability that
offspring survive to independence. Mechanism: protection.
**P24.** Bright, nectar-rich flowers attract pollinators, which
carry pollen between flowers, raising the probability that seeds
form. Mechanism: attraction + pollen transport.

## Unit assessment

**A1.** Model: "Asexual reproduction uses one parent, which copies
its genetic information into identical offspring, as when a
strawberry runner roots. Sexual reproduction combines genetic
information from two parents, so offspring vary, as when pea seeds
form in a pod."
**A2.** Mitosis: 2 cells, 14 each, identical. Meiosis: 4 cells,
7 each, varied.
**A3.** Chain: the gene's instructions changed → the chlorophyll
(or pigment) protein stopped working → the seedling is pale and
small. Judgment: harmful in a sunlit garden, because without the
working protein the seedling cannot photosynthesize well and grow.
**A4.** Preferring meadow: beneficial (more pollinator visits,
more seeds). Equal-visit meadow: neutral (no change in visits or
seeds). This shows a mutation's effect depends on the environment,
not on the mutation alone.
**A5.** RR × Rr boxes: RR, RR, Rr, Rr. Genotypes 1 RR : 1 Rr.
Phenotypes all round (4 : 0).
**A6.** Rr × rr boxes: Rr, Rr, rr, rr. Genotypes 1 Rr : 1 rr.
Phenotypes 1 round : 1 wrinkled. Expected wrinkled in 60:
60 × 1/2 = 30.
**A7.** (a) Dog coat color — inherited (passed in genes).
(b) Muscle built by exercise — acquired (gained in life).
(c) Plant flower color — inherited. (d) Stem bent toward a
window — acquired (environmental response during life).
**A8.** Hard shells let squirrels bury nuts; buried nuts that are
not recovered can germinate in a new spot. Mechanism: transport
plus a protected germination site, raising the probability of new
plants.
**B1.** (a) Means: C sunny (12 + 13 + 11 + 12) ÷ 4 = 48 ÷ 4 =
12.0 cm. C shade (16 + 17 + 15 + 16) ÷ 4 = 64 ÷ 4 = 16.0 cm.
D sunny (9 + 10 + 8 + 9) ÷ 4 = 36 ÷ 4 = 9.0 cm. D shade
(13 + 12 + 14 + 13) ÷ 4 = 52 ÷ 4 = 13.0 cm. (b) Variety effect:
C beats D by 3.0 cm in both conditions (12.0 vs. 9.0 sunny; 16.0
vs. 13.0 shade). (c) Light effect: both varieties are 4.0 cm
taller in shade (C 12.0 → 16.0; D 9.0 → 13.0). (d) Correction:
shade plants are taller but paler and thinner — they stretch
toward light. Taller does not mean healthier; color and sturdiness
must be judged too.
**B2 (model CER).** Claim: both genetic and environmental factors
influence growth. Evidence: variety C beats D by 3.0 cm in both
sunny and shade conditions, and both varieties grow 4.0 cm taller
in shade than in sun. Reasoning: the variety pattern repeats
across light conditions, showing a genetic effect, and the light
pattern repeats across varieties, showing an environmental effect
— so neither factor alone explains the data.
