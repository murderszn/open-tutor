# Lesson 05 — Cell Models That Explain

**Goal (student-readable):** I can build labeled plant and animal cell
models and explain how each part helps the whole cell do its job.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, paper and
coloring supplies (or a digital drawing tool); the revised tasks in
the [project](../project.md) (this lesson prepares them).

## Readiness check (5 min)

A bicycle is a system: wheels, chain, pedals, and frame each do a job,
and the bike only works when they work together. Name what happens if
one part is removed (e.g., no chain). How is a cell like this — and
how is it different? (Answer: like the bike, a cell's parts each do
jobs that serve the whole; unlike the bike, a cell is alive — it
maintains itself, takes in energy, and can make more cells.)

## Teaching

A **model** in science is a representation that helps you explain or
predict something — a diagram, a physical build, a computer
simulation. Models are powerful and limited: a labeled cell diagram
shows *where* parts sit and *what* they do, but it cannot show them
moving, and it freezes one moment in time. Good scientists say what
their model shows **and** what it leaves out.

MS-LS1-2 asks you to **develop and use a model to describe the
function of a cell as a whole and the ways the parts of cells
contribute to that function.** The key phrase is *as a whole*: the
point is not memorizing a parts list, but explaining how the parts
serve the living cell. The primary roles you are responsible for:

- **Cell membrane** — the boundary: controls what enters and leaves
  the cell. (Without it, the cell's contents would spill and mix with
  the surroundings.)
- **Cell wall** (plants, not animals) — stiff outer layer that gives
  shape and support.
- **Nucleus** — holds the DNA instructions that direct the cell's
  activities.
- **Chloroplasts** (plants, not animals) — capture light energy for
  photosynthesis, making food.
- **Mitochondria** — release energy from food (cellular respiration),
  powering the cell's work.
- **Cytoplasm** — the jelly-like interior where the parts sit and
  where much of the cell's chemistry happens.

Two comparison questions do most of the explaining work:

1. **Plant vs. animal:** plants have a cell wall and chloroplasts;
   animals do not. Why? A plant cannot move to find food or hold
   itself up with muscles, so it needs a stiff wall for support and
   chloroplasts to make its own food from sunlight. An animal cell
   trades the wall for flexibility (think of a muscle cell squeezing)
   and gets energy by eating.
2. **Whose job serves the whole?** The nucleus does not "run" the
   cell like a boss — it stores instructions the whole cell uses.
   Mitochondria do not work "for themselves" — the energy they release
   powers everything from building proteins to cell division. Each
   part's job only makes sense in service of the whole living cell.

**How to build a model that explains (not just decorates):**

1. Draw or build the cell with each part in a plausible place and
   **labeled with its name and its one-sentence job**.
2. Add a caption answering: "How do these parts work together to keep
   the cell alive?"
3. Note one thing your model **cannot** show (movement? true 3-D
   shape? scale?).
4. Compare plant and animal: circle the parts that differ and write
   *why* the difference makes sense for how each organism lives.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A learner labels "mitochondria — the powerhouse of the
cell." Improve this label so it explains the part's contribution to
the whole.

"Mitochondria — release energy from food so the cell can do work
(move, build, divide)." The slogan names nothing about the whole;
the improved label says what the part *does for the cell*.
Reason check: if you removed all mitochondria, the cell could not
power its activities — that is the "contribution to the whole."

**Example 2.** Explain, using the parts list, why a plant cell needs
both chloroplasts *and* mitochondria.

Chloroplasts capture light energy and store it as food (sugar) — but
food is not usable energy until it is broken down. Mitochondria
release the energy from that food for the cell's work. So the plant
cell is a two-step system: *capture and store* (chloroplast), then
*release and use* (mitochondria). A common error is "plants don't
need mitochondria because they photosynthesize" — in fact plant cells
do cellular respiration too.

**Example 3.** A learner's model shows a nucleus floating outside the
cell membrane with an arrow labeled "controls the cell." Name two
things wrong.

(1) Placement: the nucleus must be *inside* the membrane — the
membrane is the boundary of the whole cell; nothing that is part of
the cell sits outside it. (2) The "controls" arrow suggests command
without mechanism; the accurate statement is that the nucleus holds
DNA instructions used by the rest of the cell. Models must respect
the boundary concept — this is the MS-LS1-2 assessment emphasis on
the membrane's role.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. For each part — membrane, wall, nucleus, chloroplast,
   mitochondria — write its one-sentence job *for the whole cell*.
2. A cell model has no labels. List three questions you would ask to
   test whether it *explains* (not just shows) the cell.
   *(Hint: "what does this part do for the whole cell?" is one.)*
3. Explain one structural difference between plant and animal cells
   and why it makes sense for how each lives.
4. Name one thing a flat labeled diagram cannot show about a real
   cell, and why that limit matters.

## Independent practice

5. Sketch a quick animal cell: place and label membrane, nucleus,
   cytoplasm, mitochondria. Add a two-sentence caption on how the
   parts serve the whole.
6. Error analysis: "The cell wall controls what enters and leaves the
   plant cell." Correct the error and explain which part actually does
   this job.
7. Which two organelles form the plant's energy system, and what does
   each contribute? (Answer in a short paragraph.)

In three to four sentences, explain how a cell is like a system:
name three parts and describe what each contributes to the cell as a
whole.

## Supports, extensions, and teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** part-job matching cards (part name ↔
  one-sentence job) before writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build the model from
  craft materials and narrate each part's job orally while the adult
  scribes the labels.
- **Extension:** add one more part (e.g., vacuole, ribosomes) with a
  researched one-sentence job, citing the source; note that detailed
  biochemistry of these parts is beyond this unit's assessment scope
  (MS-LS1-2 assessment boundary).
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** the "powerhouse" slogan
  and the "brain of the cell" slogan both mislead — cells have no
  bosses or power plants, only parts serving the whole. Insist on
  *contribution-to-the-whole* phrasing in every label. Also watch for
  wall/membrane confusion (item 6 above is the classic).
