# Lesson 01 — What Counts as Scientific Evidence

**Goal (student-readable):** I can tell an observation from an
inference, and I can explain what makes evidence scientific: it is
testable, measurable, repeatable, and checked by others.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, three
household objects (adult's choice — e.g., a coin, a leaf, a battery).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Look at the first object. Say (or write) two things you can detect
about it with your senses — no guessing. Then say one thing you
*think* is true about it that you cannot directly detect. Which
statement is safer to build an argument on: what you detected, or what
you guessed? (Answer: what you detected — the observation. The guess
may be right, but it is not evidence yet.)

## Teaching

An **observation** is information you gather with your senses or with
instruments: what you see, hear, feel, measure. An **inference** is a
conclusion you draw from observations: what you think it means.
Observations are the raw material; inferences are what you build from
them. Both matter — but confusing one for the other is the most common
way science goes wrong.

*Observation:* "The plant's leaves are yellow at the edges and dry to
the touch." *Inference:* "The plant is not getting enough water." The
inference might be right — or the plant might have a disease, or
sitting in too much sun. An inference is a good place to start
investigating, not the end of the story.

Not every statement is **scientific evidence**. For evidence to count
in science, it must be:

1. **Testable** — someone could check it with an investigation, not
   just an opinion. "The soil is damp" is testable (stick in a finger,
   use a moisture meter). "This plant is the prettiest" is not.
2. **Measurable** — stated as numbers with units whenever possible.
   "The stem grew" is weak; "the stem grew 3 centimeters in one week"
   is evidence.
3. **Repeatable** — if you (or someone else) do it again the same
   way, you get a similar result. One lucky reading is a hint, not a
   finding.
4. **Checked by others** — science is a team sport. Results shared,
   questioned, and re-tested by other people are trusted more than
   results kept private.

Scientists organize evidence into an argument with three parts —
**claim, evidence, reasoning**:

- **Claim:** the answer you are defending ("Yeast is alive").
- **Evidence:** the observations and measurements that support it
  ("The yeast mixture released gas bubbles and doubled in volume in
  20 minutes at 30°C").
- **Reasoning:** why the evidence supports the claim ("Living things
  carry out life processes such as using energy and growing; gas
  release and growth are signs of those processes").

Notice what reasoning is *not*: it is not just repeating the evidence.
It explains the connection. You will write your first
claim-evidence-reasoning (CER) paragraph in Lesson 04.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Sort each statement into observation or inference.

(a) "The liquid in the beaker is cloudy."
(b) "The liquid is cloudy because a chemical reaction is happening."
(c) "The thermometer reads 22°C."

(a) **Observation** — cloudiness is directly seen. (b)
**Inference** — the *cause* of the cloudiness is not seen; a reaction
is a possible explanation, but so is dust, or temperature change.
(c) **Observation** — a measured reading. Notice: (b) is the most
interesting statement, but it is the one that still needs testing.

**Example 2.** A learner writes: "I think exercise makes your heart
beat faster." Is this scientific evidence yet? How could it become
evidence?

As written it is an inference, not evidence. To make it evidence:
measure a resting heart rate (beats per minute — measurable), then
measure again after one minute of jumping jacks (testable), repeat on
three different days (repeatable), and have a family member check the
counting method (checked by others). The numbers — "resting 72 bpm,
after exercise 128 bpm, similar on all three days" — are evidence.

**Example 3.** Two learners argue. Ana says, "This rock is heavier."
Ben says, "This rock has a mass of 214 grams on the scale." Whose
statement is stronger scientific evidence, and why?

Ben's. "Heavier" is a comparison with no number; "214 grams" is
measurable, testable (weigh it again), and lets others repeat the
measurement exactly. A common error is thinking strong words make
strong evidence — "obviously," "clearly," "definitely" add no weight.
Numbers and repeatability add weight.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Sort: (a) "The bread smells sour." (b) "The bread went bad because
   someone left it out." (c) "The classroom has 24 desks."
   *(Hint: ask "did anyone directly detect this, or did they conclude
   it?")*
2. A friend says, "Dogs are smarter than cats." Explain which of the
   four evidence tests this fails first, and how you would restate it
   as a testable claim.
3. Turn this weak statement into scientific evidence: "The water got
   hot."
4. Read this mini-argument. Label the claim, the evidence, and the
   reasoning: "The seed sprouted because it was alive. It grew a root
   4 mm long in two days, and only living seeds grow. Growing is a
   life process, so the growth is evidence the seed is alive."
   *(Hint: the claim is the sentence that needs defending.)*

## Independent practice

5. Write two observations and two inferences about the weather outside
   right now. Label each.
6. A class plants 10 bean seeds; 8 sprout. A classmate claims, "All
   seeds sprout." Is the evidence strong enough? Name the test it
   fails and explain.
7. Error analysis: a learner writes, "I observed that the metal got
   hot because the particles moved faster." Identify the observation
   and the inference, and rewrite the sentence so the two are not
   mixed.

## Application

Pick one household object. Write three observations about it (use at
least one measurement with a unit), then write one inference and label
it clearly as an inference. Trade with the adult: can they tell which
is which?

## Exit check

In two or three sentences, explain why "I think it is true" is not
scientific evidence, naming at least two of the four evidence tests.

## Supports, extensions, and teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** sentence starters — "I observed ___."
  / "I infer ___ because I observed ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the sort in practice items 1–4 may be
  done by pointing to two labeled cards ("observation" /
  "inference") instead of writing.
- **Extension:** find one science news headline and identify which of
  the four evidence tests the reporter describes (or fails to describe).
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** learners often treat an
  inference as a "bad" answer. It is not — inferences are how science
  moves forward. The point is to *label* them so they get tested.
