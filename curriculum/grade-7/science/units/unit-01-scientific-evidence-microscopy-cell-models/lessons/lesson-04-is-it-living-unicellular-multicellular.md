# Lesson 04 — Is It Living? One Cell or Many

**Goal (student-readable):** I can use observations and cell-theory
evidence to decide whether something is living, and to argue that
living things are made of cells — one cell or many types of cells.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, six small
items for the sort (adult's choice: mix living, once-living, and
non-living — e.g., a leaf, a seed, a feather, a pebble, a piece of
chalk, a dead twig).

## Readiness check (5 min)

A classmate says, "This classroom chair is made of wood, wood comes
from trees, trees are living, so the chair is alive." Where does the
reasoning break? (Answer: the chair shows no life processes now — no
growth, no energy use, no response — and its cells, if any remain,
are dead wood cells. "Made from something once living" is not the
same as "is living.")

## Teaching

What makes something **living**? Biologists look for a cluster of
signs — no single one is enough:

- It is made of **cells** (cell theory's first tenet).
- It **uses energy** (takes in food or light; releases waste).
- It **grows and develops**.
- It **responds** to its environment.
- It **reproduces** (makes more of its kind).

A flame uses energy and "grows," but it is not made of cells and does
not reproduce with inherited traits — so it fails the sort. A seed
looks dead, but it is alive in a dormant state: put it in damp soil
and it grows, because its cells are intact and ready.

Living things come in two great architectures. **Unicellular**
organisms — bacteria, many protists like amoebas and paramecia, some
algae and fungi (yeast) — are a single cell that does *everything*:
eat, move, sense, reproduce. **Multicellular** organisms — plants,
animals, most fungi — are many cells, often of **different types**
with specialized jobs (a leaf cell photosynthesizes; a root cell
absorbs water; a muscle cell contracts), cooperating as one organism.

This is the claim MS-LS1-1 asks you to investigate: **living things
are made of cells — either one cell, or many different numbers and
types of cells.** The evidence comes from exactly what this unit has
been building: microscope observations (onion skin shows box-like
cells; pond water shows single-celled swimmers; your own cheek shows
flat cells), repeatable measurements, and other scientists' checked
results.

**"Is it living?" sort — how to run it like an investigation:**

1. For each item, list **observations only** (no inferences yet):
   texture, color, weight, any movement, any change over time.
2. Check each against the five living signs. Record which signs are
   *observed*, not assumed.
3. Decide: living / once-living / non-living — and write the evidence
   for the decision.
4. For anything living or once-living: **one cell or many?** What
   evidence would settle it? (A microscope view would show cells;
   without one, you reason from what is known — but label that
   reasoning as inference, per Lesson 01.)

Now assemble your first **claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph**:

> **Claim:** A mushroom is living (or: yeast is alive).
> **Evidence:** It grows larger over three days (measured 2 cm →
> 5 cm); it releases carbon dioxide gas that inflates a balloon over
> the jar; under the microscope its tissue shows cells.
> **Reasoning:** Growth, energy use (gas release from respiration),
> and cellular structure are life processes; non-living things do not
> show all three together.

The reasoning is the glue: it says *why* the observations count as
evidence for *this* claim.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Item: a dried bean seed. Observations: hard, brown,
11 mm long, no movement, no change in one hour. Living, once-living,
or non-living — and what evidence would settle it?

**Living (dormant).** The one-hour observation window is too short —
a seed's life processes are paused, not absent. Evidence to settle
it: place it in damp paper towel for three days and measure for a
root emerging (growth = life process). A common error is "no movement
means not alive" — plants barely move, and dormant seeds do not move
at all, yet both are alive.

**Example 2.** A learner writes this CER paragraph. Identify the
claim, evidence, and reasoning, and name one weakness.

> "Pond water contains living things. I saw tiny round shapes moving
> on their own under the microscope at 100×, changing direction when
> they bumped into debris. Self-directed movement and response to the
> environment are life processes, and the shapes were single cells, so
> they are unicellular organisms."

Claim: pond water contains living things. Evidence: moving round
shapes at 100×, changing direction on contact. Reasoning: movement +
response = life processes; single-celled = unicellular organisms.
Weakness: only one observation session — Lesson 01's repeatability
test is not met; a second sample on another day would strengthen it.

**Example 3.** "A bacterium and a human are both made of cells, but a
human is obviously more complex." Turn the second half into a precise,
evidence-based statement.

Complexity here means **many different types of cells with
specialized functions cooperating** (MS-LS1-1's "many different
numbers and types of cells"): a human has muscle, nerve, blood, skin,
and dozens more cell types; a bacterium is one cell doing all jobs.
"Obviously more complex" is an opinion; the cell-type count is
evidence.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Run the "is it living?" sort on three of the six items. For each,
   list two observations, then decide living / once-living /
   non-living with evidence.
2. A classmate says a virus is alive because it reproduces. Using the
   five signs (and cell theory's first tenet), explain why most
   biologists classify viruses as non-living.
   *(Hint: can a virus reproduce on its own, outside a host cell? Is
   it made of cells?)*
3. Sort: amoeba, oak tree, yeast, mushroom, E. coli — unicellular or
   multicellular? *(Hint: fungi like mushrooms are multicellular;
   yeast is the single-celled exception.)*
4. Draft a CER paragraph: "A seed is alive." Use at least two pieces
   of evidence and one reasoning sentence.

## Independent practice

5. Finish the sort for the remaining three items, including the
   "one cell or many?" inference for each living/once-living item.
6. Error analysis: "The rock is non-living because it doesn't move."
   Is the conclusion right? Is the reasoning sufficient? Rewrite with
   stronger evidence.
7. A pond-water sample shows no movement at 40×. A learner concludes
   "nothing in pond water is alive." Name the evidence test this
   fails and design a better check.

## Application

Choose one item from the sort and write a full CER paragraph
defending your classification. Trade with the adult: they play
skeptic and must find one weak link (missing evidence, untested
inference); you revise.

## Exit check

State MS-LS1-1's claim in your own words (one sentence), then list
two specific pieces of evidence from this unit that support it.

## Supports, extensions, and teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** CER sentence frames — "My claim is
  ___. My evidence is ___. This supports my claim because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the sort may be done by placing items
  on three labeled mats (living / once-living / non-living) with oral
  justification.
- **Extension:** research one extremophile (e.g., tardigrades or deep-sea
  tube-worm bacteria) and explain which living signs it shows in an
  extreme environment.
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** "once-living" collapses
  into "living" for many learners (the chair example). Keep pressing:
  *what life processes does it show right now?* Also watch for
  movement = alive (flames, rivers) — return to the full five-sign
  checklist.
