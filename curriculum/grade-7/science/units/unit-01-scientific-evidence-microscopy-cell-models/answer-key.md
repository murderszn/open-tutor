## Lesson 01

**Readiness:** detected = observation; guessed = inference; the
observation is the safer foundation.

**Guided practice**

1. (a) O — smell is directly detected. (b) I — the cause (left out)
   is concluded, not detected. (c) O — a counted number.
2. Fails **testable** first ("smarter" is not defined as something you
   can measure). Testable restatement: "In a puzzle-feeder test, dogs
   solve the puzzle in less time on average than cats" (also passes
   measurable).
3. Example: "The water's temperature rose from 20°C to 65°C in 3
   minutes." (Number + unit + time span; testable and repeatable.)
4. Claim: "The seed sprouted because it was alive." Evidence: "It grew
   a root 4 mm long in two days." Reasoning: "Only living seeds grow;
   growing is a life process, so the growth is evidence the seed is
   alive." (The middle sentence "only living seeds grow" is the
   reasoning link.)

**Independent practice**

5. Example — observations: "The sky is gray and clouds cover all of
   it"; "The air feels cold on my face." Inferences: "It will rain
   this afternoon" (concluded from the clouds); "A storm is coming"
   (concluded, not detected). Accept any correctly labeled set.
6. No — "all seeds" overreaches 8 of 10. Fails **repeatable/**
   sufficient-evidence: one small trial cannot support "all." The
   evidence supports "most of these seeds sprouted," not a universal
   claim.
7. Observation: "the metal got hot" (felt/measured). Inference:
   "the particles moved faster" (molecular explanation, not directly
   detected). Rewrite: "I observed that the metal felt hot to the
   touch. I infer that its particles are moving faster, because
   temperature measures average particle motion."

**Exit check (acceptable):** "I think it is true" is an opinion, not
evidence — it is not measurable and not testable by anyone else, so it
fails at least two of the four tests (testable, measurable,
repeatable, checked by others).

## Lesson 02

## Lesson 03

**Readiness:** Apparent size depends on distance — eyes alone cannot
settle true size; instruments that change apparent size let us study
what is too small to judge directly.

**Guided practice**

1. Eyepiece (top tube) · nosepiece (rotating lens holder) · objective
   lenses (4×, 10×, 40× on the nosepiece) · stage (flat platform) ·
   slide (glass, clipped on stage) · light source (beneath stage) ·
   coarse focus knob (larger) · fine focus knob (smaller) · arm
   (curved handle) · base (bottom support).
2. (a) 10 × 4 = **40×**. (b) 10 × 40 = **400×**.
3. 100 ÷ 2 = **50 times smaller** than the eye's resolution limit.
4. (1) All living things are made of one or more cells — Schleiden
   (plants, 1838) and Schwann (animals, 1839). (2) The cell is the
   basic unit of structure and function in life — the synthesis of
   their work. (3) All cells come from pre-existing cells — Virchow,
   1855 (*Omnis cellula e cellula*); building on Hooke (1665, first
   saw and named cells) and Leeuwenhoek (1670s, saw living cells).

**Independent practice**

5. (1) Lowest-power objective in place. (2) Slide clipped on stage.
   (3) From the side, coarse-focus the stage up near (not touching)
   the objective. (4) Through the eyepiece, coarse-focus down until
   the image appears, then fine-focus sharp. (5) Center, rotate to
   higher power, fine focus only.
6. 1 mm = 1,000 µm; 1,000 ÷ 300 ≈ **3–4 cells**.
7. Inaccurate: Leeuwenhoek did not prove the universal claim — he
   contributed the observation that single-celled living things exist
   ("animalcules"), which was one piece of evidence; the universal
   "all living things are made of cells" came later with Schleiden,
   Schwann, and Virchow.

**Exit check (acceptable):** Cells are tens of micrometers or smaller
while the eye resolves ~100 µm, so cells were invisible; Hooke's
microscope revealed cork "cells" (1665), Leeuwenhoek's lenses revealed
living single cells (1670s) — without magnification, cell theory had
no evidence base.

## Lesson 04

**Readiness:** The chair shows no life processes now; "made from
something once living" ≠ "is living."

**Guided practice**

1. Example (leaf): observations — "green, flexible, 9 cm long, no
   movement in 5 minutes"; decision — living (still shows organized
   cellular structure; would show life processes given time/water).
   (Pebble): observations — "gray, hard, 3 cm"; non-living — no cells,
   no life processes. Accept reasoned sorts.
2. A virus is not made of cells and cannot reproduce on its own — it
   must hijack a host cell's machinery. It fails the "made of cells"
   tenet and the independent-reproduction sign, so most biologists
   classify viruses as non-living.
3. Unicellular: amoeba, yeast, E. coli. Multicellular: oak tree,
   mushroom.
4. Example CER: "My claim is that a seed is alive. My evidence is
   that the seed grew a 4 mm root in two days in damp soil, and that
   a second seed did the same. This supports my claim because growth
   is a life process, and only living things grow."

**Independent practice**

5. Accept completed sorts with observations, decisions, and the
   one-cell-or-many inference labeled as inference where no
   microscope was used.
6. The conclusion is right (rocks are non-living) but the reasoning
   is insufficient — many living things barely move (plants, dormant
   seeds). Stronger: "The rock is non-living because it is not made
   of cells and shows no life processes: no growth, no energy use, no
   reproduction."
7. Fails **repeatable/sufficient evidence** (one look, one
   magnification, one sample). Better: examine several drops from
   different spots, scan multiple fields of view, repeat on another
   day, try 100×–400×.

**Exit check (acceptable):** MS-LS1-1: living things are made of
cells — one cell or many types. Evidence: (1) microscope views show
cells in onion skin, cheek samples, pond water; (2) "is it living?"
sorts consistently find cellular structure in living things and not
in non-living things.

## Lesson 05

**Readiness:** Removing the chain stops the bike — parts serve the
whole. A cell is similar (parts serve the whole system) but alive:
it maintains itself and reproduces.

**Guided practice**

1. Membrane — boundary controlling what enters/leaves, protecting
   the whole cell. Wall (plants) — stiff support holding the cell's
   shape. Nucleus — holds DNA instructions the whole cell uses.
   Chloroplasts (plants) — capture light energy to make food for the
   cell. Mitochondria — release energy from food to power the cell's
   work.
2. Example questions: "What does each part do for the whole cell?"
   "What would break if this part were removed?" "What can the model
   not show?"
3. Example: plants have a cell wall and chloroplasts; animals do not.
   A plant can't move to find food or flex muscles for support, so it
   needs a stiff wall and its own food-making (photosynthesis);
   animal cells stay flexible for movement and get energy by eating.
4. A flat diagram cannot show movement, 3-D shape, true scale, or
   chemistry — so it can mislead about how dynamic and tiny the real
   cell is.

**Independent practice**

5. Sketch check: membrane as outer boundary, nucleus inside,
   cytoplasm filling, mitochondria placed within; caption ties ≥2
   parts to the whole cell's life.
6. Correction: the **cell membrane** controls what enters and leaves;
   the **cell wall** gives the plant cell shape and structural
   support. The wall is a stiff outer layer; the membrane is the
   selective boundary.
7. Chloroplasts capture light energy and store it as food; mitochondria
   release energy from that food for the cell's work. Together they
   are the plant cell's capture-then-use energy system.

**Exit check (acceptable):** Names three parts (e.g., membrane,
nucleus, mitochondria) and states each one's contribution to the whole
living cell — boundary control, instructions, energy release.

## Unit assessment

**A1.** (a) Observation: "The flame is yellow at the tip and the wax
level drops as it burns." Inference: "The candle is burning wax as
fuel." (b) The observation is evidence as written (directly
detected); the inference becomes testable by measuring the candle's
mass before and after burning for a timed interval.
**A2.** Fails measurable and testable ("obviously"/"great" are
opinions). Testable rewrite: "Bean plants given 50 mL of fertilizer
weekly grow at least 20% taller in 4 weeks than plants given none."
**A3.** 1.5 × 3,600 = **5,400 s**; 800 ÷ 100 = **8 m**.
**A4.** Accurate (centered on 60°C) and precise (range 0.4°C); tight
clustering means high precision — repeated measurements agree.

**B1.** (a) 10 × 4 = **40×**; 10 × 40 = **400×**. (b) Widest field of
view (easiest to find the specimen); greatest working distance
(least risk of cracking the slide).
**B2.** (a) 1,000 ÷ 8 = **125 cells**. (b) 8 µm is about 12 times
smaller than the ~100 µm the eye can resolve; objects that small
cannot be seen unaided, so red blood cells were unknown until
magnification.
**B3.** (1) All living things are made of one or more cells —
Schleiden (plants, 1838) & Schwann (animals, 1839). (2) The cell is
the basic unit of structure and function — their synthesis. (3) All
cells come from pre-existing cells — Virchow, 1855 (*Omnis cellula e
cellula*). (Hooke 1665 first saw/named cells; Leeuwenhoek 1670s saw
living single cells — the observations the theory was built on.)

**C1.** Flaws: (1) single trial, one magnification, one sample —
fails repeatability; fix with multiple samples, multiple fields of
view, and a repeat on another day. (2) Overreaching conclusion —
"nothing seen at 100× once" does not prove absence; fix by checking
at 400× and considering that some cells need staining or culturing
to be visible.
**C2.** Mushroom: multicellular — a microscope shows hyphae made of
many connected cells. Yeast: unicellular — shows single oval cells,
some with buds.
**C3.** (Sample, 4–6 sentences) "My claim is that living things are
made of cells. Under the microscope, onion skin shows repeating
box-like compartments, and pond water shows single cells swimming on
their own — direct observations of cells in both a plant and a
unicellular organism. These support the claim because the same
cellular structure appears in living things of very different kinds,
exactly what cell theory predicts. The reasoning holds because
non-living things examined the same way show no such cells."
**C4.** (a) Cell wall — stiff outer layer giving shape and support;
chloroplasts — capture light energy for photosynthesis, making food
for the cell. (b) The label is wrong: the **cell membrane** controls
what enters and leaves; the **cell wall** is structural support. The
wall is a rigid outer layer; the membrane is the selective boundary.
