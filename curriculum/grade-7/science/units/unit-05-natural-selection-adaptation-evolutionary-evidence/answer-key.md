## Lesson 01

**Readiness check.** The arrowhead (bottom layer) was buried
first. Superposition: the bottom layer was laid down first, so it
is the oldest.

**Guided practice.**

1. Oldest: the trilobite (gray bottom layer). Middle: the fish
   skeleton (reddish-brown layer). Youngest: the coiled shell
   (light tan layer). Rule: superposition — deeper layers are
   older.
2. The sponge is older than the trilobite: it sits in a layer
   below the gray layer, so it was laid down first.
3. The trilobite appears in only one layer (the bottom); it lived
   earliest here and disappears above. (Mammals and birds also
   appear in only the top layer — they show up late.)
4. (4,600 − 66) ÷ 4,600 × 200 cm = 4,534 ÷ 4,600 × 200 =
   197.1 cm, about 197 cm from the left end.

**Independent practice.**

5. Model answer: "The fossil record shows that life has changed
   over time. By superposition, deeper layers are older, so the
   order of fossils in the layers is the order they lived. In the
   table, trilobites appear only in the bottom layer and then
   disappear, while mammals and birds appear only in the top
   layer. Because groups appear and disappear instead of staying
   the same in every layer, the record shows change over time,
   not one fixed set of animals."
6. No. In the worked-example table, fish and trilobites share the
   bottom (oldest) layer, so the table does not show fish living
   after trilobites. The "before mammals" part is right — mammals
   appear only in the top layer.

**Applied task.** First life: (4,600 − 3,800) ÷ 4,600 × 200 =
800 ÷ 4,600 × 200 = 34.8 cm, about 35 cm from the left end.
First fish: (4,600 − 530) ÷ 4,600 × 200 = 4,070 ÷ 4,600 × 200 =
177.0 cm, about 177 cm from the left end.

**Exit check.** (a) Superposition: in an undisturbed stack,
deeper layers were laid down first and are older. (b) Any one
pattern, for example groups appear and disappear over time
(extinction). (c) Earth is about 4.6 billion years old.

## Lesson 02

**Readiness check.** Both — the bat's wing and the whale's
flipper both share the human arm's bone plan (humerus, radius
and ulna, wrist bones, finger bones).

**Guided practice.**

1. In order from shoulder to tip: humerus, radius and ulna,
   wrist bones, finger bones.
2. Model answer: "A bat wing and a human arm contain the same
   bones in the same order, so they were inherited from the same
   four-limbed ancestor. The jobs differ because each lineage
   reshaped the same bones — long fingers and skin for flying,
   short fingers and a hand for grasping."
3. (a) Analogous — both fly, but the butterfly wing has no bones
   and a completely different structure. (b) Homologous — both
   contain the same front-leg bone plan from a common ancestor.
4. Vestigial — it is a leftover of the hind legs of whales'
   land-dwelling ancestors.

**Independent practice.**

5. Model answer: "Tiktaalik supports the idea that four-legged
   animals descended from fish because it mixes features of both
   groups. Like a fish it has scales, gills, and thin fin rays;
   like a four-legged animal it has a neck, shoulders, sturdy
   wrist bones, and thick ribs. A transitional form is an organism
   that displays a mix of features from an older group and a newer
   group, showing the steps between them."
6. Model improvement: "The mix of features shows Tiktaalik is
   related to both fish and four-legged animals, but one fossil
   alone does not prove the whole relationship. The relationship
   is shown more firmly by the pattern across many fossils — a
   sequence of transitional forms — together with anatomy, embryo,
   and DNA evidence."

**Applied task.** The tree should show human, bat, and whale
branching from one shared ancestor, with the shared forelimb plan
on the trunk. The bird's wing belongs as a homologous forelimb
because it contains the same bone plan. The insect wing does not
belong on this tree: it is analogous (same job, different origin),
not inherited from the four-limbed ancestor.

**Exit check.** (a) A homologous structure is a body part shared
because of a common ancestor, even though it now does different
jobs. (b) An analogous structure does a similar job but has a
different origin and structure. (c) Any one correct example, for
example whale hip bones — leftovers of hind legs; the human
tailbone — leftover of a tail; ostrich wings — flightless wings
kept for balance and display.

## Lesson 03

**Readiness check.** The green beetle is harder to spot on green
grass, so it is more likely to survive long enough to reproduce.

**Guided practice.**

1. The four steps, in order: (1) heritable variation exists in
   the population; (2) some variants survive and reproduce better
   in a particular environment; (3) those survivors pass the trait
   on, so differential survival and reproduction occur; (4) trait
   frequencies shift in the population over generations.
2. 40 ÷ 19 = 2.105…, about 2.1 times more often.
3. Model answer: "When the soot was gone, the tree trunks were
   light again, so light moths were camouflaged and dark moths
   stood out. Light moths survived and reproduced more, so the
   light percentage rose and the dark percentage fell."
4. Correction: "No finch's beak changed during the drought.
   Finches with deeper beaks survived better and reproduced more,
   so the next generation's average beak was deeper — the
   population changed, not the individuals."

**Independent practice.**

5. Model answer: "Peppered moths vary in a heritable trait:
   light or dark wings. On soot-darkened trunks, dark moths were
   camouflaged, so they survived bird predation better and had
   more offspring. Because the dark form is inherited, each
   generation had a higher percentage of dark moths — about 98%
   around Manchester by 1895. When clean-air laws removed the
   soot, the advantage flipped: light moths were camouflaged
   again, so the light form became common once more."
6. Green: 100 ÷ 500 = 20% at first; 40 ÷ 500 = 8% later — a drop
   of 12 percentage points. The numbers show green beetles
   survived and reproduced less on brown soil, so their share of
   the population fell.

**Applied task.** Model CER: "Claim: dark mice will become more
common. Evidence: in the peppered-moth case, the form matching
the background was recaptured about twice as often (40% dark vs.
19% light on dark trunks). Reasoning: dark mice on dark sand are
harder for predators to see, so they survive and reproduce more,
and because color is heritable, each generation will have a higher
percentage of dark mice."

**Exit check.** (a) Variation exists; some variants survive and
reproduce better; survivors pass the trait on; trait frequencies
shift over generations. (b) Fitness means survival *and*
reproduction, not strength. (c) Populations evolve — trait
frequencies change across generations; individuals do not change
their inherited traits.

## Lesson 04

**Readiness check.** The two animals share an early developmental
plan inherited from a common ancestor; their development diverges
later into different adult bodies.

**Guided practice.**

1. Tail, pharyngeal arches (neck grooves), and limb buds — plus a
   large head for the body.
2. Model answer: "The human embryo grows a tail and then mostly
   reabsorbs it, leaving the tailbone. That only makes sense if
   humans inherited the tail-bearing early plan from an ancestor
   that kept its tail — the shared plan is the evidence."
3. Correction: "Haeckel's 19th-century drawings exaggerated the
   similarities between embryos and are not accurate. Scientists
   use modern photographs of real embryos instead."
4. Model answer: "An adaptation is a heritable trait that helps a
   population survive and reproduce — for example, a dark moth's
   camouflage. An acquired trait is gained during one lifetime and
   not passed on — for example, skin tanned by the sun."

**Independent practice.**

5. Model answer: "Fish, chicken, and human embryos all share the
   same early pattern: a tail, pharyngeal arches, and limb buds.
   Later they differ — the arches become gills in the fish but
   parts of the jaw and ear in the chicken and human, and the
   human tail is mostly reabsorbed. The shared early plan shows
   the three species inherited the same developmental plan from a
   common ancestor, and the later differences show their lineages
   diverging. Adult bodies alone would hide the relationship,
   because an adult human has no gills and no tail."
6. The weightlifter's muscles are an acquired trait — gained in
   life, not inherited. The dark moth's camouflage is an
   adaptation — heritable and helpful for survival and
   reproduction. The distinction matters because only heritable
   traits are passed to the next generation: the moth's offspring
   inherit dark wings, but the weightlifter's children do not
   inherit large muscles.

**Applied task.** Acceptable answer: the two vertebrates' early
embryos should share the tail, arches, and limb buds, while their
adults differ in the structures suited to their lives (for
example, a frog's smooth skin and metamorphosis vs. a lizard's
scales and claws). Evidence against the prediction would be early
embryos with no shared features at all.

**Exit check.** (a) Embryological similarities show the species
share an early developmental plan from a common ancestor. (b)
Haeckel's drawings exaggerated the similarities and are not
accurate evidence. (c) An adaptation is a heritable trait that
helps survival and reproduction; an acquired trait is gained
during one lifetime and is not inherited.

## Lesson 05

**Readiness check.** Humans selectively bred wolves over thousands
of years, choosing different sizes, shapes, coats, and behaviors
each generation.

**Guided practice.**

1. Artificial selection is humans choosing which individuals
   reproduce, based on a trait they want.
2. Model answer: "Wild mustard already varied: some plants had
   bigger leaves, some bigger flower buds, some thicker stems.
   Farmers kept and bred the biggest-leafed plants to get cabbage
   and kale, the biggest-budded plants to get broccoli and
   cauliflower, and so on — each vegetable is one part of the same
   plant, enlarged by choosing that part over and over."
3. Natural selection's selector is the environment; artificial
   selection's selector is humans; both need heritable variation
   to work.
4. Correction: "The first farmers did not invent new genes. They
   selected plants that already had bigger flower buds and bred
   only those, generation after generation."

**Independent practice.**

5. Model answer: "Natural and artificial selection both change
   trait frequencies over generations, and both need heritable
   variation and differential reproduction. They differ in the
   selector and the goal: in natural selection the environment
   selects — birds eat the visible moths, so camouflage spreads —
   while in artificial selection humans select — breeders choose
   the dogs or plants with the trait they want. Artificial
   selection is often faster, because humans select strongly every
   generation, but the underlying mechanism is the same."
6. Any one misconception from the clinic, corrected with evidence.
   Example: "Error: 'Evolution means progress toward perfect.'
   Correct: evolution means better suited to a particular
   environment — dark moths were better on sooty trunks and worse
   on clean bark, so there is no ladder and no finish line."

**Applied task.** Acceptable if it names a verifiable ancestor,
trait, selector, and technology (for example: corn — teosinte
ancestor, bigger softer kernels, human farmers, selective
breeding). Unverifiable details must be flagged as unverified,
not guessed.

**Exit check.** (a) Artificial selection is humans choosing which
individuals reproduce based on a wanted trait. (b) Same: both
need heritable variation and change trait frequencies through
differential reproduction. Different: the selector (environment
vs. humans) or the goal (survival vs. a wanted trait). (c)
Individuals do not evolve; populations do — trait frequencies
change across generations.

## Project

**Simulation (practice dataset).** Light background: dark
percentages 50% → 40% → 34% → 30%. Dark background: 50% →
60% → 66% → 70%. Check learner tables against these values; for
own-data simulations, check that each generation's percentage is
computed as dark ÷ total × 100 and that the doubling step is
applied to both survivor counts. Accept a bar graph with labeled
axes and correct bar heights (allow small drawing error); the
trend, not the draftsmanship, is the point.

**Case study.** Accept any real species with a correctly described
adaptation, a named environment, two lines of evidence (for
example, fossils + anatomy, or embryos + DNA), and a four-step
selection explanation in order. Return for revision any case
study that describes an acquired trait as an adaptation, or that
claims an individual changed to meet a need.

**Artificial-selection comparison.** Accept any verifiable
example (dog breed, Brassica vegetable, corn, wheat, roses).
Check: the ancestor is named, the trait is named, the selector
is humans, the technology is named (selective breeding,
hybridization, genetic testing, or another verifiable one), and
the comparison names the selector in each case.

**Walkthrough prompts (if the learner stalls).** "Point to the
generation where the dark percentage changed most — what
happened there?" / "Which line of evidence in your case study
would you trust most, and why?" / "Who did the selecting in your
artificial example — and who would do it in nature?"
