# Lesson 04 — Embryos and Ancestry

**Goal (student-readable):** I can compare patterns in
embryological development across species and explain what they
show about common ancestry.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil.

## Readiness check (5 min)

A human baby and a puppy look very different. But a very early
human embryo and a very early dog embryo look strikingly similar.
Why might two animals that look so different as adults look
similar as early embryos? Write your prediction and one reason.
(You will check it against evidence below.)

## Teaching

An **embryo** is an organism in the early stages of development,
before it is born or hatches. **Embryological development** is
the sequence of changes an embryo goes through as it grows into
its adult form. Comparing embryos across species reveals
relationships that adult bodies hide: species that look very
different as adults often look alike as early embryos, because
they inherited the same early developmental plan from a common
ancestor and only later grew in different directions.

Look at the pattern in words (the same pattern appears in
photographs of real embryos in the resource pack's references —
never use the famous 19th-century drawings by Ernst Haeckel,
which exaggerated the similarities and are not accurate evidence):

| Stage | Fish embryo | Chicken embryo | Human embryo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | Tail, pharyngeal arches (grooves on the neck), limb buds, large head for its body | Tail, pharyngeal arches, limb buds, large head for its body | Tail, pharyngeal arches, limb buds, large head for its body |
| Later | Arches become gills; tail stays; fins form | Arches become parts of the jaw and ear; beak, wings, and feathers form; tail shortens | Arches become parts of the jaw and ear; arms and legs form; the tail is mostly reabsorbed, leaving the tailbone |

The early pattern is the same in all three: tail, arches,
limb buds, big head. The differences appear later, as each
species follows its own developmental path. That is the evidence:
if each species had been built independently from scratch, there
would be no reason for a human embryo to grow a tail and then
reabsorb it, or for a chicken embryo to carry the same neck
grooves as a fish. The shared early plan points to a shared
ancestor; the later differences show the lineages diverging.

A second line of evidence agrees: **DNA**. Humans and
chimpanzees share about 99% of their DNA — the instructions that
build bodies are nearly identical, and the small differences
account for the differences between the species. DNA evidence,
anatomy (Lesson 02), embryos (this lesson), and fossils
(Lesson 01) are four independent lines of evidence that all point
the same way: living things are related through common ancestors.
When independent lines of evidence agree, the conclusion is
much stronger than any one line alone.

The word **adaptation** belongs here too, and it needs care. An
adaptation is a heritable trait that helps a population survive
and reproduce in its environment — the dark moth's camouflage on
sooty trunks, the finch's deep beak in a drought. An adaptation is
a feature of a *population*, shaped over generations by natural
selection (Lesson 03). It is not something an individual decides
to grow, and it is not the same as getting used to something
during one lifetime: a person who tans in the sun has not passed
a darker skin color to their children, and a weightlifter's
muscles are not inherited. Inherited traits change across
generations; acquired traits do not.

### Worked example 1 — Comparing embryos

*Question:* Use the table to identify one pattern that is the
same in all three embryos early on, and one difference that
appears later. What do they show together?

*Reasoning:* The shared early pattern is the tail plus the
pharyngeal arches plus limb buds — all three embryos have all
three. The later difference is what the arches become: gills in
the fish, parts of the jaw and ear in the chicken and the human.
Together they show that the three species share an early
developmental plan — evidence of a common ancestor — and that
their development diverges later, producing different adult
bodies. The relationship is visible in the embryo and hidden in
the adult: an adult human has no gills and no tail, so the adult
alone would not show the connection to the fish.

### Worked example 2 — Two lines of evidence

*Question:* Species A and B share about 99% of their DNA and
have homologous forelimbs. Species C shares about 90% of its
DNA with A and has a different limb structure. Which pair is
more closely related — A and B, or A and C — and why do two
lines of evidence matter?

*Reasoning:* A and B are more closely related: they share more
DNA (99% vs. 90%) *and* the same forelimb bone plan, while C
differs on both counts. Two lines of evidence matter because
each could mislead alone — DNA could be similar by coincidence
in one short stretch, or a limb could look similar because of a
similar job (analogous structures, Lesson 02) — but when DNA,
anatomy, embryos, and fossils all point the same way, the
conclusion that A and B share a more recent common ancestor is
far stronger. Scientists trust a conclusion most when
independent evidence agrees.

A common error is to say "humans came from chimpanzees." We did
not: humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor that lived
millions of years ago, and each lineage changed separately since
then — cousins, not parent and child. Another error is to treat
embryo similarity as proof on its own: it is one line of
evidence, and it is strongest alongside fossils, anatomy, and
DNA.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Name the three features that all three embryos in the table
   share early on.
2. Explain in two sentences why a human embryo has a tail that
   is mostly reabsorbed — what does that show about ancestry?
3. A learner says, "Haeckel's drawings prove embryos are
   identical." Correct the error: what is wrong with using those
   drawings, and what should be used instead?
4. Explain in two sentences the difference between an adaptation
   and an acquired trait, using one example of each.

## Independent practice

5. Write a 5–7 sentence explanation of how embryological
   evidence supports common ancestry. Use the fish–chicken–human
   table, name the shared early pattern and one later
   difference, and state why adult bodies alone would hide the
   relationship.
6. A weightlifter has large muscles, and a dark moth is hard to
   see on a sooty trunk. One of these is an adaptation and the
   other is an acquired trait. Identify each, and explain in two
   sentences why the distinction matters for what the next
   generation inherits.

## Applied task

Choose any two vertebrates (for example, a frog and a lizard, or
a horse and a bat). Write a short paragraph predicting what their
early embryos would share and how their adult bodies would
differ, using the pattern from this lesson. Then write one
sentence stating what would count as evidence *against* your
prediction.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) state what embryological similarities
show; (b) state why Haeckel's drawings are not used as evidence;
(c) state the difference between an adaptation and an acquired
trait.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** *embryological* ("embryo" +
  "-logical", the study of) is built from its parts in the
  teaching above; a learner may answer orally or by labeling the
  table rows.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw the three embryos at the
  early stage (all with tail, arches, limb buds) and at the later
  stage (different), labeling what changed, instead of writing
  the comparison.
- **Extension:** research the DNA comparison between humans and
  chimpanzees (about 99% shared) and between humans and mice
  (about 85% shared) and explain what the numbers suggest about
  how recently each pair shared an ancestor.

## Teacher note

Watch for "humans came from chimps" and for treating Haeckel's
drawings as evidence — the lesson explicitly rules them out.
The DNA percentages are widely reported figures (human–chimp
about 99%, human–mouse about 85%); they are approximate and the
lesson says so. The embryo table is a simplified teaching
summary of the real pattern, not a set of measurements. Full
guidance and keys are in the teacher guide and answer key; do not
read the key aloud before learners attempt the exit check.
