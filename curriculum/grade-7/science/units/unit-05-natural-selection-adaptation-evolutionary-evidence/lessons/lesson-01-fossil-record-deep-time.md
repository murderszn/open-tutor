# Lesson 01 — Reading the Fossil Record

**Goal (student-readable):** I can order rock layers and fossils
by age, read patterns of appearance and disappearance in fossil
data, and place events on a deep-time timeline.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, the
fossil-layers illustration below.

## Readiness check (5 min)

A builder digs a hole and finds three layers of rock: a dark top
layer with a rusty nail in it, a middle sandy layer with a clay
pot fragment, and a bottom gray layer with a stone arrowhead. Which
object was probably buried first — the nail, the pot fragment, or
the arrowhead? Write your prediction and one reason. (You will
check it against evidence below.)

## Teaching

A **fossil** is the preserved remains or traces of a living thing
from long ago — a bone turned to stone, a shell pressed into rock,
a footprint left in mud that hardened. Most fossils form in
**sedimentary rock**: sand, mud, and other sediments settle in
layers, bury organisms, and slowly harden into rock. The
**fossil record** is the whole collection of fossils and the layers
they sit in, in order. It documents the existence, diversity,
extinction, and change of life forms through Earth's history.

The key rule for reading it is **superposition**: in a stack of
sedimentary rock layers that has not been tipped over or disturbed,
the bottom layer was laid down first, so it is the oldest, and each
layer above it is younger. Fossils in deeper layers are older than
fossils in shallower layers. Scientists also date rocks with
radioactive clocks, but superposition alone already lets you order
events — oldest at the bottom, youngest at the top.

**Deep time.** Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, and the
oldest evidence of life is about 3.8 billion years old. Those
numbers are hard to feel, so this unit builds a paper timeline:
a 2-metre strip stands for all 4.6 billion years, so 1 cm on the
strip stands for 23 million years (4,600 million years ÷ 200 cm
= 23 million years per cm). On that strip, the first life appears
35 cm from the start, the first fish at 177 cm, and the dinosaurs'
extinction at 197 cm — humans appear in the last hair's width.
Everything you will meet in this unit — dinosaurs, Tiktaalik,
the first birds — crowds into the last few centimetres.

### Worked example 1 — Ordering the illustration's fossils

*Question:* List the three fossils in the illustration from oldest
to youngest, and state the rule.

*Reasoning:* The rule is superposition: in an undisturbed stack,
deeper layers are older. The trilobite sits in the gray bottom
layer, the deepest of the three, so it is the oldest. The fish
skeleton sits in the reddish-brown layer above the gray layer, so
it is younger than the trilobite but older than the shell. The
coiled shell sits in the light tan layer above the fish, so it is
the youngest of the three. Oldest to youngest: trilobite, fish
skeleton, coiled shell. Note what this does *not* tell us: it
does not give any fossil's age in years — only its order. Exact
ages come from other dating methods, which the timeline activity
uses.

### Worked example 2 — Patterns in a fossil table

*Question:* A scientist records fossils in three layers of one
cliff. Use the table to name two patterns.

| Layer | Fossils found |
|---|---|
| Top (youngest) | Mammal jaw, bird feather imprint |
| Middle | Dinosaur bone, fern fronds |
| Bottom (oldest) | Trilobite, early fish |

*Reasoning:* First pattern — **appearance**: mammals and birds
appear only in the top layer; nothing below it contains them, so
they show up late in this record. Second pattern —
**disappearance (extinction)**: dinosaur bone appears in the
middle layer but not in the top layer, so dinosaurs were present
and then disappear from the record above — they went extinct
before the top layer formed. A third pattern is also visible:
trilobites appear only in the bottom layer, so they are the
oldest group here and also disappear higher up. The record shows
change over time, not one fixed set of animals: groups appear,
flourish, and vanish.

A common error is to read the layers upside down — to assume the
top layer is oldest because it is "on top of" the others. In a
stack of sediments, the top is laid down *last*. Another error is
to think one fossil proves a whole story: a single fossil shows
that its kind of organism existed at that time and place; the
pattern across many layers is what shows change over time.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Using the illustration, answer in your own words: which fossil
   is oldest, which is youngest, and what is the rule? Name the
   layer each fossil sits in.
2. A fourth layer is found *below* the gray layer, containing a
   fossil sponge. Where does the sponge sit in the age order —
   older or younger than the trilobite? Explain with the
   superposition rule.
3. In the worked-example table, which group appears in only one
   layer, and what does that tell you about when it lived?
4. On the paper timeline (2 m = 4.6 billion years), how far from
   the start (Earth's formation) is the extinction of the
   dinosaurs, 66 million years ago? Show the calculation:
   (4,600 − 66) ÷ 4,600 × 200 cm.

## Independent practice

5. Write a 4–6 sentence explanation of how the fossil record
   shows that life has changed over time. Use the words
   *superposition*, *extinction*, and *fossil record*, and cite
   one pattern from the worked-example table.
6. In the worked-example table, a learner says, "Fish lived
   after trilobites but before mammals." Is that conclusion
   supported by the table? Answer yes or no, and give one reason.
   (Hint: check which layer actually contains fish, and which
   contains mammals.)

## Applied task

Start the deep-time timeline: tape the 2-metre strip to a wall or
floor, mark the left end "Earth forms — 4.6 billion years ago"
and the right end "Today", and place the first two events using
the table in the teacher guide: first life, about 3.8 billion
years ago (35 cm from the left end) and the first fish, about
530 million years ago (177 cm from the left end). You will add
Tiktaalik, Archaeopteryx, the dinosaur extinction, and humans in
Session 4.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) state the superposition rule; (b) name
one pattern the fossil record shows; (c) state how old Earth is,
about, in billions of years.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** *superposition* is built from its
  parts in the teaching above ("super-" = above, "-position" =
  placed: the layers are placed one above another, oldest below);
  a learner may answer orally or by pointing at the layers.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw the four layers with the
  three fossils instead of writing the ordering, labeling each
  layer "oldest", "middle", or "youngest".
- **Extension:** research one more fossil group (for example,
  ammonites or horses) and add its first-appearance and
  last-appearance dates to the timeline table in your notebook.

## Teacher note

Watch for the upside-down reading of layers and for "one fossil
proves it" thinking — the pattern across layers is the evidence,
not a single specimen. The timeline arithmetic is checked in the
answer key: (4,600 − 66) ÷ 4,600 × 200 = 197.1 cm, about 197 cm
from the left end. Full guidance and keys are in the teacher guide
and answer key; do not read the key aloud before learners attempt
the exit check.
