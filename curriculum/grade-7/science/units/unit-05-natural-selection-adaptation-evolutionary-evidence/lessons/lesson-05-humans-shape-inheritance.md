# Lesson 05 — Humans Shape Inheritance

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain artificial selection,
compare it with natural selection, and correct common
misconceptions about evolution.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil.

## Readiness check (5 min)

A chihuahua and a Great Dane look nothing alike, yet both are
dogs — the same species. How could one species end up with such
different sizes and shapes? Write your prediction and one reason.
(You will check it against evidence below.)

## Teaching

In natural selection, the environment does the selecting: birds
eat the moths they can see, drought leaves only hard seeds for
the finches. In **artificial selection**, humans do the
selecting: people choose which individuals reproduce, based on a
trait they want, and they do it generation after generation. The
mechanism underneath is the same — heritable variation plus
differential reproduction changes trait frequencies — but the
selector and the goal are different.

**Selective breeding** is the oldest technology for it: keep the
individuals with the trait you want, breed them, and repeat.
Dogs are the oldest example. All dog breeds — chihuahua and Great
Dane, poodle and husky — descend from wolves, domesticated more
than 15,000 years ago. By choosing which wolves (and later which
dogs) reproduced, humans shaped size, coat, ears, temperament,
and behavior. No new genes were invented by the choosing; the
variation was already in the wolf population, and humans picked
winners each generation.

One wild plant shows the same power even more clearly. Wild
mustard (*Brassica oleracea*) is a scraggly coastal plant. By
selecting different parts of it over thousands of years, farmers
produced six vegetables that look unrelated: cabbage (selected
for big leaves), kale (leaves), broccoli (flower buds),
cauliflower (flower buds), kohlrabi (stem), and Brussels sprouts
(side buds). Same ancestor, same method — pick the plants with
the biggest leaves, or buds, or stems, and breed only those.
Corn is another: modern corn descends from teosinte, a Mexican
grass with tiny hard kernels, domesticated about 9,000 years ago
by selecting plants with bigger, softer kernels.

Modern technologies have changed *how* humans influence
inheritance, which is what MS-LS4-5 asks about:

- **Hybridization** — crossing two varieties or breeds to combine
  traits (for example, crossing two corn varieties for yield and
  disease resistance).
- **Artificial insemination and embryo transfer** — in livestock,
  one prize bull or cow can parent far more offspring than by
  natural mating, spreading desired traits faster.
- **Genetic testing for breeding** — testing breeding stock for
  known gene variants (for example, a disease-resistance gene in
  crops or dogs) so the choice is made from DNA, not only from
  appearance.
- **Genetic engineering** — directly changing genes in a
  laboratory. This unit names it only: the technique exists, it
  is the newest way humans influence inheritance, and its uses
  are decided by people and laws, not by this lesson.

| Feature | Natural selection | Artificial selection |
|---|---|---|
| Who selects | The environment (predators, climate, food) | Humans, choosing for a wanted trait |
| Goal | Survival and reproduction in that environment | A trait humans want (size, yield, temperament) |
| Speed | Usually slow — many generations | Often fast — humans select strongly each generation |
| Needs | Heritable variation in the population | Heritable variation in the population |
| Result | Trait frequencies shift in the population | Trait frequencies shift in the population |

### Worked example 1 — One plant, six vegetables

*Question:* Wild mustard is one species. How did farmers get
cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, kohlrabi, and Brussels
sprouts from it?

*Reasoning:* The wild plant already varied: some plants had
bigger leaves, some had bigger flower buds, some had thicker
stems. Farmers who wanted leafy food kept and bred the
biggest-leafed plants each year; after many generations the
leaves were huge — cabbage and kale. Other farmers kept the
plants with the biggest flower buds — broccoli and cauliflower.
Others selected for stem (kohlrabi) or side buds (Brussels
sprouts). Each vegetable is one part of the same plant, enlarged
by choosing the same part over and over. The selector was human
choice, not the weather — but the result, like natural selection,
is a population whose trait frequencies changed over generations.

### Worked example 2 — Same trait, two selectors

*Question:* A dark moth on a sooty trunk and a short-legged dog
bred for burrowing: who (or what) selected each trait, and what
is the same about the two cases?

*Reasoning:* The moth was selected by the environment — birds
ate the visible light moths, so dark moths survived and
reproduced more. The dog was selected by humans — breeders chose
short-legged dogs to reproduce. What is the same: in both cases
the trait was heritable, one variant reproduced more than the
other, and over generations that variant became more common in
the population. The difference is only the selector and the goal:
survival on a dark trunk vs. a dog that fits in a burrow.

**Misconception clinic** — five errors to retire:

1. *"Evolution means progress toward better or perfect."* No:
   it means better suited to a *particular* environment. Dark
   moths were "better" on soot and "worse" on clean bark. There
   is no ladder and no finish line.
2. *"Individuals evolve."* No: populations evolve. A moth's
   color does not change during its life; the *percentage* of dark
   moths changes across generations.
3. *"Need causes change."* No: variation must already exist (from
   mutation and sexual reproduction, Unit 04). Selection only
   filters what is already there.
4. *"Natural selection creates variation."* No: mutation and
   sexual reproduction create it; selection sorts it.
5. *"Humans have stopped evolving."* No: selection still acts on
   humans (for example, lactose tolerance spread in populations
   with dairy farming, and disease resistance varies by region).
   Evolution has not stopped; it is just hard to see in one
   lifetime.

## Guided practice (with the adult or a partner)

1. Define artificial selection in one sentence, and name who
   does the selecting.
2. Explain in two sentences how six different vegetables came
   from one wild mustard plant.
3. Complete the comparison: natural selection's selector is ___,
   artificial selection's selector is ___, and both need ___ to
   work.
4. Correct this claim: "The first farmers invented new genes to
   make broccoli." What did they actually do?

## Independent practice

5. Write a 5–7 sentence comparison of natural and artificial
   selection. Cover the selector, the goal, the speed, and what
   is the same (heritable variation, differential reproduction,
   trait-frequency change), using one example of each.
6. Pick one misconception from the clinic and write a three-
   sentence correction: state the error, state the correct idea,
   and give one piece of evidence from this unit.

## Applied task

Choose a trait humans have shaped (dog size, corn kernels, wheat
yield, roses' flowers — or another you can verify). Write a short
paragraph answering: what was the wild ancestor, what trait was
selected, who selected it, and what technology was used (selective
breeding, hybridization, genetic testing, or another). If you
cannot verify a detail, say so instead of guessing.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) define artificial selection; (b) name
one way natural and artificial selection are the same and one way
they differ; (c) correct the claim "individuals evolve."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** *domestication* ("domus" = home:
  bringing a wild species into human homes and farms) is built
  from its parts in the teaching above; a learner may answer
  orally or by completing the comparison table.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw the wild mustard plant with
  six arrows to the six vegetables, labeling the selected part on
  each arrow, instead of writing the explanation.
- **Extension:** research one modern breeding technology from
  the list above (for example, genetic testing in dog breeding)
  and write a paragraph on what it changed compared with
  choosing by appearance alone.

## Teacher note

Watch for "farmers invented genes" and for value judgments
("natural is good, artificial is bad") — the lesson keeps the
comparison on mechanism, not on approval. Dog domestication is
"more than 15,000 years ago" (the exact date is still debated);
corn from teosinte is about 9,000 years ago in Mexico. Genetic
engineering is named only, with no how-to detail. Full guidance
and keys are in the teacher guide and answer key; do not read the
key aloud before learners attempt the exit check.
