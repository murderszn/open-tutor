# Unit 05 — Natural Selection, Adaptation, and Evolutionary Evidence

**Grade:** 7 · **Subject:** Science · **Track weeks:** 20–23
**Backlog:** [issue #35](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/35), section U05
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Unit 04 showed that offspring vary — sexual reproduction shuffles
genetic information, mutations change it, and the environment shapes
how it grows. This unit asks what happens to that variation over
many generations: *why do some traits become more common in a
population while others fade, what evidence shows that living
things share common ancestors, and how do humans steer the same
process on purpose?* The learner reads the fossil record in rock
layers, compares forelimbs and embryos, follows peppered moths
through England's industrial soot and clean air, watches Galápagos
finch beaks shift after a drought, and runs a paper-moth
predation simulation that turns survival differences into trait
frequencies on a graph.

## Essential question

How does heritable variation, filtered by survival and
reproduction over generations, change populations — and what
evidence shows that all living things are related?

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–04 and the diagnostic weeks:

- Variation exists within a population: sexual reproduction
  produces offspring with new combinations of genetic information;
  mutations can change genes (Unit 04)
- Genes, proteins, and traits; dominant and recessive alleles;
  probability as "what fraction of equally likely outcomes"
  (Unit 04)
- Reading data tables; finding a mean; converting a fraction to a
  percentage; reading a simple bar graph (Units 01–04; grade-7 math
  ratios and percent work)
- Claim-evidence-reasoning paragraphs; labeled sketches; keeping a
  dated observation log (Units 01–04)
- No prior knowledge of fossils, homologous structures, embryos,
  natural selection, or artificial selection is assumed — all are
  taught here from zero

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. Analyze fossil-record data for patterns of existence, diversity,
   extinction, and change of life forms through Earth's history;
   order rock layers and the fossils in them by relative age using
   superposition, and place events on a deep-time timeline
   (Earth about 4.6 billion years old; first life about 3.8
   billion years ago).
2. Construct an explanation for evolutionary relationships from
   anatomical similarities and differences among modern organisms
   and between modern and fossil organisms; distinguish homologous
   structures (same underlying bones, different jobs) from
   analogous structures (similar jobs, different origins) and
   identify vestigial structures.
3. Analyze pictorial data to compare patterns of similarity in
   embryological development across species and identify
   relationships not evident in adult anatomy.
4. Construct an evidence-based explanation for how heritable
   variation increases some individuals' probability of surviving
   and reproducing in a specific environment, using the peppered
   moth and Galápagos finch cases.
5. Use mathematical representations — percentages, trait-frequency
   tables, and bar graphs — to support explanations of how natural
   selection increases or decreases specific traits in a population
   over time.
6. Gather and synthesize information about how humans influence
   the inheritance of desired traits through artificial selection
   and related technologies, and compare artificial selection
   with natural selection, naming who (or what) does the
   selecting in each case.

**Teaching notes on scope and sensitivity.** This unit teaches the
scientific consensus that populations change over generations by
natural selection and that living things share common ancestors,
as described by the NGSS performance expectations above. It does
not ask learners to report personal or family beliefs, and it does
not present any religious position. When a learner raises a belief
question, the adult acknowledges it respectfully, keeps the lesson
on the evidence and the mechanism, and notes that questions of
belief belong in family conversation. Human evolution is treated
only at the level the standards require: humans share anatomical
and embryological patterns with other vertebrates, and the human
lineage is about 300,000 years old. Learners never report their own
or their family's traits. The peppered-moth and finch cases use
published historical data; the simulation in the project uses
paper moths, not live animals.
