# Project — Adaptation Case-Study Portfolio

**Type:** investigation + modeling portfolio · **Sessions:** 11
(launch), 12 (data work), 13 (workshop), 14 (final measurements)
· **Adult-supervised throughout.**

## Question

How does an environment change a population over generations,
and how do the four lines of evolutionary evidence fit together
for one real species?

## What you will deliver

A portfolio with three parts, plus a walkthrough (spoken or written,
learner's choice):

1. **Moth simulation report** — the paper-moth predation
   simulation (or the practice dataset below), with a data table
   of light/dark counts and dark percentages across four
   generations, a bar graph of the dark percentage, and a CER
   paragraph answering how the environment changed the population.
2. **Adaptation case study** — one species (plant or animal, not
   a person, and never yourself or your family): the adaptation,
   the environment it suits, at least two lines of evidence for
   its evolutionary relationships (fossil, anatomy, embryo, or
   DNA), and a four-step natural-selection explanation for how
   the adaptation could have become common.
3. **Artificial-selection comparison** — one human-shaped trait
   (a dog breed, a Brassica vegetable, corn, or another you can
   verify): the wild ancestor, the trait selected, who selected
   it, the technology used, and a two-sentence comparison with
   natural selection naming the selector in each case.

## Investigation steps (adult-led)

1. Cut 40 small paper circles (about 2 cm across): 20 light
   (white paper) and 20 dark (dark paper). These are Generation 0:
   20 light, 20 dark, 50% dark.
2. Choose a background: a light cloth or sheet (clean-bark
   woodland) or a dark cloth or sheet (sooty woodland). Scatter
   the 40 moths on it.
3. The "predator" (the adult, or the learner with eyes averted
   for 15 seconds — the adult should be the predator for the
   first round so the learner sees the method) picks up 10 moths
   in 15 seconds — the most visible ones first. Count the
   survivors of each color and record them.
4. Reproduction: each survivor "has" 2 offspring of the same
   color. Double both survivor counts to get the next
   generation. Record the new counts and the dark percentage.
5. Repeat steps 2–4 for three rounds of predation (Generations 1,
   2, and 3). Keep the same background for all rounds.
6. Graph the dark percentage across Generations 0–3 as a bar
   graph, and write the CER paragraph.

**Model simplifications (say these in the report).** The predator
always catches exactly 10 moths; every survivor has exactly 2
offspring of the same color; the population grows every
generation. Real moth populations do not behave so neatly — but
the simplification isolates the one thing this model tests:
whether being harder to see changes a trait's frequency.

**Safety and alternatives.** No live animals are used. Paper
circles only; scissors used with the adult. If paper moths are
unavailable, use the practice dataset below — it teaches the same
objectives, with no penalty, and the report must say "practice
dataset" wherever it uses those numbers.

## Practice dataset (labeled fictional — not measurements anyone made)

The predator removes 10 moths per round; each survivor has 2
offspring of the same color. Two backgrounds are given — use the
one your simulation used, or compare both.

**Light background (clean-bark woodland):**

| Generation | Light moths | Dark moths | Total | Dark % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (start) | 20 | 20 | 40 | 50% |
| 1 | 36 | 24 | 60 | 40% |
| 2 | 66 | 34 | 100 | 34% |
| 3 | 126 | 54 | 180 | 30% |

Predation detail: round 1 removed 2 light and 8 dark (18 light
and 12 dark survived, then doubled); round 2 removed 3 light and
7 dark; round 3 removed 3 light and 7 dark.

**Dark background (sooty woodland):**

| Generation | Light moths | Dark moths | Total | Dark % |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (start) | 20 | 20 | 40 | 50% |
| 1 | 24 | 36 | 60 | 60% |
| 2 | 34 | 66 | 100 | 66% |
| 3 | 54 | 126 | 180 | 70% |

Predation detail: round 1 removed 8 light and 2 dark (12 light
and 18 dark survived, then doubled); round 2 removed 7 light and
3 dark; round 3 removed 7 light and 3 dark.

Learners using their own paper moths replace this table with
their own counts, computed the same way.

## Rubric (adult-scored; 20 points)

| Criterion | Proficient (full points) | Developing (partial) | Beginning (little/no) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simulation data and graph (6 pts) | Table complete for all four generations; dark percentages correct (own data or practice dataset, labeled); bar graph correct with labeled axes | Table complete but one percentage wrong, or graph missing labels | No table, or percentages wrong throughout |
| Adaptation case study (5 pts) | Species and adaptation named; environment stated; two lines of evidence given; four-step selection explanation correct and in order | One line of evidence, or one selection step missing or out of order | Adaptation named but no evidence or no mechanism |
| Artificial-selection comparison (4 pts) | Wild ancestor, trait, selector, and technology all named and correct; two-sentence comparison names the selector in each case | One element missing or wrong | Comparison missing or confused with natural selection |
| Natural-selection CER (3 pts) | Claims the environment changed the population; cites the simulation percentages as evidence; reasons from differential survival and reproduction | CER claims correctly but evidence thin, or reasoning missing a step | No CER or claim contradicts the data |
| Walkthrough (2 pts) | Learner explains all three parts in their own words and answers one follow-up question | Explains two parts | Cannot explain the portfolio |

**Readiness signal:** 16/20 or above with no criterion at
"Beginning" indicates readiness for Unit 06 (population thinking
carries into ecosystems). Below that, reteach the weakest
criterion with the teacher-guide prompts before moving on.
