# Lesson 01 — Cell Structures and Whole-Cell Function

**Goal (student-readable):** I can name the main parts of plant and
animal cells, describe what each part does, and explain how each part
helps the whole cell work as a system.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, your labeled
plant and animal cell diagrams from Unit 01 (or a printed plant/animal
cell diagram), colored pencils (optional).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Look at your Unit 01 animal cell diagram. Point to three structures
you can name without looking at the labels. Now answer: *why* does the
cell need that structure — what job would go undone if it were
missing? If you get stuck, say what the structure is near or attached
to; location often hints at function.

## Teaching

In Unit 01 you built labeled plant and animal cell models and argued
that each part contributes to the cell's function as a whole. This
unit goes one level deeper: *how* does each structure serve the whole
cell? The big idea is **structure and function** — a structure's shape
and position are clues to its job, and every job serves the cell's
single overarching purpose: staying alive, growing, and (for many
cells) dividing.

A helpful way to think of a cell is as a small, self-running workshop
— not a bag of loose parts. Like a workshop, a cell has a control
room, a boundary that decides what comes in and out, power supplies,
an assembly area, a shipping department, and storage. Keep the limits
of this comparison in mind (Lesson 05 returns to model limits): a real
cell's parts are molecules, not machines, and they all work at the
same time in the same tiny space.

Here are the main structures and what each does **for the cell as a
whole**:

- **Cell membrane** — the boundary around every cell. It is
  *selectively permeable*: some materials pass through and others do
  not. It lets food and water in and lets wastes out, and it keeps the
  cell's contents together. (Both plant and animal cells.)
- **Cell wall** — a stiff extra layer outside the membrane in plant
  cells (and in fungi, algae, and most bacteria — not in animal
  cells). It gives the cell shape and protects it, the way a fence
  protects a yard.
- **Nucleus** — the control center. It holds the cell's DNA, the
  instructions for everything the cell does: which proteins to build,
  when to grow, when to divide.
- **Mitochondria** — release energy from food so the cell can use it.
  When a muscle cell contracts or a root cell takes in water, the
  energy comes from mitochondria breaking down food molecules. Cells
  that need a lot of energy (muscle cells, for example) have many
  mitochondria.
- **Chloroplasts** — in plant cells (and some protists): capture light
  energy and use it to build food (sugar) — photosynthesis. An animal
  cell has none, which is why animals must eat food made by others.
- **Cytoplasm** — the jelly-like fluid filling the cell. Organelles
  are suspended in it, and many of the cell's chemical activities
  happen there.
- **Ribosomes** — tiny structures that build proteins by following the
  instructions from the nucleus. Some float in the cytoplasm; some sit
  on the endoplasmic reticulum.
- **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** — a network of folded membranes like
  hallways. Rough ER (studded with ribosomes) helps build and move
  proteins; smooth ER helps with other materials such as lipids.
- **Golgi apparatus** — receives proteins from the ER, finishes and
  packages them, and ships them where they are needed — inside the
  cell or out through the membrane. Think of it as the shipping
  department.
- **Vacuole** — storage sac. Plant cells usually have one large
  central vacuole holding water and dissolved materials; it also
  pushes against the cell wall to keep the plant firm. Animal cells
  have smaller vacuoles.
- **Lysosomes** — the recycling and cleanup crew. They break down
  worn-out cell parts and invaders so the materials can be reused.

Compare plant and animal cells and the pattern becomes clear: both
have a membrane, nucleus, cytoplasm, mitochondria, ribosomes, ER,
Golgi, and vacuoles — because both must control themselves, use
energy, build proteins, and store materials. Plants *add* a wall,
chloroplasts, and a large vacuole — because they stand upright without
a skeleton, make their own food, and store water. **Structures that do
different jobs are what make the two cell types different; structures
they share do the jobs every cell needs.**

A common trap: memorizing the parts as a list ("the mitochondria is
the powerhouse of the cell") without connecting them. A cell with
mitochondria but no membrane would spill its contents; a cell with a
nucleus but no ribosomes could never build the proteins its DNA
describes. Ask of every structure: *what would the whole cell fail to
do without it?*

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A plant cell is kept in a dark closet for a week (with
water). Which organelle can no longer do its job, and what must the
cell do instead to stay alive?

The **chloroplasts** cannot capture light in the dark, so the cell
cannot make new food. To survive, the cell must break down stored
food (starch, stored in the plant) using its **mitochondria** to
release energy. *Reasoning:* the whole cell's job is staying alive;
when one part's input (light) disappears, another part
(mitochondria) must carry the energy work. This also shows why plants
have *both* chloroplasts and mitochondria — making food and using
food are different jobs.

**Example 2.** Two unlabeled cell diagrams: Cell A has a cell wall, a
large central vacuole, and chloroplasts. Cell B has none of these but
has many small vacuoles and many lysosomes. Which is the plant cell,
and what does each difference tell you about its whole-cell job?

Cell A is the plant cell. The wall tells you the cell supports
itself; the chloroplasts tell you it makes its own food; the large
vacuole tells you it stores a lot of water. Cell B is the animal
cell: no wall (animals have skeletons or other support), no
chloroplasts (it eats food instead of making it), small vacuoles and
more lysosomes (an animal cell handles varied incoming materials that
need breaking down). *Reasoning:* do not just match the label "plant"
— read each structure as evidence about the cell's way of life.

**Example 3.** A learner says, "The nucleus is the most important
part, because it controls everything." Evaluate this claim.

Partly right, partly wrong. The nucleus *directs* the cell through
DNA instructions — but a nucleus alone cannot build a single protein
(it needs ribosomes), cannot power anything (mitochondria), and cannot
hold the cell together (membrane). In a system, "most important" is
usually the wrong question: remove *any* part and the whole system
fails differently. *Reasoning:* structure-and-function thinking asks
what each part contributes to the whole, not which part wins.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. For each pair, name one structure the two cell types share and one
   that differs, and state what job the shared/different structure
   does for the whole cell: (a) a leaf cell and a muscle cell;
   (b) a root cell and a skin cell. *(Hint: ask first "does it make
   its own food?")*
2. A cell has a membrane, nucleus, and cytoplasm, but no
   mitochondria. What whole-cell job can it *not* do well, and what
   would you predict about this cell's energy needs? *(Hint: think
   about what mitochondria release.)*
3. Sort these structures into "boundary and support," "energy," and
   "building and shipping": cell wall, chloroplast, ribosome, cell
   membrane, Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, ER, vacuole. *(Hint: some
   structures serve more than one group — note where.)*
4. Explain in two sentences why a plant cell needs *both* a cell
   wall *and* a cell membrane. *(Hint: what would each one fail to do
   alone?)*

## Independent practice

5. Choose three organelles. For each, write one sentence naming its
   job and one sentence explaining what the whole cell could not do
   without it.
6. A new single-celled organism is discovered. It has a nucleus, a
   cell wall, chloroplasts, and flagella (whiplike tails for
   swimming). Is it more like a plant cell or an animal cell?
   Defend your answer with two pieces of structural evidence.
7. Error analysis: a learner writes, "Ribosomes are unimportant
   because they are the smallest organelles." Identify the flawed
   reasoning and rewrite the claim correctly.

## Application

Take your Unit 01 plant or animal cell diagram. Add one new annotation
to *each* labeled structure: "If this were missing, the cell could
not ___." Trade with the adult: can they find a structure whose
"missing" sentence you left vague? Tighten it until it names a
specific whole-cell failure.

## Exit check

In three to four sentences, explain how the structures of a plant
cell work together as a system: name at least three structures and
connect each one to the cell's overall job of staying alive.

## Supports, extensions, and teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** glossary cards for the 12 vocabulary
  terms; sentence frame — "The ___ helps the whole cell by ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the sort in guided practice may be
  done by placing labeled cards into three drawn circles instead of
  writing; exit check may be dictated.
- **Extension:** research one structure (e.g., the cytoskeleton) not
  covered in this lesson; write a paragraph explaining how it, too,
  serves the whole cell — and why textbooks sometimes leave it out.
- **Teacher note — likely misconceptions:** (1) learners picture
  cells as flat 2D shapes; remind them diagrams are slices and cells
  are three-dimensional; (2) "plants don't need mitochondria because
  they photosynthesize" — plants respire too (Example 1); (3)
  memorizing the "powerhouse" nickname without connecting energy
  release to the cell's actual work. **Answer-key reference:**
  [answer key, Lesson 01](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
