# Lesson 05 — The Cell as a System

**Goal (student-readable):** I can argue, with evidence from this
unit, that a cell is a system of interacting parts — and I can name
what our cell models show well and what they leave out.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, your Unit
01 cell diagrams and Unit 02 notes, the project planning sheet
([project.md](../project.md)).

## Readiness check (5 min)

Write your own one-sentence definition of a *system* — no peeking at
earlier lessons. Then test it: is a pile of sand a system? Is a
bicycle? Is a cell? Your definition should include the piles and
exclude the sand.

## Teaching

A **system** is a set of interacting parts that work together as a
whole — and the interaction is the point. A pile of sand is not a
system (remove half the grains and the rest behave exactly the same);
a bicycle is (remove the chain and the pedals become useless). By
that test, a cell is unambiguously a system: the membrane's traffic
control, the mitochondria's energy release, the ribosomes' protein
building, and the nucleus's instructions all depend on each other.

Scientists use two thinking tools constantly with systems, and you
have been practicing both all unit:

- **Structure and function** — a part's shape and position reveal its
  job, and the job serves the whole. (Lessons 01–03: the membrane's
  selective boundary, the root hair's absorptive shape, the guard
  cells' valve shape.)
- **Systems and system models** — a model is a simplified
  representation built to explain something. Your labeled cell
  diagrams, the organization-levels diagram, and even the
  food-coloring diffusion demo are all models: each one shows some
  things clearly and hides others.

Being good at models means knowing their **strengths and limits**.
Your Unit 01 plant cell diagram is strong at showing *which*
structures exist and *where* they sit relative to each other. It is
weak at showing that the cell is three-dimensional, that materials
are constantly moving (diffusion never stops), that everything
happens at once, and that the real scale is thousands of times
smaller than the drawing. A model that showed all of that would be a
real cell — which is why scientists use *many* models, each honest
about what it leaves out.

Now build the unit's capstone argument — a **claim-evidence-reasoning**
paragraph defending this claim:

> **Claim:** A cell is a system of interacting parts, not a
> collection of independent structures.

Strong evidence comes from *interactions you observed or reasoned
about this unit* — not from restating the claim:

- **Evidence 1:** In the osmosis demonstration (Lesson 02), water
  moved across the membrane into or out of the potato cells, and the
  whole piece changed firmness. The membrane, the vacuole's water
  storage, and the cell wall acted together — no single part explains
  the result alone.
- **Evidence 2:** In the dark-closet thought experiment (Lesson 01),
  chloroplasts went idle but mitochondria kept the cell alive on
  stored food — parts compensating for each other, which only makes
  sense in a system.
- **Evidence 3:** From Lesson 03, a red blood cell gave up its nucleus
  to carry more oxygen — one part sacrificed for the whole cell's job,
  which then serves the whole organism.

**Reasoning** connects each piece of evidence to the claim: in each
case, removing or changing one part changes what the *whole* can do —
the definition of interacting parts. Reasoning is not repeating the
evidence; it explains *why the evidence counts*.

One honest limit to state in your paragraph: our evidence comes from
models, diagrams, and kitchen demonstrations — not from watching
molecules cross a real membrane. That does not make the argument
weak; it makes it *honest about its evidence*, which is exactly what
Unit 01 taught you to do.

**Looking ahead:** Unit 07 will scale this argument up — the human
body as a *system of interacting subsystems* (digestive, circulatory,
respiratory) made of groups of cells. The thinking you practice here
on one cell transfers directly.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Read this paragraph and label the claim, evidence, and
reasoning: "A cell is a system of interacting parts. When a plant
cell's vacuole loses water by osmosis, the whole cell goes limp even
though the wall and membrane are undamaged. The limpness happens
because the wall needs the vacuole's water pressure to stay rigid —
the parts depend on each other, which is what makes the cell a
system rather than a pile of parts."

Claim: "A cell is a system of interacting parts." Evidence: the
vacuole loses water and the whole cell goes limp while wall and
membrane are undamaged. Reasoning: the wall needs the vacuole's
pressure to stay rigid — parts depend on each other. *Note the
reasoning move:* it explains the *dependence*, not just the
observation.

**Example 2.** A learner writes as evidence: "The cell has a nucleus,
mitochondria, and a membrane." Why is this weak evidence for the
claim?

It lists parts but shows no *interaction*. A pile of parts is exactly
what the claim argues against — so a parts list cannot support it.
Strong evidence must show parts *affecting each other*: water moving
through the membrane changing the whole cell's firmness, or one
organelle compensating when another goes idle. *Reasoning check:* ask
"does this evidence mention two parts interacting?" If not, it is a
list, not an argument.

**Example 3.** Name one strength and one limit of the
organization-levels diagram from Lesson 04.

Strength: it makes the building order visible — you can see at a
glance that a system is built from organs built from tissues. Limit:
it hides the *interactions* — the arrows show building order, not
how the circulatory system actually exchanges materials with the
digestive system. *Reasoning:* every model answers some questions
and not others; naming the unanswered ones is part of using the
model well.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Write one strength and one limit of your own Unit 01 cell
   diagram. *(Hint: strength = what it shows clearly; limit = what
   is true about a real cell that the drawing cannot show.)*
2. Which of these is evidence of *interaction* (not just a parts
   list)? (a) "The cell has chloroplasts and mitochondria."
   (b) "When light disappears, mitochondria keep the cell alive on
   stored food." Explain your choice. *(Hint: look for one part
   affecting another.)*
3. Draft the reasoning sentence for this evidence: "Guard cells open
   the stoma, carbon dioxide enters, and the mesophyll cells
   photosynthesize faster." *(Hint: the reasoning must connect the
   evidence to "interacting parts.")*
4. Your readiness-check definition of *system*: revise it now in one
   sentence, using the word *interacting*.

## Independent practice

5. Write the full CER paragraph defending "A cell is a system of
   interacting parts." Use at least two pieces of evidence from this
   unit and one reasoning sentence per piece of evidence. End with
   one sentence naming an honest limit of your evidence.
6. Choose one model from this unit (cell diagram, organization
   diagram, diffusion demo). Write a paragraph: what question does
   it answer well, what question can it not answer, and what second
   model would you pair it with to cover the gap?
7. Error analysis: "Models are just simplified drawings, so they are
   less scientific than real observations." Explain what is right and
   what is wrong in this statement.

## Application

With the adult, pick one system you both use (a kitchen, a car, a
school). List four parts, describe one interaction between two of
them, and name one thing a diagram of the system would show well and
one thing it would hide. Present it as a 2-minute talk: claim ("X is
a system"), evidence (the interaction), reasoning (why it counts).

## Exit check

In four to five sentences, argue that the cell membrane is *not*
just a wall around the cell but an interacting part of the cell
system. Cite diffusion or osmosis evidence from Lesson 02.

## Supports, extensions, and teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** CER sentence frames — "My claim is
  ___. One piece of evidence is ___. This supports my claim because
  ___."; word bank — *interacting, system, model, strength, limit*.
- **Alternate response mode:** the CER paragraph may be dictated;
  the model strength/limit analysis may be a two-column chart.
- **Extension:** read about the endosymbiotic theory (mitochondria
  and chloroplasts were once free-living cells) at the adult's
  chosen reference; write a paragraph on how this theory is itself a
  systems argument about parts becoming wholes.
- **Teacher note — likely misconceptions:** (1) "system" used as a
  synonym for "big thing" — keep returning to the *interaction*
  test; (2) treating model limits as model failures — limits are
  normal and must be named, not hidden; (3) CER paragraphs that
  restate evidence as reasoning — require the "why does this count"
  sentence explicitly. **Answer-key reference:**
  [answer key, Lesson 05](../answer-key.md#lesson-05).
