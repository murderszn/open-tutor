# Lesson 02 — The Membrane and Moving Materials

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain how the cell membrane
controls what enters and leaves the cell, and I can use evidence from
a safe investigation to describe diffusion and osmosis.
**Time:** 45–50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, three
clear cups, water, food coloring, spoon, two same-size potato pieces
(or apple slices), salt, ruler, kitchen scale (optional), adult
supervision for all steps.

## Readiness check (5 min)

A cell sits in pond water. The water around it has dissolved minerals
the cell needs — but also a dissolved pollutant. The membrane lets the
minerals in and keeps the pollutant out. How could a thin boundary be
*selective* like that? Write one idea. (There is no single right
answer yet — this lesson builds the explanation.)

## Teaching

The **cell membrane** is *selectively permeable*: some substances pass
through easily, others slowly, and others not at all. This selectivity
is the membrane's whole job — the cell constantly needs food, water,
and oxygen in, and wastes out, while keeping its insides stable. A
membrane that let everything through would be no boundary at all; one
that let nothing through would starve the cell. The membrane solves
both problems at once.

Materials move across the membrane mostly by **diffusion**: the
movement of particles from where they are **more concentrated** to
where they are **less concentrated**. Diffusion needs no energy from
the cell — it happens because particles are always moving and
colliding, spreading out until they are evenly distributed
(**equilibrium**). A drop of food coloring in still water spreads on
its own; perfume spreads across a room on its own. Warm water spreads
faster than cold, because its particles move faster.

When the moving substance is **water** crossing a membrane, diffusion
has a special name: **osmosis**. Water moves from where it is more
concentrated (less dissolved stuff) to where it is less concentrated
(more dissolved stuff). The dissolved substances themselves may be too
big to cross the membrane — water slips through instead.

Think about a plant root in salty soil. The soil water has a *high*
concentration of dissolved salt, so the *water* concentration outside
the root is *low*. Water moves by osmosis **out of** the root cells
into the soil — the plant wilts even though the soil is wet. The same
physics in reverse keeps a freshwater plant firm: plain water around
the roots has a higher water concentration than the cell's insides, so
water moves **into** the cells, filling the central vacuole and
pressing against the cell wall. That pressure is what holds a plant
upright.

Connect this to the whole cell: the membrane, the vacuole, and the
wall are a team. The membrane controls traffic, the vacuole stores the
water, and the wall resists the pressure. Remove any one and the
system fails — which is exactly the structure-and-function reasoning
from Lesson 01, now with a moving-parts example.

**Safety:** food coloring stains — wear old clothes and cover the
table. The potato investigation uses only kitchen-safe materials, but
the adult handles all cutting and supervises every step. No tasting
during a science investigation.

### The two demonstrations (adult-led)

**Demo A — diffusion you can watch.** Fill two clear cups with the
same amount of water: one hot (from the tap, not boiling — adult
handles it), one cold. Add one drop of food coloring to each at the
same time, without stirring. Watch for two minutes. Record what you
see every 30 seconds.

**Demo B — osmosis in potato.** Cut two equal-size potato pieces
(adult cuts). Measure each piece's length in millimeters. Place one in
plain water and one in very salty water. Leave for 30 minutes (start
the next lesson segment while you wait). Then measure again and note
firmness (bend each piece gently). Record everything in a labeled
data table.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** In Demo A the color spreads through the hot water in
about 40 seconds but takes nearly 3 minutes in the cold water. What
does this tell you about diffusion?

The coloring spreads by diffusion — particles moving from high
concentration (the drop) to low concentration (the clear water).
Hotter water's particles move faster, so collisions spread the color
faster. *Reasoning:* the pattern (faster in hot water) matches the
model (particle motion drives diffusion). A common error is saying
"heat pulls the color" — heat does not pull; faster-moving particles
collide and spread more quickly.

**Example 2.** Predict Demo B before measuring: which potato piece
will shrink, and why?

The piece in **salty water** will shrink and go limp. The salty water
has a lower water concentration than the potato cells' insides, so
water moves by osmosis **out of** the potato cells, across their
membranes, into the water. The piece in plain water will stay firm or
swell slightly, because water moves **into** the cells. *Reasoning:*
always track the *water* concentration, not the salt — water moves
toward the saltier side. If you predicted the salty-water piece would
swell "because salt goes in," check: the membrane blocks most salt,
so water does the moving.

**Example 3.** A single-celled pond organism lives in fresh water,
which constantly leaks *into* it by osmosis. It survives by pumping
the extra water back out. What does this tell you about the
relationship between the membrane and the whole cell?

The membrane alone cannot solve the problem — it is permeable to
water, so water keeps entering. The *whole cell* solves it by
spending energy to pump water out. *Reasoning:* this is a system:
the membrane sets the rules of traffic, and the cell's energy supply
(mitochondria) pays for the exceptions. Structures do not work alone.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. In which direction does oxygen diffuse when it moves from the air
   in your lungs into your blood? Name the higher-concentration side.
   *(Hint: diffusion always goes high → low.)*
2. A red blood cell is placed in pure water. Predict what happens to
   it, using the word *osmosis*. *(Hint: track the water, not the
   cell's contents.)*
3. A learner says diffusion and osmosis are "totally different
   things." Write a two-sentence correction. *(Hint: one is a special
   case of the other.)*
4. From your Demo B data table: compute the change in length (mm) for
   each potato piece and state which piece lost water, citing the
   numbers. *(Hint: change = final − initial; a negative change means
   water left.)*

## Independent practice

5. Explain why a wilted lettuce leaf firms up in cold plain water,
   using the terms *selectively permeable*, *osmosis*, and *cell
   wall*.
6. A cell needs a large sugar molecule that cannot cross its
   membrane. Diffusion alone cannot bring it in. Propose one way the
   cell could still get it, and explain why this shows the membrane
   is part of a larger system. (Accept any reasonable mechanism, e.g.,
   the membrane folding around the particle.)
7. Error analysis: "Osmosis is the diffusion of salt across a
   membrane." Identify the two errors and rewrite the definition.

## Application

Design (on paper — do not run it) a third cup for Demo A that would
test whether *stirring* speeds up spreading for a different reason
than diffusion. What would you keep the same, what would you change,
and what result would support "stirring is not diffusion"? Discuss
with the adult.

## Exit check

In three to four sentences, explain how diffusion and osmosis help a
plant cell stay firm, naming the membrane, the vacuole, and the cell
wall and saying what each contributes.

## Supports, extensions, and teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** word bank — *concentration,
  equilibrium, selectively permeable, diffusion, osmosis*; sentence
  frame — "Water moves by osmosis from ___ to ___ because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** Demo observations may be dictated or
  drawn (a before/after sketch of the color spreading); the exit
  check may be given orally.
- **Extension:** investigate (with the adult) how a real
  semi-permeable barrier works in everyday life — e.g., a tea bag or
  a coffee filter — and write a paragraph comparing it to the cell
  membrane, including one way the comparison breaks down.
- **Teacher note — likely misconceptions:** (1) learners track the
  *solute* instead of the water in osmosis — always redirect to
  "where is the water more concentrated?"; (2) "diffusion only
  happens in liquids" — it happens in gases too (perfume); (3)
  thinking osmosis requires a living cell — it requires only a
  selectively permeable membrane and a concentration difference.
