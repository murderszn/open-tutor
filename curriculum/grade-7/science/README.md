# Grade 7 Science

Independent, grade-specific materials in the public OpenTutor curriculum library.
This track is a **draft in progress**: the audit and scope-and-sequence are delivered;
the eight units are planned, not yet written. See [scope-and-sequence.md](scope-and-sequence.md).

**Do not refer a learner to another grade track.** This folder is self-contained for
one grade-7 learner at a time; a guiding adult adapts pacing, materials, and
supports. Keep completed learner work, names, photos, schedules, and grades in
private storage — never in this public repository.

Existing `stem/` science material stays available and is reviewed for reuse in
this track; new core science belongs in this `science/` folder, and coding or
engineering activities remain optional enrichment. See also: [Grade 7
track](../README.md), [curriculum index](../../README.md).

## What this track covers

A full-year (about 36 weeks) grade-7 life-science course: scientific evidence
and microscopy, cell structures and organism organization, photosynthesis and
cellular respiration, reproduction and genetic variation, natural selection and
evolutionary evidence, ecosystems and biodiversity, human body systems and
homeostasis, and a capstone of independent life-science investigations with an
engineering-design thread. Sessions are about 45–50 minutes, four per week,
blending observation and safe hands-on investigations (prepared slides or
printed micrographs, seed-germination tests, food-web and population modeling),
data tables and graphs, and written claim-evidence-reasoning practice that the
adult reviews the same day.

The track follows the Next Generation Science Standards middle-school (grades
6–8) life-science performance expectations as a reference pathway — verified
code by code during the audit — without claiming state adoption, accreditation,
or complete alignment.

## Track objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the year the learner will be able to:

1. Use a microscope (or verified micrographs when no microscope is available)
   to observe cells; prepare scale-aware sketches; distinguish observation from
   inference; and cite evidence that living things are made of cells — either
   one cell or many types of cells.
2. Build and label models of plant and animal cells; explain how major
   organelles (nucleus, chloroplasts, mitochondria, cell membrane, cell wall)
   contribute to the function of the cell as a whole.
3. Compare unicellular and multicellular organization; describe how cells form
   tissues, tissues form organs, and organs form systems specialized for
   particular functions.
4. Trace matter and energy through photosynthesis and cellular respiration
   using word and balanced chemical equations; explain the role of chlorophyll
   and chloroplasts in capturing light energy.
5. Model how food molecules are rearranged through chemical reactions into new
   molecules that support growth and release energy as matter moves through an
   organism.
6. Distinguish asexual from sexual reproduction; model why asexual reproduction
   produces offspring with identical genetic information while sexual
   reproduction produces offspring with genetic variation.
7. Explain how environmental and genetic factors influence the growth of
   organisms; describe how structural changes to genes (mutations) can have
   harmful, beneficial, or neutral effects.
8. Analyze fossil-record data for patterns of existence, diversity, extinction,
   and change of life forms over Earth's history.
9. Use anatomical similarities and differences, and embryological-development
   patterns, to infer evolutionary relationships among organisms.
10. Explain natural selection: how heritable variation increases some
    individuals' probability of surviving and reproducing, shifting trait
    frequencies in a population over time; support the explanation with simple
    mathematical representations.
11. Describe how humans influence the inheritance of desired traits through
    artificial selection and related technologies.
12. Analyze and interpret data on how resource availability affects organisms
    and populations in an ecosystem.
13. Build food-web models that show the cycling of matter and the flow of
    energy among living and nonliving parts of an ecosystem; predict patterns
    of interactions among organisms.
14. Argue from evidence that changes to physical or biological components of an
    ecosystem affect populations; evaluate competing design solutions for
    maintaining biodiversity and ecosystem services.
15. Argue with evidence that the human body is a system of interacting
    subsystems composed of groups of cells (digestive, circulatory,
    respiratory, and others working together to maintain stable internal
    conditions).
16. Describe how sensory receptors respond to stimuli by sending messages to
    the brain for immediate behavior or storage as memories.
17. Design and carry out controlled investigations: identify independent and
    dependent variables and controls, name sources of error, keep a lab
    notebook, and write claim-evidence-reasoning conclusions; label any
    practice dataset as fictional when it is not a real observation.
18. Follow lab-safety rules and use observation or simulation alternatives when
    materials, equipment, or adult supervision are unavailable.

## Standards reference

Objectives reference the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) middle-school
life-science performance expectations (codes verified against the NGSS
life-science list on 2026-10-05; descriptions are paraphrases, not
reproductions). NGSS organizes middle school as a single grades 6–8 band; this
grade-7 track takes the life-science (MS-LS) performance expectations as its
reference pathway. Physical-science and Earth/space-science expectations are
not core to this track; the existing `stem/` physical-science material remains
as optional enrichment. Full crosswalk in
[scope-and-sequence.md](scope-and-sequence.md#4-standards-crosswalk).

## Safety and accessibility notes

Investigations use household and classroom-safe materials only; the adult
previews every procedure. Prepared slides or printed micrographs substitute
for live cultures; no mouth pipetting; handwashing after handling soil, seeds,
or pond water. No live-animal experiments, no dissections, no heat sources or
chemicals beyond kitchen-safe items used with the adult. Every diagram and
generated image ships with a text-only description carrying the same
information; no question depends on color alone. Word-heavy tasks are available
as adult read-aloud; scribed or oral response modes are accepted for
reflections and exit checks.
