# Answer Key — Unit 02: Literary Perspective, Figurative Language, and Poetry

**For the adult only.** Every response below was worked independently of
the student pages. Open responses accept defensible alternatives that cite
evidence and use unit vocabulary — the samples show the reasoning bar, not
the only wording.

## Lesson 01

**Guided (illustration activity):** 1. Human-like things: branches reaching
down like arms; the tree seeming to stand guard over the path; mist curling
as if the tree is breathing or thinking. 2. Sample: "The old oak stood
guard over the path all night." (human action: *stood guard*). "The ancient
branches whispered to the ferns below." (*whispered*). 3. "Watched over"
fits: it connotes care and protection, matching the warm dawn light and
peaceful scene; "loomed" connotes menace, which clashes with the golden,
gentle picture.

**Independent practice:** 1. A bird — "the thing with feathers." 2. Two of:
"perches in the soul" (hope given a bird's roosting action); "sings the
tune without the words" (hope given a voice); "It asked a crumb — of me"
(hope given the ability to beg/ask). 3. "Sweetest" connotes pleasantness
and preciousness — hope is most beautiful exactly when it is most needed;
"sore" connotes pain and severity — only a truly terrible storm could
defeat hope. The contrast pits hope's resilience against maximum adversity.
4. Sample: The poem can't be literally about a bird because "it" is heard
"in the chillest land / And on the strangest Sea" — abstractions, not
places a bird visits — and because a bird cannot literally perch "in the
soul." The bird is the comparison vehicle; hope is the subject.

**Exit check (secure):** "The fog comes on little cat feet" means the fog
arrives quietly and gently, the way a cat pads into a room unnoticed; the
fog is gentle — "little" connotes tenderness, and the fog merely "sits
looking" rather than attacking.

## Lesson 02

**Guided:** 1. *wall / all* — exact rhyme; *wide / countryside* — slant
(near) rhyme. 2. "so wide" — repeated soft *w* drifts like the view
spreading out (also acceptable: "garden green"). 3. *Blue* connotes a
clear, open, happy sky — freedom and delight; *gray* would connote gloom
and break the stanza's joy.

**Independent practice:** 1. Both. Literally the child is physically above
the garden and roof. The idiom "look down on" usually means "despise" —
the poem plays with it: the child looks down with delight, not scorn.
Context (the joyful tone) decides. 2. Sample marking: **Up** in the **air**
and **o**ver the **wall** — the strong beats bounce like the swing's arc:
rise (stressed), glide (unstressed), rise, glide. 3. Sample rebuttal: "The
poem is full of figurative language: 'Ever a child can do' is hyperbole —
no one surveyed every child — showing the speaker's overflowing joy. The
repeated refrain 'Up in the air' is a sound device whose returns lift the
reader the way the swing lifts the child."

**Exit check (secure):** Names one device (e.g., the "Up in the air"
refrain) and a feeling it creates (lifting joy) with the mechanism (each
return mimics the swing rising).

## Lesson 03

**Guided (scansion investigation):** 1. Line 2: "Thou **art** | more
**love**- | ly **and** | more **tem**- | per-**ate**" — regular iambic
pentameter. Line 4: "And **sum**- | mer's **lease** | hath **all** | too
**short** | a **date**" — regular iambic pentameter. 2. Q1 = lines 1–4,
Q2 = 5–8, Q3 = 9–12, Couplet = 13–14; turn bracketed at line 9 ("But").
3. Q1 poses the comparison and finds summer wanting; Q2 lists summer's
specific flaws; Q3 declares the beloved exempt from decay; the couplet
credits the poem itself with granting immortality.

**Independent practice:** 1. Metaphor: summer is a tenant holding a
*lease* with an expiry *date*. It adds that summer's brevity isn't
accidental — it's contractual, built-in — which strengthens the "summer is
flawed" case. 2. Sample: The 2-line couplet works as an ending because its
brevity feels final, like a verdict; after twelve lines of argument, the
short form delivers the proof — "this gives life to thee" — as one
decisive punch. 3. Sample rebuttal: "The arrangement *is* the argument:
the first two quatrains build the case against summer, the turn at line 9
pivots to the beloved's exemption, and the couplet resolves it. As one
unbroken block you'd get the words but lose the problem → turn → proof
architecture."

**Exit check (secure):** The turn word is "But" (line 9); after it the
argument shifts from summer's flaws to the beloved's eternal, unfading
summer.

## Lesson 04

**Guided:** 1. Sample pair: A's lightning "split the sky like a cracked
windshield" (spectacle, excitement) vs. B's lightning "found every crack
in the old roof" (invasion, threat). 2. Voice B (line 5: "the summer the
oak came down"). The memory lets B feel dread and protective caution that
A — with no such memory — cannot feel. 3. A frames the dad as a fellow
celebrant ("whooped"); B frames him as naive about danger ("he has
forgotten"). The framing reveals A's thrill-seeking stance vs. B's
protective stance.

**Independent practice:** 1. Sample B-voice rewrite: "Let it pass. Let the
roof hold. / I barred the door; let the night spend itself." (Accept any
two lines keeping the storm but shifting to caution/protection.) 2.
Sample: "The contrast teaches that the same storm is two different events
depending on experience — A's thrill and B's fear coexist in one night.
Neither voice alone shows how memory filters facts: A's joy needs B's
memory to look naive, and B's fear needs A's joy to look like more than
gloom." 3. Sample: "RL.7.6 asks how viewpoints are built and contrasted,
not which one wins. B's fear is valid from her experience and A's joy is
valid from his; the task is to see what each reveals and hides."

**Exit check (secure):** Voice A is first person ("I pressed my forehead
to the screen and grinned"); it cannot show what the grandmother remembers
or what the dad is thinking.

## Lesson 05

**Guided:** 1. "Comes" (Sandburg — arrival as a visit) vs. "came down"
("City Fog" — arrival as an assault). 2. "Sandburg's fog acts like a *cat*
— quiet, independent, a calm observer; the 'City Fog' fog acts like an
*intruder* — hands pressing on windows. The first choice reveals serene
acceptance; the second reveals trapped fear." 3. Sandburg: soft *s* sounds
("sits," "silent," "haunches") match the calm; "City Fog": hard consonants
("locked door," "pressed," "cut") match the tension. One sound–tone link
with the mechanism earns full credit.

**Independent practice:** 1–2. Sample farmer stanza: "The clouds unbuckled
their gray belts at last, / And the dry field drank with a thousand
mouths. / My grandfather's ghost nodded in the doorway — / Rain, you've
come home." (tone: grateful). Sample child stanza: "The sky ripped open
like a torn grocery bag, / And dumped its water on our perfect day. / The
blanket's soaked. The sandwiches drowned. / Stupid rain. Go away."
(tone: resentful). Explanation must cite one craft choice per stanza
(e.g., "drank with a thousand mouths" personification = welcome vs.
"ripped open like a torn grocery bag" simile = violent ruin) and state
that identical facts (rain falling) carry opposite meanings. 3. Thesis
sentence sample: "In Sonnet 18, the turn at line 9 and the closing couplet
turn a list of summer's flaws into proof that the poem itself grants
eternal life."

**Exit check (secure):** Subject: fog arriving over a city. Difference:
verb connotations — "comes"/"sits" (gentle, temporary) vs.
"swallowed"/"pressed" (violent, trapping) — make one tone serene and the
other menacing.

## Practice

**Session A:** Figure sort — 1. "Hope is the thing with feathers":
**metaphor**; in context, hope is delicate, alive, able to lift and
carry. 2. "That perches in the soul": **personification**; hope rests
inside a person the way a bird rests on a branch. 3. "And sings the tune
without the words": **personification**; hope expresses itself without
needing explanation. 4. "And sore must be the storm": **personification**
(the storm "feels" soreness/effort); only a terrible storm could silence
hope. 5. "I've heard it in the chillest land": **none** — a literal
statement of experience. 6. "It asked a crumb — of me":
**personification**; hope is imagined as a bird that could beg but never
does — hope gives without demanding. Connotation pairs: *sweetest*
(pleasant, precious) vs. *sore* (painful, severe) — hope shines brightest
against worst adversity; *little Bird* (vulnerable, tender) vs. the mighty
storm; *chillest land / strangest Sea* (extreme, inhospitable — hope
reaches everywhere). Exit: metaphor — hope *is* the thing with feathers;
no *like* or *as*.

**Session B:** Sound rewrite sample: "Do you enjoy riding high on a swing?
It goes way up into the blue sky." Lost: the lift of the repeated refrain,
the bounce of the end rhymes, the physical swing-feel of the rhythm — the
prose says the same facts but the *motion* is gone. Impact statements must
name a device, a feeling, and a mechanism (e.g., "The repetition of 'Up in
the air' makes the stanza feel soaring, because each return lifts the
reader the way the swing lifts the child").

**Session C:** Paragraph frame sample: "Shakespeare's closing **couplet**
makes the reader feel the argument *click shut*, because two short lines
after twelve long ones sound like a verdict. For example, 'So long lives
this, and this gives life to thee' claims the poem itself defeats time.
Without this form choice, the poem would trail off instead of landing its
proof." Line-break lab: accept either defended answer — e.g., "Four short
lines slow the ending and give each phrase weight, but the original two
long lines land harder as one decisive punch; breaking it into four
diffuses the verdict." Exit (secure): the turn at line 9 ("But") shifts
the argument from summer's flaws to the beloved's exemption.

**Session D:** Narrator cards — 1. Voice A (line 1 simile, line 5
"grinned"). 2. Voice B (line 5). 3. **Both** (A line 4, B line 4 — framed
differently). 4. Voice A (lines 2–3, savoring). 5. Voice B (lines 2–3,
measuring danger). 6. Voice B (line 6). 7. Voice A (line 6 "Let it pour").
8. Voice A (introduced as "Mara, 12"). Contrast paragraph sample: "Voice
A's stance is thrilled welcome — 'Let it pour. Let the gutters sing'
(line 6). Voice B's stance is protective dread — 'Let it pass. Let the
house hold' (line 6). The craft differs in verbs: A's verbs invite
(*pour, sing*), B's verbs barricade (*pass, hold*). The contrast reveals
that the same storm is filtered by experience — neither voice alone shows
how memory reshapes facts."

## Quiz

1. **Extended metaphor (2 pts + 1 explanation):** fog = a cat. Quoted
   lines: "on little cat feet" and "It sits looking / over harbor and
   city / on silent haunches" (any two carrying lines accepted). The
   comparison suggests the fog arrives quietly and unnoticed, observes
   calmly, and leaves on its own — gentle, independent, a little
   mysterious.
2. **Connotation (2 pts):** "Little" connotes smallness, gentleness, almost
   tenderness — the fog is a quiet visitor, not a threat. Without it,
   "cat feet" keeps the quietness but loses the affection; the fog could
   read as stealthy or predatory instead of welcome.
3. **Figure in context (3 pts):** Metaphor — summer is a tenant holding a
   *lease* that expires (*date*). It adds inevitability to the case: summer
   doesn't just happen to end; its shortness is contractual, built into the
   deal, which deepens the flaw list of lines 1–8.
4. **Sound impact (3 pts):** Repetition (refrain). Each return of "Up in
   the air" lifts the reader the way the swing lifts the child; the
   repeated phrase mimics the swing's rising motion, creating a bouncy,
   soaring, joyful feeling.
5. **Form (3 pts):** Line 9, "But." Before the turn the poem lists summer's
   flaws (rough winds, hot sun, fading beauty); after it, the beloved is
   declared exempt — "thy eternal summer shall not fade" — and the final
   couplet credits the poem with granting immortality.
6. **POV (2 pts):** Both first person. A: "I pressed my forehead to the
   screen and grinned" (line 5). B: "I pulled the curtains" (line 6) or "I
   counted the seconds" (line 2).
7. **Contrast (3 pts):** Sample: "The contrast reveals that one storm holds
   two opposite truths — A's thrill and B's dread — and that experience
   filters facts: the same lightning is spectacle to the child and threat
   to the grandmother. Neither voice alone shows that meaning lives in the
   viewer, not just the event."
8. **Error analysis (3 pts):** Sample: "The poem is about *fog*, not a cat
   — 'The fog comes' is the subject of line 1, and the cat is only the
   comparison. A literal cat couldn't sit 'over harbor and city' (lines
   3–4); the scale proves the cat language is figurative."

**Quiz scoring:** 22–24 = secure; 16–21 = developing (reteach the missed
objective's lesson); below 16 = reteach and re-quiz with the adult.

## Unit assessment

1. **Figures (4 pts, 2 per figure):** Accept any two correctly named, e.g.:
   - Line 4 **simile**: "Like ladies' skirts across the grass" — the sound
     of wind moving through grass is compared to skirts swishing; it makes
     the wind sound soft, sweeping, almost graceful.
   - "O wind, that sings so loud a song!" (lines 6, 12) **personification**
     — the wind is given a voice; in context it means the wind's sound is
     bold and musical, not just noise.
   - "I saw you toss the kites" (line 1) **personification** — the wind is
     addressed as "you" and given hands/agency; the wind becomes a
     playful companion rather than a weather event.
2. **Sound (3 pts):** Sample: "The refrain 'O wind, a-blowing all day long,
   / O wind, that sings so loud a song!' closes both stanzas. The repeated
   long *o* sounds and the exclamation marks make it feel like a chant or
   chorus — celebratory and songlike — which turns the whole poem into the
   'loud song' it describes." (Rhyme/alliteration answers accepted with a
   feeling + mechanism.)
3. **Connotation (3 pts):** *Push* connotes force, insistence, physical
   power — the wind shoves against the speaker. *Touch* connotes gentleness
   and lightness; with "touch" the line would feel caressing, and the
   speaker's awe at the wind's strength would vanish.
4. **POV (4 pts):** First person — "Under the buzzing lights I stood"
   (line 1). It lets the reader *feel* the weary waiting from inside: the
   ticket softening in the fist, feet refusing to move. It *hides* the
   bigger picture — where the speaker is going, why this train matters,
   whether they will ever leave.
5. **Form (3 pts):** The break separates two ideas: stanza 1 catalogs what
   the wind *does* (toss, blow, pass, sing — its observable effects);
   stanza 2 turns to the mystery — "always you yourself you hid" — the
   unseen cause behind the effects. The white space marks the shift from
   observation to reflection.
6. **Synthesis paragraph (5 pts):** Secure sample: "The two speakers feel
   opposite things about waiting and weather. Stevenson's speaker feels
   delighted wonder: the personification 'O wind, that sings so loud a
   song!' turns the wind into a joyful musician, and the chanting refrain
   makes the reader want to sing along. The 'Last Train' speaker feels
   weary stuckness: the simile 'They stayed, like roots, in the concrete'
   (line 8) makes motionlessness feel permanent and heavy, and the
   personified clock that 'had somewhere to be' (line 6) deepens the
   abandonment — even time has left. Both poems personify, but Stevenson's
   personification befriends its subject while the train poem's
   personification emphasizes what has gone."

**Assessment scoring:** 20–22 = secure; 14–19 = developing (targeted
reteach on missed objectives, then a revision task); below 14 = reteach
the unit's core lessons (01, 03, 04) and reassess with new poems.
