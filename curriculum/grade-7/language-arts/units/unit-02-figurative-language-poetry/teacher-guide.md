# Teacher Guide — Unit 02: Literary Perspective, Figurative Language, and Poetry

**For the guiding adult.** This unit assumes no teaching background — only
willingness to read poems aloud and ask "which word makes you feel that?"
Full solutions are in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md); keep it closed during
student work time.

## How to run each lesson (40–50 min)

- **Read every poem aloud first, twice.** Poems are sound before they are
  print. Read once for the music, then again slowly for meaning. Invite the
  learner to read a stanza aloud too.
- **Work the worked examples together.** They model the exact thinking the
  practice asks for. Cover the sample, ask the learner to attempt the move
  on a new line, then reveal.
- **Exit checks are your data.** Record Secure / Developing / Not yet
  (criteria in each lesson). Two "Not yet" marks on one objective = use
  the reteaching moves below before the quiz.

## Lesson-by-lesson notes

**L1 — Figurative and connotative meanings.** The connotation test is the
heart: keep asking "what feeling does this word carry?" If the learner can
only give definitions, do the swap test aloud ("little cat feet" vs.
"heavy boots") until the feeling-difference clicks. The illustration
activity works best if the learner describes the image *before* reading any
caption — let them discover the personification.

**L2 — Figures in context; sound.** Read "The Swing" at three speeds
before analyzing (see Practice B). Learners often stop at naming devices;
the sentence frame "___ makes me feel ___ because ___" forces the impact
step. For the idiom question ("look down on"), let them sit with the
double meaning — ambiguity is the point.

**L3 — Form and structure.** Scansion feels technical; keep it physical
(tap the beat, clap the stresses). The investigation's payoff is the turn:
once learners *feel* line 9 pivot, the whole sonnet architecture makes
sense. If iambic pentameter overwhelms, scan just line 1 together and let
the learner mark only the turn and the couplet.

**L4 — Point of view.** The two-voice poem is the unit's hinge. Read the
voices as a dialogue (adult takes one). Learners often pick a "winner" —
gently refuse: "The unit doesn't ask who's right. It asks what each voice
reveals and hides." The rewrite task (Voice B's version of A's line) is
the deepest check; give it full time.

**L5 — Perspective and voice.** Adjective-first: have the learner commit to
one tone adjective per poem *before* hunting evidence. This prevents the
common drift into plot summary. The portfolio thesis sentence (task 3) is
homework-grade important — it becomes the project analysis.

## Misconception alerts

| You'll hear | Redirect |
|---|---|
| "That's personification!" (for any vivid line) | The test: "Could a non-human thing *literally* do this?" If yes, it's not personification. |
| "It rhymes, so it's good." | "What does the rhyme *do*? What feeling? What would change without it?" |
| "The sonnet is just 14 lines." | "What does the *arrangement* do? Find the turn, then the couplet's job." |
| "Voice B is right because storms are dangerous." | "RL.7.6 doesn't pick winners. What does each voice reveal — and hide?" |
| "Tone is what happens." | "Which *word* makes you feel that? Tone lives in word choice." |
| Hyperbole = lying; idiom = literal | The context test: "What does the *situation* need this phrase to mean?" |

## Reteaching moves

- **Figures of speech shaky:** replay Lesson 01's readiness check with five
  new sentences; sort them into literal / simile / metaphor /
  personification on index cards. Two rounds, timed for fun.
- **Sound impact missing:** do Practice B's three-speed read-aloud, then
  the sound rewrite — *removing* the music teaches what the music did.
- **Form analysis = description:** ban the verb "has" for one session
  ("the poem *has* three quatrains" → "the three quatrains *build*…").
  Require every form sentence to end with "which makes the reader…".
- **POV contrast thin:** draw two overlapping circles (Venn): facts both
  voices share in the middle, stance-only details on the sides. The middle
  proves the facts didn't change; the sides prove meaning did.

## Readiness signals

- **Ready for the quiz (Session 15):** exit checks from L1–L5 mostly
  Secure; the learner can name a figure, a sound effect, the sonnet turn,
  and one POV contrast without prompting.
- **Ready for the assessment (Session 17):** quiz score 16+; project draft
  shows labeled figures and a form choice; the learner cites line numbers
  unprompted.
- **Reteach before assessing if:** connotation answers stay at definition
  level, or the learner summarizes poems instead of analyzing craft moves.

## Answer-key use

Check student work against [answer-key.md](answer-key.md) *after* the
learner commits to an answer. For open responses, accept any defensible
reading that cites evidence and uses unit vocabulary — the key lists the
bar, not the only wording. Never show the key to the learner; restate
feedback as questions ("Which line proves the fog is gentle?").
