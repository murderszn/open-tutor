# Lesson 05 — Perspective and Voice: The Same Subject, Two Different Poems

**Student goal:** I can compare how two different perspectives reshape the
same subject, and I can analyze a poem's tone.
**Time:** 40–50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 10 of 18**
**Standards:** RL.7.4, RL.7.6

## Materials

- Notebook; the Lesson 01 notes on Sandburg's "Fog"

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Recall Sandburg's "Fog" from Lesson 01: the fog arrives "on little cat
feet," sits, looks, "and then moves on." Ask: "Did the speaker seem afraid
of the fog?" Accept: no — calm, almost fond. Today you meet the *same*
fog through a different speaker and watch the whole poem change.

## Explicit teaching (10 minutes)

**Perspective** is the speaker's overall stance — what they notice, value,
and fear. **Tone** is the attitude the words carry (playful, solemn,
uneasy); **mood** is the feeling the reader gets. Perspective shapes tone:
the same subject through a different perspective gets a different tone.

The analysis pattern for comparing perspectives:

1. **Same subject:** name what both texts describe.
2. **Different stance:** for each speaker, name the attitude in one
   adjective, with a cited line.
3. **Craft evidence:** which *choices* build each stance? (word
   connotations, figures of speech, sound, form)
4. **So what:** what does the comparison reveal about how perspective works
   — that facts don't change, but meaning does?

This is RL.7.6 at full stretch: not just naming viewpoints, but showing
how viewpoint *re-makes* the subject.

## Anchor text B — "City Fog" (original, written for this unit)

Read against Sandburg's "Fog" (Lesson 01).

> The fog came down like a locked door (1)
> and swallowed the streetlamps one by one. (2)
> It pressed its gray hands to my windows (3)
> and would not answer when I called. (4)
> I waited for the morning to cut it open. (5)

Margin gloss: *swallowed* — here, hid completely, as if eaten.

## Worked example 1 — stance in one adjective each

Sandburg's speaker: **serene**. Evidence: "little cat feet" — *little*
connotes gentleness; the fog "sits looking," unhurried, "and then moves
on" like a guest who was never a threat. "City Fog" speaker: **trapped**.
Evidence: "like a locked door" (simile — the fog *shuts*), "swallowed the
streetlamps" (the fog *eats* the light), "pressed its gray hands to my
windows" (personification as an intruder). **Same weather, opposite
poems** — the difference is entirely the speaker's stance.

## Worked example 2 — craft choices build the stance

Compare the personification: Sandburg gives the fog a *cat's* body —
small, quiet, independent. "City Fog" gives it *hands* that press on
windows — human, but menacing. Compare the verbs: "comes," "sits,"
"moves on" (calm, temporary) vs. "came down," "swallowed," "pressed"
(heavy, aggressive). Even the endings differ: Sandburg's fog leaves on
its own; the "City Fog" speaker must *wait* to be rescued by morning.
**A common error is saying "one is happy, one is sad" and stopping.** The
analysis names the *choices* — verbs, connotations, personification
targets — that manufacture each feeling.

## Guided practice (12 minutes, with the adult)

1. Find one **connotation** contrast: a word in Sandburg that feels gentle
   and a word in "City Fog" that feels threatening, describing the same
   kind of action (arriving). ("Comes" vs. "came down" — arrival as visit
   vs. arrival as assault.)
2. Both poems use **personification**. Fill in: "Sandburg's fog acts like
   a ___; the 'City Fog' fog acts like a ___." Then say what each choice
   tells you about the speaker.
3. Which poem has a stronger **sound pattern**? (Sandburg: free verse,
   quiet repetitions of soft *s* sounds — "sits," "silent," "haunches" —
   matching the calm. "City Fog": hard consonants — "locked door,"
   "pressed," "cut" — matching the tension.) Connect one sound choice to
   one poem's tone.

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. Write a 4-line stanza about **rain** from the perspective of a farmer
   waiting for it, then a 4-line stanza about the same rain from the
   perspective of a child whose picnic is ruined. Label each stanza's tone
   in one adjective.
2. In three sentences, explain how your two stanzas prove that perspective
   changes meaning even when the facts stay the same. Cite one craft
   choice from each stanza.
3. **Portfolio prep:** choose one unit poem for your project analysis
   (Lesson 01 "Fog," Lesson 02 "The Swing," Lesson 03 Sonnet 18, or one of
   today's two). Write one sentence stating what its form or point of view
   does for its meaning — this becomes your analysis paragraph's thesis.

## Exit check

In two sentences: name the subject both fog poems share and explain one
craft choice that makes their tones different.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** Start with the adjective: pick "calm" or "scared" for each
  speaker first, *then* hunt for the lines that prove it. Adjective-first
  keeps the hunt focused.
- **Alternate mode:** Draw the two fogs — Sandburg's as a cat silhouette
  in mist, "City Fog" as hands on a window — and label each drawing with
   its tone adjective and one quoted line.
- **Extension:** Find a third perspective: rewrite "City Fog" from the
  viewpoint of the fog itself (first person). What changes when the
  "intruder" gets to speak?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners think tone is "what happens" (fog arrives)
rather than "how the words feel about what happens." Keep asking: "Which
*word* makes you feel that?" Answer-key reference:
