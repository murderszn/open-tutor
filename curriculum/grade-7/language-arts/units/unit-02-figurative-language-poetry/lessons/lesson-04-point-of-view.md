# Lesson 04 — Point of View: Whose Eyes Are You Reading Through?

**Student goal:** I can identify a narrator's point of view and explain how
it shapes — and limits — what a text shows.
**Time:** 40–50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 8 of 18**
**Standard:** RL.7.6

## Materials

- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "Your little brother and your grandmother both describe the same
thunderstorm. Will the descriptions match?" Accept: no — the brother might
be thrilled, the grandmother worried. Same storm, different *point of
view*. Today you track how authors build and contrast viewpoints on purpose.

## Explicit teaching (12 minutes)

**Point of view (POV)** is the vantage point a text is told from — whose
eyes, whose knowledge, whose feelings.

- **First person** — the narrator is a character: *I, me, my*. They know
  their own thoughts but can't read other minds.
- **Third person** — the narrator stands outside: *he, she, they*. A
  **limited** third-person narrator follows one character's inner life; an
  **omniscient** third-person narrator knows everyone's.
- **Speaker** — in poetry, the voice speaking the poem (not necessarily the
  poet).

**Why it matters:** POV decides what you *can* know. A first-person
narrator can be wrong, biased, or hiding something — the reader has to read
*around* the narrator. When an author gives you **two** viewpoints on the
same events, the contrast itself is the meaning: you see what each
perspective reveals and what it hides.

**The RL.7.6 moves:** (1) name each viewpoint and how you know (pronouns,
what the narrator can/can't know); (2) state what each viewpoint
*emphasizes*; (3) explain what the *contrast* teaches that one viewpoint
alone couldn't.

## Anchor text — "Two Voices, One Storm" (original, written for this unit)

Two narrators describe the same night. Read both.

**Voice A — Mara, 12, watching from the porch:**

> The lightning split the sky like a cracked windshield (1)
> and I counted — one-Mississippi, two — before the thunder
> punched the roof. (2–3)
> Dad whooped from the kitchen doorway, "Now *that's* a storm!" (4)
> I pressed my forehead to the screen and grinned. (5)
> Let it pour. Let the gutters sing. (6)

**Voice B — Mara's grandmother, watching from the kitchen:**

> The lightning found every crack in the old roof (1)
> and I counted the seconds, the way my mother taught me,
> measuring how close the danger walked. (2–3)
> My son laughed in the doorway — he has forgotten
> the summer the oak came down. (4–5)
> I pulled the curtains. Let it pass. Let the house hold. (6)

Margin gloss: *whooped* — gave a loud joyful shout.

## Worked example 1 — developing a point of view

Voice A is **first person** ("I counted," "I pressed") — a child's
inside-the-moment view. The author *develops* it through sense verbs and
invitation language: "Let it pour. Let the gutters sing" — the storm is
welcomed, almost personified as a performer. Voice B is also first person
("I counted," "I pulled"), but the author develops a *different* inner
life: memory ("the summer the oak came down") and protection ("Let the
house hold"). **Same pronouns, opposite worlds** — POV is not just
grammar; it's a whole stance toward the event.

## Worked example 2 — the contrast does the work

Line-by-line, the author *contrasts* the two: A's lightning "split the sky
like a cracked windshield" (exciting, a simile of spectacle) vs. B's
lightning "found every crack in the old roof" (threatening, a metaphor of
invasion). A counts seconds to *savor* the thunder; B counts to *measure
danger*. The son's laugh delights A and worries B ("he has forgotten").
**Neither voice alone gives the full storm.** Together they show how
experience — a child's thrill, a grandmother's memory — filters the same
facts. A common error is summarizing each voice separately and stopping;
the RL.7.6 analysis is the *comparison*: what does the pairing reveal?

## Guided practice (10 minutes, with the adult)

1. Find one line in Voice A and one in Voice B that describe the *same
   detail* differently. Name what each version emphasizes.
2. Which narrator knows about "the summer the oak came down"? What does
   that memory let Voice B feel that Voice A can't?
3. The dad appears in both voices. How does each narrator *frame* him?
   (A: fellow celebrant — "whooped." B: someone naive about danger — "he
   has forgotten.") What does that framing tell you about each narrator?

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. Rewrite Voice A's line 6 ("Let it pour. Let the gutters sing.") from
   Voice B's point of view in two lines. Keep the storm the same; change
   the stance.
2. In three sentences, answer: what does the *contrast* between the two
   voices teach about the storm that neither voice teaches alone?
3. **Error-analysis task:** A classmate says Voice B is the "correct" view
   because storms are dangerous. Explain in two sentences why RL.7.6 asks
   you to analyze viewpoints, not to pick a winner.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: name Voice A's point of view and one thing it
*can't* show the reader.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** Use two colors — underline everything Voice A *feels* in
  one color, everything Voice B *remembers* in another. Feelings vs.
  memories is the core contrast.
- **Alternate mode:** Read the two voices aloud as a dialogue with the
  adult; hearing the clash makes the contrast concrete.
- **Extension:** Take one event from your week and write two 4-line
  stanzas about it from two different people's viewpoints; label each
  POV.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners equate POV with opinion ("she just likes
storms"). Redirect to *knowledge and stance*: what can this narrator know,
what do they remember, what do they value — and how does the author show
it? Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) § Lesson 04.
