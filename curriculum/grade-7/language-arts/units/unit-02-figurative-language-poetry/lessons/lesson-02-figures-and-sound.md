# Lesson 02 — Figures of Speech in Context and the Music of a Poem

**Student goal:** I can interpret idioms, hyperbole, and allusions in
context, and I can explain how a poem's sounds shape its feeling.
**Time:** 40–50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 3 of 18**
**Standards:** RL.7.4, L.7.5.a

## Materials

- Notebook; one colored pencil for **sound patterns** (rhyme, alliteration,
  repetition)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Read aloud: *"I've told you a million times to clean your room."* Ask: has
the speaker literally counted to a million? Accept: no — it's exaggeration
for emphasis. That's **hyperbole**, and today you interpret figures of
speech where they live: inside a text.

## Explicit teaching (12 minutes)

**More figures of speech.** You know simile, metaphor, personification. Add:

- **Idiom** — a phrase whose meaning can't be built from its words: "break
  a leg" means "good luck," not "fracture a bone." Interpret idioms from
  context, never word by word.
- **Hyperbole** — deliberate exaggeration for effect: "I'm so hungry I
  could eat a horse." The point is the *size* of the feeling, not the menu.
- **Allusion** — a brief reference to a well-known story, person, or event:
  "He met his Waterloo" alludes to Napoleon's 1815 defeat, meaning a final,
  crushing loss. If you miss the reference, you miss the meaning — so check
  what the allusion points to.
- **Onomatopoeia** — a word that sounds like what it names: *buzz, crash,
  whisper.*

**Sound devices.** Poems are built to be heard:

- **Rhyme** — matching end sounds (*glee / me*); **internal rhyme** sits
  inside a line.
- **Alliteration** — repeated starting consonant sounds: "silent, soft
  snow."
- **Rhythm** — the beat pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables.
- **Repetition / refrain** — repeated words or lines that hammer an idea
  home.

RL.7.4 asks you to analyze the *impact* of these sounds: don't just name
the device — say what it *does* to the verse. Fast, bouncy sounds can make a
poem feel playful; slow, heavy ones can make it feel serious.

## Anchor poem — "The Swing" by Robert Louis Stevenson (1885; public domain)

> How do you like to go up in a swing,
> Up in the air so blue?
> Oh, I do think it the pleasantest thing
> Ever a child can do!
>
> Up in the air and over the wall,
> Till I can see so wide,
> Rivers and trees and cattle and all
> Over the countryside —
>
> Till I look down on the garden green,
> Down on the roof so brown —
> Up in the air I go flying again,
> Up in the air, and down!

Margin glosses: *pleasantest* — most pleasant (Stevenson's word; today we'd
say "most pleasant"); *cattle* — cows and bulls.

## Worked example 1 — sound serving meaning

Lines 1–4 rhyme *swing / thing* and *blue / do*. But listen to the
repetition: "Up in the air" appears in lines 1, 2, 5, 11, 12. The phrase is
a **refrain** — each return lifts the reader the way the swing lifts the
child. The short, bouncing lines mimic the swing's rhythm: up, pause, down.
**So the sound isn't decoration — the poem *moves* like a swing because the
words move like one.** A common error is stopping at "it rhymes"; the
analysis move is always *what does the sound make you feel, and how?*

## Worked example 2 — interpreting a figure in context

Line 3: "Oh, I do think it the pleasantest thing / Ever a child can do!"
"Ever a child can do" is **hyperbole** — no one has surveyed every child.
In context it means the speaker's joy is so big it overflows into
exaggeration; the hyperbole *shows* delight rather than stating it. Now the
allusion test: if a friend said the swing "took me to the moon," you'd
recognize an allusion to space travel meaning an amazing ride. **Interpret
figures from the situation around them, not from the dictionary.**

## Guided practice (10 minutes, with the adult)

1. Mark every end rhyme in stanza 2 (*wall / all*, *wide / countryside*).
   Which pair is exact and which is slant (near) rhyme? ("Wall/all" is
   exact; "wide/countryside" is near rhyme — close but not identical.)
2. Find one example of **alliteration** in the poem. Say how it sounds when
   read aloud. ("So wide" — the soft *w* sounds drift, like the view
   spreading out.)
3. **Connotation check:** the speaker says "Up in the air so blue." Why
   *blue* and not *gray*? What does *blue* connote here? (Clear, open,
   happy sky — freedom. Gray would connote gloom and break the mood.)

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. The speaker looks "down on the garden green, / Down on the roof so
   brown." Is "look down on" literal here, an idiom, or both? Explain.
   (Literal: the child is physically above. The idiom "look down on"
   usually means "despise" — the poem *plays* with that: the child looks
   down with delight, not scorn. Context decides.)
2. Copy one line with strong rhythm and mark its stressed syllables (e.g.,
   **Up** in the **air** and **o**ver the **wall**). How does the beat match
   the swing's motion?
3. **Error-analysis task:** A classmate claims the poem has no figurative
   language "because it's just describing a swing." Write three sentences
   proving them wrong, naming at least one figure of speech and one sound
   device with line references.

## Exit check

In two sentences: name one sound device in "The Swing" and explain what
feeling it creates in its stanza.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** Clap the rhythm of lines 1–2 before marking stresses;
  hearing the beat first makes the marks easy.
- **Alternate mode:** Draw the swing's arc and write the refrain "Up in the
  air" at the top of the arc and "and down" at the bottom — sound mapped
  to motion.
- **Extension:** Find one allusion in a song, show, or book you know;
  write what it refers to and what it means in context (L.7.5.a in the
  wild).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners treat hyperbole as lying and idioms as
literal. Keep returning to the context test: "What does the *situation*
need this phrase to mean?" Answer-key reference:
