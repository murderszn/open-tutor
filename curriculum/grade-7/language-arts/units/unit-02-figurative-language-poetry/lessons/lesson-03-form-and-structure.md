# Lesson 03 — Form and Structure: How a Poem's Shape Does Meaning-Work

**Student goal:** I can explain how a poem's form — stanzas, line breaks,
sonnet structure — contributes to its meaning.
**Time:** 40–50 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 18**
**Standard:** RL.7.5

## Materials

- Notebook; ruler or straight edge for marking stressed syllables

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Write this sentence two ways and read both aloud:

1. "I am not going to the store today."
2. "I am NOT / going to the store / today."

Same words, different *breaks* — the second version hits harder. In poetry,
where the poet breaks a line is a meaning choice. Today you analyze form as
a tool, not a container.

## Explicit teaching (12 minutes)

**Form vocabulary.**

- **Stanza** — a grouped set of lines, like a paragraph in prose. A
  4-line stanza is a **quatrain**; a 2-line stanza is a **couplet**.
- **Line break** — where the poet ends a line. A break can create suspense
  (the thought hangs), emphasis (a lone word gets a spotlight), or speed
  (short lines rush; long lines stroll).
- **Sonnet** — a 14-line poem with a strict architecture. Shakespeare's
  sonnets: three **quatrains** (4 + 4 + 4 lines) plus a final **couplet**
  (2 lines). The quatrains build an idea; the couplet delivers the punch —
  often a twist or summary. The first 8 lines are sometimes called the
  **octave** (problem) and the last 6 the **sestet** (answer), with a **turn**
  (a shift in thought) between them.
- **Meter and scansion** — **meter** is a poem's beat pattern. An **iamb**
  is one unstressed syllable followed by a stressed one (da-DUM, like
  "a-**rise**"). **Scansion** is the act of marking the stresses to reveal
  the pattern. Shakespeare's sonnets run in **iambic pentameter**: five
  iambs per line — ten syllables, heartbeat rhythm.

**The RL.7.5 question** is always: *what does the form do for the meaning?*
A sonnet's couplet doesn't just end the poem — it *resolves* it. A stanza
break doesn't just add white space — it *separates* ideas.

## Anchor poem — Sonnet 18 by William Shakespeare (1609; public domain)

> Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? (1)
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate: (2)
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May, (3)
> And summer's lease hath all too short a date; (4)
> Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines, (5)
> And often is his gold complexion dimm'd; (6)
> And every fair from fair sometime declines, (7)
> By chance or nature's changing course untrimm'd; (8)
> But thy eternal summer shall not fade, (9)
> Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st; (10)
> Nor shall Death brag thou wander'st in his shade, (11)
> When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st: (12)
> So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, (13)
> So long lives this, and this gives life to thee. (14)

Margin glosses: *thee/thou* — you; *temperate* — mild, balanced; *lease
hath all too short a date* — summer's rental runs out too soon (a metaphor:
summer is a tenant); *fair* — beauty; *ow'st* — own.

## Worked example 1 — the turn and the couplet

Lines 1–8 (the octave) list summer's *flaws*: rough winds, too-hot sun,
fading beauty. Line 9 pivots with **"But"** — that single word is the
**turn**. Lines 9–12 (the sestet's first half) reverse everything: *thy*
summer will *not* fade. Then the final **couplet** (13–14) delivers the
punch: the poem itself — "this" — gives the beloved eternal life. **The
form does the argument's work:** problem (quatrains 1–2) → turn (line 9) →
answer (quatrain 3) → proof (couplet). If you read the poem as one
unbroken block, you'd still get the words — but you'd lose the *architecture
of the argument*.

## Worked example 2 — scansion

Scan line 1: "Shall **I** | com-**pare** | thee **to** | a **sum**- | mer's
**day**?" Five iambs (da-DUM × 5) = **iambic pentameter**. Now line 5:
"Some-**time** | too **hot** | the **eye** | of **heav'n** | **shines**."
Regular heartbeat — until a line breaks the pattern, and Shakespeare *wants*
you to notice. A common error is treating scansion as decoration; the point
is that the steady beat makes the poem feel timeless and certain — which is
exactly what the poem *claims* about the beloved's beauty.

## Guided practice — sonnet scansion investigation (15 minutes, with adult)

1. Scan lines 2 and 4, marking stressed syllables. (Both scan as regular
   iambic pentameter: "Thou **art** | more **love**- | ly **and** | more
   **tem**- | per-**ate**.")
2. Label the structure in the margin: Q1 (lines 1–4), Q2 (5–8), Q3 (9–12),
   Couplet (13–14). Draw a bracket around the **turn** at line 9.
3. In one sentence per quatrain, state what each section *does* in the
   argument. (Q1: poses the comparison and finds summer wanting. Q2: lists
   summer's specific flaws. Q3: declares the beloved exempt from decay.
   Couplet: credits the poem with granting immortality.)

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. Line 4: "And summer's lease hath all too short a date" — name the
   figure of speech (*lease/date*: summer as a tenant with a rental
   contract — a **metaphor**) and explain what it adds to the "summer is
   flawed" case.
2. Why does the couplet work as an *ending*? Write two sentences connecting
   the 2-line form to the idea of a final proof.
3. **Error-analysis task:** A classmate says, "The sonnet is just 14 lines
   — the number doesn't matter." Using the turn and the couplet, explain in
   three sentences why the *arrangement* of the 14 lines matters.

## Exit check

Name the turn word in Sonnet 18 and, in one sentence, say what changes in
the argument after it.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** Tap a steady beat on the desk while reading each line aloud;
  the stressed syllables land on the taps.
- **Alternate mode:** Cut the 14 lines into strips, sort them into
  quatrains and couplet, and physically move the "But" strip to show the
  turn.
- **Extension:** Scan the full final couplet independently and write a
  modern-speech "translation" of it; defend one thing your translation kept
  and one thing it lost.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners think form analysis means *describing* the
form ("it has three quatrains"). Push every answer to the *contributes*
verb: "…which makes the argument feel ___ because ___." Answer-key
