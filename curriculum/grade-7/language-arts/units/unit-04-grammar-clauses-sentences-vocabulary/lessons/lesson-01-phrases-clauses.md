# Lesson 01 — Phrases, Clauses, and Their Jobs

**Goal:** I can explain what a phrase and a clause are, tell them
apart, and name the job each one does in a sentence.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** L.7.1a
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, two colored pencils (one for
subjects and verbs, one for modifiers).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: in "The dog barked," which word names who
or what the sentence is about, and which word says what it did?
(Answer: *dog* is the subject; *barked* is the verb. Every clause you
meet today has both — that pair is your test.)

## Teaching: the subject-and-verb test

A **phrase** is a group of related words that works as a unit but does
**not** contain both a subject and a verb. A **clause** is a group of
words that **does** contain both a subject and a verb.

- *under the old oak tree* — phrase. There is no subject doing
  anything and no verb. It tells *where*.
- *the rain fell all night* — clause. Subject *rain*, verb *fell*.
  It can stand alone as a sentence, so it is an **independent clause**.
- *because the rain fell all night* — clause, but a **dependent
  clause**. It has a subject (*rain*) and a verb (*fell*), yet it
  cannot stand alone: *because* makes it lean on another clause to
  finish the thought. Say it aloud and you hear the unfinished feeling:
  "Because the rain fell all night…" — *what happened?*

A phrase or clause also has a **job** — what it does in its sentence.
Three jobs cover nearly everything in this unit:

1. **Noun jobs:** acts as a subject, an object, or a renamed noun.
   *Running every day* builds endurance. (The whole phrase is the
   subject.)
2. **Adjective jobs:** describes a noun or pronoun. *The student who
   scored highest won a prize.* (The clause *who scored highest*
   describes *student*.)
3. **Adverb jobs:** describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb —
   telling when, where, why, or how. *She smiled when she heard the
   news.* (The clause *when she heard the news* tells *when* she
   smiled.)

Signal words help you spot dependent clauses: adjective clauses often
start with *who, whom, whose, which, that*; adverb clauses with
*because, although, when, while, if, since, unless, until*; noun
clauses with *that, what, whoever, whether, how*.

### Worked example 1 — phrase or clause? What job?

Sentence: *Covered in mud, the dog ran inside.*

1. Find the subject-and-verb pair of the whole sentence: *dog ran*.
   That is the independent clause.
2. Look at *Covered in mud*. Subject? No. Verb? *Covered* looks like a
   verb, but here it has no subject doing the covering — it is a
   participle acting as a modifier. No subject + verb pair, so it is a
   **phrase**.
3. Its job: it describes *the dog* (the muddy one ran inside), so it
   is an **adjective job** — a participial phrase. Note the placement:
   it sits right beside *the dog*, the noun it describes. That
   placement rule returns in Lesson 03.

### Worked example 2 — two clauses, two jobs

Sentence: *What you said surprised everyone when the bell rang.*

1. *What you said* — subject *you*, verb *said*: a clause. It is the
   subject of the whole sentence (what surprised everyone?), so it is
   a **noun clause** doing a noun job.
2. *surprised everyone* — the main verb and object: the independent
   clause, with subject *What you said*.
3. *when the bell rang* — subject *bell*, verb *rang*: a clause. It
   tells *when* the surprising happened, so it is an **adverb clause**
   doing an adverb job.
4. Check: three groups, two dependent clauses, one independent clause
   — and the sentence still has exactly one main subject-verb pair
   driving it (*What you said surprised*).

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. *He sat under the old oak tree.* Is *under the old oak tree* a
   phrase or a clause? What job does it do, and how do you know?
2. *She wanted to win the championship.* Is *to win the championship*
   a phrase or a clause? What job does it do?
3. *The bird sang because it was spring.* Name each clause, say
   whether it is independent or dependent, and name its job.
4. Error analysis: a classmate says "every group with a verb is a
   clause." Give one counterexample from this lesson and explain why
   the rule fails. (Hint: look at Worked example 1.)

## Independent practice

1. Label each group as phrase or clause, and independent or dependent
   if it is a clause: (a) *after the storm passed*; (b) *the river
   rose quickly*; (c) *a talented musician*; (d) *who practices
   every morning*.
2. In "My brother, a talented musician, plays guitar," what job does
   *a talented musician* do? Which noun does it rename?
3. In "Running every day builds endurance," what job does *Running
   every day* do? Prove it with the subject-and-verb test.
4. Write one sentence containing an adjective clause and one sentence
   containing an adverb clause. Underline the clause and label its
   job.
5. Explain in your own words why *because the rain fell all night*
   cannot stand alone even though it has a subject and a verb.

## Application

Take this sentence and rebuild it twice: *The team practiced after
school.* First, add an adjective clause describing *team*. Then, add
an adverb clause telling *why* they practiced. Read both versions
aloud — which added information changes what a reader pictures most?

## Exit check

Is *while the soup simmered* a phrase or a clause? Is it independent
or dependent? What one word could you add to make the whole thing a
complete sentence?

## Supports, extensions, misconceptions

- **Support:** if spotting subjects is hard, cross out prepositional
  phrases first (*under the tree*, *after school*) — subjects almost
  never hide inside them.
- **Alternate mode:** answer the guided practice by pointing and
  labeling on a printed sentence instead of writing full sentences.
- **Extension:** find a sentence in your own recent writing with two
  or more clauses; label every clause and job, and rewrite it so each
  clause's job is unmistakable.
- **Misconception:** a verb-looking word is not always a verb doing a
  clause's work. Participles (*covered*, *running*) and infinitives
  (*to win*) can act as modifiers or nouns — the subject-and-verb
  *pair* is the test, not the presence of one verb-like word.
