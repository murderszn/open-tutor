# Lesson 03 — Misplaced and Dangling Modifiers

**Goal:** I can spot misplaced and dangling modifiers and repair them
by putting the modifier next to the word it describes.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** L.7.1c
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, a colored pencil for modifiers.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: in "Running to the bus, Maya dropped her
lunchbox," who was running? (Answer: Maya — the phrase *Running to
the bus* sits beside *Maya*, the noun it describes. If it sat beside a
different noun, the sentence would claim a different runner.)

## Teaching: ask "who or what does this describe?"

A **modifier** is a phrase or clause that describes another word. The
rule is placement: **a modifier must sit next to the word it
modifies**. When it does not, two faults occur:

- **Misplaced modifier:** the modifier is in the sentence but beside
  the wrong word, so the sentence claims something silly or untrue.
  *I saw a dog on my way to school wearing a red collar.* As written,
  *wearing a red collar* sits beside *school* — the school wears the
  collar. The writer meant the dog.
- **Dangling modifier:** the word being described is missing from the
  sentence entirely, so the modifier dangles with nothing to hold it.
  *Walking home from the market, the rain started.* As written, the
  rain was walking home. The walker — the person — never appears.

The repair question is always the same: **who or what does this
describe?** Then either move the modifier beside that word, or, for a
dangling modifier, add the missing word to the sentence.

### Worked example 1 — misplaced modifier, repaired two ways

Faulty: *She served sandwiches to the children on paper plates.*

1. Ask: who or what is *on paper plates*? The sandwiches are on the
   plates — but the phrase sits beside *children*, so the sentence
   claims the children are on paper plates.
2. Repair by moving the modifier beside its word: *She served
   sandwiches on paper plates to the children.* Now the plates hold
   the sandwiches.
3. Alternative repair, same meaning, different structure: *She served
   the children sandwiches that were on paper plates.* Moving the
   modifier is the usual fix; rewording is allowed when moving alone
   sounds awkward. Both repairs pass the test: the phrase now touches
   *sandwiches*.

### Worked example 2 — dangling modifier, repaired two ways

Faulty: *After finishing the race, the medal was awarded to Jonas.*

1. Ask: who finished the race? Jonas — but Jonas appears only as the
   receiver of the medal, late in the sentence, and the opening phrase
   *After finishing the race* grammatically attaches to the subject
   that follows it, *the medal*. The sentence claims the medal ran
   the race.
2. Repair by adding the missing doer as the subject: *After finishing
   the race, Jonas received the medal.* Now the finisher and the
   subject are the same person.
3. Alternative repair: *After Jonas finished the race, the medal was
   awarded to him.* Here the modifier becomes a full clause with its
   own subject (*Jonas*), so nothing dangles. Both repairs pass the
   test: the finisher is named and placed where the phrase points.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Name the fault (misplaced or dangling) and the word the modifier
   *should* describe: *Hanging on the wall, I admired the painting.*
2. Repair this misplaced modifier by moving it: *The teacher handed
   back the essays to the students covered in comments.*
3. Repair this dangling modifier by adding the missing person:
   *While reading in the library, the bell rang.*
4. Error analysis: a classmate "repairs" *Running down the street,
   my hat blew off* as *Running down the street, my hat blew off
   quickly.* Why is this not a repair at all? What does the sentence
   still claim?

## Independent practice

Repair each sentence. Name the fault, say what the faulty version
wrongly claims, and write the repaired sentence.

1. *I bought a bike from a man with two flat tires.*
2. *To win the game, the final goal was scored in the last minute.*
3. *Maya found a wallet walking home from practice.*
4. *Covered in mud, Sam washed the car.*
5. Explain in your own words why moving a misplaced modifier usually
   fixes it, but moving a dangling modifier never can.

## Application

Read this paragraph and find its two modifier faults (do not fix them
yet — just mark them and name each fault): "Riding my bike to school,
the hill looked steep. I stopped at the shop with a flat tire to buy a
snack." Then write the repaired paragraph. (Both faults and both
repairs are in the answer key; your wording may differ if the meaning
and placement are right.)

## Exit check

Repair: *Yawning widely, the movie finally ended.* Is the fault
misplaced or dangling? Who was yawning, and where does that person
belong in the repaired sentence?

## Supports, extensions, misconceptions

- **Support:** draw an arrow from each opening phrase to the noun
  right after it. If the arrow points at the wrong thing (or at
  nothing), you have found the fault.
- **Alternate mode:** act out the faulty sentence literally (who
  wears the collar? who ran the race?) — the laugh is the diagnosis.
- **Extension:** write three deliberately faulty sentences — one
  misplaced, one dangling, one correct but tricky — swap with a
  partner (or the adult), and repair each other's.
- **Misconception:** a sentence can sound fine and still be faulty.
  *Walking home, the rain started* is easy to understand, but it
  still claims the rain walked. "Understandable" is not the same as
  "correct" — and in formal writing, the reader may be a stranger who
  takes your words literally.
