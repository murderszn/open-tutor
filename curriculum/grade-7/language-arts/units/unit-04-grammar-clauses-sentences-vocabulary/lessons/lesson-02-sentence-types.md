# Lesson 02 — Four Sentence Types: Choosing the Shape on Purpose

**Goal:** I can build simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex
sentences, and choose the type that shows the relationship between my
ideas.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** L.7.1b
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, the market-scene illustration
below.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: how many independent clauses are in "The
bird sang, and the flowers bloomed"? (Answer: two — *The bird sang*
and *the flowers bloomed* — joined by *and*. Count independent
clauses first; the type follows from that count.)

## Teaching: count clauses, then choose

Sentence type, by structure, is decided by counting clauses:

| Type | Recipe | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | One independent clause | *The bird sang.* |
| **Compound** | Two or more independent clauses, joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*) or a semicolon | *The bird sang, and the flowers bloomed.* |
| **Complex** | One independent clause + one or more dependent clauses | *Because it was spring, the bird sang.* |
| **Compound-complex** | Two or more independent clauses + one or more dependent clauses | *Although it was cold, the bird sang, and the crowd cheered.* |

The type is not decoration — it *signals a relationship*:

- **Compound** says: these two ideas are equal partners. (*The dog
  sniffed the apple, and the cat watched the pigeon.* Neither idea
  causes the other; they share the scene.)
- **Complex** says: one idea depends on the other — cause, condition,
  time, or contrast. (*Because the dog was leashed, it could not chase
  the pigeon.* The leash explains the limit.)
- **Compound-complex** says: a scene has both equal partners *and* a
  dependent idea. Use it when a sentence genuinely needs all three
  parts; do not force it.

Watch the punctuation: a compound sentence needs a comma before the
coordinating conjunction (*sang, and*), or a semicolon with no
conjunction (*sang; the flowers bloomed*). A dependent clause at the
start of a sentence takes a comma after it (*Because it was spring,
the bird sang*); at the end, usually no comma (*The bird sang because
it was spring*).

### Worked example 1 — one scene, four shapes

All four sentences below describe the *same* market facts. Only the
shape — and therefore the relationship — changes:

- **Simple:** *The dog sniffed the fallen apple.*
- **Compound:** *The dog sniffed the fallen apple, and the cat
  watched the pigeon.* (Two equal observations.)
- **Complex:** *Because the dog was leashed, it could only sniff the
  fallen apple.* (The leash causes the limit — cause and effect.)
- **Compound-complex:** *Because the dog was leashed, it could only
  sniff the fallen apple, and the cat watched the pigeon undisturbed.*
  (Cause and effect *plus* an equal second observation.)

Notice what each version makes the reader notice: the compound version
balances two animals; the complex version explains one animal; the
compound-complex version does both jobs at once.

### Worked example 2 — choosing the type to match the meaning

Draft idea: a shopper buys carrots. The musician keeps playing. Are
these equal partners, or does one cause the other? Neither causes the
other, so **compound** fits: *The shopper bought carrots, and the
musician kept playing.* If instead the idea were "the shopper stopped
because the music caught her ear," the relationship is cause — so
**complex** fits: *The shopper stopped because the music caught her
ear.* Picking the wrong type is not a grammar crime; it is a meaning
mistake, because the reader will infer the relationship your type
signals.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Label each sentence simple, compound, complex, or
   compound-complex, and count its clauses: (a) *The vendor smiled.*;
   (b) *The vendor smiled, and the customer paid.*; (c) *When the
   customer paid, the vendor smiled.*; (d) *When the customer paid,
   the vendor smiled, and the musician played louder.*
2. Combine these two facts as a complex sentence showing cause:
   "The cat stayed on the crate. The dog was leashed." Then combine
   them as a compound sentence. Which version changes the meaning,
   and how?
3. Fix the punctuation: *Although the sun was bright the shoppers
   carried baskets and the flowers glowed.* (Hint: two fixes — one
   after the opening dependent clause, one before a conjunction
   joining independent clauses.)

## Independent practice

Write four sentences about the market scene — one of each type. Each
sentence must be factually true of the picture (or of the text-only
alternative). Label each sentence's type and underline its dependent
clause(s), if any.

1. Simple: ________
2. Compound: ________
3. Complex: ________
4. Compound-complex: ________
5. Explain in one or two sentences: why did you choose complex (not
   compound) for your complex sentence — what relationship were you
   signaling?

## Application

Rewrite this flat paragraph using at least one compound and one
complex sentence, without changing any fact: "The market opened at
nine. Shoppers arrived. The musician played. The dog sniffed an
apple. The cat watched a pigeon." Read your version aloud. Does it
sound like a scene now, or still like a list? If it still sounds like
a list, your conjunctions are joining facts but not *relating* them —
try a *because*, *while*, or *although* clause.

## Exit check

Make a compound-complex sentence about the market scene that includes
the musician and the shoppers. Circle its two independent clauses and
underline its dependent clause.

## Supports, extensions, misconceptions

- **Support:** count independent clauses by finding every
  subject-verb pair that could stand alone; cross out dependent
  clauses (starting with *because, when, although, who, that*) first.
- **Alternate mode:** build sentences with clause cards (one clause
  per card) and physically join them with *and / because / although*
  cards before writing.
- **Extension:** rewrite one paragraph of your project draft in three
  different type patterns (mostly simple; mixed compound/complex;
  one compound-complex anchor sentence) and compare their effects in
  two or three sentences.
- **Misconception:** length does not decide type. A long simple
  sentence (*The vendor in the green apron at the wooden stall handed
  a smiling customer a bunch of carrots.*) is still simple — it has
  one independent clause. Count clauses, not words.
