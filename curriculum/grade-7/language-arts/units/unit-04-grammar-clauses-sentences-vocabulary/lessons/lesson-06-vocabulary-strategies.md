# Lesson 06 — Vocabulary Strategies: Clues, Roots, References, Relationships

**Goal:** I can figure out unknown words with context clues, Greek
and Latin affixes and roots, and reference materials — and verify my
guess — and I can use word relationships and connotations to choose
words well.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** L.7.4a–d, L.7.5b–c
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, a dictionary and thesaurus
(print or digital; see the [Resource Pack](../resource-pack.md) for
free, no-account options).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: without a dictionary, what could
*transport* mean? Break it apart. (Answer: *trans-* = across,
*port* = carry — *transport* means carry across. Affixes are clues
you already own; this lesson teaches you to use them on purpose.)

## Teaching: four strategies, in order, plus verification

When you meet an unknown word, work in this order:

1. **Context clues (L.7.4a).** Read the whole sentence, then the
   sentences around it. Clues come in kinds: a definition in the
   sentence (*Photosynthesis, the process by which plants make
   food,…*); an example (*…fruits such as mangoes, papayas, and
   guavas*); a contrast (*Unlike her timid brother, Ana was
   outspoken*); a cause (*Because the road was treacherous, drivers
   slowed to a crawl* — treacherous must mean dangerous).
2. **Affixes and roots (L.7.4b).** Common Greek and Latin parts:
   *photo-* light (*photograph*), *graph* write (*autograph*),
   *port* carry (*transport, import*), *dict* say (*predict,
   dictate*), *bene-* good (*benefit*), *mal-* bad (*malfunction*),
   *bell-* war (*belligerent, bellicose, rebel* — all carry the war
   idea: hostile, warlike, one who fights back), *scrib/script*
   write (*describe, manuscript*), *tele-* far (*telephone*),
   *therm* heat (*thermometer*). A root gives a *likely* meaning,
   not a guarantee — always check it against the context.
3. **Reference materials (L.7.4c).** Consult a dictionary (meaning,
   pronunciation, part of speech), a glossary (subject words), or a
   thesaurus (related words). Read the *whole* entry: many words
   have several meanings, and only one fits your sentence.
4. **Verification (L.7.4d).** Check your guess twice: does it fit
   the sentence's context? Does the dictionary agree? If the two
   disagree, trust the dictionary and re-read the context — you may
   have misread the clue.

Then two relationship skills for *choosing* words (L.7.5b–c):

- **Synonym / antonym / analogy:** knowing a word's relatives sharpens
  it. *Brave* is clearer beside its synonym *courageous*, its
  antonym *cowardly*, and an analogy: *whisper : shout :: glance :
  stare* (both pairs move from soft to strong). To solve an analogy,
  name the relationship in plain words first ("soft version to strong
  version"), then apply it.
- **Denotation vs. connotation:** denotation is the dictionary
  meaning; connotation is the feeling a word carries. *Refined,
  respectful, polite, diplomatic,* and *condescending* can all describe
  a careful manner — but *refined* praises, *diplomatic* suggests
  tactful skill, and *condescending* insults (talking down). Same
  neighborhood of meaning, opposite feelings. Choosing among them is
  choosing what you want the reader to feel.

### Worked example 1 — context + affix + verification

Sentence: *The belligerent crowd shouted at the officials and refused
to leave the hall.*

1. Context: shouting at officials and refusing to leave suggests
   hostility, not friendliness.
2. Affix/root: *bell-* = war; *belligerent* sounds warlike.
3. Guess: hostile and ready to fight.
4. Verify: a dictionary confirms *belligerent* = hostile and
   aggressive. Guess and reference agree, and the guess fits the
   sentence. Done — and note the part of speech (adjective) for your
   word log.

### Worked example 2 — analogy and connotation

Analogy: *frugal : thrifty :: brave : ___* (options: *cowardly,
courageous, fearful*).

1. Name the relationship: *frugal* and *thrifty* are synonyms (both
   mean careful with money, positively).
2. Apply it: the synonym of *brave* is *courageous*. (*Cowardly* and
   *fearful* are antonyms — the trap answers.)

Connotation: a writer describing a careful spender can choose
*thrifty* (praise), *economical* (neutral report), or *stingy*
(criticism). All three denote careful spending. The choice tells the
reader whether to admire, merely note, or judge the spender. That is
why Lesson 05's precision rule includes feeling, not just meaning.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Use context to guess *treacherous* in: "Because the road was
   treacherous after the ice storm, drivers slowed to a crawl." What
   kind of clue is it (definition, example, contrast, cause)?
2. Use affixes to guess *photograph* and *telephone*, naming each
   part's meaning. Then verify both in a dictionary and report the
   part of speech of each.
3. Solve: *author : book :: composer : ___* (options: *song,
   reader, library*). Name the relationship first.
4. Choose the word with a positive connotation for a compliment:
   "Maya kept trying after failing three times. She was ___."
   (options: *stubborn, persistent, obstinate*). Explain what the
   other two connote.

## Independent practice

1. Guess *benevolent* from its parts (*bene-* + *vol* wish), then
   verify in a dictionary. Give the verified meaning and part of
   speech.
2. Use context to guess the meaning of *reluctant* in: "Ana was
   reluctant to speak first, so she waited until two classmates had
   shared." Name the clue kind.
3. Solve two analogies, naming each relationship: (a) *hot : scalding
   :: cold : ___* (options: *freezing, cool, mild*); (b) *painter :
   brush :: writer : ___* (options: *pen, book, reader*).
4. For the word family "not giving up," sort *persistent, stubborn,
   determined, obstinate* from most praising to most criticizing, and
   write one sentence using the most praising word correctly.
5. Pick one word from this lesson you did not know before. Write a
   word-log entry: the sentence you found it in (or this lesson's),
   your guess and strategy, the dictionary meaning, and a new
   sentence using it correctly.

## Application

Write a four-sentence paragraph about learning something hard (a
sport, an instrument, a subject). Use two words from this lesson's
word families (*belligerent, benevolent, transport, photograph,
persistent, determined*) correctly, and use one word with a deliberate
connotation choice — then, in a note below the paragraph, name the
connotation you chose and why.

## Exit check

Without a dictionary: what does *malfunction* probably mean? Name the
parts, your guess, and the one check you would run to verify it.

## Supports, extensions, misconceptions

- **Support:** keep a root card list (*photo-, graph-, port-, dict-,
  bene-, mal-, bell-, scrib-, tele-, therm-*) beside you during
  practice; roots are tools, not a memorization test.
- **Alternate mode:** answer analogy and connotation items orally
  with the adult, explaining the relationship aloud before choosing.
- **Extension:** build an original analogy set (three pairs, one
  relationship) and a connotation spectrum for one word family
  (five words from most praising to most criticizing, each in a
  sentence that proves its placement).
- **Misconception:** a root gives a hint, not a verdict. *Rebel*
  carries *bell-* (war) but means one who resists authority — the
  war idea is present but the meaning is specific. Always verify.
  Also: synonyms are rarely exact — *thrifty* and *stingy* share a
  denotation but opposite connotations, and swapping them changes
  the sentence's judgment.
