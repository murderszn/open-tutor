# Lesson 04 — Coordinate Adjectives and Spelling Proofreading

**Goal:** I can use a comma to separate coordinate adjectives, and I
can proofread for spelling and commonly confused words.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** L.7.2a–b
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, a dictionary (print or digital).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: which sentence needs a comma, and why?
(a) *It was a fascinating, enjoyable movie.* (b) *He wore an old
green shirt.* (Answer: (a) needs the comma — *fascinating* and
*enjoyable* are coordinate adjectives. In (b), *old* and *green* are
cumulative: *green* joins *shirt* first, and *old* describes the
green shirt. The two tests below prove it.)

## Teaching: two tests for coordinate adjectives

When two or more adjectives sit before a noun, ask two questions:

1. **The *and*-test:** can you put *and* between them without changing
   the meaning? *a fascinating and enjoyable movie* — yes.
2. **The reversibility test:** can you swap their order without
   changing the meaning? *an enjoyable, fascinating movie* — yes.

If **both** tests pass, the adjectives are **coordinate**: separate
them with commas. (*a fascinating, enjoyable movie*; *a tall, dark,
handsome stranger*.) If either test fails, they are **cumulative**:
no comma. (*an old green shirt* — *a green and old shirt* sounds
wrong, and *a green old shirt* changes the grouping: *old* no longer
describes a green shirt but a shirt that is somehow both.)

Three cautions: (a) never put a comma between the last adjective and
the noun (*a fascinating, enjoyable, movie* is wrong); (b) numbers,
colors that name a single shade, and adjectives that form a unit with
the noun (*hot water bottle*) are usually cumulative; (c) when in
doubt, read both tests aloud — your ear usually knows.

## Teaching: spelling is proofreading, not memory

"Spell correctly" (L.7.2b) does not mean memorizing a dictionary. It
means having a proofreading routine:

1. **Read backwards, sentence by sentence**, so meaning does not
   carry you past errors.
2. **Hunt the confusions** — words that sound alike but mean
   different things: *their / there / they're*, *its / it's*,
   *your / you're*, *to / too / two*, *affect / effect*,
   *then / than*, *accept / except*.
3. **Check, don't guess** — if you are unsure, look it up. Using a
   dictionary is the skill, not a failure of it.
4. **Keep a personal confusion list** — the three words you personally
   mix up, checked every time you write.

### Worked example 1 — coordinate or cumulative?

Sentence: *She wore a bright, cheerful dress.*

- *And*-test: *a bright and cheerful dress* — works, same meaning.
- Reversibility: *a cheerful, bright dress* — works, same meaning.
- Both pass → coordinate → comma: *a bright, cheerful dress.*

Contrast: *She carried a heavy leather bag.*

- *And*-test: *a heavy and leather bag* — sounds wrong; leather is
  the material of the bag, not a second equal description.
- Reversibility: *a leather heavy bag* — changes the grouping.
- At least one test fails → cumulative → no comma: *a heavy leather
  bag.*

### Worked example 2 — proofreading catches what spellcheck misses

Draft: *Their going to the store even though its to far to walk.*

A spellchecker passes every word — all are real words. Proofreading
by meaning catches three errors: *Their* should be *They're* (they
are going); *its* should be *it's* (it is too far); *to* should be
*too* (too far). The repaired sentence: *They're going to the store
even though it's too far to walk.* The lesson: a spellchecker checks
spelling, not meaning. Only a reader asking "what does this word mean
here?" catches confusions — and that reader is you, on the second
pass.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Apply both tests to *a cold winter morning* and to *a cold, rainy
   morning*. Which pair takes a comma, and why?
2. Punctuate: *It was a long difficult hike up the steep rocky trail.*
   (Hint: test each pair separately — *long/difficult* and
   *steep/rocky*.)
3. Find and fix the confusions: *Your going to love there new house,
   its bigger then yours.*
4. Error analysis: a classmate writes *a red, brick house*. Apply the
   tests and explain whether the comma belongs there.

## Independent practice

1. Punctuate each pair correctly (comma or no comma), and name the
   test that decides it: (a) *a happy energetic puppy*; (b) *a small
   wooden box*; (c) *the deep dark forest*; (d) *a deep, dark
   secret*.
2. Proofread this paragraph and list every correction: "Me and Jake
   went to the libary to study. We new the test was on Friday, so we
   reviewed are notes until we were threw. Their teacher said practice
   makes perfect, and we beleived her."
3. Write two sentences of your own: one with coordinate adjectives
   (with comma), one with cumulative adjectives (without comma).
   Label which is which and show one test for each.
4. Start your personal confusion list: name three word pairs you mix
   up (or might), and write one correct sentence for each word.

## Application

Edit this sentence for both skills at once — commas and spelling:
*The tired hungry hikers reached a cozy warm cabin, but they realised
their was no firewood, so they ate there sandwiches cold.* Write the
corrected sentence and list each change with its reason.

## Exit check

Punctuate: *She gave a clear confident answer.* Then explain, using
one test, why your punctuation is right.

## Supports, extensions, misconceptions

- **Support:** say the *and*-test aloud — if *and* sounds natural,
  the comma usually belongs.
- **Alternate mode:** sort adjective pairs on cards into "comma" and
  "no comma" piles by testing each pair before writing anything.
- **Extension:** collect five adjective pairs from your own reading;
  test each, and find one pair where the two tests disagree — then
  decide (with the adult) which test wins and why.
- **Misconception:** "two adjectives = comma" is false. The tests,
  not the count, decide. Also: a spellchecker cannot be trusted with
  confusions — *their/there/they're* are all correctly spelled words.
