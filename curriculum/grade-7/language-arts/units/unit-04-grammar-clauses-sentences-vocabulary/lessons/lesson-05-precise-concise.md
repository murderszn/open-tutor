# Lesson 05 — Precise, Concise Language

**Goal:** I can choose language that says exactly what I mean, and
cut wordiness and redundancy without losing meaning.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** L.7.3a
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: which sentence is stronger, and why?
(a) *Due to the fact that it was raining, we stayed inside.*
(b) *Because it rained, we stayed inside.*
(Answer: (b) — it says the same thing in fewer words. (a) pads a
simple cause (*because*) into a five-word phrase (*due to the fact
that*). Padding is not formality; it is fog.)

## Teaching: two cuts, one test

**Wordiness** is using more words than the meaning needs. **Redundancy**
is saying the same thing twice. The test for every cut is: *does the
shorter version mean exactly the same thing?* If yes, keep the cut. If
the shorter version loses information, tone, or emphasis you need,
keep the words.

Common wordy phrases and their concise replacements:

| Wordy | Concise | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| due to the fact that | because | same cause, one word |
| at this point in time | now | same time, one word |
| in order to | to | the *in order* adds nothing |
| a large number of | many | same quantity |
| in the event that | if | same condition |
| is able to | can | same ability |
| for the purpose of | to / for | same purpose |

Common redundancies (one word already contains the other):

- *repeat again* (repeat already means again)
- *return back* (return already means back)
- *free gift* (a gift is free by definition)
- *past history* (history is past)
- *final outcome* (an outcome is final by definition)
- *true fact* (a fact is true)

**Precision** is the other half: choose the exact word, not the vague
one. *The dog went fast* could mean ran, sprinted, bolted, dashed,
or raced — each pictures something different. *Nice, good, big,
thing, stuff, very* are warning lights: they usually mean the writer
has not yet decided what they mean. Replace a vague word with a
specific one, and check that the specific word is actually true (a
*trot* is not a *sprint*).

Register note (SL.7.6): concise is not the same as casual. A formal
paragraph — a letter to a principal, a project briefing — can be
concise and formal at once: *Because the field was flooded, practice
moved indoors.* Formal English means complete sentences, precise
words, and no slang — not extra padding.

### Worked example 1 — cutting wordiness

Draft: *Due to the fact that a large number of students were absent,
the teacher decided to postpone the test until a later date in the
future.*

1. *Due to the fact that* → *Because*.
2. *a large number of* → *many*.
3. *until a later date in the future* → *until later* (a later date
   is necessarily in the future — *in the future* is redundant).
4. Result: *Because many students were absent, the teacher postponed
   the test until later.* Word count: 24 → 13. Meaning: unchanged.
   Check each cut with the test: does anything the reader needed
   disappear? No — *many* keeps the quantity vague in exactly the
   same way *a large number of* did.

### Worked example 2 — precision, not just shortening

Draft: *The very big dog did a good thing when it went to the kid.*

Shortening alone gives: *The big dog helped the kid.* Better, but
still vague — *helped* how? Precision asks what actually happened:
*The large dog blocked the gate so the toddler could not reach the
street.* Now the reader sees the act, the reason, and the stakes.
Concision removes words; precision chooses words. A strong revision
does both, in that order: first say exactly what happened, then cut
every word that does not carry meaning.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Cut the wordiness, keeping the meaning: *In order to be able to
   finish on time, we need to start at this point in time.*
2. Find the redundancy and remove it: *Please repeat again the final
   outcome of the vote so everyone can return back with the true
   facts.*
3. Make this precise (you may invent reasonable specifics, but keep
   them plausible): *The teacher did a nice thing for the class.*
4. Error analysis: a classmate "concises" *Because the bridge was
   closed, traffic jammed for miles* to *Bridge closed, traffic
   jammed.* What was lost, and why does the shorter version fail the
   test?

## Independent practice

1. Revise each sentence to be concise with no meaning lost:
   (a) *At this point in time, we are in the process of making a
   decision about the trip.*
   (b) *In the event that it rains, the game will be cancelled due to
   the fact that the field will be wet.*
   (c) *She is able to play a large number of songs on the piano.*
2. Remove the redundancy in each: (a) *He nodded his head yes in
   agreement.* (b) *The unexpected surprise made us all jump.*
   (c) *We will combine together our ideas into one plan.*
3. Rewrite this vague sentence precisely, in one sentence: *The
   student did stuff after school that was really good.*
4. Revise this paragraph (50 words) to 40 words or fewer with no
   meaning lost: "Due to the fact that the library was closed, a large
   number of students went to the park in order to study. In the event
   that it rained, they planned to return back to the school. The
   unexpected surprise was that the park was quiet, so they finished
   their work."

## Application

Take one paragraph of your own recent writing (or the project draft's
first paragraph). Circle every wordy phrase and redundancy, replace
each vague word (*nice, good, very, thing*) with a precise one, and
count words before and after. A good revision cuts at least a fifth
with no meaning lost; if yours cut nothing, you probably wrote it
tight already — say so, and show the adult one sentence you checked
and kept deliberately.

## Exit check

Cut this sentence in half (words) without losing meaning: *Due to the
fact that she practiced every day, she was able to improve her skills
a great deal.* Write the revision and its word counts.

## Supports, extensions, misconceptions

- **Support:** keep the replacement table beside you while revising;
  cutting is easier when the concise phrase is already in front of
  you.
- **Alternate mode:** read the draft aloud and clap on every word
  that adds no meaning — the claps are your cut list.
- **Extension:** take a wordy paragraph and produce two revisions:
  one maximally concise (fewest words, same meaning) and one formal
  and concise (for a letter to a principal). Compare what each
  version keeps and drops.
- **Misconception:** shorter is not automatically better. Cutting a
  word that carries meaning (a cause, a condition, a quantity) fails
  the test. Concision serves meaning; it never outranks it.
