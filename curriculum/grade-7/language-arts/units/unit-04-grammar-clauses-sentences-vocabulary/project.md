# Project — Sentence Surgeon Portfolio

**Time:** two workshop sessions (pacing sessions 13–14) plus revision
at home if needed · **Standards:** L.7.1a–c, L.7.2a–b, L.7.3a,
L.7.4a–d, L.7.5b–c, SL.7.6

You are the surgeon. A flawed draft paragraph needs diagnosis and
repair, and then you prove the skills by writing cleanly yourself.

## Part 1 — Diagnose the flawed draft (workshop 1)

Read the draft below. Mark every fault by type: misplaced modifier
(MM), dangling modifier (DM), missing coordinate-adjective comma
(CC), spelling/confusion error (SP), wordiness/redundancy (W), vague
word (V). A sentence may hold more than one fault. List each fault
with the sentence number, the fault code, and one sentence explaining
what is wrong.

> (1) Walking home from school, the rain soaked my jacket.
> (2) I saw a dog on my way home wearing a red collar.
> (3) Due to the fact that I was tired, I went to bed early in order
> to rest.
> (4) It was a long difficult day, and I was happy to see my cozy warm
> room.
> (5) Their was a free gift on my desk, but I new it was from my
> friend, so I was very happy.

## Part 2 — Repair and rewrite (workshop 1–2)

1. Rewrite the whole paragraph with every fault repaired. Keep every
   fact; change only what the faults require (plus the wording needed
   to make repairs grammatical).
2. Your repaired paragraph must contain at least one simple, one
   compound, one complex, and one compound-complex sentence. Label
   each sentence's type in the margin (or in a note below).
3. Read your repaired paragraph aloud to the adult in formal English
   (SL.7.6): complete sentences, precise words, no slang. The adult
   notes one strength and one fix in delivery, if any.

## Part 3 — Vocabulary log (workshop 2)

Choose six words: at least two from this unit's lessons, and up to
four from your own reading. For each word, write:

- the sentence where you found it (or the lesson it came from);
- your guess and the strategy used (context, affix/root, or both);
- the verified dictionary meaning and part of speech;
- a synonym and an antonym (or "none" with a reason);
- the word's connotation (praising, neutral, or criticizing) with one
  near-synonym carrying a different connotation;
- one original sentence using the word correctly.

## Deliverables

1. Fault list for Part 1 (each fault coded and explained).
2. Repaired paragraph with sentence types labeled, plus a note on any
   place where you chose between two valid repairs (and why).
3. Six-word vocabulary log in the format above.
4. A short reflection (3–5 sentences): which fault do you most often
   make in your own writing, and what check will you run for it from
   now on?

## Rubric (16 points)

| Criterion | 4 — Strong | 3 — Solid | 2 — Partial | 0–1 — Missing/unclear |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fault diagnosis (4) | All faults found (see key: 10 faults), each coded and explained correctly | 8–9 faults, mostly correct codes/explanations | 5–7 faults, or several wrong codes | Fewer than 5, or faults invented |
| Repair quality (4) | Every fault repaired; facts kept; all four sentence types present and labeled correctly | Most repairs correct; types present with at most one label error | Several faults remain or facts changed | Paragraph largely unrepaired |
| Vocabulary log (4) | All 6 words: guess + strategy, verified meaning, part of speech, synonym/antonym, connotation contrast, correct original sentence | 5 words complete, or minor errors in 1–2 entries | 3–4 words, or several unverified meanings | Fewer than 3 words, or meanings wrong |
| Formal delivery + reflection (4) | Read aloud in formal English; reflection names a real personal fault and a workable check | Read aloud with minor slips; reflection names a fault and a check | Read aloud with frequent slang/informality, or reflection vague | No read-aloud, or no reflection |

**Scoring note for the adult:** accept any repair that fixes the
fault and keeps the facts, even if the wording differs from the
answer key's model. For Part 1, the key counts 10 faults; award full
diagnosis credit for 10 found, and do not penalize a learner for
flagging the debatable *very happy* (sentence 5) as vague — it is
counted as a fault in the key (V), but a learner who defends it as
deliberate emphasis may still earn credit if the reasoning is sound.
