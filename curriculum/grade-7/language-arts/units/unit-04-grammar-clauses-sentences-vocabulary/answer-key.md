# Answer Key — Unit 04

For the guiding adult only. Student materials never contain answers.
Accept any answer that is correct in meaning even if its wording
differs from the model below; the notes say where variation is
acceptable. Open responses were solved independently of the lessons:
where a lesson models one repair, the key accepts every repair that
fixes the fault and keeps the facts.

## Lesson 01 — Phrases, clauses, and their jobs

- **Readiness:** subject *dog*, verb *barked*.
- **Guided practice:**
  1. Phrase (no subject + verb pair); adverb job — it tells *where*
     he sat.
  2. Phrase (infinitive *to win* has no subject); noun job — it is
     the object of *wanted* (she wanted *what?* — to win).
  3. *The bird sang* — independent clause, main clause. *because it
     was spring* — dependent clause, adverb job (why it sang).
  4. Counterexample: *Covered in mud* (Worked example 1) contains the
     verb-like word *covered* but no subject doing the covering, so
     it is a phrase, not a clause. The rule fails because participles
     and infinitives look like verbs without doing a clause's work.
- **Independent practice:**
  1. (a) Dependent clause, adverb job (when). (b) Independent clause.
     (c) Phrase. (d) Dependent clause, adjective job.
  2. Appositive phrase; noun job (it renames a noun); it renames
     *My brother*.
  3. Noun job — it is the subject. Subject-and-verb test: subject
     *Running every day*, verb *builds*.
  4. Sample: adjective clause — "The book that I borrowed was
     fascinating." (clause *that I borrowed* describes *book*).
     Adverb clause — "I stayed home because it rained." (clause
     *because it rained* tells why). Accept any correct pair.
  5. *Because* subordinates the clause: it has a subject and verb
     but expresses an unfinished thought that must lean on a main
     clause.
- **Exit check:** dependent clause. Complete sentence sample: "While
  the soup simmered, Maya set the table." (Any added independent
  clause works.)

## Lesson 02 — Four sentence types

- **Readiness:** two independent clauses.
- **Guided practice:**
  1. (a) Simple. (b) Compound. (c) Complex. (d) Compound-complex.
  2. Complex (cause): "Because the dog was leashed, the cat stayed
     on the crate undisturbed." Compound: "The dog was leashed, and
     the cat stayed on the crate." The complex version changes the
     meaning: it claims the leash *caused* the cat's safety, while
     the compound version only reports two facts side by side.
  3. "Although the sun was bright, the shoppers carried baskets, and
     the flowers glowed." (Comma after the opening dependent clause;
     comma before *and* joining two independent clauses.)
- **Independent practice (samples — any true-of-picture set works):**
  1. Simple: "The vendor handed the customer carrots."
  2. Compound: "The vendor handed the customer carrots, and the
     musician played guitar."
  3. Complex: "Because the dog was leashed, it could only sniff the
     apple."
  4. Compound-complex: "Because the dog was leashed, it could only
     sniff the apple, and the cat watched the pigeon."
  5. Sample explanation: "I chose complex because the leash causes
     the limit — the sentence explains *why* the dog could only
     sniff, and a compound sentence would hide that cause."
- **Application (sample):** "When the market opened at nine, shoppers
  arrived while the musician played. The dog sniffed an apple, and
  the cat watched a pigeon." (One complex, one compound; all facts
  kept.) It should sound like a scene: the *when/while* clauses
  relate events in time instead of listing them.
- **Exit check (sample):** "While the musician played, the shoppers
  carried baskets, and the cat watched the pigeon." Independent
  clauses: *the shoppers carried baskets*; *the cat watched the
  pigeon*. Dependent clause: *While the musician played*.

## Lesson 03 — Misplaced and dangling modifiers

- **Readiness:** Maya was running.
- **Guided practice:**
  1. Misplaced (the described word, *painting*, is present but not
     adjacent). Repair: "I admired the painting hanging on the wall."
  2. Misplaced. Repair: "The teacher handed back the essays covered
     in comments to the students."
  3. Dangling (the reader is missing). Repair: "While Maya was
     reading in the library, the bell rang." (Any named reader works.)
  4. Not a repair: adding *quickly* changes nothing about who runs.
     The sentence still claims the hat was running down the street;
     the person is still missing.
- **Independent practice:**
  1. Misplaced — claims the man has two flat tires. Repair: "I
     bought a bike with two flat tires from a man."
  2. Dangling — claims the goal intended to win. Repair: "To win the
     game, the team scored the final goal in the last minute."
  3. Misplaced — claims the wallet was walking home. Repair:
     "Walking home from practice, Maya found a wallet."
  4. Misplaced — claims Sam is covered in mud. Repair: "Sam washed
     the car, which was covered in mud." (Also acceptable: "Sam
     washed the mud-covered car.")
  5. Moving a misplaced modifier works because the described word
     exists in the sentence and only needs adjacency. A dangling
     modifier's word is absent, so no move can supply it — the writer
     must add the missing person or thing.
- **Application:** Fault 1 — dangling: the hill did not ride a bike.
  Fault 2 — misplaced: the shop does not have a flat tire (the bike
  does). Repaired paragraph: "Riding my bike to school, I saw a steep
  hill. I stopped at the shop to buy a snack, although my bike had a
  flat tire." (Other repairs keeping the facts are acceptable.)
- **Exit check:** Dangling — the movie did not yawn. Repair sample:
  "Yawning widely, Lena watched the movie finally end." The yawner
  belongs as the subject right after the opening phrase.

## Lesson 04 — Coordinate adjectives and spelling

- **Readiness:** (a) needs the comma; (b) does not.
- **Guided practice:**
  1. *a cold winter morning* — no comma: *winter* names the kind of
     morning (cumulative; the *and*-test fails — *a cold and winter
     morning* sounds wrong). *a cold, rainy morning* — comma: both
     tests pass (*a cold and rainy morning*; *a rainy, cold
     morning*).
  2. "It was a long, difficult hike up the steep, rocky trail."
     (Both pairs pass both tests.)
  3. "You're going to love their new house; it's bigger than yours."
     (*Your* → *You're*; *there* → *their*; *its* → *it's*;
     *then* → *than*. The semicolon avoids a comma splice.)
  4. No comma: *brick* names the material, and the tests fail (*a red
     and brick house* sounds wrong; *a brick red house* means a
     shade of red, not a red house made of brick).
- **Independent practice:**
  1. (a) *a happy, energetic puppy* — comma (both tests pass).
     (b) *a small wooden box* — no comma (*wooden* = material,
     cumulative). (c) *the deep, dark forest* — comma (both tests
     pass). (d) *a deep, dark secret* — comma (both tests pass).
  2. Corrections (6): *Me and Jake* → *Jake and I*; *libary* →
     *library*; *new* → *knew*; *are notes* → *our notes*;
     *threw* → *through*; *beleived* → *believed*. *Their teacher*
     is correct and stays. Corrected paragraph: "Jake and I went to
     the library to study. We knew the test was on Friday, so we
     reviewed our notes until we were through. Their teacher said
     practice makes perfect, and we believed her."
  3. Samples: coordinate — "She wore a bright, cheerful dress."
     Cumulative — "She carried a heavy leather bag." (Learner's own
     pair with one test shown is acceptable.)
  4. Learner's own confusion list (3 pairs with one correct sentence
     per word).
- **Application:** "The tired, hungry hikers reached a cozy, warm
  cabin, but they realized there was no firewood, so they ate their
  sandwiches cold." Changes: comma after *tired*; comma after
  *cozy*; *realised* → *realized*; *their was* → *there was*;
  *there sandwiches* → *their sandwiches*.
- **Exit check:** "She gave a clear, confident answer." *And*-test:
  *a clear and confident answer* works, so the comma belongs.

## Lesson 05 — Precise, concise language

- **Readiness:** (b) is stronger — same meaning, fewer words.
- **Guided practice:**
  1. "To finish on time, we need to start now."
  2. "Please repeat the outcome of the vote so everyone can return
     with the facts." (Removed: *again*, *final*, *back*, *true*.)
  3. Sample: "The teacher stayed after school to help Maya understand
     fractions." (Any plausible specifics work.)
  4. Lost: the cause relationship (the bridge closure *caused* the
     jam) and the extent (*for miles*), plus grammatical completeness
     (two fragments). The shorter version fails the test because it
     means less than the original.
- **Independent practice:**
  1. (a) "Now we are deciding about the trip." (b) "If it rains, the
     game will be cancelled because the field will be wet."
     (c) "She can play many songs on the piano."
  2. (a) "He nodded in agreement." (b) "The surprise made us all
     jump." (c) "We will combine our ideas into one plan."
  3. Sample: "After school, Maya practiced violin for an hour and then
     helped her brother with his homework." (Any precise sentence
     works.)
  4. Model revision (50 → 33 words): "Because the library was closed,
     many students went to the park to study. If it rained, they
     planned to go back to school. Surprisingly, the park was quiet,
     so they finished their work." (Meets "40 words or fewer"; any
     revision ≤40 words keeping the meaning is acceptable.)
- **Exit check:** "Because she practiced daily, she improved greatly."
  (19 words → 7 words; less than half, same meaning.)

## Lesson 06 — Vocabulary strategies

- **Readiness:** *trans-* across + *port* carry → carry across.
- **Guided practice:**
  1. *Treacherous* = dangerous/unsafe. Clue kind: cause (drivers
     slowed *because* the road was treacherous).
  2. *Photograph*: *photo-* light + *graph* write → a picture drawn
     by light; noun (also a verb). *Telephone*: *tele-* far + *phone*
     sound → sound from far; noun (also a verb). Verify both in a
     dictionary during the lesson.
  3. Relationship: creator : creation. *Author : book :: composer :
     song.*
  4. *Persistent* — it praises steady effort. *Stubborn* connotes
     refusing to change even when wrong; *obstinate* connotes
     unreasonable refusal.
- **Independent practice:**
  1. *Benevolent*: *bene-* good + *vol* wish → wishing good. Verified:
     adjective, kind and generous (e.g., *a benevolent donor*).
  2. *Reluctant* = unwilling/hesitant. Clue kind: cause (she waited
     *because* she was reluctant).
  3. (a) Degree of intensity (hot → scalding is hotter; cold →
     *freezing* is colder). (b) Tool : user (a painter uses a brush;
     a writer uses a *pen*).
  4. Most praising → most criticizing: *determined, persistent,
     stubborn, obstinate*. (Either order within the praising pair is
     acceptable with reasoning.) Sample sentence: "The determined
     student revised her essay three times."
  5. Learner's own word-log entry (guess + strategy, verified
     meaning, new sentence).
- **Exit check:** *Malfunction*: *mal-* bad + *function* → works
  badly/breaks down. Verify in a dictionary (verb or noun: a failure
  to work correctly).

3. "The old lighthouse stood on the cliff, warning ships." (One

4. "When the bell rang, Maya closed her notebook."

## Practice C — Modifier repair clinic

1. Dangling — claims the phone jogged. Repair: "While I was jogging
   in the park, my phone fell out of my pocket."
2. Misplaced — claims the horse wears the cowboy hat. Repair: "I saw
   a man wearing a cowboy hat on a horse."
3. Dangling — claims the dishes finished dinner. Repair: "After
   finishing dinner, Leo washed the dishes."

   waiter served dinner on silver trays to the guests."

   read the book, the movie seemed disappointing to her." (Also
   acceptable: "After reading the book, Lena found the movie
   disappointing.")

   a dog with a broken leg in the alley."
7. Dangling — claims the traffic jam drove to work. Repair: "Driving
   to work, Priya was delayed by the traffic jam."

   bought a phone with a cracked screen from a store."

## Practice D — Editing pass

9 changes: commas — *tired, hungry*; *cozy, warm*; *bright,
cheerful*; *long, steep*; *happy, tiring*. Spelling/confusions —
*Their was* → *There was*; *there sandwiches* → *their sandwiches*;
*there bags* → *their bags*; *they new* → *they knew*. *small
wooden table* takes no comma (wooden = material, cumulative) and is
the distractor. Corrected paragraph: "The tired, hungry hikers
reached a cozy, warm cabin just as the sun set. There was a small
wooden table inside, but there was no firewood, so they ate their
sandwiches cold. The next morning, a bright, cheerful sun woke them,
and they packed their bags for the long, steep trail home. It was a
happy, tiring day, but they knew they would return."

## Practice F — Word-study lab (sample entries)

1. *transport* — Guess: carry across (*trans-* across + *port*
   carry). Verified: verb (also noun), to carry goods or people from
   one place to another. Synonym: *haul*. Antonym: no exact opposite;
   nearest opposite idea is *keep in place*. Sentence sample: "Trains
   transport coal to the port."
2. *benevolent* — Guess: wishing good (*bene-* good + *vol* wish).
   Verified: adjective, kind and generous. Synonym: *kindhearted*.
   Antonym: *cruel* (or *malevolent*). Sentence sample: "The
   benevolent neighbor shared vegetables with everyone."
3. *predict* — Guess: say beforehand (*pre-* before + *dict* say).
   Verified: verb, to say what will happen before it happens.
   Synonym: *forecast*. Antonym: no exact opposite; nearest opposite
   idea is *report after the fact*. Sentence sample: "Forecasters
   predict rain by reading pressure and wind."
4. *malfunction* — Guess: work badly (*mal-* bad + *function*).
   Verified: verb or noun, to fail to work correctly. Synonym: *break
   down*. Antonym idea: *work properly*. Sentence sample: "The
   printer's malfunction delayed the whole class."
5. *photograph* — Guess: picture drawn by light (*photo-* light +
   *graph* write). Verified: noun (also verb), a picture made with a
   camera. Synonym: *picture*. Antonym: none — it names a thing, not
   a point on a scale. Sentence sample: "She took a photograph of the
   sunrise."
6. *persistent* — Guess: keeps trying (context: finished despite the
   heat). Verified: adjective, continuing firmly despite difficulty.
   Synonym: *determined*. Antonym idea: *giving up easily*.
   Connotation: praising; opposite-feeling family word: *stubborn*
   (criticizing).

**Part 1 fault list (10 faults):**

1. (1) DM — the rain did not walk home from school.
2. (2) MM — the way home does not wear a red collar (the dog does).
3. (3) W — *due to the fact that* (→ *because*).
4. (3) W — *in order to* (→ *to*).
5. (4) CC — missing comma: *long, difficult*.
6. (4) CC — missing comma: *cozy, warm*.
7. (5) SP — *Their* → *There*.
8. (5) SP — *new* → *knew*.
9. (5) W (redundancy) — *free gift* (a gift is free by definition).
10. (5) V — *very happy* (vague; a precise feeling word is better).

**Part 2 model repair (full paragraph, sentence types labeled):**

"Walking home from school, I got soaked by the rain. (Simple — the
walker, *I*, sits right after the opening phrase, so nothing dangles.)
On my way home, I saw a dog wearing a red collar. (Simple.) Because I
was tired, I went to bed early to rest. (Complex.) It was a long,
difficult day, and I was glad to see my cozy, warm room. (Compound.)
Although there was a gift on my desk, I knew it was from my friend,
and I felt grateful. (Compound-complex.)"

All facts are kept: rain on the walk home; dog with red collar; tired
→ early bed; long difficult day; cozy warm room; gift on desk from
friend; happiness (now precise: *glad, grateful*). The paragraph
contains all four sentence types, satisfying the requirement.
- Accept any repair set that fixes all 10 faults, keeps the facts,
  and contains all four types labeled correctly.
