# Answer Key — Unit 05

For the guiding adult only. Student materials never contain these
answers. Open responses accept any answer that meets the stated
criteria; samples below show the standard, not the only correct
wording. Where a sample is given, the adult checks the learner's
answer against the criteria in bold, not against the sample's exact
words.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. Where: "The rain started the minute I left the bakery" (outside a
   bakery, rainy evening). Who tells it: "My name is June. I am
   thirteen." (first person — June herself). What is at stake: "The
   cake was for my grandmother's seventieth birthday, and the bus home
   was twenty minutes late." (Accept also: "the box was getting damp
   at the corners.")
2. First person — June is the narrator and the main character. The
   reader cannot know what is happening at June's grandmother's house,
   what her grandmother is thinking, or what Mrs. Alvarez is thinking
   — only what June sees, hears, and feels.
3. The five beats: (1) June leaves the bakery with the cake and the
   rain starts; (2) June explains the three weeks of saving and the
   missed bus; (3) June meets Mrs. Alvarez at the shelter; (4) Mrs.
   Alvarez tells her own cake story; (5) the bus comes, June rides
   home, and she reflects. **Beat 2 is the most removable** — cut it
   and the plot still works, though the stakes drop (the reader would
   not know what the cake cost). **Beat 4 is load-bearing** — remove
   it and the ending reflection has nothing to answer, because
   "Because I carried it" is where the story's meaning first appears.
4. The flashback lands after June's worry is established, so it
   answers a question the reader is already asking ("why does this
   cake matter so much?"). At the very beginning it would be a
   stranger's anecdote with nothing to answer.

**Independent practice**

1. Sample orientation (criteria in bold: **where/when, narrator,
   stakes**): "The library closed at six, and I was still inside when
   the lights went out. I am Rosa, I am twelve, and I had promised my
   little sister I would bring home the book she needed for school.
   The front door was locked, my phone was dead, and the book was the
   only thing standing between her and a zero." Any three sentences
   meeting the three checks pass.
2. Beats and what changes: (1) June leaves the bakery, rain starts —
   the cake is in danger; (2) saving and missed bus — the stakes rise,
   because the cake cost three weeks and the bus is already late; (3)
   meeting Mrs. Alvarez — the waiting becomes a conversation and June
   is no longer alone; (4) Mrs. Alvarez's story — June hears the idea
   that carrying is the gift; (5) bus arrives and ride home — the
   problem resolves and June understands what the trip meant.
3. Sample rewrite of "There was a boy. He was at school. Something
   happened.": "The fire alarm went off during third-period math,
   which was the worst possible minute, because I was holding the
   class hamster and the teacher had just told us not to move."
   **Where/when** (school, third-period math), **narrator** (first
   person), **stakes** (the hamster, the order not to move).
4. The two missing beats: (a) the saving and the missed bus — without
   them the reader does not know why the cake matters so much or why
   June is late; (b) Mrs. Alvarez's cake story — without it the ending
   reflection has no source and the theme never arrives. (The summary
   also drops the meeting itself, which is the same beat as (b)'s
   setup — accept that as the second missing beat with the same
   reasoning.)

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice**

1. Two sensory details (any two, with the feeling shown, not named):
   smell/touch — "the umbrella smelled like rain and oranges" shows
   the shelter is close and sheltering, and that Mrs. Alvarez is a
   real person with groceries; sight — "the rain ran down the windows
   in rivers" shows the rain is heavy and June is enclosed and
   waiting; touch — "the box was getting damp at the corners" shows
   the danger to the cake is real and growing.
2. "Crawled" makes the bus feel exhausted and late — it increases the
   worry, because a crawling bus might not come at all. "Eased" makes
   it feel careful and gentle — the driver is being careful in the
   rain. Both change the feeling of the sentence without changing the
   facts.
3. Sample (detail not in the story's words — the yellow sweater):
   "Under her raincoat, June's yellow sweater was the only bright
   thing at the whole gray stop, and she kept pulling the coat closed
   over it, as if the color were something to protect too." Any
   sentence that takes a visible detail and attaches a feeling to it
   without a feeling word passes.
4. Three feeling words say less because they name the feeling three
   times without giving any evidence — the reader is still only told.
   Fix (one action, no feeling word): "June pressed the box against
   her chest with both arms and did not let go, even when her fingers
   went numb."

**Independent practice**

1. a. "Theo was embarrassed." → "Theo stared at his shoes, and his
   ears went red all the way to the tips." b. "The kitchen was warm
   and welcoming." → "The kitchen smelled like baking bread, and the
   oven light made the whole room glow gold." c. "Mrs. Alvarez was
   proud of June." → "Mrs. Alvarez nodded like June had just said the
   most sensible thing she had heard all day." d. "The test was hard."
   → "By question four, Mara had erased her answer three times, and
   the eraser had worn down to the metal." **Check: no feeling word
   from the original appears.**
2. Taste: "The rain on June's lips tasted like cold metal." Sound:
   "Every few seconds a car hissed past, throwing up a sheet of spray
   that rattled the shelter roof." Any two sentences for the chosen
   sense pass if they show rather than tell.
3. Sample (three showing sentences, no feeling words): "The
   streetlights came on one by one, and their light pooled in the
   puddles like spilled honey. Rain drummed on the shelter roof and
   ran off the edge in a thin curtain. At the end of the street, where
   the bus should have been, there was only wet asphalt and the
   blinking of a traffic light." **Check with the cover test: no
   "sad," "lonely," "worried," or "happy" appears.**
4. a. "June went to the shelter." → "June darted to the shelter." The
   precise verb shows hurry and escape from the rain. b. "She held the
   box." → "She cradled the box." The precise verb shows care and
   fragility — the box is precious, not just carried. c. "The rain
   came down." → "The rain hammered down." The precise verb adds
   force and sound. Any precise verb passes if the learner can say
   what changes.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. Jobs per line (one line can do two jobs): "You look like a drowned
   cat" — reveals character (Mrs. Alvarez jokes; she is warm and
   direct) and relationship (immediate ease). "I feel like one" —
   reveals relationship (June jokes back, so she feels safe) and
   character (June has humor under worry). "The bus is twenty minutes
   late." — moves the story (states the problem). "Then it will come
   in five, because buses are like that." — reveals character (calm,
   experienced, reassuring) and moves the story (reassurance). "Come
   under. Your cake is getting wet." — reveals character (practical
   kindness) and moves the story (June steps under). "It's for my
   grandmother. Seventy today. I saved for three weeks." — reveals
   character (June's pride and care) and moves the story (prompts Mrs.
   Alvarez's story). "My mother turned seventy in the rain, too." —
   reveals character (Mrs. Alvarez's history) and moves the story (the
   theme arrives). "Because I carried it." — reveals character and
   moves the story (the line the ending answers). "Go. She is
   waiting." — moves the story (ends the waiting, starts the ride).
2. "You will miss your bus," he said, twice. "I know," I said, twice.
   The repetition and the short answers show ease and mutual
   understanding: Theo worries about June, and June does not need to
   explain.
3. Fixed exchange:
   June said, "The cake is getting wet."
   "I know," Mrs. Alvarez said. "Come under the umbrella."
   Fixes: capital J in June; comma inside the closing quote after
   "wet" (not a period outside it); capital M in Mrs. Alvarez;
   comma inside the closing quote after "know"; capital C in "Come"
   (new sentence after "said."); new paragraph when the speaker
   changes.
4. Sample beat: "I'm fine." Dev stared at his shoes and kicked at a
   crack in the gym floor. The beat shows the sadness (avoiding eyes,
   kicking the floor) without naming it. Any action that shows the
   feeling passes.

**Independent practice**

1. Sample six-line exchange (rules: new paragraph per speaker; at
   least one beat; only "said"/"asked" tags; moves the story):
   "Did you pass?" June asked.
   "Barely," Theo said. "Fractions still hate me, but I got the quiz back."
   "And the cake?" Theo asked. "Did it survive the rain?"
   June laughed. "The frosting is a little wet on one side. My grandmother said it was the best cake she ever had."
   "Because it was lopsided?" Theo asked.
   "Because I carried it," June said.
   **Check:** we learn Theo passed (barely) and the cake survived;
   "June laughed." is the beat.
2. Punctuated exchange:
   "Are you sure the cake is all right?" June asked.
   "It is fine," Mrs. Alvarez said. "The frosting is only a little wet."
3. Sample: "Because I carried it," Mrs. Alvarez said. It reveals that
   she believes effort and showing up matter more than perfection —
   and the narrator never states that directly; her own words and her
   own story prove it, so the reader concludes it. Any line with a
   two-sentence explanation of what it reveals passes.
4. Story dialogue is selected and shaped, not transcribed: real talk
   wanders and repeats, while every line in a story must do a job.
   Evidence: June and Theo's whole afternoon of homework help is
   compressed into "You will miss your bus" / "I know" — two lines do
   the work of a real five-minute conversation.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**

1. Scene: the shelter conversation — dialogue, senses, moment by
   moment; the writer chose scene because that is where the story
   changes (June meets Mrs. Alvarez and hears the theme). Summary:
   "I had spent three weeks saving for the cake" — three weeks in one
   sentence; the writer chose summary because the saving matters for
   stakes but does not need to be lived through.
2. Three transitions: "the minute I left the bakery" (time/setting,
   forward); "I had missed the earlier bus because" (flashback and
   cause); "On the bus" (setting shift, forward). Accept also "Then
   it will come in five" (sequence) or "The bus turned the corner
   then" (sequence).
3. Sample scene version: "On Monday I put two dollars in the jar. On
   Tuesday I skipped the pizza line and put the money in the jar."
   Better without them because saving is stakes, not story: the scene
   would slow the opening to a halt before the cake is even in danger.
4. Two craft problems: **speed** — everything is told at the same flat
   speed (all summary, no scene); **steering** — no transitions, so
   the reader cannot tell how much time passed or where the scene
   changes. Fix (with transitions and a scene moment): "June waited
   twenty minutes in the rain, holding the box tighter every time a
   car passed. Then the bus turned the corner, slow through the
   puddles, and June stepped forward before the doors even opened."

**Independent practice**

1. Scene is good for moments that change the character or the story —
   the reader lives through them. Summary is good for stretches
   between those moments — the reader only needs to know they
   happened. (Check the learner can label their own two versions
   correctly.)
2. Scene version: "'Dev,' the coach called. Dev stood up so fast the
   ball slipped from his hands and rolled to the wall. Inside, the gym
   smelled like rubber and sweat, and the hoop looked a mile high. He
   missed. He missed again. Then — the ball went up, hung, and dropped
   through clean." Summary version: "Dev waited outside the gym, got
   called in, missed twice, and made his third shot." (One sentence.)
3. Sample transitions (a different family for each gap): "Just then,
   the rain started." (time passing) "Earlier, June had missed the
   earlier bus." (flashback) "Then the bus turned the corner."
   (sequence) "On the bus, June sat down with the box on her knees."
   (setting shift) Other valid answers accepted if the four families
   differ.
4. Speed-up: the three weeks of saving in one sentence — the reader
   gains the stakes without the boredom. Slow-down: the shelter
   conversation — the reader gains the theme and the relationship,
   moment by moment. Any clearly identified pair with a reason passes.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. First idea: "I thought the gift was the cake." New understanding:
   "Showing up is the gift. The cake is just how you carry it."
2. It is a reflection, not a moral, because it follows from *these*
   events: June carried the cake through rain, late, worried the whole
   way. It names that specific experience; it does not give a general
   rule that could apply to any story.
3. The ending echoes "Because I carried it" (Mrs. Alvarez). The echo
   makes the ending feel finished because the story returns to the
   line where its meaning first appeared — the ending answers the
   middle.
4. Two problems: (a) June was late because she helped Theo — lateness
   was not carelessness, so "be on time" misreads the story; (b) it
   is a general rule (a moral), not a reflection on these events — it
   does not follow from carrying the cake.

**Independent practice**

1. Sample (2–3 sentences, no "always"/"never"): "Mara put her violin
   down and looked at her hands. They were still shaking — but they
   had played the second piece all the way through, and that was the
   part she would remember. The shaking had not gone away; it had just
   stopped being the whole story."
2. (a) stops: "Mara put her violin away and went home. It was a good
   day." The events end, but nothing is reflected on. (b) reflects:
   "The shaking had not gone away — but it had stopped being the whole
   story." It names what changed (the shaking's meaning) and follows
   from the events (shook, then played through).
3. Sample revision of (a) into a reflection (two sentences, no new
   event): "Mara put her violin away, her hands still shaking a
   little. But the second piece had gone all the way through without a
   mistake — and that was the part she would carry home, not the
   shaking."
4. Sample: "June understands that the cake mattered not just because
   it was a gift, but because she worked and worried to bring it home
   — the effort of carrying it was part of the gift." Any sentence
   naming a change in understanding that depends on the story's events
   passes.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice**

1. The stumble/boredom place is the string of "was" sentences —
   especially "She was nice." It tells the reader the draft has
   stopped showing; the reader is being told facts instead of entering
   a scene.
2. The **craft** question: it is telling instead of showing, and the
   dialogue/description is not doing its job.
3. The telling sentence is "It was for her grandmother's seventieth
   birthday, and the bus home was twenty minutes late." **Leave it.**
   Orientation facts (who, what, when) are the right place for
   telling — showing everything would bury the reader. The test is
   whether the telling carries a feeling that should have been shown;
   this sentence carries facts, so it stays.
4. Sample log entry: Move: Cut the sentence "It was raining and she
   was sad." | Why: It tells the weather and the feeling instead of
   showing them, and the next sentences (the damp box, the late bus)
   already carry both facts.

**Independent practice**

1. Sample revision log (for the Lesson 03 applied task — June arrives
   home; the learner's own piece will differ, so check the *moves and
   whys*, not the wording): Move: Added the grandmother's face at the
   door | Why: the scene had no second character to react, so the
   arrival fell flat. Move: Cut "June was happy" | Why: telling;
   replaced with "June laughed and set the box down gently." Move:
   Slowed the door-opening into a scene | Why: the arrival is the
   moment that matters, and the draft rushed past it.
2. Record both reader answers exactly as given, e.g.: "I got lost
   when the scene changed from the bus to the house." / "I could
   picture the cake box and the rain." Any two honest answers pass;
   the adult checks they were actually asked and recorded.
3. Sample move from the reader's answers: Move: Added the transition
   "On the bus" before the ride and "At the door" before the arrival
   | Why: the reader got lost at the scene change, so the steering was
   missing.
4. Sample editing fixes (two at most): "Fixed the comma in 'Come
   under,' she said." / "Capitalized the first word after a beat."
   Check: the learner lists real fixes from their own piece, not
   invented ones.

## Practice sessions

### Session A

1. Sample orientation (criteria: **where/when, narrator, stakes**):
   "The last train had already left, and the station was empty except
   for me and a wet brown dog with no collar. I am Sam, I am twelve,
   and I had promised my mother I would be home by six. The dog
   followed me to the gate, and I knew that if I left it there, it
   would spend the night alone in the rain." Any three sentences
   meeting the three checks pass; "one day" must not appear.
2. Chronological order: (4) June saves for three weeks and misses the
   earlier bus → (2) June leaves the bakery in the rain → (5) June
   meets Mrs. Alvarez → (3) Mrs. Alvarez tells her story → (1) June
   rides the bus home and reflects. Note: the story *tells* them in
   the order 2 → 4 → 5 → 3 → 1, because the saving is a flashback.
3. Backstory beats: saving for three weeks, and missing the earlier
   bus. Signals: "I had spent…" and "I had missed…" — the past
   perfect "had" marks a move backward in time.
4. Sample rewrite: "The rain had just started outside the bakery on
   Alder Street, and I was holding my grandmother's birthday cake with
   both hands. I am June, I am thirteen, and the bus home is twenty
   minutes late. If the cake gets wet, the birthday I spent three
   weeks saving for will arrive ruined." **Checks: where/when,
   narrator, stakes.**
5. First person (June). The reader cannot know what her grandmother is
   doing or thinking at home, what the bus driver noticed, or what
   Mrs. Alvarez is thinking — only what June sees, hears, and feels.
6. Sample third person: "June left the bakery with the cake under her
   arm, and the rain started at once. She was thirteen, the cake was
   for her grandmother's seventieth birthday, and the bus was twenty
   minutes late." What changes: the reader is further from June — we
   watch her instead of worrying with her, and her feelings must be
   shown from outside (actions) rather than told from inside.

### Session B

1. a. "The principal was angry." → "The principal set the paper down
   without a sound, folded his hands, and looked at us for a long
   time before he spoke." b. "The basement was spooky." → "The
   basement light buzzed and went out, and the dark smelled like wet
   concrete." c. "Dad was proud." → "Dad read the letter twice, then
   folded it carefully and put it in his wallet, on top of his
   driver's license." d. "The hike was exhausting." → "By the last
   mile, Mara was counting her steps out loud and stopping at every
   flat rock." **Check: no feeling word from the original appears.**
2. "Ana set her bag down, missed the chair, and sat on the floor
   instead." shows; "Ana was exhausted." tells. Cover "exhausted" and
   nothing remains; cover any one word in the second sentence and the
   exhaustion is still visible in the missed chair and the floor.
3. Touch: "The seat was cold and damp through June's jeans." Sound:
   "The engine hummed under her feet, and the driver called each stop
   in a tired, practiced voice." Any two showing sentences for touch
   and sound pass, if they stay inside what the story allows (a bus,
   rain, a cake box).
4. a. "June went to the shelter." → "June darted to the shelter." The
   precise verb shows hurry and escape from the rain. b. "The bus
   went down the street." → "The bus crawled down the street." The
   precise verb shows slowness and the driver's care in the rain. c.
   "The rain went on all evening." → "The rain hammered on all
   evening." The precise verb adds force and sound.
5. Repeating feeling words does not help because repetition adds
   emphasis, not evidence — the reader is still only told. Fix (one
   showing sentence): "At the top of the stairs, my hands shook so
   hard I had to set the flashlight down before I could take another
   step."
6. Scene (three sentences, showing only): "June shifted the bag
   against her chest so the rain could not reach the box. The corners
   were already damp, and she could feel the cardboard softening under
   her fingers. She held it tighter and stepped under the shelter
   roof." Summary (one sentence): "June protected the cake from the
   rain while she waited for the bus."

### Session C

1. Fixed exchange:
   "Are you coming to the recital?" Mara asked.
   "I wouldn't miss it," her mother said. "I'll be in the front row."
   Fixes: capital A in "Are"; comma inside the closing quote after
   "recital"; capital M in "Mara"; capital I in "I wouldn't"; comma
   inside the closing quote after "it"; capital I in "I'll"; new
   paragraph when the speaker changes.
2. Sample six-line exchange (rules: new paragraph per speaker; at
   least one beat; only "said"/"asked" tags; moves the story):
   "Did you pass?" June asked.
   "Barely," Theo said. "Fractions still hate me, but I got the quiz back."
   "And the cake?" Theo asked. "Did it survive the rain?"
   June laughed. "The frosting is a little wet on one side. My grandmother said it was the best cake she ever had."
   "Because it was lopsided?" Theo asked.
   "Because I carried it," June said.
   **Check:** we learn Theo passed (barely) and the cake survived;
   "June laughed." is the beat.
3. (b) best reveals character: "Then it will come in five," Mrs.
   Alvarez said, "because buses are like that." It reveals her calm,
   experience, and reassurance — she knows buses, she is not worried,
   and she comforts June by stating a fact rather than offering empty
   comfort. (a) only states a fact; (c) only agrees.
4. Sample beat: "I'm fine." Dev looked down at his shoes and kicked
   at a crack in the gym floor. The beat shows the sadness without
   naming it.
5. Sample (two lines revealing the relationship): "Again," the coach
   said. "Feet set, Dev. You know this." / "I know, Coach," Dev said,
   and set his feet the way he had a hundred times before. The coach
   uses Dev's name and expects him to know the drill; Dev answers with
   respect and familiarity — a coach who knows his player, and a
   player who trusts his coach.
6. Sample: friends — "You coming to the game or what?" / "Obviously.
   Save me a seat, yeah?" Strangers — "Excuse me — is this seat
   taken?" / "No. Go ahead." What changes: **both** — the words
   (contractions, shared knowledge, directness) and the beats
   (friends: casual actions; strangers: polite distance).

### Session D

1. a. **Summary** — "practiced every morning for a month" compresses
   a month into one clause. b. **Scene** — quoted speech, moment by
   moment, senses (the bow rattling). c. **Summary** — "Three weeks
   later" jumps time in one sentence. d. **Scene** — slow,
   moment-by-moment action (opened slowly, read the first line twice).
2. Compressed: "Dev waited outside the gym, was called in, missed
   three shots, made the fourth, and walked out smiling." (One
   sentence.)
3. Sample scene (three sentences): "Mara stood in the kitchen for a
   whole minute with the envelope in her hands, turning it over and
   over. She slid one finger under the flap and opened it slowly, one
   corner at a time. The first line said she had been accepted, and
   she read it twice before she believed it."
4. Sample transitions (a different family for each gap): "Just then,
   the rain started." (time passing) "Earlier, June had missed the
   earlier bus." (flashback) "Then the bus turned the corner."
   (sequence) "On the bus, June sat down with the box on her knees."
   (setting shift) Other valid answers accepted if the four families
   differ.
5. Two craft problems: **speed** — everything is told at the same flat
   speed (all summary, no scene); **steering** — no transitions, so
   the reader cannot tell how much time passed or where the scene
   changes. Fix: "June waited twenty minutes in the rain, holding the
   box tighter every time a car passed. Then the bus turned the
   corner, slow through the puddles, and June stepped forward before
   the doors even opened."
6. Sample framing sentences: before — "The next morning at school,
   June found Theo by his locker." After — "By lunch, the whole story
   had been told twice, and Theo had promised to come for cake next
   time." The learner's own sentences will differ; accept any two
   summary sentences that frame the scene, underlined.

### Session E

1. (a) **Sudden stop** — the events end, but nothing is reflected on.
   (b) **Moral** — a general rule ("never give up on your dreams")
   that does not follow from these events. (c) **Reflection** — it
   follows from the events (three misses, one make) and says what Dev
   understands now that he did not understand while waiting outside.
2. Sample (two sentences, no "always"/"never"): "Sam stood in the
   yard a long time, looking at the tree where his rocket had landed.
   It had flown straight up, just like he had built it to, and that
   was the part he would remember — not the tree."
3. Sample revision of the moral into a reflection (two sentences, no
   new event): "Mara put her violin away. The shaking had not gone
   away, but the second piece had gone through without a mistake — and
   she understood, finally, that a shaky start and a good finish could
   both be true."
4. The ending echoes "Because I carried it" (Mrs. Alvarez). The
   story's meaning first appears in that line, and June's reflection
   — "The cake is just how you carry it" — answers it.
5. (b) follows from the events: Lena lost, then walked the course and
   learned the hill that beat her — the ending names what she did and
   what she learned from it. (a) is a general moral ("winning is not
   everything") that could be said about any loss.
6. Answers vary. Check: the stronger ending is the one that follows
   from *this* story's events and names something only this story
   could teach — not the one with the bigger feeling or the nicer
   sentence.

### Session F

1. Sample revision moves (any three moves with a why pass): Move: Cut
   "It was hot and he was bored." | Why: It tells the weather and the
   feeling; the kite and the bad wind already show a hot, still
   afternoon. Move: Replaced "He was sad." with "Tom let the string
   out, watched the kite drop, and wound it back in without a word."
   | Why: Showing instead of telling. Move: Replaced "A girl was
   there too. She was nice." with "'Need a hand?' the girl asked,
   holding out a spare kite." | Why: Dialogue reveals character and
   moves the story; "nice" is telling.
2. Fixed exchange:
   "Did you bring the kite?" Tom asked.
   "Yes," the girl said, "but the wind is wrong for it."
   Errors: missing comma inside the closing quote after "kite";
   missing comma inside the closing quote after "Yes".
3. "I got lost when the scene changed" → **audience** (can a new
   reader follow it?). Move: add a transition at the scene change
   ("On the bus," "At the park gate"). "I could picture the dog" →
   **craft** (description is working). Move: none needed — protect
   that description in revision, and use the same sensory technique
   in the weaker scene.
4. Correct order: (1) check that the ending reflects [purpose]; (2)
   ask whether a new reader can follow the sequence [audience]; (3)
   cut a scene that does not belong [craft]; (4) fix the spelling
   [conventions].
5. Sample log entry: Move: Replaced "He was very happy" with "He
   laughed and spun the chair around." | Why: The action shows the
   feeling instead of naming it (craft — showing vs. telling).
6. Sample: slower — "Leo's hands curled into fists. He kicked the
   locker once, and the sound went down the hallway, and he kicked it
   again, harder, and stood there breathing hard, staring at the
   dent." Faster — "Leo kicked the locker twice and walked away."
   Either choice passes with a reason: slower suits a moment that
   changes something; faster suits a passing beat.

## Week-04 quiz

## Project scoring notes

Score with the rubric in [project.md](project.md) — one criterion at
a time, not a single overall impression. Check the word count of the
final draft (600–900 words); a narrative under 600 words is unfinished
and scores Developing on orientation/sequence until it is expanded.
Check the core scene is 150–250 words with sensory detail in at least
three places and one dialogue exchange, and that the revision log has
three or more moves, each with a why. A polished story with no log is
not finished work: return it with the checklist, not with a grade. The
extension (a scene from another character's point of view) is optional
and is not scored.
