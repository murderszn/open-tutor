## Part 1 — Read and analyze (W.7.3.a, W.7.3.b, W.7.3.d)

# The Tryout

Dev had been outside the gym for twenty minutes, bouncing the ball
against the wall and catching it, bouncing it and catching it, because
stopping would mean thinking. Tryouts were for the seventh-grade team,
and Dev was twelve, and the gym door was the color of a dentist's
office.

"You're going to wear a hole in that ball," said Marcus, who was sitting
on the bench with his shoes already tied, double-knotted, like a person
who had done this before.

"I'm fine," Dev said. His voice came out higher than he wanted it to.

The door opened. "Devon Carter," the coach called. "You're up."

Inside, the gym was bigger than it looked from the hallway, and the hoop
was higher than it looked from the bleachers. Dev missed his first shot.
He missed his second. The ball hit the rim the third time and spun off,
and somewhere behind him, somebody laughed — or maybe it was just a shoe
squeaking, and Dev chose to believe it was a shoe.

"Again," the coach said.

Dev took a breath. He bent his knees the way his older brother had shown
him, the way that felt wrong until it felt right. He shot. The ball went
up, hung for a second at the top of its arc, and dropped through the net
without touching the rim.

"Good," the coach said. "Next."

Walking home, Dev thought about the fourth shot. It had not erased the
first three. But it had proved that the first three were not the whole
story.

**Questions:**

1. State the context and point of view of "The Tryout": where, when,
   and who tells the story.
2. Name one showing detail from the passage and say what it shows
   about Dev — without naming the feeling in your answer.
3. What does Marcus's line "You're going to wear a hole in that ball"
   reveal about Marcus, and about his relationship with Dev? Two
   sentences.
4. How does the writer slow down for the fourth shot, and speed up
   through the walk home? Name one technique for each speed.

## Part 3 — Plan and write (W.7.3.a, W.7.3.b, W.7.3.c, W.7.3.e)

Write a story of your own about a character who has to deliver
something important in bad weather. Do not copy "The Cake" — invent
your own character, object, and weather.

9. Write a three-sentence orientation: sentence 1 names where and
   when, sentence 2 introduces the narrator or a character, sentence
   3 names what is at stake.
10. List four beats of your story's event sequence, in order, with one
    sentence for each beat saying what changes.
11. Write a four-line dialogue exchange for your story, with correct
    punctuation, a new paragraph per speaker, and at least one beat.
12. Write a two-sentence reflective conclusion for your story. It must
    follow from your events and say something the narrator could not
    have said at the start.
