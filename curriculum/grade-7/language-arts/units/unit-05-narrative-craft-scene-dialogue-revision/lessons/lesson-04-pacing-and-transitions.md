# Lesson 04 — Pacing and Transitions: Slowing Down and Speeding Up

**Goal:** I can use narrative techniques of pacing and description to
develop experiences, and use transitions to convey sequence and signal
shifts in time frame or setting.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** W.7.3.b, W.7.3.c
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, your scene and summary versions
from the Lesson 02 extension (if you wrote them).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: in "The Cake," which takes more words —
the three weeks June spent saving, or the two minutes when the bus
arrives? (Answer: the bus arriving takes more words, even though it
takes less time. The story slows down for what matters most and speeds
up through what matters least.)

## Teaching: scene and summary

A story has two speeds.

- **Scene** is slow time: moment by moment, with dialogue, senses,
  and action. The reader lives inside it. The shelter conversation in
  "The Cake" is a scene — every line of dialogue, the smell of rain
  and oranges, the hand on June's shoulder.
- **Summary** is fast time: the narrator compresses hours, days, or
  weeks into a sentence or two. "I had spent three weeks saving for
  the cake" is a summary — three weeks in one sentence. The reader is
  told it happened; they do not live through it.

**Pacing** is the writer's choice of when to slow down into scene and
when to speed up into summary. The rule of thumb: slow down for the
moments that change the character or the story (the bus arriving, the
hand on the shoulder); speed up through the stretches between those
moments (the saving, the walk to the bakery). A story that is all scene
drags; a story that is all summary feels like a list. The interesting
choice is always *where* to change speed.

**Transitions** are the words, phrases, and clauses that carry the
reader across those speed changes and across shifts in time or setting.
They answer the reader's silent question: *how much time passed, and
where are we now?* "The rain started the minute I left the bakery"
moves us from saving to leaving. "I had missed the earlier bus because
I stayed after school" moves us *backward* in time, into the reason.
"On the bus" moves us forward again, into the ride home. Without those
signals, the reader gets lost between the pieces.

Useful transition families:

| Signal | Examples |
|---|---|
| Sequence (what happened next) | first, then, next, after that, finally |
| Time passing | later, by evening, three weeks earlier, the next morning |
| A shift in setting | across town, at the stop, on the bus, back home |
| A flashback (moving backward) | I had missed…, earlier that day…, she remembered… |
| Cause (why it happened) | because, so, that is why, which meant |

### Worked example 1 — the same stretch, two speeds

Summary speed (from the story): "I had spent three weeks saving for
the cake in the pale-blue box under my arm — birthday money, lunch
money I did not spend, two dollars my little brother finally paid me
back."

Scene speed (imagined — the story does *not* do this, on purpose):
"On Monday I put two dollars in the jar. On Tuesday I skipped the
pizza line and put the money in the jar. On Wednesday my brother said
he would pay me back tomorrow, and on Thursday he said it again…"

The scene version is boring on purpose. Saving money is important to
the story (it raises the stakes), but watching it happen day by day
adds nothing. The writer summarizes the saving and saves the scene for
the bus stop — where the cake is actually in danger. Ask of every
stretch: *does the reader need to live through this, or just know it
happened?*

### Worked example 2 — transitions doing the steering

Read the story's movement, transition by transition:

1. "The rain started **the minute I left the bakery**" — setting and
   time, forward.
2. "**I had missed the earlier bus because** I stayed after school" —
   backward in time, and *because* gives the reason.
3. "**Then** it will come in five" — Mrs. Alvarez's line, forward.
4. "The bus turned the corner **then**" — the waiting ends.
5. "**On the bus**, I set the box on my knees" — new setting, forward
   again.

Now remove the transitions and read it broken: "I left the bakery. I
missed the earlier bus. The bus came. I sat on the bus." The events are
the same, but the reader has to guess the order and the gaps. The
transitions are the steering — the events are only the road.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Mark two stretches of "The Cake": one that is told as a **scene**
   and one that is told as a **summary**. For each, say why the writer
   chose that speed.
2. Find three transitions in the story. For each, name what it signals:
   sequence, time passing, a setting shift, a flashback, or a cause.
3. The story summarizes the three weeks of saving in one sentence.
   Write two sentences that would turn it into a scene — then explain
   in one sentence why the story is better without them.
4. Error analysis: "June waited. The bus came. June got on. June went
   home." What is missing besides description? Name the two craft
   problems (hint: speed and steering).

## Independent practice

1. Take your Lesson 02 extension versions (scene and summary of the
   bus stop). Label which is which, then write one sentence explaining
   what each speed is good for.
2. Rewrite this flat stretch twice — once as a scene (slow, with
   dialogue or senses), once as a summary (one sentence): "Dev waited
   outside the gym for tryouts. He was nervous. His name was called."
3. Add a transition to each gap so the sequence is clear. Choose a
   *different* transition family for each gap: "___ the rain started.
   ___ June had missed the earlier bus. ___ the bus turned the corner.
   ___ June sat on the bus."
4. Find one place in "The Cake" where the writer speeds up and one
   place where the writer slows down. For each, write one sentence
   explaining what the reader gains from that speed.

## Applied task

Write a short paragraph (5–7 sentences) about a character waiting for
something important — a bus, a phone call, a result. Use **both**
speeds: summarize the waiting in one sentence, then slow into a scene
for the moment the wait ends. Include at least two transitions from
different families.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) define pacing in your own words; (b) name
one transition from the story and what it signals.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if you cannot tell scene from summary, count the
  dialogue: a stretch with quoted speech is almost always a scene. A
  stretch with no dialogue and a big time jump ("three weeks," "the
  next morning") is almost always a summary.
- **Extension:** rewrite the ending of "The Cake" (the bus ride home)
  as a summary of two sentences — then decide which version, scene or
  summary, serves the story's reflection better, and say why in two
  sentences. (You will use this judgment again in Lesson 05.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners think "pacing" means "writing faster"
or "making the story exciting." It means *controlling time* — the
choice of scene vs. summary. The worked example 1 pair is the
corrective: the boring scene version shows that more detail is not
always better. Another common slip: transitions that all say "then"
(guided practice 2 and independent practice 3 exist for this; require
different families). The Lesson 02 extension versions are reused here
in independent practice 1 — if the learner did not write them, use the
story's saving sentence vs. the shelter scene as the pair instead.
