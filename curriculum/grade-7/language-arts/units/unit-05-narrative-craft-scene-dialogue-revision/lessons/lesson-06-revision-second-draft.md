# Lesson 06 — Revision: The Writer's Second Draft

**Goal:** I can develop and strengthen my writing through planning,
revising, editing, and rewriting, focusing on how well purpose and
audience have been addressed.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** W.7.5, W.7.4
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, your drafts from Lessons 01–05
(the orientation, the illustration scene, the dialogue scene, the
waiting paragraph, the reflective ending).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: what is the difference between revising
and editing? (Answer: revising changes *what the writing says and
how it works* — the scene, the dialogue, the pacing, the ending.
Editing fixes *conventions* — spelling, punctuation, capital letters.
Revise first, edit last: there is no point in perfectly punctuating a
paragraph you are about to cut.)

## Teaching: revision is re-seeing

The word "revision" means *re-seeing*: reading your draft as a reader
would, not as the writer who already knows what you meant. A revision
pass asks four questions, in this order:

1. **Purpose:** does the piece do what it set out to do? (Does the
   orientation orient? Does the ending reflect?)
2. **Audience:** can a reader who was not there follow it? (Is the
   sequence clear? Is every "he" and "she" attached to a person?)
3. **Craft:** do the techniques do their jobs? (Does the dialogue
   reveal character? Does the showing show? Is the pacing right — scene
   where it matters, summary where it does not?)
4. **Conventions (editing, last):** spelling, punctuation, dialogue
   commas, capital letters, sentence boundaries.

The order matters. Fixing a comma in a scene you are about to cut is
wasted work, and polishing a sentence that does not belong is still a
sentence that does not belong.

**How to revise with a reader's eyes:** read the draft aloud, slowly.
Anywhere you stumble, the reader will stumble — mark it. Anywhere you
have to explain ("what I meant was…"), the draft is not doing the
explaining — mark it. Then make *moves*, not just fixes. A revision
move is a decision: cut a sentence, add a beat, slow a moment into a
scene, compress a stretch into a summary, swap a telling sentence for a
showing one, rewrite an ending. Keep a **revision log**: each move, and
one sentence saying why.

**Peer revision** follows the same four questions, with one rule: the
reader describes what they *experienced* ("I got lost here," "I could
picture this"), and the writer decides what to change. The reader never
rewrites the writer's sentences for them.

### Worked example 1 — a draft paragraph, revised

First draft (a learner's version of the bus stop):

> June was at the bus stop. It was raining and she was sad. She had a
> cake. The bus was late. She was worried about the cake getting wet.
> An old lady was there too. She was nice.

Revision pass, question by question:

- **Purpose:** the paragraph is supposed to open a story, but it does
  not orient — no street, no time, no stakes. *Move: add the bakery,
  the birthday, the lateness.*
- **Audience:** "an old lady" is nobody; the reader cannot picture her.
  *Move: name her and give her one concrete detail (red umbrella,
  oranges).*
- **Craft:** every sentence tells ("was sad," "was worried," "was
  nice"). *Move: show the worry through the box, and show the
  kindness through an action.*
- **Conventions:** the sentences are all the same shape (subject-verb).
  *Move: vary them (U04 sentence types).*

Second draft:

> The rain started the minute June left the bakery, and the cake in the
> pale-blue box was already getting damp at the corners. It was for her
> grandmother's seventieth birthday, and the bus home was twenty
> minutes late. At the shelter, Mrs. Alvarez from the third floor stood
> under a red umbrella with a bag of oranges. "Come under," she said.
> "Your cake is getting wet." June stepped under, holding the box
> tighter, and the umbrella smelled like rain and oranges.

The second draft is not longer by much — but it orients, it shows, and
it has a voice. That is revision: same facts, better writing.

### Worked example 2 — a revision log

For the paragraph above, the log reads:

| Move | Why |
|---|---|
| Added the bakery, the birthday, and the lateness to the opening | The first draft did not orient the reader (purpose) |
| Replaced "an old lady" with "Mrs. Alvarez… red umbrella… oranges" | The reader could not picture her (audience) |
| Replaced "was worried" with "holding the box tighter" | Showing instead of telling (craft) |
| Added the spoken line "Come under. Your cake is getting wet." | Dialogue reveals character and moves the story (craft) |
| Varied the sentence shapes | The first draft was all subject-verb (craft, U04) |
| Fixed "June was at the bus stop" → "June left the bakery" | The first sentence now starts the story in motion (purpose) |

A log with a *why* for every move is what makes revision teachable:
the next draft starts from reasons, not from luck.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Read the first draft in worked example 1 aloud. Mark the exact
   place you stumble or get bored. What does that place tell you about
   the draft?
2. Which of the four revision questions catches the problem in "She
   was nice"? Explain in one sentence.
3. The second draft still has one telling sentence in it. Find it, and
   say whether you would revise it or leave it — and why. (Hint: look
   for a feeling word.)
4. Write a one-line revision-log entry for this move: cutting the
   sentence "It was raining and she was sad." from the first draft.
   (Use the format: Move | Why.)

## Independent practice

1. Take your Lesson 03 applied task (June arrives home) or your Lesson
   04 applied task (waiting paragraph). Do a revision pass with the
   four questions, in order. Make at least three revision moves and
   record each in a revision log with a why.
2. Exchange your revised piece with a partner (or read it aloud to the
   adult). The reader answers only these two questions: "Where did you
   get lost?" and "What could you picture?" Record both answers.
3. Based on the reader's answers, make one more revision move. Log it.
4. Editing pass (last): check your piece for dialogue punctuation
   (Lesson 03 rules), spelling, and sentence boundaries. List the
   fixes you made — two at most should be needed if the draft was
   revised first.

## Applied task

Choose one piece you wrote in this unit (orientation, illustration
scene, dialogue scene, waiting paragraph, or reflective ending) and
bring it to final-draft quality in this session: one full revision pass
(four questions, in order), one reader check, one editing pass. Staple
or attach the revision log to the final draft — the log is part of the
work, not an extra.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) state the difference between revising and
editing; (b) name the revision question you would ask first, and why
it comes first.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the four questions feel like too much at once, do
  only the first two (purpose, audience) in this pass and save craft
  and conventions for the next pass. Revision is allowed to take two
  passes — that is normal, not slow.
- **Extension:** revise the *same* paragraph twice, in two different
  directions: once to make it slower (more scene, more senses) and
  once to make it faster (more summary, fewer details). Two versions.
  Which one serves the story better, and why?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners "revise" by fixing spelling and calling
it done. The order rule is the corrective: conventions come last, and
the adult should refuse to check spelling until the revision log has
three moves with whys. Another common slip: accepting every peer
suggestion (guided practice 2 and the peer rule exist for this — the
reader reports experience, the writer decides). The applied task's
final draft is the learner's portfolio piece for the project workshops
— keep it with the revision log attached, because the project rubric
scores the revision log, not just the story.
