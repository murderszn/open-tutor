# Lesson 03 — Dialogue That Does Work

**Goal:** I can use dialogue to develop experiences, events, and
characters — writing exchanges that reveal character and move the
story, with correct punctuation.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** W.7.3.b
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, two colored pencils (one for
dialogue, one for description).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: in "The Cake," which line tells you more
about Mrs. Alvarez — "Mrs. Alvarez was wise" or "My mother turned
seventy in the rain, too"? (Answer: the second. We learn she
understands June's exact situation because she lived it — and we learn
it from her own words, not from the narrator telling us she is wise.)

## Teaching: what dialogue is for

Dialogue is not conversation typed out. Real conversation wanders,
repeats itself, and says "um" a lot; story dialogue is cut down to the
lines that do a job. Every line of dialogue in a story should do at
least one of these three jobs:

1. **Reveal character** — what a person says, and how they say it,
   shows who they are. Mrs. Alvarez says "Come under. Your cake is
   getting wet." She does not say "Hello, I am your neighbor." Her
   first move is to protect the cake, which is how the reader learns
   she is kind and practical.
2. **Reveal relationship** — how two people talk to each other shows
   how they stand with each other. June says "I feel like one" (a
   drowned cat) — she jokes back, which tells us she feels safe with
   Mrs. Alvarez within seconds. Compare that with how she talks to
   Theo: "You will miss your bus," he said, twice. "I know," I said,
   twice. Short, repeated, easy — that is a friendship.
3. **Move the story** — a line of dialogue can change what happens
   next. "Go. She is waiting." ends the waiting scene and starts the
   ride home. Without that line, June might have kept standing there.

Dialogue has two parts: the **spoken words** (in quotation marks) and
the **tag** ("she said") or **beat** (an action that shows who is
speaking, like "Mrs. Alvarez put a hand on my shoulder"). A beat does
double work: it identifies the speaker *and* shows feeling or action.
Tags should mostly be plain — "said" and "asked" disappear for the
reader; fancy tags ("she exclaimed joyfully") are telling in disguise.

### Dialogue punctuation — the rules

1. Put quotation marks around the exact spoken words, and capitalize
   the first word of the spoken sentence: `"The bus is twenty minutes
   late."`
2. When a tag follows the spoken words, use a **comma** inside the
   closing quotation mark instead of a period: `"You look like a
   drowned cat," she said.` (Not: `"You look like a drowned cat." she
   said.`)
3. A question mark or exclamation point stays as it is, inside the
   quotes: `"Because it was lopsided?" I asked.` — and the tag still
   starts lowercase: `I asked`, not `I Asked`.
4. When the tag comes first, put a comma after the tag and capitalize
   the first spoken word: `She said, "Come under."`
5. Start a **new paragraph every time the speaker changes**. This is
   the rule that makes dialogue readable — a reader should never have
   to hunt for who is talking.
6. A **beat** is a separate sentence, not a tag: `"Go. She is
   waiting." Mrs. Alvarez put a hand on my shoulder.` The beat ends
   with a period and starts with a capital, because it is its own
   sentence — it is not "she said."

### Worked example 1 — flat dialogue vs. working dialogue

Flat (dialogue that only reports information):

> "Hello, June," Mrs. Alvarez said. "It is raining. The bus is late.
> I have oranges. My mother once had a birthday in the rain."

Why it fails: every line only delivers a fact. No line reveals
character, no line changes anything, and the speakers could be anyone.

Working (the same information, from the story):

> "You look like a drowned cat," she said.
>
> "I feel like one," I said. "The bus is twenty minutes late."
>
> "Then it will come in five," she said, "because buses are like that.
> Come under. Your cake is getting wet."

The facts are all still there (rain, late bus, oranges implied by the
umbrella's smell), but now the lines *do* things: the first line jokes,
the second answers the joke and states the problem, the third reassures
and acts. Notice the middle line is split by its tag — `"Then it will
come in five," she said, "because buses are like that."` — which is how
a speaker sounds when they pause mid-thought.

### Worked example 2 — a beat instead of a tag

Tag version: `"Go. She is waiting," Mrs. Alvarez said kindly.`

Beat version: `"Go. She is waiting." Mrs. Alvarez put a hand on my
shoulder — just for a second.`

The beat version is stronger for two reasons. First, "kindly" is
telling; the hand on the shoulder *shows* kindness. Second, "just for
a second" is a precise detail that tells us Mrs. Alvarez is the kind of
person who comforts briefly and then sends you on your way — character
in five words.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Reread the shelter scene in "The Cake" (from "The shelter at Alder
   and Fifth was already taken" to "Go. She is waiting."). For each
   line of dialogue, name its job: reveal character, reveal
   relationship, or move the story. One line can do two jobs — say so.
2. Which line of dialogue in the story tells you that June and Theo
   are friends? Quote it and explain in one sentence.
3. Fix the punctuation and capitalization in this exchange:
   > june said "the cake is getting wet". "I know" mrs. alvarez said
   > "come under the umbrella."
4. Rewrite this telling tag as a beat: `"I am so worried about the
   cake," June said nervously.` (Hint: what do June's hands do?)

## Independent practice

1. Write a six-line dialogue between June and the bus driver when the
   bus finally arrives. Rules: new paragraph per speaker; at least one
   beat; at most one tag that is not "said" or "asked"; the exchange
   must move the story (June gets on the bus with the cake safe).
2. Punctuate this exchange correctly, in paragraphs:
   > are you sure the cake is all right june asked / it is fine mrs
   > alvarez said / the frosting is only a little wet
3. Choose one line of dialogue from "The Cake" and explain in two
   sentences what it reveals about the speaker that the narrator never
   states directly.
4. Error analysis: "Dialogue should sound exactly like real talking,
   with lots of 'um' and repeating." Why is that wrong? Give one
   sentence from the story as your evidence.

## Applied task

Write a short scene (6–8 lines of dialogue plus beats) in which June
arrives home and her grandmother opens the door. The dialogue must do
all three jobs: reveal June, reveal her grandmother, and move the
story (the cake gets inside, safe). Do not narrate what the dialogue
itself can show.

## Exit check

In two sentences: (a) write one line of dialogue with a correct tag
after it (comma inside the quotes); (b) write one line with a beat
instead of a tag.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if you cannot hear how a line should sound, read it
  aloud twice — once as the character, once as yourself. If it sounds
  the same both times, it is not in character yet. Ask: would *this*
  person say it this way?
- **Extension:** rewrite the shelter scene's dialogue so Mrs. Alvarez
  is impatient instead of kind — same facts, different character.
  Three lines. What changes: the words, the beats, or both?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners write dialogue that reports the plot
("As you know, the bus is late because…") instead of dialogue that
*does* something. The three-jobs test catches it: if a line's only job
is to inform the reader, cut it or move the information into narration.
Another common slip: tags doing the feeling work ("she said angrily")
— the beat rewrite in guided practice 4 is the repair; insist on an
action, not an adverb. The punctuation rules are standard dialogue
conventions; the adult checks them against the examples in this lesson
rather than assigning a separate grammar sheet.
