# Lesson 05 — Endings That Reflect

**Goal:** I can provide a conclusion that follows from and reflects on
the narrated experiences or events.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** W.7.3.e
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: how does "The Cake" end — with an event,
or with a thought? (Answer: both, in order. The last event is June
sitting on the bus with the box on her knees. The last *sentences* are
a thought: "Showing up is the gift. The cake is just how you carry
it." The event stops the story; the thought finishes it.)

## Teaching: what a conclusion has to do

A narrative conclusion has two jobs, and a good ending does both:

1. **It follows from the events.** The ending must be *earned* — it
   has to grow out of what actually happened. June's reflection works
   because the story showed her carrying the cake, worrying about it,
   and hearing Mrs. Alvarez say "Because I carried it." If June had
   never carried anything, the reflection would be a slogan, not a
   conclusion.
2. **It reflects on the events.** The narrator steps back and says
   what the experience *meant*. Reflection is not a moral ("Always be
   kind") and not a summary ("Then I went home"). It is the narrator
   understanding something they did not understand at the start.

Three endings that fail, and why:

- **The sudden stop:** "Then the bus came and I went home. The end."
  The events stop, but nothing is reflected on. The reader is left
  asking, "So what?"
- **The tacked-on moral:** "And that is why you should always help
  your friends." The story never said that — June helped Theo, but
  the story is about carrying the cake, not about helping friends. A
  moral that does not follow from the events feels pasted on.
- **The new event:** "The next day, June won a prize." A conclusion
  is not a sequel. New events belong in a new scene, not in the
  ending.

A reflection test for your own ending: cover the last paragraph. Does
the story still make sense? (It should — the events are complete.) Now
read only the last paragraph. Does it say something that is *true
because of* the events, and that the narrator could not have said on
page one? (It must. If June could have said "Showing up is the gift"
before the rain, the story did not need to happen.)

### Worked example 1 — the story's ending, taken apart

The last paragraph of "The Cake":

> "I thought the gift was the cake. It was, partly. But sitting there
> with the box safe on my knees, I understood something else: the cake
> mattered because I had carried it — through the rain, late, worried
> the whole way. Showing up is the gift. The cake is just how you carry
> it."

- "I thought the gift was the cake" — the narrator's *first* idea,
  stated so the reader can see it change.
- "It was, partly" — the honest correction. The cake does matter; the
  reflection does not pretend otherwise.
- "because I had carried it — through the rain, late, worried the whole
  way" — the reflection names the *events* that earned it (rain, late,
  worried). Every word points back to something the reader saw.
- "Showing up is the gift" — the new understanding, in one sentence.
- "The cake is just how you carry it" — the closing line, which echoes
  Mrs. Alvarez's "Because I carried it" from the middle of the story.
  An ending that echoes an earlier line feels finished, because the
  story has come back to where its meaning started.

### Worked example 2 — three endings for one scene, judged

The scene: Dev waits outside the gym for basketball tryouts; his name
is called; he goes in and misses his first three shots, then makes the
fourth.

- Ending A (sudden stop): "Then tryouts were over and Dev went home."
  Fails: no reflection. The reader does not know what Dev made of it.
- Ending B (tacked-on moral): "And that is why you should never give
  up on your dreams." Fails: it does not follow from *these* events.
  Dev made one shot out of four — the story is about a small recovery,
  not about dreams.
- Ending C (reflection): "Dev walked home thinking about the fourth
  shot. It had not erased the first three — but it had proved that the
  first three were not the whole story. He could miss and still
  belong on the court." Works: it follows from the events (three
  misses, one make) and says what Dev understands now that he did not
  understand while waiting outside.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Reread the last paragraph of "The Cake." Which sentence is the
   narrator's first idea, and which sentence is the new understanding?
   Quote both.
2. Why is "Showing up is the gift" a reflection and not a moral? Give
   one reason that points to the story's events.
3. Which earlier line of the story does the ending echo? Why does that
   echo make the ending feel finished?
4. Error analysis: "June learned that you should always be on time."
   Why is that a bad ending for this story? Name the two problems
   (hint: what does June actually learn, and was she late on purpose?).

## Independent practice

1. Write a reflective ending (2–3 sentences) for this scene: "Mara
   practiced her violin every morning for a month. On the day of the
   recital, her hands shook, she missed two notes in the first piece —
   and then she played the second piece all the way through without a
   mistake." Do not use the word "always" or "never."
2. Judge these two endings for the same scene. Which one reflects, and
   which one only stops? Give one reason each:
   a. "Mara put her violin away and went home. It was a good day."
   b. "Mara put her violin away. The shaking had not gone away — but
      it had stopped being the whole story. She could shake and still
      play."
3. Take ending (a) above and revise it into a reflection in two
   sentences, without adding any new event.
4. Write one sentence that states what June understands at the end of
   "The Cake" that she did not understand at the beginning — in your
   own words, without quoting the story.

## Applied task

Return to your Lesson 02 applied task (the 4–6 sentence scene from the
illustration). Add a reflective ending of 2–3 sentences to it. The
ending must name something the character understands now, and it must
point back to a detail from your scene. Read the whole piece aloud:
does the ending sound earned?

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) state the difference between a conclusion
and a sudden stop; (b) give one test that tells you whether an ending
reflects or moralizes.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if reflection feels hard, use the sentence frame "I
  thought ___, but now I understand ___." Fill the first blank with
  the character's starting idea and the second with what the events
  proved. Then delete the frame and keep the sentence.
- **Extension:** rewrite the ending of "The Cake" so June reflects on
  Mrs. Alvarez instead of on herself. Two to three sentences. What
  changes about the story's meaning — and what stays the same?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners write morals ("you should always…")
because school has trained them to end with a lesson. The reflection
test (could the narrator have said this on page one?) is the
corrective — a moral can be said on page one; a reflection cannot. The
Dev example in worked example 2 is reused in the quiz and the
assessment, so keep it straight: three misses, one make, and the
reflection is about belonging, not about dreams. The Lesson 02 applied
task returns here as the applied task — if the learner did not write
it, use the bus-stop scene from Lesson 04's applied task instead.
