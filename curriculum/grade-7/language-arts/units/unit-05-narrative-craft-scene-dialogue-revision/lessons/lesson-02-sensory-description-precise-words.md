# Lesson 02 — Sensory Description and Precise Words: Show, Don't Tell

**Goal:** I can use precise words and phrases, relevant descriptive
details, and sensory language to capture the action and convey
experiences and events.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** W.7.3.d, L.7.3
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, the generated illustration below.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: which sentence tells you more — "June was
worried" or "June held the box tighter and stepped forward before the
doors even opened"? (Answer: the second. The first *names* the feeling;
the second *shows* it through an action the reader can picture.)

## Teaching: showing vs. telling

**Telling** names a feeling or a fact: "She was nervous." "It was a
rainy night." **Showing** gives the reader the evidence the feeling is
made of: what the character does, what the senses report, what the
place looks like. The reader does the feeling themselves.

Showing runs on two tools:

1. **Sensory language** — details for the five senses. Not all five
   every time; one or two sharp ones beat a list. In "The Cake," the
   umbrella "smelled like rain and oranges" (smell), the rain "ran down
   the windows in rivers" (sight), the box was "getting damp at the
   corners" (touch). Each detail does a job: the oranges tell us Mrs.
   Alvarez is a real person with groceries, not a symbol.
2. **Precise words** — the exact verb, the exact noun. "Walked" is
   vague; "hurried," "stepped forward," "held tighter" each say
   something different about how June feels. A precise word replaces a
   whole sentence of explanation. Choosing precise language and cutting
   wordiness is the L.7.3 habit from U04, applied to story now.

A useful test: cover the feeling word. If the sentence still lets the
reader feel it, the sentence is showing. "June was worried" fails the
test — cover "worried" and nothing is left. "June held the box tighter"
passes — cover any one word and the worry is still there.

### Worked example 1 — telling to showing

Telling: "Mrs. Alvarez was kind."

Showing (from the story): "Come under. Your cake is getting wet." She
moves over under a small umbrella to make room, and she notices the
cake before she notices June's wet coat. Kindness is *shown* by what
she does first: she protects the thing June cares about.

Now try the same move on a new sentence. Telling: "The street was
quiet." Showing: "The bus turned the corner then, slow through the
puddles, its headlights doubling in the wet street." No one says
"quiet" — but a street where you can hear a bus coming and watch its
lights double is a quiet, empty street, and the reader feels the
waiting end.

**Text alternative (for learners who cannot see the image, or as a
check on what the image shows):** A girl of about thirteen with a long
dark braid stands alone inside a bus-stop shelter on a rainy evening.
She wears a green raincoat over a yellow sweater and holds a crumpled
paper grocery bag; a pale-blue box peeks out of the bag, and through
the box's window you can see a small cake with pink frosting and one
candle. She is looking down an empty street. The street is wet, with
puddles reflecting the amber light of streetlamps, and houses with lit
windows line the far side. A round blue sign with a white bus picture
stands beside the shelter. Rain is falling. No bus is visible yet.

Now use the image the way a writer uses a scene: pick three details and
turn each one into a *showing* sentence.

- Detail: the cake box in the paper bag. Showing sentence: "June
  shifted the bag against her chest so the rain could not reach the
  box — the cake inside was the only thing she had not let get wet."
- Detail: the empty street. Showing sentence: "She watched the end of
  the street until her eyes hurt, but the only thing moving was the
  rain in the puddles."
- Detail: the lit windows. Showing sentence: "Every house on the
  street had its lights on, which meant every family was already home,
  dry, and together — everyone except her."

Notice what each sentence does: it takes a visible detail and attaches
June's feeling to it, without ever writing the word "worried" or
"lonely."

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Find two sensory details in "The Cake" (one for smell or touch, one
   for sight or sound). For each, say what it shows about June's
   feeling — in your own words, without naming the feeling.
2. The story says the bus came "slow through the puddles." Replace
   "slow" with two other precise verbs (for example, "crawled" or
   "eased") and explain how each one changes the feeling of the
   sentence.
3. Look at the illustration again. Name one detail that is *not* in the
   story's words (for example, the yellow sweater, or the lit windows)
   and write one showing sentence from it.
4. Error analysis: "June was very, very sad and upset and miserable."
   Why do three feeling words say less than one showing sentence? Fix
   the sentence by replacing the feeling words with one action.

## Independent practice

1. Rewrite each telling sentence as showing, in one or two sentences
   each. Do not use the feeling word from the original:
   a. "Theo was embarrassed."
   b. "The kitchen was warm and welcoming."
   c. "Mrs. Alvarez was proud of June."
2. Choose one sense you have *not* used yet in this lesson (taste, or
   sound if you used sight) and write two sentences about the bus-stop
   scene for that sense.
3. Write a three-sentence description of the illustration's street
   using only showing sentences — no feeling words ("sad," "lonely,"
   "worried," "happy") allowed. Check your work with the cover test.
4. Precise-word clinic: replace the vague verb in each sentence with a
   precise one, and say what changes: (a) "June went to the bus stop."
   (b) "She held the box." (c) "The rain came down."

## Applied task

Write the scene the illustration shows, in 4–6 sentences, as if it were
the opening of a new story (not "The Cake"). Use at least two senses,
one precise verb that does real work, and no feeling words. End with
June looking down the street — and tell the reader, through what she
does, whether she is hopeful or afraid.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) give one showing sentence for "the bus is
late" that uses a sense other than sight; (b) name the precise word in
it that does the most work, and why.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if showing feels hard, start with the body: what are
  the character's hands doing? Hands are the easiest showing tool —
  tightening, shaking, hiding, reaching. Write the hands first, then
  the sentence around them.
- **Extension:** write the same bus-stop moment twice — once as a
  *scene* (slow, sensory, moment by moment) and once as a *summary*
  ("June waited twenty minutes in the rain. The bus came."). One
  paragraph each. Which version would you use to open a story, and
  why? (You will use this exact choice again in Lesson 04.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners think "showing" means "more adjectives."
It means *evidence*: an action, a sense detail, a precise verb. If a
sentence grows longer but still names the feeling, it is still telling
— send it back through the cover test. Another common slip: describing
the illustration instead of writing from it (guided practice 3 exists
for this; the sentence must attach a feeling to the detail, not just
list what is visible). The extension's scene-vs-summary pair is the
bridge to Lesson 04's pacing work; keep both versions for that lesson.
