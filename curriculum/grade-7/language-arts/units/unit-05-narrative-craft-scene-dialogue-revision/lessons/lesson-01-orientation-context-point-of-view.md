# Lesson 01 — Orientation: Context, Point of View, and Event Sequence

**Goal:** I can engage and orient a reader by establishing a context and
point of view, introducing a narrator and characters, and organizing an
event sequence that unfolds naturally and logically.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** W.7.3.a, RL.7.3
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, two colored pencils (one for
dialogue, one for description).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: what is the difference between a story's
*setting* and its *situation*? (Answer: the setting is where and when
the story happens; the situation is what is wrong, wanted, or at stake
for the character right now. A story needs both before anything else
can matter.)

## Anchor story (read together first)

*The story below is original, written for this unit. The people, places,
and events are fictional. June is thirteen; her grandmother is turning
seventy; the cake is a small white cake with pink frosting roses and one
tall candle, in the bakery's pale-blue box.*

# The Cake

The rain started the minute I left the bakery, which felt personal. I
had spent three weeks saving for the cake in the pale-blue box under my
arm — birthday money, lunch money I did not spend, two dollars my little
brother finally paid me back — and now the sky was doing its best to
ruin it before I got it home.

My name is June. I am thirteen. The cake was for my grandmother's
seventieth birthday, and the bus home was twenty minutes late.

I had missed the earlier bus because I stayed after school to help Theo
with his math. He had a quiz the next day, and fractions were eating him
alive. "You will miss your bus," he said, twice. "I know," I said, twice.
It was worth it. It was still worth it, standing in the rain, except
that now the box was getting damp at the corners and my grandmother was
waiting with seventy candles — no, one candle, seventy years — and I was
going to walk in late with a soggy cake.

The shelter at Alder and Fifth was already taken. Mrs. Alvarez from the
third floor was standing under it with a red umbrella and a bag of
oranges, watching the empty street the way you watch a pot that refuses
to boil.

"You look like a drowned cat," she said.

"I feel like one," I said. "The bus is twenty minutes late."

"Then it will come in five," she said, "because buses are like that.
Come under. Your cake is getting wet."

I stepped under the umbrella. It smelled like rain and oranges. "It's
for my grandmother," I said. "Seventy today. I saved for three weeks."

Mrs. Alvarez nodded like this was the most sensible thing she had heard
all day. "My mother turned seventy in the rain, too," she said. "I
carried her cake across town on a bus that broke down. I walked the last
eight blocks with it over my head like a hat. The frosting melted a
little on one side. She said it was the best cake she ever had."

"Because it was lopsided?" I asked.

"Because I carried it," she said.

The bus turned the corner then, slow through the puddles, its headlights
doubling in the wet street. I held the box tighter and stepped forward
before the doors even opened. Mrs. Alvarez put a hand on my shoulder —
just for a second — and said, "Go. She is waiting."

On the bus, I set the box on my knees and kept both hands on it, the way
you hold something that matters. The rain ran down the windows in
rivers. The driver called out the stops, and the cake rode along, a
little damp at the corners, pink roses still standing.

I thought the gift was the cake. It was, partly. But sitting there with
the box safe on my knees, I understood something else: the cake mattered
because I had carried it — through the rain, late, worried the whole
way. Showing up is the gift. The cake is just how you carry it.

## Teaching: orientation, point of view, sequence

A narrative opens with an **orientation**: the reader learns the
*context* (where, when, and what is going on), the *point of view*
(who is telling the story), and the *narrator and characters* (who is
involved). Then the events follow an **event sequence** that unfolds
naturally and logically — each event causes, or leads to, the next.

Test any opening with three questions:

1. **Where and when are we?** ("The Cake" answers in the first two
   paragraphs: a rainy evening, outside a bakery, then a bus stop.)
2. **Who is telling the story, and how do we know?** (June, first
   person — "I am June. I am thirteen." The narrator *is* the main
   character.)
3. **What is at stake right now?** (The cake must get home dry and on
   time for a seventieth birthday; the bus is late.)

Point of view is a choice, not an accident. In first person, the reader
knows only what June knows — her worry, her memory of saving, Mrs.
Alvarez's words — and nothing about what her grandmother is thinking at
home. That limit is the point: the reader worries *with* June.

An event sequence is not a list of everything that happened. It is the
events that *change* something, in the order they change it. Saving for
the cake, missing the bus, meeting Mrs. Alvarez, the bus arriving, the
ride home: each one makes the next one necessary.

### Worked example 1 — a weak opening vs. a strong opening

Weak: "One day, a girl had a cake and it was raining."

Why it fails the test: no *where* (which street? which town?), no *when*
(after school? evening?), no point of view (who is "a girl"?), and no
stakes (why does this cake matter?). "One day" could be any day.

Strong: "The rain started the minute I left the bakery, which felt
personal."

Why it works: *where* (outside a bakery), *when* (the minute she left),
point of view (first person, "I"), character feeling ("felt personal"
tells us June takes the rain as an insult — that is voice), and stakes
(the cake is already in danger in the very first sentence).

### Worked example 2 — mapping the sequence

"The Cake" in five beats:

1. **June leaves the bakery with the cake; rain starts.** (Situation
   set: the cake is in danger.)
2. **June explains the saving and the missed bus.** (Backstory that
   raises the stakes: the cake cost three weeks.)
3. **June meets Mrs. Alvarez at the shelter.** (A new character enters;
   the waiting becomes a conversation, not just standing.)
4. **Mrs. Alvarez tells her own cake story.** (The theme arrives in
   dialogue: carrying it is the gift.)
5. **The bus comes; June rides home and reflects.** (The problem
   resolves, and the reflection gives the events meaning.)

Beat 4 could not come before beat 3, and beat 5 could not come before
beat 4 — Mrs. Alvarez's line "Because I carried it" is what June's
ending reflection answers. That is a sequence unfolding logically.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Underline the three sentences in the first two paragraphs that
   answer the orientation test: where, who is telling, and what is at
   stake. Read them aloud.
2. Whose point of view is the story told from, and what is one thing
   the reader *cannot* know because of that choice? (Hint: what is
   happening at June's grandmother's house?)
3. Retell the story's five beats in your own words, in order. Which
   beat could you remove without breaking the story — and which beat
   would break it if removed?
4. Mrs. Alvarez's story about her mother's cake is a *flashback* inside
   the waiting scene. Why does the writer put it there instead of at
   the very beginning?

## Independent practice

1. Write a three-sentence orientation for a story of your own: sentence
   1 names where and when, sentence 2 introduces the narrator (first
   person) or a character, sentence 3 names what is at stake. Do not
   use the words "one day."
2. List the event sequence of "The Cake" as five beats in your own
   words. For each beat, write one sentence explaining what changes
   because of it.
3. Rewrite this weak opening so it passes the three-question test:
   "There was a boy. He was at school. Something happened."
4. Error analysis: a classmate says the story's sequence is "June buys
   a cake, June waits, June rides the bus." What two important beats
   are missing from that summary, and why does each one matter?

## Applied task

Your friend has never read "The Cake." In four sentences, orient them:
where and when the story happens, who tells it, what is at stake, and
how it ends. Do not retell the whole story — orient, then stop.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) state the story's context and point of view;
(b) name the beat where the story's meaning first appears in dialogue.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the three-question test feels hard to hold at once,
  answer it as a table: Where? | When? | Who tells it? | What is at
  stake? Fill one cell at a time from the first two paragraphs.
- **Extension:** rewrite the first two paragraphs of "The Cake" in
  third person ("June left the bakery…"). What is gained, and what is
  lost? Two sentences each.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners treat "orientation" as "describe the
setting for a long time." The test is the three questions, not the
length — June's orientation is two paragraphs, and the second one is
three short sentences. Another common slip: listing events instead of
sequencing them (guided practice 3 exists for exactly this; insist on
the "what changes" sentence for each beat). The flashback question
(guided practice 4) is the lesson's hardest idea: Mrs. Alvarez's story
lands *after* June's worry is established, so it answers a question the
reader is already asking.
