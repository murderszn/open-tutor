# Lesson 05 — Setting Shapes the Story

**Student goal:** I can explain how a story's setting forces characters
into choices that drive the plot.
**Time:** 40–50 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**
**Standard:** RL.7.3

## Materials

- "The Lantern on Greyrock Point" (Lesson 01)
- The generated illustration below (also at
  [`assets/storm-lighthouse-setting.png`](../assets/storm-lighthouse-setting.png))
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "Could this story happen on a calm, sunny beach in July?" (No — no
gale, no icing gallery, no overdue boat, no leaking-lamp crisis.) Setting
is not wallpaper; remove it and the plot collapses.

## Teaching

**Setting** has four parts: **place** (where), **time** (when — historical
era, season, time of night), **weather and physical conditions**, and
**social conditions** (who holds authority, what rules and duties apply).
In "The Lantern on Greyrock Point":

- Place: an isolated lighthouse on a rocky point above the sea
- Time: the last evening of October, night
- Weather: a rising gale — wind, ice, black water
- Social: a keeper's duty to the boats; a grandfather's authority over his
  granddaughter

Setting **interacts** with character and plot through chains:

> **Setting detail → character choice → plot event.**
> The gale ices the gallery (setting) → Hale forbids Mara from going up
> (character choice shaped by setting) → Mara must disobey to reach the
> lamp (plot). The leaking oil (setting/condition) → Mara must tend the
> lamp *all night*, not just once (plot pressure).

The analytical move is always to ask: *"What choice does this setting
force, and what would change if the setting were different?"* If nothing
would change, the detail is decoration; if the plot breaks, the detail is
structural.

**Key vocabulary:** *setting* (place, time, weather, social conditions),
*interact* (elements affecting each other, not just sitting side by side).

## Worked example 1 — building a setting → choice → plot chain

The adult thinks aloud:

> "Setting detail: 'the gallery walk ices in a gale' (¶5). Character choice:
> Hale orders Mara to stay inside — a choice he makes *because* of the ice;
> on a calm night there is no order. Plot event: Mara must now choose
> between obedience and the lamp, which creates the story's central
> conflict. Chain complete: ice → order → disobedience dilemma. Test it by
> removal: no ice, no order, no dilemma — the story's engine stalls. So the
> ice is structural, not decorative."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** I didn't just *name* the setting; I showed the
causal arrow from setting to choice to plot, then proved it with the
removal test.

## Worked example 2 — a decorative detail vs. a structural one

> "Decorative: 'clouds the color of wet slate' (¶2). Beautiful, but if the
> clouds were merely gray, nothing in the plot changes — Mara still climbs,
> the *Alder* still needs the light. Structural: the oil leak (¶3). Remove
> the leak and the lamp burns all night unattended — Mara has no reason to
> climb, Hale's order stands unchallenged, and there is no story. A close
> reader spends analysis on structural details and enjoys decorative ones
> without over-claiming."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** not every detail carries equal weight. The
removal test separates atmosphere from machinery.

## Guided practice

With the adult, build a chain for: "the *Alder* is three hours overdue past
the reef" (¶2). Setting detail → whose choice? → what plot event? Then run
the removal test.

## Independent practice

1. Build a complete setting → choice → plot chain for the **social**
   setting: Hale's authority as grandfather and keeper (¶¶5, 10–12).
2. Identify one decorative detail and one structural detail not used in the
   worked examples. Justify each with the removal test.
3. **Explain-your-reasoning:** A classmate says, "The setting doesn't
   matter; Mara would have climbed anyway because she's brave." Using two
   setting details, argue that the setting is doing real causal work.

## Application

Rewrite the story's crisis for a **different setting**: a desert relay
station, a mountain fire lookout, or a city bridge control room at rush
hour. Keep Mara's dilemma (duty vs. orders). In 5–7 sentences show how the
new setting forces equivalent choices. What survives the move — and what
does that tell you about the theme?

## Exit check

"Name one structural setting detail from the story and complete this chain:
detail → character choice → plot event." Secure = all three links present
with citations; Developing = two links; Not yet = detail named but no causal
chain.

## Supports and extension

- **Chain frame:** "Because ___ (setting), ___ (character) decides to ___,
  which causes ___ (plot)."
- **Draw it:** sketch three boxes with arrows — setting, choice, plot —
  before writing.
- **Extension:** analyze how the *time* setting (night, October, the 2 a.m.
  deadline) creates urgency. Would the story work at noon? Write a
  paragraph defending your answer with the removal test.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners list setting details ("it's stormy") without connecting them to
choices — require the arrow, not the inventory. They also confuse *mood*
(the story feels scary) with *causal setting* (the ice forces the order);
both are real, but this lesson grades the causal chain. Watch for chains
that skip the middle link (setting → plot with no character choice) —
RL.7.3 is about elements *interacting*, and the character's decision is the
hinge. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) § Lesson 05.
