# Lesson 01 — Citing Textual Evidence: Prove It From the Page

**Student goal:** I can support an answer about a story with several pieces
of evidence quoted or pointed to in the text.
**Time:** 40–50 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**
**Standard:** RL.7.1

## Materials

- The anchor story below (printed or on screen)
- Two colored pencils or highlighters: one for **explicit facts**, one for
  **clues that support an inference**
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Ask: "In your own words, what does *evidence* mean?" Accept: proof,
reasons, details from the text. Then ask: "If I claim it rained yesterday,
what evidence could prove it?" (wet ground, puddles, weather report). Note:
in reading, our "weather report" is the text itself — we may only claim what
the page supports.

## The anchor story — "The Lantern on Greyrock Point" (original, written for this unit)

> **1.** Greyrock Point juts into the sea like a clenched fist, and at its tip
> stands the lighthouse where thirteen-year-old Mara lives with her
> grandfather, Keeper Hale. For eleven years the lamp has burned every night
> without fail — Hale's pride, and, he says, his promise to the fishing boats
> of Harrow Bay.
>
> **2.** On the last evening of October, the wind rose before supper. Clouds
> the color of wet slate stacked over the water, and the radio crackled: a
> gale by midnight, and the fishing boat *Alder* still out past the reef,
> overdue by three hours.
>
> **3.** Hale checked the lamp and frowned. The oil reservoir was lower than
> it should have been — a slow leak he had meant to fix all week. "If she
> burns all night at full trim, she'll run dry by two in the morning," he
> said. "Trim her low, and the *Alder* may never see her."
>
> **4.** Mara watched the black water climb the rocks. "Let me sit the
> watch," she said. "I can trim the lamp and mind the oil. You need sleep
> before the morning repair."
>
> **5.** Hale shook his head. "The gallery walk ices in a gale. You stay
> inside. That's an order, not a suggestion."
>
> **6.** At eleven the wind found its voice, howling through the tower seams.
> Mara lay in her bunk listening to the lamp-room door rattle. *If the lamp
> dies at two, the* Alder *dies with it.* She thought of her father, lost to
> these same rocks six years ago, when the old light had failed in a storm.
>
> **7.** She rose, pulled on her oilskin, and took the stairs two at a time,
> the lantern from the kitchen swinging in her hand. On the gallery the wind
> struck her like a wall. Ice needled her cheeks. She crawled the last yards
> to the lamp room on her hands and knees, lashed the door, and trimmed the
> wick with numb fingers.
>
> **8.** Through the glass she saw it: a dark shape beyond the reef, rolling
> in the troughs — the *Alder*, fighting for the channel. Mara fed the lamp
> carefully, drop by drop, watching the flame hold steady and bright.
>
> **9.** At half past one the *Alder* rounded the point, her running lights
> winking. Mara laughed out loud into the wind. She stayed until the boat's
> lights reached the harbor mouth, then descended, soaked and shaking, to
> find her grandfather waiting at the stair foot, his face unreadable.
>
> **10.** "You disobeyed a direct order," he said.
>
> **11.** "The lamp would have died at two," Mara said. "And the *Alder* —"
>
> **12.** "I know." He was quiet a long moment. Then, slowly, he unpinned the
> brass keeper's badge from his own coat and pressed it into her palm. "A
> keeper's first duty is to the light, Mara. You understood that before I
> could teach it. But next time — you tell me first."

## Teaching

A claim about a story is only as strong as the evidence behind it.
**Textual evidence** is a detail from the text — a quotation, a paraphrase,
or a paragraph you can point to — that supports what you say. The standard
for this unit says *several pieces*: one detail can be a coincidence, but
two or three details pointing the same way build a case.

**How to cite:** name the paragraph and quote or paraphrase the key words.
Example form: *"The text says ___ (¶3), which shows ___."* You may quote
short phrases exactly ("run dry by two in the morning") or paraphrase in
your own words — but the detail must come from the page, not from your
memory of a movie or from guessing.

**Explicit meaning** is what the text *states outright*. "The oil reservoir
was lower than it should have been" is explicit: the words say it. Much of
close reading is simply slowing down enough to catch what the text actually
states before jumping to what it might mean.

**Key vocabulary:** *explicit* (stated directly in words), *textual
evidence* (details from the text that support a claim), *cite* (to point to
the text as support).

## Worked example 1 — one claim, three pieces of evidence

**Claim:** The lamp was in danger of going out that night.

The adult thinks aloud:

> "Let me hunt for details. Paragraph 3: the oil reservoir was low because
> of a slow leak — that's piece one. Same paragraph: Hale says if the lamp
> burns all night at full trim, 'she'll run dry by two in the morning' —
> piece two, a direct quotation. Paragraph 6: Mara herself thinks, 'If the
> lamp dies at two, the Alder dies with it' — piece three, and it shows even
> the character believes the danger is real. One detail could be
> misremembered; three details from three different paragraphs all point the
> same way. That's *several pieces* of evidence."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** each piece is *explicit* — the words are on the
page. I didn't have to guess. And I spread my evidence across the story
rather than quoting one sentence three times.

## Worked example 2 — what counts as evidence (and what doesn't)

**Question:** Why does Mara go up to the lamp room?

> Weak answer: "Because she's brave." — *Brave* is a judgment with no
> citation. The text never calls her brave.
>
> Strong answer: "Mara goes up because she believes the lamp will die at two
> and the *Alder* will be lost without it. Paragraph 6 says she thinks, 'If
> the lamp dies at two, the Alder dies with it.' Paragraph 7 shows her
> climbing the stairs with the kitchen lantern, and paragraph 11 has her tell
> her grandfather, 'The lamp would have died at two.' Three citations, all
> explicit."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** "because she's brave" might be *true*, but it
is not *evidence-based* — it's my opinion dressed as an answer. In this
unit, every answer about the story must be traceable to the page.

## Guided practice

With the adult, answer each in the "The text says ___ (¶__), which shows
___" frame. The adult models the first.

1. What is the weather like on the night of the story? Cite two details.
2. What order does Hale give Mara? Quote his exact words.
3. What happened to Mara's father? Cite the paragraph that states it.

## Independent practice

Write full answers with citations (paragraph numbers required).

4. Why can't Hale simply fix the problem himself that night? Cite two
   details.
5. What does Hale do at the very end of the story? What does he say? Quote
   or paraphrase both the action and the words.
6. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The *Alder* sank that night."
   Is this supported? Cite the text to agree or correct.

## Application

Choose any two of your answers above. For each, add a *third* piece of
evidence from a different paragraph. Then trade with the adult: can the
adult find the detail you cited from your paragraph number alone?

## Exit check

Answer in writing: "What time does Hale believe the lamp will run dry, and
what two text details support that?" Secure = correct time (two in the
morning) with two cited details; Developing = correct time with one detail;
Not yet = time wrong or no citation.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading access:** read the story aloud together, one numbered paragraph
  at a time, pausing to highlight explicit facts after each.
- **Sentence frames:** "The text says ___ (¶__). This shows ___."
- **Extension:** find one detail in the story that is stated but easy to
  miss on a first read (e.g., the badge, the father's story). Explain why a
  fast reader might skip it.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often cite their *memory of the discussion* ("we talked about the
leak") instead of the text, or cite one detail repeatedly as if it were
several. Insist on distinct paragraph numbers. Another common slip: quoting
without explaining what the quote *shows* — the "which shows ___" clause is
the analysis, and it is required. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) § Lesson 01.
