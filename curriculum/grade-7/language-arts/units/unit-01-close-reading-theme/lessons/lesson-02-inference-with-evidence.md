# Lesson 02 — Inference: Reading Between the Lines (With Evidence)

**Student goal:** I can tell the difference between what a text states and
what I infer, and I can defend an inference with textual evidence.
**Time:** 40–50 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 3 of 16**
**Standard:** RL.7.1

## Materials

- "The Lantern on Greyrock Point" (Lesson 01)
- Two colored pencils: one for **explicit facts**, one for **clues that
  support an inference**

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Read this sentence: "Mara laughed out loud into the wind" (¶9). Ask: does
the text *state* that Mara feels relieved, or do we *infer* it? (Infer —
the word "relieved" never appears; the laugh plus the *Alder* rounding the
point are clues.) If the learner struggles, contrast with an explicit
sentence: "She crawled the last yards on her hands and knees" — stated.

## Teaching

An **inference** is a reasonable conclusion drawn from evidence in the text
plus what you know about how the world works. It is *not* stated outright —
that is exactly what makes it an inference rather than a fact.

Two rules keep inferences honest:

1. **Every inference needs evidence.** "Mara feels relieved" is defensible
   because the text shows her laughing the moment the *Alder* rounds the
   point (¶9). An inference with no textual evidence is just a guess.
2. **Admit alternatives.** A strong reader can say, "The evidence also
   supports a different reading." If two inferences both fit the evidence,
   the text allows both — and saying so shows confidence, not weakness.

A useful frame: *"The text states ___ (¶__), and I also know ___, so I
infer ___."* The middle step — what *you* bring — must be ordinary,
defensible knowledge (e.g., "people laugh when tension breaks"), never a
private fact about the author.

**Key vocabulary:** *inference* (a conclusion drawn from evidence, not
stated), *implicit* (suggested rather than stated), *defensible* (able to
be supported with evidence).

## Worked example 1 — building an inference step by step

**Question:** Why does Mara disobey her grandfather's direct order?

The adult thinks aloud:

> "Stated facts first. Paragraph 5: Hale orders her to stay inside — 'That's
> an order, not a suggestion.' Paragraph 6: Mara thinks, 'If the lamp dies at
> two, the Alder dies with it,' and remembers her father, lost to these
> rocks when the old light failed. Paragraph 7: she goes up anyway.
>
> Now the inference. The text never says, 'Mara disobeyed because of her
> father.' But I know that losing a parent in a similar accident would make
> someone desperate to prevent a repeat — that's ordinary human knowledge.
> So I infer: **Mara disobeys partly because she cannot bear to watch
> another ship die the way her father did.** Evidence: the father's death is
> the very thought in her mind (¶6) in the moment she decides.
>
> Alternative: she might disobey simply from duty — Hale himself says 'a
> keeper's first duty is to the light' (¶12). Both readings fit. I find the
> father-reading stronger because ¶6 places the memory at the decision
> point."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** I separated *stated* (order, thought, action)
from *inferred* (her deeper motive). I brought in only ordinary knowledge,
and I named the alternative instead of hiding it.

## Worked example 2 — an inference that goes too far

**Question:** What does Hale feel at the end?

> Overreach: "Hale feels guilty for being a bad grandfather." — The text
> never suggests Hale thinks he failed as a grandfather; "guilty" and "bad
> grandfather" have no textual support. This is a guess wearing an
> inference's clothes.
>
> Defensible: "Hale feels proud, mixed with worry. Evidence: he gives Mara
> his own brass keeper's badge (¶12) — people give badges to honor someone.
> He says she 'understood [a keeper's duty] before I could teach it' — that
> is praise. The worry: his face is 'unreadable' (¶9) and he still calls it
> disobedience (¶10). Pride + worry both fit the evidence; guilt does not."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the test is always *point to the page*. If no
detail supports the feeling-word, the inference fails — however interesting
it sounds.

## Guided practice

For each, label **stated** or **inferred**, then give the evidence.

1. The *Alder* is a fishing boat.
2. Mara is afraid on the gallery walk.
3. Hale has been keeper for at least eleven years.
4. The crew of the *Alder* sees the lamp and steers for harbor.

The adult models #1 (stated — ¶1 "the fishing boats of Harrow Bay" and ¶2
"the fishing boat *Alder*").

## Independent practice

5. Infer: why does Mara crawl the last yards to the lamp room instead of
   walking? Use the frame: "The text states ___, and I know ___, so I
   infer ___."
6. Infer: why does Hale wait at the foot of the stairs instead of stopping
   Mara? Cite two details and name one alternative reading.
7. **Explain-your-reasoning:** A classmate says, "Mara's father is alive and
   works on the *Alder*." Use two pieces of evidence to show why this
   inference fails.

## Application

Write two inferences of your own about any character — one about a feeling,
one about a motive. For each, list the stated details that support it and
one alternative reading the evidence also allows.

## Exit check

"Is this sentence stated or inferred? 'Mara cares more about the *Alder's*
crew than about obeying her grandfather.' Defend your label with one piece
of evidence." Secure = "inferred" with a cited detail (e.g., she disobeys
the order to keep the lamp lit for the boat, ¶¶5–7); Developing = correct
label, weak citation; Not yet = mislabeled.

## Supports and extension

- **Frames:** "The text states ___ (¶__). I also know ___. So I infer ___."
- **Color coding:** highlight stated facts in one color, inference-clues in
  the other, before writing.
- **Extension:** find the *weakest* inference in your Application answers —
  the one with the thinnest evidence — and strengthen it with one more
  detail, or downgrade it to "possible but unsupported."

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

Learners often treat inferences as *lesser* than facts ("it's just a
guess") or as *equal* to facts ("the text says she's relieved"). Insist on
the label every time: stated or inferred. Another slip: bringing in
outside knowledge about lighthouses or storms as if it were textual
evidence — outside knowledge may *support* an inference but never
*replace* the citation. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) § Lesson 02.
