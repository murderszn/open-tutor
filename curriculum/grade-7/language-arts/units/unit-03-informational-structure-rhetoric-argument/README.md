# Unit 03 — Informational: Structure, Rhetoric, and Argument Evaluation

**Grade:** 7 · **Subject:** Language Arts · **Track weeks:** 11–14
**Backlog:** [issue #36](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/36), section U03
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Literary reading asks what a story *means*. Informational reading asks
something harder to dodge: is this true, is it well supported, and what is
the author doing to your opinion while you read? In this unit the learner
works with two original articles about one town question — should the town
plant more street trees? — and learns the informational reader's core moves:
finding two or more central ideas and tracing how they develop, mapping how
people, events, and ideas push on each other, naming the structure an author
uses and what each section does for the whole, hearing the author's point of
view and purpose, tracing an argument claim by claim, and finally comparing
two authors who use the *same* facts to build *different* cases.

## Essential question

How can two honest writers look at the same town, the same trees, and the
same numbers — and still disagree?

## Prerequisites

From the diagnostic weeks, the grade-6 track, U01, and U02:

- Citing several pieces of textual evidence for explicit meaning and
  inferences (U01; now applied to informational claims — "this sentence
  supports the central idea because…")
- Determining a theme/central idea and writing an objective summary
  distinct from opinion (U01; deepened here to *two or more* central ideas
  and their development across sections)
- Determining figurative and connotative word meanings and analyzing word
  choice's impact on tone (U02; now applied to informational word choice)
- Analyzing how an author develops and contrasts points of view (U02;
  now applied to an author's *position* in an argument, not a narrator)
- Basic discussion habits: prepared, evidence-based, collegial (all units)

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. Determine **two or more central ideas** in an informational text, analyze
   how they develop over the course of the text, and write an **objective
   summary** distinct from personal opinion (RI.7.2).
2. Analyze the **interactions between individuals, events, and ideas** in a
   text — for example, how an idea influences individuals or events, or how
   individuals influence ideas or events (RI.7.3).
3. Analyze the **structure** an author uses to organize a text, including
   how the major sections contribute to the whole and to the development of
   the ideas; determine **technical, figurative, and connotative word
   meanings** and analyze a specific word choice's impact on meaning and
   tone (RI.7.5; RI.7.4).
4. Determine an author's **point of view or purpose** and analyze how the
   author **distinguishes** that position from the positions of others
   (RI.7.6).
5. **Trace and evaluate an argument** and its specific claims, assessing
   whether the reasoning is **sound** and the evidence is **relevant and
   sufficient** to support the claims (RI.7.8).
6. Analyze how **two or more authors** writing about the same topic shape
   their presentations of key information by **emphasizing different
   evidence** or advancing **different interpretations** of facts, and
   compare a text to a **visual/multimedia version** of its subject,
   analyzing each medium's portrayal (RI.7.9; RI.7.7).

All anchor articles in this unit are **original texts written for this
unit**. Any numbers inside them are a **fictional practice dataset**,
labeled as such where they appear — they are for practicing analysis, not
real measurements, and learners must never cite them as real-world facts.
Background facts about heat islands in the Resource Pack come from the
U.S. Environmental Protection Agency and the U.S. Forest Service (checked
2026-10-26) and are kept separate from the fictional articles.

## 16-session pacing (4 weeks × 4 sessions, ~45 minutes each)

Sessions rotate: modeled informational lesson → structure/purpose practice
→ argument-evaluation practice → review and discussion. **Lessons alone do
not fill the unit** — the named practice, project, quiz, and assessment
sessions below are the rest of the 16.

| Wk | Session | Activity | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|---|

| 11 | 2 | **Practice A** — central-idea hunt and summary repair | Every summary sentence is traceable to the text; opinions removed |
| 11 | 3 | [Lesson 02](lessons/lesson-02-interactions.md) — how individuals, events, and ideas interact | Draws a 3-link interaction chain with cited evidence |

## Differentiation, supports, and extensions

- **Reading access:** articles are printed in the lessons so the learner
  never hunts for a text; the adult reads any section aloud on request, and
  the learner may answer orally, by dictation, or in writing.
- **Response modes:** charts, chains, and tables count as full answers
  wherever a paragraph is not the skill being tested; speech-to-text is
  always allowed for longer writing.
- **Scaffolds:** sentence starters are given in every lesson ("One central
  idea is… It develops when…"); the project provides a partially filled
  evidence table for learners who need it.
- **Extensions:** the project extension asks the learner to find one real
  source (EPA or Forest Service, via the Resource Pack) and add a line to
  the briefing distinguishing real data from the articles' fictional
  practice data; Lesson 06's extension asks for a third "author" — a
  one-paragraph position written from a shopkeeper's point of view.
- **Reteaching signals:** if the learner's summaries keep opinions, return
  to Lesson 01's summary-repair routine before Lesson 06; if claim
  judgments are all "good" or all "bad," return to Lesson 05's
  relevance-vs-sufficiency split.
