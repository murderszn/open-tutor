# Answer Key — Unit 03

For the guiding adult only. Do not hand this file to the learner.
Acceptable variations are noted; wording may differ if the reasoning
and the cited detail match.

## Lesson 01

**Readiness:** topic = "street trees"; central idea = a sentence about
the topic, e.g., "Street trees cool sidewalks."

**Guided practice:**

1. A topic names the subject; a central idea states, in a full
   sentence, what the author shows about the subject.
2. Example: "its leaves block sunlight before it reaches the pavement"
   (§2). It supports central idea 1 by giving a *mechanism*.
3. Central idea 2 is developed by §4 (street life: lingering,
   strollers, baskets, chairs) and §5 (the vote — the idea becomes a
   choice). §4 adds effects on people; §5 adds a decision.
4. §1 introduces both: the cool Maple Street walk introduces central
   idea 1, and the contrast in how the author behaves (lingering vs.
   hurrying) hints at central idea 2.

**Independent practice:**

1. CI1: "Street trees measurably cool sidewalks and the air above
   them." CI2: "Cooler streets change how people use their town — more
   walking, meeting, and time outside."
2. CI2 is first hinted in §1 (the author's different behavior on the
   two streets) and fully developed in §4 ("neighbors linger," chairs,
   strollers, corner court).
3. The birds detail supports central idea 2: it is in §4, about street
   life, and it shows the street becoming livelier — an effect, not a
   temperature.
4. Accept any three-sentence summary with no opinions and every
   sentence traceable to a section; the opinion word most often
   smuggled in is "should" or "wonderful" — it must be replaced with
   text-based wording (e.g., "the article ends with a council vote").

**Applied task:** topic sentence + two central-idea sentences + one
ending sentence (the fall vote). No "should" and no personal opinion.

**Exit check:** (a) either central idea in a full sentence; (b) a
section that develops it and what it adds (feeling / mechanism /
numbers / effects / decision).

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice:**

1. Chain (arrows may be labeled in either order as long as cause words
   are used): hot sun heats pavement (event) → idea that shade cools
   (idea) → trees planted (event) → shaded sidewalk (event) →
   neighbors linger (individuals) → council considers more trees
   (event). Accept equivalent chains with labeled arrows.
2. Shopkeepers setting out chairs is both: an individual action *and*
   a small event that changes the street. Either label earns credit if
   defended with the §4 detail.
3. Event → idea: the hot walk home (§1) gives the author the idea that
   two streets can "feel like different seasons."
4. Without the walk, there would be no comparison, no science-club
   measurements framed by it, and no article — the walk is the
   article's originating event.

**Independent practice:**

1. Four-link chain, e.g.: transpiration releases water vapor → heat
   carried away → sidewalk and air cooler → people linger → council
   votes. Every arrow labeled.
2. It is a central idea (about the article's topic), stated as a
   decision-image — not an interaction, because it names no cause chain.
3. Two-way example: cooler streets → people linger → livelier street →
   more people come. Details: §4 lingering/chairs (one direction),
   §4 "when people use a street more, they meet each other more"
   (the other).
4. Error: "trees cause friendliness" skips mechanism and states a
   personality claim the text never makes. Careful chain: trees shade
   the street → the walk feels comfortable → people linger → people
   meet. Friendliness is not in the text; meeting is.

**Applied task:** five sentences using because/so/then, with one cited
detail (e.g., 88 vs. 104 degrees).

**Exit check:** one individual (e.g., shopkeepers), one event (e.g.,
the council vote), one idea (e.g., shade cools streets), connected in
one sentence.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice:**

1. §4 mixes cause/effect (cooler → lingering) and examples (strollers,
   baskets, chairs, birds).
2. If §5 came first, the reader would not yet know *why* trees cool
   streets (§2) or *how much* (§3) — the vote would be baseless.
3. "Linger" connotes unhurried, pleasant staying; "stay" is neutral.
   The connotation carries central idea 2's claim that cooler streets
   invite street life.
4. "Transpiration" is defined where it first appears because the
   explanation section must be understood by a reader who has never
   met the word; an undefined technical term would break trust.

**Independent practice:**

1. §1 description + compare/contrast, introduce; §2 cause/effect,
   explain; §3 evidence/elaboration, prove; §4 cause/effect + examples,
   widen; §5 problem/solution, decide.
2. Connotative example: "beat" (§1) — denotation: shone directly;
   connotation: pounding/hostile; effect: makes Oak Street feel harsh
   and Maple Street's softness stand out. Technical example:
   "transpiration" (§2) — precise meaning: release of water vapor
   through leaf openings; effect: makes the explanation sound exact and
   trustworthy.
3. "Problem and solution" is too narrow: it describes only §5. §§2–3
   explain and prove rather than solve, and §1 introduces rather than
   poses a problem.
4. Pleasant version: "On Oak Street, people strolled through." Worse
   version: "On Oak Street, people fled through." The changed word
   must be named in each case.

**Applied task:** a "What Could Go Wrong" section belongs after §4
(once benefits are established) and before §5 (so the vote weighs both
sides); placed before the numbers, it would ask the reader to doubt
claims not yet proven.

**Exit check:** §3 is evidence/elaboration; without it the article
would have mechanism and feeling but no measurement — central idea 1
would be unproven.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice:**

1. Position: the town should plant 200 street trees, for fuller
   reasons than temperature alone (street life and the kind of place
   the town wants to be).
2. Concession (§3): "Shade does not change the weather, but it clearly
   changes the street you walk on." It gives up the exaggerated claim
   (trees fix the weather) and protects the real claim (trees change
   the street).
3. Positions contrasted in §5: numbers-only (plant for cooling) vs.
   fuller-story (plant for street life too).
4. No quoted opponent. She distinguishes by contrast (numbers vs.
   fuller story) and by concession (weather limit) — a visible
   difference, not a named enemy.

**Independent practice:**

1. Frame: "The author's purpose is to *persuade* by *informing*."
   Persuade detail: §5's vote ("will vote this fall"); inform detail:
   §§2–3's mechanism and numbers.
2. Precise position: "The town should plant 200 street trees, because
   they will cool sidewalks and change how residents use their
   streets."
3. Additional distinguishing move: "A tree is not just a thermometer
   change; it is an invitation" (§4) — contrast between the narrow
   temperature view and the fuller street-life view.
4. Error: point of view in RI.7.6 means stance, not grammatical
   person. Correct: the author's point of view is pro-planting with a
   street-life emphasis; "I walked" is first-person narration, not a
   position.

**Applied task:** two sentences: one conceding a true point from
Article A (e.g., the 16-degree ground difference), one stating the
resident's different position (e.g., plant fewer, or later, or
elsewhere). No invented facts.

**Exit check:** purpose (persuade by informing) + position (plant 200
trees for fuller reasons) + one quoted distinguishing sentence (§3
concession or §5 contrast).

## Lesson 05

**Claims:** (1) trees cool streets; (2) everyone will walk everywhere
and buses will be unnecessary; (3) most people agree, so the plan is
right; (4) trees will remove all air pollution.

**Guided practice:**

1. Four claims as above, one sentence each.
2. Claim 2's leap: cool streets → everyone walks (skips choice,
   distance, weather, ability) → no buses needed (skips everyone who
   cannot walk, and all other bus purposes).
3. Claim 4's evidence (leaves catch dust) is relevant to "trees help
   air quality" but not to "remove *all* pollution": relevant in part,
   insufficient for the absolute claim.
4. Strongest: Claim 1 (sound, relevant, limited sufficiency). Weakest:
   Claim 3 (unsound popularity reasoning; agreement is not evidence
   about outcomes).

**Independent practice:**

1. Chart: Claim 1 — sound / relevant / limited sufficiency (one
   street, one afternoon). Claim 2 — unsound leap / no evidence
   offered / insufficient. Claim 3 — unsound (popularity) /
   irrelevant / not applicable. Claim 4 — unsound absolute ("all") /
   partly relevant / insufficient.
2. Sound rewrite of Claim 2, e.g.: "Cooler streets may encourage more
   people to walk short trips." (Narrowed, conditional, no invented
   facts.)
3. The title's "every problem above is solved" is a conclusion. It
   would need evidence for every claim, including the leaps, plus
   evidence that no new problems arise.
4. The classmate is half right: one street *is* thin evidence
   (sufficiency), but that does not make the claim "bad" outright.
   Correct judgment: sound reasoning, relevant evidence, limited
   sufficiency — fix by measuring more streets and days.

**Applied task:** four sentences: what it argues; strongest claim
(Claim 1) with verdicts; weakest claim (Claim 3 or 2) with verdicts;
what evidence would change the learner's mind (e.g., multi-street,
multi-day measurements).

**Exit check:** "Most students like sleeping in" is a popularity
appeal: unsound reasoning, and liking something is not evidence about
school start times. Verdict: unsound.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice:**

1. Table row example — temperature study: A uses the 16-degree ground
   gap to argue the street "clearly changes"; B uses the 6-degree air
   gap to argue shade alone "will not make a street safe."
2. A emphasizes street life; B never mentions it. B emphasizes costs
   and maintenance; A never mentions them.
3. They disagree about *how*, not *whether*: A — "will vote this fall
   on whether to plant 200 new street trees" (with the article's
   support clear); B — "So plant the trees — but plant them with a
   budget…" Both favor planting.
4. The illustration portrays the shade/sun feeling well; it cannot
   portray costs, pollen, maintenance, or numbers. It supports
   Article A's case more.

**Independent practice:**

1. Comparison paragraph must include: same study, 16-degree vs.
   6-degree emphasis, A's interpretation ("clearly changes the
   street") and B's ("shade alone will not make a street safe").
2. B's position: plant trees, but phased, budgeted, and planned.
   "Enthusiasm plants a tree. Planning keeps it." distinguishes B's
   careful position from A's faster, fuller enthusiasm.
3. Image answer: the left (shaded) side feels cooler — shade covers
   the sidewalk, people walk and cycle comfortably; the right shows
   direct sun, heat lines, and a person fanning themselves. Plus one
   sentence on what the image leaves out (costs, pollen, maintenance).
4. Error: B does not "prove A wrong" — both favor planting, and B
   uses the same study. Accurate rewrite: "A and B use the same study
   but emphasize different numbers: A stresses the cooler ground, B
   stresses the smaller air gap, and B adds costs and care that A does
   not discuss."

**Applied task:** five sentences covering costs, the 6-degree
reading, pollen/storms, agreement (both favor planting), and the
learner's own recommendation with a reason.

**Exit check:** (a) A reads 16 degrees on the ground as a clear street
change; B reads 6 degrees in the air as too small to make a street
safe alone. (b) The illustration shows feeling instantly; it cannot
show numbers, costs, or conditions.

## Practice sessions

### Session A

1. §1 hints: cool pavement under shoes (CI1); hurrying vs. lingering
   behavior (CI2 hint); "different seasons" (both).
2. Both central-idea sentences must be matchable without help.
3. Faults: (a) opinion — "trees are wonderful and every town should
   plant them"; (b) missing central idea — street life (CI2) is
   absent; (c) invented fact — "birds sing beautifully at dawn" (the
   article says birds were *counted twice as many* in *afternoon*
   counts, and says nothing about singing or dawn).
4. Four sentences, each traceable to a named section; no opinions.
5. §5 turns ideas into a decision. A central idea states what the text
   is about; a conclusion states what should happen.
6. Section-4 idea, e.g.: "On cooler streets, neighbors linger and meet."
   It is a section idea because only §4 develops it; the article's
   main ideas span several sections.

### Session B

1. Five-link chain with labeled arrows (see Lesson 02 key for the
   pattern).
2. Two-way: people lingering makes the street livelier (§4 chairs,
   strollers); the livelier street draws more people ("when people use
   a street more, they meet each other more").
3. The measurement was caused by the existing idea that shade cools
   streets; it might cause the council vote and further planting.
4. Careful rewrite: "Hot streets are uncomfortable, so people hurry
   through them; shaded streets feel comfortable, so people linger and
   meet." (Mechanism restored; "happy" replaced with text-based
   behavior.)
5. Event caused by an idea: the science club's measurement (caused by
   the suspicion that shade cools streets).
6. Personal chain accepted if three links are labeled and causal.

### Session C

1. Passage X: compare/contrast. Article A §2: cause/effect; §3:
   evidence/elaboration; §5: problem/solution.
2. Passage X's job: introduce (it sets up a contrast the reader will
   explore) — or compare, if the learner defends it as the passage's
   whole job; accept either with a reason.
3. "Soft" connotes gentle, pleasant, comfortable air; "cool" states
   temperature only. Lost: the feeling that invites lingering.
4. "Safe" implies danger avoided (heat-wave risk), not just comfort;
   it serves B's caution: shade is not enough when air itself is
   dangerous.
5. A §2: "transpiration" — release of water vapor through leaf
   openings; needed for an exact mechanism. B §2: "pruning" — cutting
   branches to shape and maintain a tree; needed because care costs
   must name real tasks, not vague "care."
6. §3 is not mere description: it is evidence/elaboration — its job is
   to *prove* central idea 1 with numbers, not to paint a scene.

### Session D

1. Passage Y: explain (how-to instructions). Passage Z: persuade
   ("should keep," reasons about study space). Article A §5: persuade
   (vote language, "choosing… what kind of place").
2. A: plant 200 trees, for cooling and street life. B: plant trees, but
   phased, budgeted, and planned. Differing words: *phased / budgeted /
   planned* vs. *200 trees this fall's vote*.
3. B's concession: "None of this means planting is wrong." It gives up
   the anti-planting reading and protects the plan-carefully position.
   (Also acceptable: "That gap is real, and it matters.")
4. Missing: purpose's persuasive goal and its distinguishing position.
   Correct: "B's purpose is to persuade the town to plant carefully —
   phased, budgeted, planned — by informing readers about costs and
   limits."
5. Two sentences using the §5 numbers-vs-fuller-story contrast: the
   author sets her fuller position against the numbers-only position
   without naming an opponent.
6. Accept any sentence conceding B's strongest point (costs, the
   6-degree air gap, or pollen) while keeping A's position (plant the
   trees).

### Session E

1. Claim 4 chart row: reason — leaves catch dust; evidence — leaves
   catch dust (from the passage); sound? No — "all" is absolute, and
   dust is not all pollution; relevant? Partly — dust bears on air
   quality but not on "all"; sufficient? No — one mechanism cannot
   support an absolute; fix — narrow to "trees can reduce some airborne
   dust."
2. Absolute word: "everyone" (also "will" as certainty). Rewrite, e.g.:
   "Cooler streets may encourage more people to walk short trips."
3. Defensible answers either way if argued from the passage; the
   expected answer: irrelevant evidence is worse, because no amount of
   it helps (Claim 3's popularity point), while insufficient evidence
   can be fixed by gathering more (Claim 1).
4. Relevant but insufficient: Claim 1 (temperatures support cooling but
   rest on one street, one afternoon). Unsound regardless of amount:
   Claim 3 (popularity never proves a plan right).
5. Careful conclusion, e.g.: "Planting trees may cool our streets, but
   the town needs more measurements and a maintenance budget before
   deciding how many to plant."
6. Specific study design: several streets (shaded and sunny), several
   days including a heat-wave day, ground and air temperatures, and
   counts of walkers — any specific design naming streets, days, and
   measurements earns credit.

### Session F

1. Table rows: temperatures — A: 88 vs. 104, 90 vs. 96, headline 16;
   B: same numbers, headline 6. Street life — A: full section; B:
   absent. Costs — A: absent; B: $300/$50/$60,000/$10,000/$8,000.
   Pollen/storms — A: absent; B: full section. Conclusion — A: plant
   200; B: plant, but plan.
2. Paragraph beginning "Both authors use the same temperature study,
   but…" covering emphasis (16 vs. 6) and interpretation (street
   change vs. not safe alone).
3. Left side feels cooler: shade, comfortable walkers, cyclist; the
   right shows sun, heat lines, a fanning person. Cannot show: costs,
   pollen, maintenance, numbers.
4. Video of B's cost section: cracked sidewalks would hit harder
   visually than words; the 6-degree vs. 16-degree number comparison
   would land less strongly, because numbers need pausing and rereading.
5. Video-review frame applied to the illustration: claim — shaded
   streets feel cooler; visual evidence — shade over the sidewalk and
   comfortable walkers/cyclist; contrasting evidence — sun, heat
   lines, fanning person; follow-up question — any question about
   costs, pollen, or measurement (e.g., "How much cooler, in degrees?").
6. Errors: (a) B *does* have numbers (costs and the 6-degree gap) —
   it is not opinion-only; (b) having numbers does not make A "right"
   — claims are judged claim by claim, and the authors largely agree
   about planting. Correction: "Both authors use numbers; they differ
   in which numbers they emphasize and what they conclude."

## Unit assessment

### Part 1

1. CI1: "Green roofs measurably cool roof surfaces and the rooms
   below them." CI2: "Green roofs are a good tool only for suitable
   buildings — they have structural, cost, and care limits."
2. CI1 introduced in §1 (classrooms under the green roof "stay
   noticeably cooler") and developed in §3, which adds measurements
   (138 vs. 94 degrees on the surface; 4 degrees in classrooms).
   Accept CI2's development (§4 adds limits) with the same structure.
3. Chain, e.g.: plants shade the roof → surface cooler → less heat
   enters → classroom cooler. Arrows labeled.
4. §3: evidence/elaboration; job: prove (central idea 1 with numbers).
5. "Acts like a meadow" suggests a living, green, natural place — a
   small ecosystem — where "covered in plants" states only coverage.
   The figure adds pleasantness and life.
6. Position: cautious support. Proof: "A green roof is a good tool for
   the right building — not a magic blanket for all of them."

### Part 2

7. Claims: (a) every school should install a green roof; (b) cooler
   classrooms mean students learn better and schools save millions;
   (c) most famous schools have green roofs, so every school should.
8. "Save millions": not relevant — the passage contains no cost or
   savings evidence at all; not sufficient — a money claim needs money
   data (installation cost, energy savings, maintenance cost).
9. Flaw: appeal to popularity/prestige ("most famous schools") —
   unsound; fame is not evidence about outcomes.
10. Careful conclusion, e.g.: "Schools whose roofs can hold wet soil
    should consider a green roof, since the Lincoln measurements show
    cooler surfaces and rooms — after a structural check and a
    maintenance plan." Accept variations that narrow "every" and
    condition on the passage's limits.

### Part 3

11. Similarity: both qualify their support instead of cheering
    unconditionally — green-roof author: "not a magic blanket for all
    of them"; Okafor: "plant — but plan." Difference: the green-roof
    author qualifies by *building suitability* (weight, leaks, care),
    while Okafor qualifies by *budget, species, and phasing* (costs,
    pollen, sidewalk checks). Paragraph must quote one detail from
    each text.
12. The photograph portrays the green, living roof surface well; it
    cannot portray temperatures, insulation, costs, structural needs,
    or maintenance — all of which the passage states in words.

## Project scoring notes

Use the rubric in [project.md](project.md). Common scoring traps:

- A briefing that quotes long stretches of the articles instead of
  writing in the learner's own words cannot score "Meeting" on briefing
  writing, even if its facts are right.
- A claim-evaluation chart that labels every claim "good" or "bad"
  without separating soundness, relevance, and sufficiency cannot score
  above "Developing" on claim evaluation.
- A sources note that calls the articles' numbers real, or omits the
  practice-data label, fails sources honesty regardless of the rest
  of the briefing — return it for revision before portfolio.
- A recommendation of "do not plant" is acceptable if supported by the
  table (e.g., from B's cost and care evidence); score the support,
  not the side chosen.
