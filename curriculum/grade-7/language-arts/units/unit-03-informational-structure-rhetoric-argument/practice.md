# Practice Sessions — Unit 03

Six practice sessions, one per skill cluster. Each session takes about
45 minutes. All items use Anchor Articles A and B (Lessons 01 and 06)
unless a new short passage is printed here. Answers are in
[answer-key.md](answer-key.md).

## Session A — Central-idea hunt and summary repair (Week 11, Session 2)

Use Article A.

1. Underline (or list) every sentence in section 1 that hints at a
   central idea. How many hints did you find, and which central idea
   does each hint belong to?
2. Write the two central ideas of Article A in your own words, one
   sentence each. Swap papers with a partner (or read yours aloud to the
   adult): can the listener match each sentence to the right idea
   without your help?
3. Summary repair: here is a faulty summary. Find and fix its three
   faults (one opinion, one missing central idea, one invented fact).
   Faulty summary: "Trees are wonderful and every town should plant
   them. The article explains how shade cools streets. It also says
   birds sing beautifully on Maple Street at dawn."
4. Write a four-sentence objective summary of Article A. Rule: every
   sentence must be traceable to a named section.
5. Which section of Article A develops *neither* central idea directly,
   but instead turns them into a decision? What does that tell you
   about the difference between a central idea and a conclusion?
6. Short challenge: write a one-sentence central idea for section 4
   alone. Then explain in one sentence why it is a *section* idea, not
   one of the article's two main central ideas.

## Session B — Interaction chains (Week 11, Session 4)

Use Article A.

1. Build a chain of five links from "sunlight hits pavement" to "the
   town council votes." Label each arrow (idea → individuals,
   individuals → events, or events → ideas).
2. Find a two-way interaction in section 4: something people do that
   changes the street, and a way the changed street changes what people
   do. Quote one detail for each direction.
3. The science club appears in section 3. Name one thing that caused
   their measurement and two things their measurement might cause.
4. Rewrite this weak chain so it is careful and text-based: "Heat makes
   people unhappy, so trees make towns happy."
5. What event in Article A happens *because* of an idea, not because of
   a person? Name the idea and the event.
6. Extension: draw the same kind of chain for your own morning (idea →
   person → event). One chain, three links, labeled.

## Session C — Structure sort and word-choice clinic (Week 12, Session 6)

1. Sort these four short passages by structure (description,
   cause/effect, problem/solution, compare/contrast). One passage is
   printed below; the other three are Article A sections 2, 3, and 5 —
   name the section for each.
   - Passage X: "Two lunchrooms serve the same school. In the north
     lunchroom, students eat in twenty minutes and leave. In the south
     lunchroom, round tables and windows slow everyone down, and
     students stay to talk. Same food, same students — different
     rooms, different lunch."
2. For Passage X, name one job the passage does (introduce, explain,
   prove, widen, decide) and defend it in one sentence.
3. Word choice: Article A says the air on Maple Street "felt soft."
   What does "soft" connote here? Replace it with "cool" — what is lost?
4. Word choice: Article B says shade "will not make a street safe" on a
   heat-wave day. What does "safe" imply that "cool" would not? How
   does that word choice serve B's position?
5. Find one technical word in Article A section 2 and one in Article B
   section 2 (costs section). For each: give its precise meaning and
   explain why the author needed a technical word there instead of an
   everyday word.
6. Error analysis: "Section 3 of Article A is description because it
   describes temperatures." Why is that label incomplete? Give the
   better label and one reason.

## Session D — Purpose and position sort (Week 12, Session 8)

1. Label the purpose of each short passage (inform, explain, persuade)
   and quote the words that prove it:
   - Passage Y: "To change a tire, first loosen the lug nuts while the
     car is still on the ground. Then jack up the car…"
   - Passage Z: "Our school should keep the library open late. Students
     with no quiet room at home need somewhere to study, and the
     library is already staffed until five."
   - Article A, section 5 (reread it before answering).
2. State Article B's position in one sentence. Then state Article A's
   position in one sentence. Underline the words where the two positions
   differ.
3. Find one concession in Article B. What claim does it give up, and
   what claim does it protect?
4. A classmate says Article B's purpose is "to inform people about
   trees." What is missing from that answer? Rewrite it correctly.
5. Article A never names an opponent. Write two sentences explaining
   how it still distinguishes its position (use the section 5
   numbers-vs-fuller-story contrast).
6. Extension: write one sentence in which Article A's author concedes
   Article B's strongest point without abandoning her own position.

## Session E — Argument-evaluation clinic (Week 13, Session 10)

Use the Lesson 05 argument passage.

1. Complete this chart row for Claim 4 ("trees will remove all air
   pollution"): reason / evidence / sound? / relevant? / sufficient? /
   one-sentence fix.
2. The passage says "everyone will walk everywhere." Circle the
   absolute word. Rewrite the claim without it so the reasoning could
   become sound.
3. Which is worse for an argument: irrelevant evidence or insufficient
   evidence? Defend your answer with one example from the passage.
4. Find one claim in the passage whose evidence is relevant but
   insufficient, and one whose reasoning is unsound no matter how much
   evidence is added. Explain the difference.
5. The passage's conclusion is "every problem above is solved." What
   would a careful writer conclude instead? Write the replacement
   conclusion in one sentence.
6. Extension: take the passage's strongest claim and write the
   additional evidence you would need to call it fully sufficient. Be
   specific: how many streets, how many days, what measurements?

## Session F — Two-author comparison and media comparison (Week 13, Session 12)

Use Articles A and B and the Lesson 06 illustration.

1. Complete the comparison table: rows = temperature numbers, street
   life, costs, pollen/storms, conclusion; columns = Article A,
   Article B.
2. Write a paragraph that begins "Both authors use the same temperature
   study, but…" and covers emphasis and interpretation.
3. Answer the illustration question from the text alternative: which
   side feels cooler, with what visible evidence? Then add one sentence
   about what the illustration cannot show.
4. Imagine a video version of Article B: a narrator reads the cost
   section over photos of cracked sidewalks. What would the video
   portray *more* strongly than the text, and what would it portray
   *less* strongly? Two sentences each.
5. Use the video-review template
   (`../../templates/video-reviews.md`) to review the illustration as
   if it were a short video: record its "claim," two pieces of visual
   evidence, and one follow-up question.
6. Error analysis: "Article A is right and Article B is wrong because
   A has numbers and B only has opinions." Name two errors in that
   sentence and correct each.
