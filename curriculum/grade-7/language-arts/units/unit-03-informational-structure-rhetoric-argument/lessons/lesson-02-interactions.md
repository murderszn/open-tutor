# Lesson 02 — How Individuals, Events, and Ideas Interact

**Goal:** I can analyze how individuals, events, and ideas push on each
other in an informational text.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** RI.7.3
**Materials:** Article A (Lesson 01), notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Quick chain, aloud: the sun heats pavement (event) → pavement gives off
heat (effect) → people hurry through (response). What happens next in
real life — do hurrying people change the street? Keep that question; it
is today's whole lesson.

## Teaching: interactions run both ways

Informational texts are full of arrows, not just facts. An **idea** can
change what **individuals** do; **individuals** can change what
**events** happen; **events** can change which **ideas** people believe.
Most texts contain chains with at least three links, and good readers
notice when an arrow runs *both* ways: cooler streets make people walk
more, and people walking more make a street feel safer and livelier,
which makes more people walk.

Three interaction patterns to look for:

1. **Idea → individuals:** a new understanding changes behavior. (People
   learn shade cools streets, so they choose the shaded route.)
2. **Individuals → events:** people's actions create or change an event.
   (The science club measures temperatures, so the town council gets
   numbers to vote on.)
3. **Events → ideas:** something that happens changes what people think.
   (The hot walk home gives the author the idea that two streets can
   feel like different seasons.)

### Worked example 1 — a three-link chain

Claim: "Cooler streets change how people use their town."

- Link 1 (idea → individuals): trees shade the sidewalk, so the walk
  feels comfortable (section 1: the Maple Street walk).
- Link 2 (individuals → event): comfortable walkers linger — parents
  with strollers, teenagers at the corner court, shopkeepers setting out
  chairs (section 4).
- Link 3 (event → idea): a street full of people becomes evidence for a
  bigger idea — a tree is "an invitation," not just a thermometer change
  (section 4's last line), which feeds the council vote (section 5).

One chain, three link types, all cited.

### Worked example 2 — the same event, two arrows

The science club's measurement (section 3) is an event with arrows in
two directions:

- **Backward:** the club measured *because* the author and others already
  suspected shade cools streets — an idea caused the event.
- **Forward:** the measurements give the council numbers, which may
  cause the planting vote — the event may cause a new event.

A common error is to draw only the forward arrow. Ask always: *what made
this happen, and what does it make happen next?*

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Draw this chain from Article A and label each arrow with its pattern
   (idea → individuals, individuals → events, or events → ideas):
   hot sun → trees planted → shaded sidewalk → neighbors linger →
   council considers planting more trees.
2. In section 4, shopkeepers set out chairs. Is that an individual action,
   an event, or both? Defend your answer in two sentences.
3. Find one place where an *event* changes an *idea* in Article A.
   Quote the sentence and name the event and the idea.
4. The author walks two streets in section 1. Which later event in the
   article would not have happened without that walk? Explain.

## Independent practice

1. Write a four-link chain that starts with "a tree releases water vapor
   (transpiration)" and ends with "the town council votes." Label every
   arrow.
2. Section 5 says the town "is choosing, degree by degree, what kind of
   place it wants to be." Is that sentence describing an interaction or
   stating a central idea? Explain in two sentences.
3. Name one interaction in Article A that runs *both* ways (A changes B,
   and B changes A). Quote one detail for each direction.
4. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Trees cause people to be friendly."
   What is wrong with that chain? Rewrite it as a careful, text-based
   chain of at least three links.

## Applied task

Explain to a younger reader, in five sentences, how one idea from
Article A (shade cools streets) ends up changing what a whole town
decides. Use the words *because*, *so*, and *then* at least once each,
and cite one detail from the article.

## Exit check

Name one individual, one event, and one idea from Article A, and draw
the arrows between them in one sentence ("X led to Y, which led to Z").

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** use the frame "___ happened, so ___ did ___, which meant
  ___." Fill it three times from different sections before attempting a
  four-link chain.
- **Extension:** add a *broken* link to your chain: one place where the
  article's chain could snap (for example, if nobody maintains the
  trees). Write one sentence about what breaks and why. This prepares
  Lesson 06, where the second author attacks exactly such links.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners confuse a *sequence* (first this, then
that) with an *interaction* (this *causes* that). Insist on a cause
word — because, so, led to — and a quoted detail for every arrow. The
error-analysis item (independent practice 4) is the lesson's key check:
"trees cause friendliness" skips the mechanism (cooler → comfortable →
lingering → meeting) and states a personality claim the text never makes.
