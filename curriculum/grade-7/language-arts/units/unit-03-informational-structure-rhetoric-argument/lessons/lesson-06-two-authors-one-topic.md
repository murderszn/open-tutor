# Lesson 06 — Two Authors, One Topic — and Text vs. Image

**Goal:** I can compare how two authors shape the same topic with
different evidence and interpretations, and compare a text to a visual
version of its subject.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** RI.7.9, RI.7.7
**Materials:** Article A (Lesson 01), Article B below, the generated
illustration in this lesson, notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Two weather reports describe the same day. One says "a pleasant 75
degrees with a light breeze." The other says "only 75 degrees — the
coldest day this month." Same facts, different stories. Which report
would you pack a jacket for? That gap — same facts, different emphasis
— is today's lesson.

## Anchor Article B (read together first)

*The article below is original, written for this unit. Its author,
Daniel Okafor, is fictional, and its numbers are a **fictional practice
dataset** — the same fictional dataset as Article A, plus fictional
cost figures for analysis practice. Never cite these numbers as
real-world facts.*

# Not So Fast: The Other Side of Planting More Street Trees

*by Daniel Okafor*

## The Same Numbers, Read Differently

You have probably read that shaded sidewalks run cooler — 88 degrees
in the shade against 104 in the sun, on one measured afternoon. That gap
is real, and it matters. But look at the second number in that same
study: the air two feet above the sidewalks was 90 degrees over the
shaded street and 96 over the sunny one — a difference of only 6
degrees. Shade cools the ground you walk on far more than it cools the
air you breathe. On a true heat-wave day, when the air itself is
dangerous, shade alone will not make a street safe. Trees help; they do
not fix heat.

## What Trees Cost

A street tree is not free, and it is not one purchase. Planting one tree
costs about $300, and caring for it costs about $50 every year after
that — watering, pruning, and inspection. Plant 200 trees and the town
has spent $60,000 before the first leaf falls, plus $10,000 every year to
keep them alive. Roots add their own bill: on one block of Elm Street,
roots lifted the sidewalk so badly that repairs cost $8,000. A town that
plants without budgeting for care is buying a problem it will meet in
ten years.

## Not Everyone Loves Pollen

Some residents dread planting season for a different reason: pollen.
Certain popular street trees release clouds of it each spring, and
neighbors with allergies pay for other people's shade with sneezing
weeks. Storms add risk, too — a weak limb over a sidewalk can fall on
exactly the people the shade was meant to comfort. None of this means
planting is wrong. It means the *choice of tree* and the *plan for care*
matter as much as the number planted.

## Plant — But Plan

So plant the trees — but plant them with a budget, a species list that
avoids heavy pollen, and a schedule for pruning and sidewalk checks.
Spread the 200 trees over several years instead of one, and measure the
results street by street. A town that plans its planting gets the shade
without the surprise bills. Enthusiasm plants a tree. Planning keeps it.

## Teaching: emphasis and interpretation

When two authors write about the same topic, compare them on two axes:

1. **Emphasis:** which evidence does each author spend words on, and
   which does each author skip or shrink? Article A spends a whole
   section on street life and none on costs. Article B spends a whole
   section on costs and none on street life. Neither author lies; each
   author *selects*.
2. **Interpretation:** where both authors use the *same* fact, what
   different conclusion does each draw? Article A reads 88 vs. 104 as
   "clearly changes the street." Article B reads the same study's 90 vs.
   96 as "only 6 degrees — shade alone will not make a street safe."
   Same study, different headline.

Also notice what the two authors *agree* on: both favor planting trees.
Their disagreement is not yes-or-no; it is *how* — fast and many (A's
implied pace) versus phased and budgeted (B's plan). Most real
disagreements look like this, and a reader who turns every disagreement
into enemies vs. heroes will misread both authors.

**Text vs. image (RI.7.7):** a visual version of a subject portrays it
differently from words. An image shows feeling instantly — you *see*
shade vs. sun — but it cannot show numbers, costs, or conditions. Ask:
*what does this medium emphasize, and what can it not show at all?*

### Worked example 1 — same numbers, different interpretation

| | Article A | Article B |
|---|---|---|
| Fact used | 88 vs. 104 (ground); 90 vs. 96 (air) | Same four numbers |
| Number emphasized | 16-degree ground difference | 6-degree air difference |
| Interpretation | "It clearly changes the street you walk on" | "Shade alone will not make a street safe" |
| Conclusion drawn | Plant 200 trees | Plant — but phased, budgeted, planned |

Neither author misquotes the study. The difference is *which* number
gets the headline and what conclusion is drawn from it.

### Worked example 2 — image vs. text

- The illustration **emphasizes** what Article A emphasizes: the felt
  contrast of shade vs. sun. It agrees with A's central idea 1.
- The illustration **cannot show** anything Article B cares about:
  no price tags, no pollen, no maintenance schedule, no 6-degree air
  gap. A reader who only saw the image would never learn B's case.
- Verdict: the image portrays the subject as a *feeling* (relief vs.
  discomfort); the articles portray it as a *decision* (with numbers,
  costs, and conditions). Medium changes the message.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Fill one row of a comparison table: one piece of evidence that
   appears in *both* articles, what A does with it, and what B does
   with it.
2. Name one topic A emphasizes that B never mentions, and one topic B
   emphasizes that A never mentions.
3. Do A and B disagree about *whether* to plant trees, or about *how*?
   Quote one sentence from each article to defend your answer.
4. Look at the illustration. Name one thing it portrays well and one
   thing it cannot portray. Which article's case does it support more?

## Independent practice

1. Write a comparison paragraph: "Both authors use the same
   temperature study, but…" Include the 16-degree and 6-degree numbers
   and each author's interpretation.
2. Article B says "Enthusiasm plants a tree. Planning keeps it." What
   is B's position in one sentence, and how does that sentence
   distinguish B's position from A's?
3. Using the text alternative above, answer the image question in
   writing (which side feels cooler, with what evidence) — then add one
   sentence about what the image leaves out that Article B raises.
4. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Article B proves Article A
   wrong." What is wrong with that sentence? Rewrite it as an accurate
   comparison in two sentences.

## Applied task

You are advising a friend who read only Article A. In five sentences,
tell them what Article B adds: one sentence on costs, one on the
6-degree reading, one on pollen/storms, one on what the authors agree
on, and one recommendation of your own (phased planting, fast planting,
or something else) with a reason.

## Exit check

In two sentences: (a) how do A and B interpret the same temperature
study differently? (b) What can the illustration show that the articles
cannot, and what can it not show?

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** comparison paragraphs are easier from a table. Fill the
  table (evidence / A's use / B's use) before writing any paragraph.
- **Extension:** write a one-paragraph "Article C" from a shopkeeper's
  point of view on the same street: one thing the shopkeeper gains from
  shade, one cost or worry, and a position. Keep all numbers fictional
  and labeled as practice data.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners turn comparison into a contest with a
winner ("B debunks A"). Both authors favor planting; the skill is
describing *emphasis and interpretation differences*, not crowning a
victor. The error-analysis item targets this directly. Second
misconception: treating the illustration as evidence ("the picture
proves trees work"). The image is an *illustration of a feeling*, not
data — it cannot prove anything, and Lesson 06's verdict language
should be reused whenever images appear in later units.
