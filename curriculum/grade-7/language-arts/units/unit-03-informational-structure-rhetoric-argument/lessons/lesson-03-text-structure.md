# Lesson 03 — Text Structure and Word Choice

**Goal:** I can name the structure an author uses, explain what each
major section does for the whole, and analyze how a word choice affects
meaning and tone.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** RI.7.5, RI.7.4
**Materials:** Article A (Lesson 01), notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Look at Article A's five headings. Without rereading, guess: which
section explains a *cause*, which one gives *numbers*, and which one
argues for a *decision*? Check your guesses after the teaching.

## Teaching: structure is the author's skeleton

Authors organize informational text with a few reusable structures:

- **Description:** paints a scene or lists features ("A Hot Walk Home").
- **Cause and effect:** shows why something happens and what follows.
- **Problem and solution:** names a problem, then proposes a fix.
- **Compare and contrast:** sets two things side by side.
- **Chronological order:** events in time order.
- **Evidence and elaboration:** a claim followed by numbers, examples,
  or expert detail.

The question that matters is never just "what structure is this?" It is
"**what does this section do for the whole?**" A section can introduce
an idea, explain it, prove it, widen it, or turn it into a decision. An
article that only proved and never decided would feel unfinished; one
that decided without proving would feel unconvincing.

**Word choice** works the same way. A *technical* word (transpiration)
carries a precise meaning. A *connotative* word (soft, beat, hurried)
carries a feeling. Authors choose connotative words to set tone: the
same sun can "shine" (neutral), "beat down" (hostile), or "warm"
(pleasant). Ask: *what feeling does this word add that a plainer word
would not?*

### Worked example 1 — mapping sections to jobs

| Section | Main structure | What it does for the whole |
|---|---|---|
| A Hot Walk Home | Description + compare/contrast (two streets) | **Introduces** both central ideas as felt experience, before any explanation |
| How Shade Cools a Street | Cause and effect | **Explains** the mechanism behind central idea 1 |
| What the Numbers Show | Evidence and elaboration | **Proves** central idea 1 with measured numbers |
| More Than Cool Air | Cause and effect + examples | **Widens** to central idea 2: effects on people and street life |
| A Choice for the Town | Problem and solution (vote as solution) | **Turns** both ideas into a decision the reader can picture |

Notice the order is not random: feel → why → how much → so what → now
what. Move "What the Numbers Show" before "How Shade Cools" and the
numbers would mean nothing, because the reader would not yet know what
mechanism they measure.

### Worked example 2 — one word, two tones

Section 1: "the same sun **beat** straight down" on Oak Street.

- Denotation (dictionary meaning): the sun shone directly.
- Connotation: "beat" suggests attack, pounding, something to escape
  from — it matches "I hurried" in the same sentence.
- Tone effect: if the author had written "the same sun *shone* straight
  down," the sentence would be neutral and the contrast with Maple
  Street's "soft" air would nearly vanish. Word choice does argument
  work here: it makes the reader *feel* why the shaded street matters
  before any number is given.
- Technical contrast: "transpiration" in section 2 does the opposite
  job — it is precise and unemotional, which is exactly what an
  explanation section needs to sound trustworthy.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Section 4 ("More Than Cool Air") mixes two structures. Name both and
   give one detail for each.
2. What would break if section 5 came *first*? Name two things the
   reader would not yet know.
3. Find the word "linger" in section 4. What does it connote that
   "stay" would not? What does that connotation do for central idea 2?
4. Section 2 uses the technical word "transpiration" and then defines it
   in the same sentence. Why define it there instead of assuming the
   reader knows it?

## Independent practice

1. Label each section of Article A with one structure word and one job
   word (introduce / explain / prove / widen / decide). One section may
   have two structure words.
2. Choose one connotative word from section 1 and one technical word
   from section 2. For each, write: denotation, connotation or precise
   meaning, and the tone or trust effect.
3. Error analysis: a classmate labels the whole article "problem and
   solution" because it ends with a vote. Why is that label too narrow?
   Name two sections it fails to describe.
4. Rewrite this sentence twice, changing only one word each time to
   change its tone: "On Oak Street, people hurried through." First make
   it sound pleasant, then make it sound worse. Name the word you changed
   each time.

## Applied task

Imagine the author adds a sixth section, "What Could Go Wrong," after
section 4. In three sentences, argue where it should go and what job it
would do for the whole — and name one risk if it came *before* the
numbers section. (Lesson 06 will show you a real author who wrote that
section as a whole second article.)

## Exit check

In two sentences: name the structure of section 3 and explain what the
article would lose without it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if structure names blur together, sort by question: does
  this section ask *what happened* (description), *why* (cause/effect),
  *how much* (evidence), or *what should we do* (problem/solution)?
- **Extension:** diagram Article A's structure as a staircase with five
  steps, labeling each step's job. Then diagram a school textbook page
  the same way and note one structural difference.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners label a section by its surface content
("this section is about birds, so it is description") instead of its
function (the birds are *evidence* for central idea 2's widening). Push
for the job word every time: *what does this section do that no other
section does?* The "What Could Go Wrong" applied task previews Lesson
06's second author; do not resolve it now — let it hang.
