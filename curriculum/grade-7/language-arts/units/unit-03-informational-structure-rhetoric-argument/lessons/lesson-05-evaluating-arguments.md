# Lesson 05 — Tracing and Evaluating an Argument

**Goal:** I can trace an argument's claims and judge whether the
reasoning is sound and the evidence is relevant and sufficient.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** RI.7.8
**Materials:** notebook. (Article A is reused for one claim's evidence.)

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Which is stronger evidence for "this medicine works": "my aunt felt
better after taking it" or "in a study of 200 patients, 150 improved"?
Why? (Keep your answer — it is the difference between *relevant* and
*sufficient*.)

## Argument passage (read together first)

*The passage below is original, written for this unit. It is an
argument, not a report — your job is to test it, not to agree with it.*

# Plant 1,000 Trees and Our Problems Are Solved!

*An opinion piece by a fictional town resident*

Our town should plant 1,000 trees this year. First, trees cool our
streets. On Maple Street, the shaded sidewalk measured 88 degrees while
the sunny sidewalk on Oak Street measured 104 degrees, so shade clearly
works. Second, once the streets are cool, everyone will walk everywhere
and we will not need buses at all — cool streets solve transportation.
Third, most people in this town already agree that trees are wonderful,
which proves this plan is right. Finally, trees clean the air: leaves
catch dust from the air, so planting trees will remove all air
pollution from our town. One thousand trees, and every problem above is
solved.

## Teaching: claim, reason, evidence — then three verdicts

An argument is a stack of **claims** (what the author wants you to
believe), each held up by a **reason** (why it should be true) and
**evidence** (facts, numbers, examples). Tracing an argument means
separating the stack into claims before judging any of it. Then give
three verdicts per claim:

1. **Is the reasoning sound?** Does the conclusion actually follow from
   the reason, or does it leap? (A leap: "cool streets, therefore no
   buses needed." An appeal to popularity: "most people agree, therefore
   it is right." An absolute: "all," "every," "never" — one exception
   breaks it.)
2. **Is the evidence relevant?** Does it actually bear on *this* claim?
   (Dust caught by leaves is relevant to "trees help air quality" but
   not to "trees remove *all* pollution.")
3. **Is the evidence sufficient?** Is there *enough* of it — more than
   one example, a big enough sample, a representative case? (One street,
   one afternoon, is a start, not a proof.)

A claim can pass one verdict and fail another. That split is the whole
skill: "relevant but insufficient" is a different judgment from
"irrelevant," and it leads to a different fix.

### Worked example 1 — Claim 1: "Trees cool our streets"

- Reason: shade blocks sun and cools surfaces.
- Evidence: Maple Street 88 degrees in shade, Oak Street 104 in sun.
- Sound? Yes — the reason connects to the claim without a leap.
- Relevant? Yes — sidewalk temperatures bear directly on cooling.
- Sufficient? Only partly — one street pair on one afternoon is thin
  evidence for a whole town's plan. More streets and days would
  strengthen it.
- Verdict: **sound reasoning, relevant evidence, limited sufficiency.**
  Fix: gather more measurements, not a different argument.

### Worked example 2 — Claim 3: "Most people agree, so the plan is right"

- Reason: popularity.
- Evidence: "most people agree trees are wonderful."
- Sound? No — popularity is not proof. Many people can agree on a bad
  plan, and "wonderful" is an opinion, not a measurement.
- Relevant? No — agreement does not bear on whether the plan *works*.
- Sufficient? Not applicable — no amount of agreement would fix an
  unsound link.
- Verdict: **unsound reasoning; evidence irrelevant to the claim.**
  Fix: replace the popularity point with evidence about outcomes.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. List the passage's four claims in your own words, one sentence each.
2. For Claim 2 ("everyone will walk everywhere, no buses needed"), name
   the leap: what two steps does the author skip between "cool streets"
   and "no buses"?
3. Claim 4 says trees will remove *all* air pollution, and its evidence
   is that leaves catch dust. Is that evidence relevant, sufficient,
   both, or neither? Explain in two sentences.
4. Which claim is the strongest, and which verdicts does it pass? Which
   is the weakest, and why?

## Independent practice

1. Fill a claim-evidence-verdict chart for all four claims: claim,
   reason, evidence, and three verdicts (sound? relevant? sufficient?)
   with one reason each.
2. Rewrite Claim 2 so its reasoning becomes sound. You may narrow the
   claim, add a condition, or both — but you may not invent new facts.
3. The passage's title says "every problem above is solved." Is that a
   claim, a reason, or a conclusion? What evidence would be needed to
   support it?
4. Error analysis: a classmate judges Claim 1 "bad, because one street
   is not enough evidence." What is right and what is wrong in that
   judgment? Rewrite it using the three-verdict language.

## Applied task

Write a four-sentence evaluation of the whole passage for a reader who
has not read it: one sentence saying what it argues, two sentences
naming its strongest and weakest claims with verdicts, and one sentence
saying what evidence would change your mind.

## Exit check

Judge this new claim in two sentences: "School should start later
because most students like sleeping in." Name the reasoning flaw and
the verdict it earns.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** use the sentence frame for every verdict: "The reasoning
  is ___ because ___. The evidence is ___ because ___." Fill it before
  writing any paragraph.
- **Extension:** take Claim 1's verdict ("relevant but insufficient")
  and design the smallest study that would make it sufficient: how many
  streets, how many days, what measurements? One paragraph.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners judge an argument by whether they *agree*
with its conclusion ("I like trees, so the argument is good") instead
of by its reasoning and evidence. The exit check is designed to catch
this: most learners like sleeping in, and the claim is still unsound.
Keep agreement and evaluation in separate sentences all unit long. Also
watch for "all evidence is good evidence" — relevance must be judged
claim by claim, not passage by passage.
