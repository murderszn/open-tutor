# Lesson 04 — Author's Point of View, Purpose, and Distinguishing Positions

**Goal:** I can determine an author's point of view or purpose and
analyze how the author distinguishes that position from the positions of
others.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** RI.7.6
**Materials:** Article A (Lesson 01), notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence: does Article A want you to *know* something, or
does it want you to *do* something? Point to one sentence that proves
your answer.

## Teaching: purpose and position

Every informational author writes from a **point of view** — a stance
toward the topic — and with a **purpose**: usually to inform, to explain,
or to persuade. Purpose is not declared; it is shown by what the author
spends the most words on and what the ending asks of the reader. An
article that ends with a vote is not merely informing you.

Strong authors also **distinguish** their position from other positions.
They do it in a few recognizable moves:

- **Concession:** admitting a limit of your own case ("Shade does not
  change the weather…") so you do not sound exaggerated — this separates
  your careful position from an extreme version of it.
- **Contrast:** setting your position against a narrower or opposite
  one ("The numbers say… The fuller story says…") so the reader sees
  what makes yours different.
- **Answering an unspoken objection:** raising the doubt a skeptic would
  raise, then answering it, before the skeptic raises it.

A position is not the same as a central idea. A central idea says what
the text is *about* ("trees cool streets"). A position says what the
author *wants* ("the town should plant 200 trees, and for fuller reasons
than temperature alone").

### Worked example 1 — purpose from the ending

Section 5: "Our town council will vote this fall on whether to plant 200
new street trees."

- If the purpose were only to inform, the article could end at section
  3's numbers. It does not.
- Sections 4 and 5 spend their words on street life and on a vote — the
  language of persuasion ("choosing… what kind of place it wants to be").
- Verdict: the purpose is **to persuade** the reader (and, through the
  reader, the town) to support planting, **by informing** — explanation
  and numbers are the persuasion's tools, not its goal. A purpose can
  have a tool; name the goal first.

### Worked example 2 — distinguishing by concession and contrast

Two distinguishing moves in Article A:

1. **Concession** (section 3): "Shade does not change the weather, but
   it clearly changes the street you walk on." The author gives up the
   exaggerated claim (trees fix the weather) to protect the real one
   (trees change the street). Her position is now distinct from the
   overclaiming version of her own side.
2. **Contrast** (section 5): "The numbers say the trees will cool our
   sidewalks. The fuller story says they will also change how we live
   on those sidewalks." The author distinguishes her fuller position
   (plant for street life, not just temperature) from the narrower
   numbers-only position — and she does it without naming an opponent.
   Distinguishing does not require a quoted enemy; it requires a visible
   difference.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. In one sentence, state Article A's position (not a central idea — what
   the author *wants*).
2. Find the concession sentence in section 3. What exaggerated claim
   does it give up, and what claim does it protect?
3. Section 5's "numbers say… fuller story says…" contrasts two
   positions. Name both positions in your own words.
4. Is there any sentence where the author quotes or names an opponent?
   If not, how does she still distinguish her position? Answer in two
   sentences.

## Independent practice

1. Write the author's purpose in this frame: "The author's purpose is to
   ___ (inform/explain/persuade) by ___." Give one quoted detail for
   each blank.
2. A classmate says the author's position is "trees are good." Rewrite
   that as a precise position statement in one sentence, using the vote
   and the fuller reason.
3. Find one more distinguishing move in Article A that the worked
   examples did not name. Quote it and name the move (concession,
   contrast, or answered objection).
4. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "The author's point of view is
   first person because she says 'I walked.'" What is confused here?
   Rewrite the answer correctly in two sentences. (Hint: point of view
   in RI.7.6 means stance, not pronouns.)

## Applied task

Write a two-sentence paragraph in the voice of a town resident who
*opposes* planting 200 trees, but who has read Article A. The paragraph
must concede one true point from Article A and then state the resident's
different position. Do not invent facts; use only Article A's numbers
and ideas.

## Exit check

One sentence for purpose, one sentence for position, one quoted
distinguishing sentence — all about Article A.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if purpose and position blur, use this test: purpose
  answers "why did she write this?"; position answers "what does she
  want to happen?" Different questions, different answers.
- **Extension:** rewrite section 5's last paragraph as pure information
  (no persuasion, no vote, no "choosing"). What exactly did you have to
  remove? List the removed words — that list is the persuasion.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners equate point of view with grammatical
person ("I" = first person) or with topic ("trees"). RI.7.6 point of
view means the author's stance and how it is set apart from others. The
error-analysis item exists to catch the pronoun confusion early, because
it will otherwise resurface in Lesson 06 when two authors' stances must
be compared. Also watch for learners who claim the author "has no
position because she gives numbers" — numbers are evidence, and evidence
can serve a position.
