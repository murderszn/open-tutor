# Lesson 01 — Central Ideas, Their Development, and Objective Summary

**Goal:** I can find two or more central ideas in an informational text,
trace how each one develops, and write an objective summary.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Standards:** RI.7.2
**Materials:** this lesson, notebook, two colored pencils (one for central
ideas, one for evidence).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer aloud or in writing: what is the difference between a *topic* and a
*central idea*? (Answer: a topic is a word or short phrase — "street
trees." A central idea is a full sentence that says what the author thinks
*about* the topic — "Street trees cool a town's sidewalks.")

## Anchor Article A (read together first)

*The article below is original, written for this unit. Its author, Maya
Chen, is fictional, and its numbers are a **fictional practice dataset**
for analysis practice — not real measurements. Never cite these numbers
as real-world facts.*

# Cooler Streets: What Trees Do for a Town

*by Maya Chen*

## A Hot Walk Home

On the first hot Saturday of July, I walked home two ways. Down Maple
Street, where old oaks arch over the sidewalk, the air felt soft and the
pavement was cool under my shoes. Down Oak Street, which has no trees at
all, the same sun beat straight down, and I hurried from one thin strip of
building shade to the next. Same town, same afternoon, same sun — but the
two streets felt like different seasons.

## How Shade Cools a Street

A tree cools a street in two ways. First, its leaves block sunlight before
it reaches the pavement. Pavement in full sun soaks up heat all day and
gives it back slowly all evening; pavement in shade never heats up that
way in the first place. Second, a tree sweats. Through tiny openings in
its leaves, a tree releases water vapor into the air — a process called
transpiration — and as that water evaporates, it carries heat away with
it. Shade stops heat from arriving; transpiration helps remove the heat
that is already there.

## What the Numbers Show

Last summer, our town's student science club measured both streets on a
sunny 95-degree afternoon. The surface of the shaded sidewalk on Maple
Street measured 88 degrees, while the sunny sidewalk on Oak Street
measured 104 degrees — a difference of 16 degrees on the ground itself.
The air two feet above the sidewalks showed a smaller gap: 90 degrees
over Maple Street, 96 over Oak Street. Shade does not change the weather,
but it clearly changes the street you walk on.

## More Than Cool Air

Cooler streets do more than feel better. On Maple Street, neighbors
linger: parents push strollers, teenagers shoot baskets at the corner
court, and shopkeepers set out chairs. On Oak Street, people hurry
through. When a street is cooler, people use it more — and when people
use a street more, they meet each other more. Birds follow, too: the
science club counted twice as many birds on Maple Street as on Oak
Street during their afternoon counts. A tree is not just a thermometer
change; it is an invitation.

## A Choice for the Town

Our town council will vote this fall on whether to plant 200 new street
trees. The numbers say the trees will cool our sidewalks. The fuller
story says they will also change how we live on those sidewalks — more
walking, more neighbors, more time outside. A town that plants trees is
choosing, degree by degree, what kind of place it wants to be.

## Teaching: topic, central idea, development, summary

An informational text usually has **two or more central ideas**, not one.
Find them by asking: *what does the author keep coming back to, in
different sections, with new evidence each time?* Then trace
**development**: the first section introduces the idea, middle sections
add mechanism or numbers, later sections widen it to effects. A
**central idea** is never a single word, and it is never your opinion
about the topic.

An **objective summary** retells only what the text says, in your own
words, without "I think," "should," or praise/blame. Test every sentence
of a summary: *can I point to the part of the text that says this?*

### Worked example 1 — topic vs. central idea

- Topic: street trees. (Too small — it says nothing.)
- Not a central idea: "Trees are great." (That is an opinion, and it
  could be about any trees anywhere.)
- Central idea 1: "Street trees measurably cool the sidewalks and the
  air above them." (A sentence, about this text's topic, and sections 2
  and 3 both support it — mechanism, then numbers.)
- Central idea 2: "Cooler streets change how people use their town —
  more walking, meeting, and time outside." (Sections 4 and 5 support it.)

### Worked example 2 — tracing development

Central idea 1 develops in three steps:

1. **Introduced** (section 1): the walk down two streets *shows* the
   difference before any explanation — cool pavement vs. beating sun.
2. **Explained** (section 2): shade blocks sunlight; transpiration
   carries heat away. Now we know *why*.
3. **Measured** (section 3): 88 vs. 104 degrees on the ground, 90 vs.
   96 in the air. Now we know *how much*.

Each step adds something the earlier step did not have: feeling, then
mechanism, then numbers.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. In your own words, what is the difference between the article's topic
   and its first central idea?
2. Find one sentence in section 2 that supports central idea 1. Why does
   it support it — does it give a mechanism, a number, or an effect?
3. Central idea 2 is about people, not temperatures. Which two sections
   develop it, and what new piece does each section add?
4. Which section introduces *both* central ideas at once, and how can you
   tell? (Hint: look for the two-street comparison.)

## Independent practice

1. Write central idea 1 and central idea 2 in your own words (one
   sentence each). Neither sentence may contain "I think," "great," or
   "should."
2. For central idea 2, name the section where it is first hinted and the
   section where it is fully developed. Quote one detail from each.
3. The article says birds were counted "twice as many" on Maple Street.
   Does that detail support central idea 1, central idea 2, or both?
   Explain in two sentences.
4. Write a three-sentence objective summary of the whole article. Then
   underline any word in your summary that is an opinion, and replace it.

## Applied task

A friend asks, "What is that tree article about?" Answer in four
sentences: one sentence naming the topic, two sentences stating the two
central ideas, and one sentence saying how the article ends. Do not add
your own opinion about whether the town should plant trees.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) state a central idea of Article A; (b) name
one section that develops it and what that section adds.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if two central ideas feel hard to separate, color-code:
  underline temperature/shade sentences in one color and people/street-life
  sentences in the other. Sections with both colors are doing double work.
- **Extension:** write a one-sentence central idea for section 3 alone
  ("What the Numbers Show"), then explain why it is *not* one of the
  article's two main central ideas. (It is evidence for an idea, not an
  idea the whole article develops.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners write the topic ("street trees") or a
judgment ("trees are good") as a central idea. Require a full sentence
that the text itself supports in more than one section. Another common
slip: summaries that smuggle in "should" — the summary-repair step in
independent practice 4 exists for exactly this; keep it every time a
summary is assigned in this unit.
