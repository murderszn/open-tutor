# Grade 7 Social Studies

Independent, grade-specific materials in the public OpenTutor curriculum library.
This track is a **draft in progress**: the audit and scope-and-sequence are

[scope-and-sequence.md](scope-and-sequence.md).

**Do not refer a learner to another grade track.** This folder is self-contained

## What this track covers

A full-year (about 36 weeks) grade-7 social studies course in **medieval world
history, circa 500–1700 CE**: framing compelling and supporting historical
questions and choosing source kinds to answer them; reading and constructing
maps as historical evidence; analyzing connections among medieval developments
and classifying change and continuity; Byzantine, Islamic, and Mediterranean
societies in their own contexts; African kingdoms' trade networks, centers of
learning, and cultural exchange; South, Southeast Asian, and Pacific societies
and their regional networks; medieval European institutions and everyday life
with multi-cause explanations; Indigenous American societies as living peoples
with regional networks, evaluated through archaeology and oral tradition where
the written record is thin; Renaissance, Reformation, and early-modern change
argued from organized evidence; and global encounters — exploration,
colonization, and resistance — compared across secondary arguments and
synthesized into one connected narrative. Sessions are about 45 minutes, four
per week, blending explicit lessons, map and source practice, investigation,
discussion, and presentation — every formative check reviewed by the adult the
same day. The track stays nonpartisan: multiple perspectives are analyzed in
their own contexts, never as a single narrative.

## Track objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the year the learner will be able to:

1. Frame historical inquiries: write compelling and supporting questions about the medieval world and determine which source kinds will answer them (D1.1–1.5.6-8).
2. Read maps as historical evidence: construct and interpret maps of the medieval world with scale, key, and grid; explain what a map shows and what it cannot show (D2.Geo.1–3.6-8).
3. Analyze connections among medieval developments in broader historical contexts; classify developments as change and/or continuity across the medieval era (D2.His.1, 2.6-8).
4. Explain Byzantine, Islamic, and Mediterranean societies in their own contexts — institutions, belief systems, scholarship, and exchange — analyzing multiple factors that shaped people's perspectives (D2.His.1, 2, 4, 5.6-8; D2.Civ.3, 5, 6.6-8).
5. Explain African kingdoms' trade networks, centers of learning, and cultural exchange; evaluate how environmental characteristics of places shaped the spatial patterns of trade (D2.His.1, 2, 3.6-8; D2.Geo.11.6-8; D2.Eco.14, 15.6-8).
6. Explain South, Southeast Asian, and Pacific societies and their regional networks, comparing cultural and environmental characteristics across regions (D2.His.1, 2, 4.6-8; D2.Geo.5, 10, 12.6-8).
7. Explain medieval European institutions and everyday life — manorialism, feudal relationships, towns, guilds, the church — evaluating the relative influence of causes behind institutional change (D2.His.14, 15.6-8; D2.Civ.3, 4, 6.6-8; D2.Eco.1, 3.6-8).
8. Explain Indigenous American societies as living peoples with regional networks — agriculture, cities, trade, belief — evaluating sources where the written record is thin (D2.His.1, 4, 9, 10.6-8; D2.Geo.4, 5, 8.6-8).
9. Explain Renaissance, Reformation, and early-modern change with multiple causes and effects, organizing evidence into coherent arguments (D2.His.2, 14, 15, 16.6-8; D2.Eco.7.6-8; D2.Civ.13, 14.6-8).
10. Explain global encounters — exploration, colonization, and resistance — comparing central arguments in secondary works and analyzing how perspectives shaped the histories produced (D2.His.4, 6, 16, 17.6-8; D2.Civ.14.6-8; D4.6.6-8).
11. Evaluate historical sources: gather relevant information using origin, authority, structure, context, and corroborative value; classify source kinds; detect limitations; infer maker, date, place, audience, and purpose (D3.1, 3.2.6-8; D2.His.9–13.6-8).
12. Communicate conclusions: develop claims and counterclaims noting strengths and limitations, construct explanations with reasoning and sequence, critique arguments for credibility, and present findings to audiences beyond the adult (D3.3, 3.4.6-8; D4.1–4.5.6-8).

Standards reference: the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for
Social Studies State Standards (National Council for the Social Studies),
grades 6–8 band — used as a subject reference only; no state adoption,
accreditation, or alignment certification is claimed.

## For the guiding adult

- **Placement:** the learner should enter with the grade-6 social studies
  end-of-year skills (framing questions, reading maps, evaluating sources,
  multi-cause explanation) — see scope-and-sequence §2. Weeks 1–2 of the
  sequence are diagnostic; do not skip them.
- **Sources:** every unit's Resource Pack is built with
  [resource_finder.md](../../../teachers/ai-assistants/resource_finder.md).
  Preview all videos, links, and primary-source excerpts for accuracy,
  advertising, and age suitability before assigning. Medieval primary
  sources are used in public-domain or licensed translations only.
- **Sensitive content:** U08 (colonization and resistance) centers
  resistance perspectives and avoids graphic detail; frame it with the
  learner before assigning.
- **Volatile data:** any quiz or task citing current statistics (trade
  figures, commodity producers, exchange rates) carries a check date —
  re-verify before assigning.
