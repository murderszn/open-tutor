# Unit 03 — African Kingdoms: Trade, Knowledge, and Cultural Exchange

**Grade:** 7 · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#37](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/37)
**Status:** validated draft — delivered 2026-10-26; not yet educator-reviewed or merged.
**Weeks:** 11–14 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, about 45 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

Between about 800 and 1600 CE, African kingdoms and city-states sat at the
center of two of the world's great trading systems: the camel caravans that
crossed the Sahara and the sailing ships that rode the Indian Ocean's
seasonal monsoon winds. Gold from West African mines and from the Zimbabwe
Plateau, salt from desert mines, ivory, cloth, books, and ideas all moved
along these routes — and the places that controlled the routes grew rich
enough to build stone cities, pay scholars, and keep libraries. This unit
follows that movement west to east: the gold–salt kingdoms of Ghana, Mali,
and Songhai; the learning city of Timbuktu; the Swahili trading cities of
the East African coast; and the stone city of Great Zimbabwe inland. It
ends by asking why kingdoms rise and fall — and why a single cause is
almost never the whole story.

The unit reuses two pieces of the legacy track library, as the A00 audit
required, both re-anchored to stable concepts: the global-agriculture quiz
becomes a review seed about why staples and raw materials move along
trade routes (Practice Set E, with a check date on any producer data), and
the chokepoints quiz supplies the strait/corridor concept, applied here to
Saharan crossings and Indian Ocean passages rather than to volatile
modern oil fractions. The volatile "fraction of the world's oil" item is
not used. The two quizzes the audit flagged as unassignable
(`weight-distance-quiz.md`, `global-energy-precious-metals-quiz.md`) are
not used here either.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (mapped to the track
objectives in parentheses):

1. **Explain** the West African gold–salt trade — what moved, in which
   direction, through which cities — and **explain how environmental
   characteristics** (desert, savanna, and forest zones) shaped where trade
   routes ran and which goods each zone could offer. (Track objective 5;
   D2.Geo.11.6-8; D2.Eco.14, 15.6-8)
2. **Explain** how Ghana, Mali, and Songhai governed trade — taxes,
   protection, and royal control — and **compare** the three kingdoms on
   trade, cities, and governance, citing evidence. (Track objective 5;
   D2.His.1, 2.6-8; D2.Eco.14, 15.6-8)
3. **Explain** Timbuktu as a center of learning and cultural exchange:
   what scholars studied and copied, who traveled there and why, and why
   historians judge it historically significant. (Track objective 5;
   D2.His.3.6-8; D3.3.6-8)
4. **Explain** the Swahili coast and Great Zimbabwe networks — monsoon
   sailing, coral-stone and dry-stone building, and the goods that linked
   the African interior to the Indian Ocean world — and **classify**
   developments in them as change, continuity, or both. (Track objective 5;
   D2.His.1, 2.6-8; D2.Geo.4.6-8)
5. **Evaluate** a claim about why a kingdom rose or fell using **multiple
   causes**, evidence from at least two source kinds, and an explicit
   statement of what the evidence cannot prove. (Track objectives 5, 11,
   12; D2.His.1, 2.6-8; D3.3.6-8)

## Essential question

*How did trade make some African cities centers of wealth and learning —
and what happened when the routes changed?*

## Vocabulary

**Savanna** — grassland with scattered trees, between desert and forest;
good for herding and farming, and the zone where West African farming
kingdoms grew. · **Sahel** — the semi-dry belt at the Sahara's southern
edge; a meeting zone of herders, farmers, and traders. · **Caravan** — a
group traveling together for safety, here camel trains crossing the
desert. · **Entrepôt** — a port or city where goods are collected, stored,
and traded onward. · **Monsoon** — a seasonal wind that reverses direction
during the year; Indian Ocean sailors timed voyages to it. · **Dhow** — a
lateen-sailed wooden trading ship of the western Indian Ocean. ·
**Madrasa** — a school for Islamic learning, often attached to a mosque. ·
**Manuscript** — a book or text written by hand, before printing. ·
**Chronicle** — a written record of events in time order, often by one
writer with a viewpoint. · **Tax (royal)** — a payment a ruler required on
goods passing through or sold in a kingdom; a main source of royal wealth
here. · **Tribute** — payments from a lesser ruler or town to a more
powerful one, acknowledging its authority. · **Coral stone** — building
stone cut from coral limestone, used in Swahili coastal towns. ·
**Dry-stone masonry** — stone walls built without mortar, fitted block by
block. · **Cultural exchange** — the movement of ideas, beliefs, languages,
technologies, and arts between peoples, alongside goods. · **Significance**
— why historians judge a person, place, or development worth remembering
and studying. · **Change / continuity** — what became different over time
/ what stayed the same.

Historical content notes (checked 2026-10-26): West African sequence and
dates follow standard reference summaries — Ghana flourishing by about
800 CE and fading in the 1200s; Sundiata's Mali rising in the 1230s;
Mansa Musa's reign 1312–1337 and hajj of 1324, reported in Arabic
chronicles to have spread so much gold in Cairo that gold's value there
fell for years; Sunni Ali's reign 1464–1492 and Askia Muhammad's
1493–1528; the Battle of Tondibi in 1591, when Moroccan forces with
firearms defeated Songhai. Timbuktu's Sankoré mosque-madrasa, its
manuscript culture, and the later chronicles *Tarikh al-Fattash* (16th
century) and *Tarikh al-Sudan* (17th century, by al-Sa'di) are documented
in museum and university sources; manuscripts are preserved today by
institutions including Mali's Ahmed Baba Institute, working with UNESCO.
Swahili coast background — independent merchant city-states (Kilwa,
Mombasa, Sofala, Mogadishu), monsoon sailing, coral-stone building,
Kilwa's Great Mosque and coin minting, Ibn Battuta's admiring 1331 visit
to Kilwa — follows UNESCO's Kilwa Kisiwani listing and standard
reference summaries. Great Zimbabwe — a Shona kingdom capital of about
1100–1450 CE, dry-stone granite walls including the Great Enclosure
(walls up to about 11 m high), trade in gold and ivory through Sofala to
the Indian Ocean, and a peak population historians estimate at roughly
10,000–20,000 — follows Britannica and UNESCO-linked summaries; the
Conical Tower's purpose remains debated, and the unit says so. Where
sources disagree on a number (Timbuktu's student counts, caravan sizes),
the unit gives no single number and says estimates vary.

All "source excerpts" in lessons are **original classroom model texts
written for this unit**, clearly labeled — they are not quotations from
real historical works. Real works and writers they model (Ibn Battuta's
*Rihla*, al-Sa'di's *Tarikh al-Sudan*, Leo Africanus's description of
Timbuktu) are named only as kinds, with links for the adult in the
resource pack. The generated caravan image is an **AI illustration of a
generic trading town**, not a portrait of any real place, and is labeled
as such wherever it appears.

## 16-session pacing (Weeks 11–14)

Sessions are ~45 minutes. Each names its activity and success criterion.

| # | Week | Session | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 11 | **Lesson 01** — West African kingdoms and the gold–salt trade | Learner traces gold south→north and salt north→south through 4 cities and explains the desert/savanna/forest pattern |
| 2 | 11 | Practice Set A — trade-flow and tax reasoning | Learner completes the caravan-load table (labeled practice data) and explains what a king's tax bought |
| 3 | 11 | **Lesson 02** — Timbuktu: learning and cultural exchange | Learner explains what Sankoré scholars studied/copied and judges Timbuktu's significance with 2 reasons |
| 4 | 11 | Practice Set B — source kinds and significance | Learner classifies 4 model sources by kind, names one limit of each, and revises a weak significance claim |
| 5 | 12 | **Lesson 03** — Swahili coast and the monsoon sea | Learner explains the monsoon round trip and lists 3 goods in and 3 goods out at Kilwa |
| 6 | 12 | Practice Set C — monsoon map and "where in the modern world?" | Learner traces a dhow round trip on the schematic and matches 6 unit cities to modern countries |
| 7 | 12 | **Lesson 04** — Great Zimbabwe: stone, cattle, and gold | Learner explains what the stone walls prove and cannot prove, and what linked Great Zimbabwe to the coast |
| 8 | 12 | Practice Set D — evidence: walls, beads, and pottery | Learner sorts 6 finds into "proves / suggests / cannot prove" with reasons |
| 9 | 13 | **Lesson 05** — Why kingdoms rise and fall: multiple causes | Learner builds a 3-cause explanation of Songhai's fall and names one cause often overstated |
| 10 | 13 | Project work — trade-network map draft + source log | Draft map has 8 labeled places and 2 routes; source log has 4 entries with kinds and limits |
| 11 | 13 | Discussion — change, continuity, and the routes that moved | Learner classifies 3 developments as change / continuity / both with cited evidence |
| 12 | 13 | Review session — trade, learning, coast, stone self-check | Learner corrects every missed review-seed item and can explain the fix |
| 13 | 14 | **week-04 quiz** + portfolio trade-network map | Quiz complete; portfolio map finished with key and route explanations |
| 14 | 14 | **Unit assessment** | Assessment complete under adult supervision |
| 15 | 14 | Project presentations | Learner presents map and significance paragraph; answers one challenge question |
| 16 | 14 | Reflection — what trade leaves behind | Learner names one question the unit left unanswered and one source kind that could answer it |

Sessions 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 draw on [practice.md](practice.md); the
project spans Sessions 10, 13, and 15 ([project.md](project.md)); formal
checks are Sessions 13–14 ([assessments/](assessments/)). Retrieval
practice is built in: Session 11 revisits West Africa (W11) beside the
coast and stone city (W12); Session 12 revisits all four strands; and
the project reuses map, source-kind, significance, and multiple-cause
skills together.
