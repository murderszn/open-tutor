## Lesson 01 — West African kingdoms and the gold–salt trade

- **Readiness:** (1) any U01 corridor (Mediterranean, Indian Ocean,
  trans-Saharan, Silk Road); (2) salt preserves food and replaces sweat
  loss; (3) cloth, books, horses, manufactured goods.
- **Guided:** (1) Taghaza → Timbuktu → Gao on the schematic;
  (2) "Ghana grew rich by taxing trade, not by mining gold — the mines
  lay further south"; (3) observation: walled town, minaret, loaded
  camels; inference: salt loads, organized town; cannot prove: which
  town, which century, bundle contents.
- **Independent:** (1) desert — salt; savanna — grain, cattle; forest —
  gold; (2) Ghana — Koumbi Saleh, trade taxes; Mali — Timbuktu/Gao,
  control of mines and routes plus taxes; Songhai — Gao, provincial
  taxes and guarded routes; (3) Timbuktu sat at the savanna/sahel
  junction where desert caravans met river boats, grain, wells, and
  protection — things the open desert could not provide;
  (4) chroniclers' caravan numbers disagree and are reports, not a
  census — stating one number as fact hides that disagreement.

## Lesson 02 — Timbuktu: learning and cultural exchange

- **Readiness:** (1) trade taxes; (2) wealth, safety, copyists, texts;
  (3) a city paying for many copies attracts students and teachers.
- **Guided:** (1) law, grammar, theology, astronomy, medicine,
  mathematics, history (any three); travelers: merchants, pilgrims,
  students, judges; (2) proves: a visitor observed markets and
  scholars; inference: books were valued; cannot prove: exact prices or
  scholar counts; (3) sample revision: "Timbuktu is significant
  because its scholars wrote chronicles such as the Tarikh al-Sudan
  (chronicle kind) that still inform historians, and because its trained
  judges served Mali's and Songhai's courts (chronicle/administrative
  evidence)."
- **Independent:** (1) trade → wealth to pay teachers → students
  arrive → fame spreads → more travelers arrive with books and goods;
  (2) the comparison measures Timbuktu by Oxford instead of by what it
  did, and Sankoré was a mosque-madrasa quarter, not a chartered
  university; (3) (a) continuity — market function persisted across
  dynasties; (b) change (with continuity of the city itself) —
  scholars increasingly served royal administration under Songhai;
  (4) "exactly" is unsafe because surviving estimates vary widely and
  no enrollment census exists.

## Lesson 03 — Swahili coast and the monsoon sea

- **Readiness:** (1) entrepôt = collection/storage/onward-trade city;
  (2) wind/season schedules sailing; (3) exports: gold, ivory; imports:
  cloth, porcelain, beads.
- **Guided:** (1) Arabia → Kilwa carrying cloth/porcelain; wait for
  wind reversal; return carrying gold/ivory; (2) correction: Kilwa was
  one independent city-state among several, not an empire's capital;
  (3) building: Great Mosque; coin: Kilwa-minted currency; account:
  Ibn Battuta's admiring 1331 description.
- **Independent:** (1) exports: gold, ivory, timber, iron (any three);
  imports: cloth, porcelain/pottery, glass beads, silver (any three);
  (2) months of waiting in port meant daily contact — language,
  religion, food, building — while an inland village met few outsiders;
  (3) languages borrow words from trading partners; borrowing proves
  contact, not that the speakers came from Arabia; (4) Kilwa taxed and
  controlled coastal gold trade; the mines were inland on the Zimbabwe
  Plateau.

## Lesson 04 — Great Zimbabwe: stone, cattle, and gold

- **Readiness:** (1) Sofala; (2) buildings, artifacts, burials, imports,
  later accounts; (3) organization, labor command, surplus food.
- **Guided:** (1) Great Zimbabwe → Sofala → Kilwa; (2) (a) proves
  organization; (b) suggests (royal/ritual use debated); (c) cannot
  prove (the king's name); (3) "Archaeology shows local stonework
  traditions at 200+ plateau sites and African artifacts with Indian
  Ocean imports — no foreign builders' traces. Great Zimbabwe was built
  by ancestors of the Shona people."
- **Independent:** (1) Hill Complex — oldest stonework on a granite
  hill; Great Enclosure — elliptical wall, Conical Tower; Valley Ruins —
  town area; (2) gold/ivory went to Sofala and Kilwa; cloth, beads, and
  ceramics came back; (3) (a) continuity — cattle herding persisted;
  (b) change — trade power shifted to other centers in the 1400s;
  (4) the tower's purpose is debated; "symbolic granary" is an
  interpretation, not a proven fact.

## Lesson 05 — Why kingdoms rise and fall

- **Readiness:** (1) Mali's rise: conquests/location/patronage;
  Great Zimbabwe's decline: gold, farmland, routes; (2) no — a last
  event is not automatically the only cause; (3) an argument against a
  claim.
- **Guided:** (1) rankings vary; accept any defended ranking that
  names evidence and a possible revision trigger; (2) farmland
  exhaustion and shifting trade routes, in addition to gold; gold alone
  cannot explain why recovery failed; (3) admitting limits shows the
  writer knows what evidence can carry — credibility, not weakness.
- **Independent:** (1) Mali paragraph must contain ≥2 causes with
  source kinds (conquest, junction geography, hajj/patronage);
  (2) sample counterclaim: "Scholars, merchants, pilgrims, and the
  manuscript trade also sustained Timbuktu's learning, and learning
  continued after Songhai's fall — royal pay alone cannot explain it";
  (3) (a) both — trade continued, cities/routes changed; (b) both —
  learning continued, wealth/power declined; (4) it confuses a date
  with a cause and a final battle with the whole explanation.

## Week-04 quiz

1. Salt south, gold north; they met at Timbuktu, Gao, or Djenné.
2. Ghana first; kings' wealth came from taxing passing trade.
3. Any two of law, grammar, theology, astronomy, medicine,
   mathematics, history.
4. A seasonal reversing wind; ships had to sail with it and wait for
   its reversal, so the wind set the sailing calendar.
5. Exports: gold, ivory (also timber, iron); imports: cloth, porcelain,
   beads, silver (any two each).
6. Dry-stone masonry (granite without mortar); the wall proves skilled
   masons, organized labor, and a surplus able to feed workers.
7. Any one of: names, laws, speeches, exact functions of enclosures.
8. Continuity — Timbuktu's market function persisted under both
   kingdoms (with change in rulers/administration also acceptable if
   argued as "both" with two reasons).
9. Any two of succession disputes (chronicles), firearms at Tondibi
   (battle accounts), trade disruption/route shifts (trade evidence).
10. The tower's purpose is debated; "symbolic granary" is an
    interpretation, not a proven fact, so stating it flatly overstates
    the evidence.

## Unit assessment

- **Task 1:** all 7 places labeled; Saharan route arrows: salt south,
  gold north; Indian Ocean route arrows: gold/ivory toward the coast
  and onward, cloth/porcelain/beads inward; one correct good-and-
  direction sentence per route. Award partial credit per correct place
  and route.
- **Task 2:** (a) traveler's account; (b) proves: a visitor observed a
  market and a mosque; inference: Kilwa was prosperous; cannot prove:
  exact wealth, or that this mosque was "the finest" by any measure;
  (c) a coin (minting authority/trade reach), a building (mosque/palace
  scale), or an artifact (imported porcelain) — each with what it shows.
- **Task 3:** paragraph must state the shared mechanism (taxing and
  protecting trade, not mining) and the difference (kingdom vs.
  independent city-state; Saharan vs. Indian Ocean system).
- **Task 4:** ≥2 causes with source kinds (gold sources, farmland,
  shifting routes, or other lesson-supported causes) plus a limit
  sentence (for example: the relative weight of causes is debated).
- **Task 5:** (a) continuity — Swahili use persisted after Kilwa's
  decline; (b) change — the dominant routes and ports shifted;
  (c) both — learning continued, wealth and power declined.
- **Task 6:** two reasons with source kinds (for example: chronicles
  and surviving manuscripts; trained judges serving courts; manuscript
  preservation today). "Famous" alone scores zero for that reason.
