# Lesson 04 — Great Zimbabwe: Stone, Cattle, and Gold

**Unit:** [U03 — African Kingdoms: Trade, Knowledge, and Cultural Exchange](../README.md) · **Week 12**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 7 of 16

## Goal

You will explain Great Zimbabwe: what its stone walls prove and cannot
prove, how cattle, farming, and gold supported it, and how it connected
to the Swahili coast.

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer aloud or in the notebook:

1. From Lesson 03: which coastal port received gold from the southern
   interior? (Sofala, carried north to Kilwa.)
2. If a city has no written records of its own, what kinds of sources
   can historians use instead? (Buildings, artifacts, burials, imported
   goods, later accounts, oral traditions handled with care.)
3. A wall 11 meters high built without mortar: what does that tell you
   about the people who built it, even before you know their names?

If question 3 is shaky, say it plainly now: *a wall that big, fitted
stone by stone with no mortar, proves organization — people to quarry,
shape, carry, and lay stone, food to feed them, and rulers who could
command that labor.* Buildings are evidence, not decoration.

## Teaching

### Part A — A stone city inland

About 1100 to 1450 CE, on the Zimbabwe Plateau in present-day
Zimbabwe, stood Great Zimbabwe, capital of a Shona kingdom. Its people
farmed, herded cattle, mined gold, and traded. The site has three main
parts: the Hill Complex, the oldest stonework, set on a granite hill;
the Great Enclosure, an elliptical stone wall enclosing about a
hectare, with walls up to about 11 meters high and a solid Conical
Tower about 10 meters tall; and the Valley Ruins, the town area between
them. The walls are **dry-stone masonry**: granite blocks fitted
together without mortar, in courses that grow thinner toward the top.
Historians estimate the town held roughly 10,000–20,000 people at its
height — a genuine city by the standards of its region and time.

Great Zimbabwe's wealth came from the same logic as Ghana's and Kilwa's:
control movement. Gold and ivory from the plateau traveled east to the
coast at Sofala, then north by dhow to Kilwa and onward across the
Indian Ocean; in return came cloth, glass beads, and ceramics from
Persia and China, which archaeologists still find in the ruins. Cattle
mattered too: herds were wealth, food, and bridewealth, and the kingdom
grew from cattle wealth as much as from gold.

**Worked example 1 — what walls prove, and what they do not.** The
Great Enclosure's wall proves: (a) skilled masons and organized labor;
(b) a ruler or elite able to command that labor; (c) a society wealthy
enough to feed workers who were not farming. It suggests, but does not
prove, that the enclosure was a royal or ritual space — its exact
function is debated, and the Conical Tower's purpose is still argued
over (a symbolic granary is one leading idea; it is not settled fact).
And the walls cannot prove what languages people spoke at court, what
the king was called, or what laws were passed — stone does not record
speeches. A good historian states all three levels: proves, suggests,
cannot prove.

**Worked example 2 — correcting an old myth.** In the colonial era,
some European writers claimed outsiders must have built Great
Zimbabwe, because they refused to believe African builders could raise
such walls. Archaeology refutes that: the stonework matches local
building traditions found at more than 200 plateau sites, the artifacts
are African with Indian Ocean imports, and no foreign builders' traces
exist. The myth is worth naming in class, because it teaches a method
lesson: when a claim about "who built it" contradicts the artifacts in
the ground, trust the ground — and ask whose interests the old claim
served. Modern scholarship is clear: Great Zimbabwe was built by
ancestors of the Shona people.

### Part B — Change and continuity on the plateau

Classify two developments:

- **Continuity:** cattle herding and farming supported plateau
  societies before, during, and after Great Zimbabwe's peak; stone
  building traditions continued at other plateau sites.
- **Change:** as gold trade grew, a single capital concentrated wealth
  and power on a scale earlier towns had not reached; when gold sources,
  farmland, and trade routes shifted in the 1400s, the capital declined
  and power moved elsewhere — including to successor states such as
  Mutapa. Decline here is not a mystery with one villain; it is a shift
  in the same conditions (resources, routes, and farmland) that had made
  the city rise. Lesson 05 builds on exactly this point.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

With the adult, work these aloud before writing:

1. Point to Great Zimbabwe and Sofala on the schematic and trace gold's
   journey to the coast.
2. Sort these claims into "proves / suggests / cannot prove": (a) the
   builders were organized; (b) the Great Enclosure was the king's
   palace; (c) the king's name.
3. A classmate says, "Foreigners built Great Zimbabwe." Give the
   two-sentence archaeological correction from Part A.

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. Name the three parts of the Great Zimbabwe site and one fact about
   each.
2. Explain in two or three sentences how Great Zimbabwe connects to
   Lesson 03: what traveled from the plateau to Kilwa, and what came
   back?
3. Classify as change, continuity, or both, with one reason: (a) plateau
   people kept herding cattle after Great Zimbabwe declined; (b) trade
   shifted from Great Zimbabwe's routes to other centers in the 1400s.
4. **Error analysis:** A caption reads, "The Conical Tower was the
   king's grain store." What is wrong with stating this as fact? (Its
   purpose is debated; "symbolic granary" is an interpretation, not a
   proven fact.)

## Application

You are an archaeologist's assistant cataloguing finds at Great
Zimbabwe: a Chinese porcelain shard, a gold bead, a locally made clay
pot, and a granite wall block. For each find, write one sentence about
what it proves, one about what it suggests, and one about what it
cannot prove. (Four finds × three sentences; the wall block's entry
must mention labor and organization.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: name Great Zimbabwe's building technique, one
good it exported, the port its goods reached, and one thing its walls
cannot prove.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads Part A aloud while the learner
  sketches the three site parts (hill, enclosure, valley) as three
  labeled boxes.
- **Alternate response mode:** the catalogue may be a table with
  "proves / suggests / cannot prove" columns instead of sentences.
- **Language access:** "dry-stone" is glossed as "stone fitted without
  mortar, like a puzzle"; "plateau" as "a high, fairly flat area."

## Extensions

- Compare Great Zimbabwe's stone with Kilwa's coral stone (Lesson 03):
  how did local materials shape what each city could build?
- Research question for the adult to vet: the name "Zimbabwe" comes
  from a Shona phrase for "stone houses." How did a medieval city's
  name become a modern country's name? Bring one fact and its source.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) outsider-built myth — correct explicitly
with the archaeological evidence; (2) Conical Tower purpose stated as
fact — keep it debated; (3) "decline = collapse overnight" — the
1400s shift was gradual, with successor states. Handle the colonial
myth plainly and briefly; the point is method (evidence over prejudice),
not a lecture on colonial historiography. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
