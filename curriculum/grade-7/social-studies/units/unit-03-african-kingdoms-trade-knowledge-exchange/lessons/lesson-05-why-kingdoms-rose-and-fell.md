# Lesson 05 — Why Kingdoms Rise and Fall: Multiple Causes

**Unit:** [U03 — African Kingdoms: Trade, Knowledge, and Cultural Exchange](../README.md) · **Week 13**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 9 of 16

## Goal

You will build a multiple-cause explanation for a kingdom's rise or
fall, using evidence from at least two source kinds, and state honestly
what the evidence cannot prove.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Your notes from Lessons 01–04

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer aloud or in the notebook:

1. Name one cause of Mali's rise and one cause of Great Zimbabwe's
   decline, from Lessons 01 and 04.
2. If a kingdom falls the same year an invading army arrives, does that
   prove the invasion alone caused the fall? Why or why not?
3. What is a **counterclaim**? (An argument against a claim — here, a
   reason the simple explanation might be wrong or incomplete.)

If question 2 is shaky, say it plainly now: *an event that happens last
is not automatically the only cause. A kingdom weakened by succession
fights, exhausted mines, and shifting trade can fall to an invasion
that a stronger kingdom might have survived.* Historians look for the
weakening as well as the blow.

## Teaching

### Part A — Songhai's fall, told with three causes

Songhai reached its greatest extent under Askia Muhammad (1493–1528):
provinces with governors, guarded trade routes, Gao as capital,
Timbuktu and Djenné as markets and learning centers. Then it declined,
and in 1591 Moroccan forces with firearms defeated Songhai's army at
the Battle of Tondibi and took Gao and Timbuktu. The empire broke apart.
A one-cause story — "Morocco invaded, so Songhai fell" — is true as far
as it goes, and badly incomplete. Here is the same story with three
causes, each tied to a source kind:

1. **Internal weakening (chronicle kind).** After Askia Muhammad was
   overthrown by his own son in 1528, succession disputes and coups
   recurred for decades. Chronicles of the period describe a court
   divided against itself — a government spending its strength on who
   should rule rather than on guarding routes or reforming the army.
   *Evidence type:* chronicles and king-lists. *Limit:* court writers
   had reasons to blame rivals; treat their villains with care.
2. **Military mismatch (battle accounts and material evidence).**
   Moroccan forces carried firearms — muskets and cannon — that
   Songhai's armies, equipped with spears, arrows, and swords, had never
   faced in battle. At Tondibi the technology gap mattered more than
   numbers. *Evidence type:* battle narratives plus the physical fact
   of firearms. *Limit:* weapons alone do not explain why the empire
   could not recover afterward; that needs causes 1 and 3.
3. **Trade disruption and shifting routes (economic evidence).**
   As the empire fragmented, its trade routes became unsafe, and
   Atlantic coastal commerce was increasingly drawing West African
   trade away from Saharan routes altogether. A kingdom whose wealth
   came from taxing movement suffers twice when movement stops: no tax
   revenue, and no way to pay soldiers or scholars. *Evidence type:*
   trade records and the later pattern of coastal commerce. *Limit:*
   route shifts were gradual, not a single event — do not date them to
   1591 alone.

**Worked example 1 — building the paragraph.** A strong multiple-cause
paragraph names its causes, links them, and admits its limit:

> "Songhai fell for several connected reasons. Decades of succession
> disputes after 1528 left its government divided (chronicle evidence).
> When Morocco invaded in 1591, its firearms gave it a battlefield
> advantage Songhai's armies had never faced (battle accounts).
> Finally, as the empire broke apart, its trade routes grew unsafe and
> commerce shifted toward the Atlantic coast, cutting the tax revenue
> that had paid for armies and scholars (trade evidence). No single
> cause explains the fall: a united Songhai might have survived the
> invasion, and a wealthy one might have bought time to adapt. What the
> evidence cannot prove is exactly how much weight each cause carried —
> historians argue about that, and this paragraph does not pretend
> otherwise."

Notice the structure: causes in order, each with a source kind, a link
("when… finally…"), and a stated limit at the end. Copy that structure,
not those sentences, in your own work.

**Worked example 2 — the same method on a rise.** Mali's rise also
needs more than one cause: (a) Sundiata's conquests united the
gold-producing regions and the trade cities; (b) the savanna-edge
location put Mali astride the gold–salt junction (geography, Lesson 01);
(c) Mansa Musa's hajj and patronage of scholars advertised the kingdom
and drew travelers, teachers, and trade (chronicle and traveler
evidence). Remove any one cause and Mali is smaller, poorer, or less
famous — but no single cause alone creates an empire. Rise and fall
both reward the same question: *what else was true at the same time?*

### Part B — Spotting the overstated cause

Every multiple-cause explanation has a temptation: to let the most
dramatic cause swallow the others. In Songhai's story the dramatic
cause is Tondibi — a battle, with guns, in a single year. It is also
the cause that explains least by itself, because it cannot explain the
decades of weakening before it or the failure to recover after it.
When you check your own paragraph, ask: *if I deleted my most dramatic
cause, would the other causes still point toward decline?* If yes, your
explanation is genuinely multiple. If no, you have written a
one-cause story with decoration.

## Guided practice (10 minutes)

With the adult, work these aloud before writing:

1. Take the three Songhai causes and rank them by which you think
   mattered most. Defend your ranking in one sentence per cause — then
   say what evidence could change your ranking.
2. A classmate writes, "Great Zimbabwe fell because its gold ran out."
   Name two other causes from Lesson 04 (farmland exhaustion, shifting
   trade routes) and explain why gold alone is insufficient.
3. Find the limit sentence in Worked example 1. Why does admitting a
   limit make an argument stronger rather than weaker? (It shows the
   writer knows what the evidence can and cannot carry — credibility.)

## Independent practice (10 minutes)

1. Write a multiple-cause paragraph (5–7 sentences) explaining Mali's
   rise, using at least two causes from Worked example 2 and naming a
   source kind for each.
2. Write a counterclaim to this claim: "Timbuktu's learning existed
   only because kings paid for it." (Counterclaim: scholars, merchants,
   pilgrims, and manuscript trade also sustained learning; royal
   patronage helped but was not the only support — and learning
   continued in Timbuktu after Songhai's fall, which royal-pay alone
   cannot explain.)
3. Classify as change, continuity, or both, with one reason: (a) West
   African trade continued after Songhai's fall, but through different
   cities and routes; (b) Timbuktu remained a place of learning after
   1591, though poorer and less powerful.
4. **Error analysis:** An essay says, "Songhai fell in 1591, so 1591 is
   the cause of Songhai's fall." What is wrong? (It confuses a date with
   a cause, and a final battle with the whole explanation.)

## Application

Choose one kingdom from this unit (Ghana, Mali, Songhai, Kilwa, or
Great Zimbabwe) and write a two-paragraph explanation of its rise or
its fall: paragraph one gives three causes with a source kind for each;
paragraph two gives one counterclaim or limit. This draft becomes the
core of your project significance paragraph's method — keep it.

## Exit check

Without looking back: name three causes of Songhai's fall, one source
kind for each, and one thing the evidence cannot prove about their
relative weight.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads Worked example 1 aloud; the
  learner marks each cause and its source kind in a different color.
- **Alternate response mode:** causes may be laid out as a three-box
  diagram (cause → evidence → link) before being written as prose.
- **Language access:** "counterclaim" is glossed as "a reason the
  simple story might be wrong"; "multiple causes" as "several reasons
  working together."

## Extensions

- Apply the method outside this unit: pick one change from U02 (for
  example, the spread of Islamic societies) and list two causes and one
  limit. Bring it to Session 11's discussion.
- Research question for the adult to vet: how do historians weigh
  causes when sources disagree — for example, when a chronicle blames a
  king and trade records blame a route shift? Bring one example of a
  historian explicitly weighing causes.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) date = cause; (2) dramatic battle swallowing
the explanation; (3) counterclaim treated as "the opposite opinion"
rather than a test of the claim's limits. Praise paragraphs that admit
limits — that is the unit's highest-order skill. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 05.
