# Lesson 02 — Rise and Spread of Islamic Societies

**Unit:** [U02 — Byzantine, Islamic, and Mediterranean societies](../README.md) · **Week 7**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 3 of 16

## Goal

You will trace how Islamic societies formed in 7th-century Arabia and
spread across three continents by 1300, and you will practice evaluating
what one source can — and cannot — tell you.

## Materials

- Inquiry notebook and pencil
- A blank outline map of Afro-Eurasia (printed or sketched by the adult)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer aloud or in the notebook:

1. Lesson 01 found an empire running on law, faith, and administration.
   What three ingredients would you look for in ANY large medieval
   society?
2. Arabia around 600 sat between Byzantium and Persia. Why might a trade
   town between empires grow rich — and restless?
3. The Islamic calendar counts Year 1 from 622. What does that choice say
   about the community?

That town was **Mecca**: a trade town in western Arabia where caravans
rested, where the cube-shaped shrine called the **Kaaba** drew pilgrims
from many tribes, and where tribal loyalties ordered nearly everything.
Into that world, around 570, a merchant named Muhammad was born.

## Teaching

### Part A — A community forms

**Muhammad** (c. 570–632) received, as Muslims believe, revelations from
God through the angel Gabriel, beginning around 610. Muslims believe the
**Qur'an** ("recitation") is God's final revelation, given in Arabic. Its
message was stark for a tribal, polytheist society: one God; the wealthy
owe the poor; judgment is coming. Many in Mecca saw a threat to their
shrines, their trade, and their ancestors' ways.

In **622**, Muhammad and his followers left Mecca for Yathrib, soon
renamed Medina. This migration is the **Hijra** (hij-RAH), and the Islamic
calendar counts Year 1 from it — the community dated itself not from a
birth or a battle, but from the founding of a new society.

Muslim practice rests on the **Five Pillars**: the **shahada** (testimony
that there is no god but God and Muhammad is His messenger), **salat**
(prayer five times daily), **zakat** (almsgiving), **sawm** (fasting in
Ramadan), and **hajj** (pilgrimage to Mecca, for those able). Belief and
law grew together. **Sharia** — the path of God's law as Muslims
understand it — derives from the **Qur'an**, the **sunna** (the Prophet's
example, preserved in reports called **hadith**), scholarly consensus, and
analogy. The scholars who interpreted all this, the **ulama**
(oo-lah-MAH), became one of the most powerful groups in Islamic
societies: where Byzantium fused law into the emperor's code, Islamic
societies placed much of the law in scholars' hands.

After Muhammad's death in 632, the community chose leaders called
**caliphs** (KAY-lifs) — successors to the Prophet's political authority.
The succession shaped everything after:

- **Rashidun ("Rightly Guided") caliphs, 632–661** — the first four,
  chosen from Muhammad's companions; oversaw the early expansion into
  Byzantine Syria and Egypt and Persian Iraq.
- **Umayyad caliphate, 661–750** — ruled from **Damascus**; the first
  great Islamic empire, from Spain to Central Asia, with Arabic as the
  language of administration and coinage.
- **Abbasid caliphate, from 750** — founded a new capital at **Baghdad in
  762**; presided over a golden age of scholarship, **8th–13th
  centuries**. (An Umayyad prince escaped to Spain and founded the
  emirate — later caliphate — of Córdoba, in **al-Andalus**, Muslim-ruled
  Iberia.)
- **Cairo, founded 969** by the Fatimid dynasty — proof that by the 900s,
  "the Islamic world" meant several rival powers, not one empire.

The **caliph** began as the community's single leader and ended, centuries
later, as one authority among many. Lesson 04 will ask what changed and
what continued.

**Worked example 1 — tracing one idea across cultures.** Watch a single
piece of knowledge travel, changing clothes at every border.

*India.* Mathematicians developed a place-value number system with a
symbol for zero — the system behind the digits 0–9.

*Baghdad, c. 825.* The scholar **al-Khwarizmi** (al-KHWAH-riz-mee) wrote
the **Kitab al-Jabr**, the book whose title gave us *algebra*. He
systematized solving equations and spread the Indian numerals. His
Latinized name gave us another word: *algorithm.*

*The Latin West, 1202.* Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* taught Europeans to
calculate with "Hindu-Arabic" numerals instead of Roman numerals. Try long
division in Roman numerals and you will feel why this mattered.

Three historian's observations:

1. **Continuity:** the core idea — place value, zero — survived the whole
   journey. A 13th-century Pisan merchant used the same mathematical truth
   as a 9th-century Baghdadi scholar.
2. **Change:** at each stop the idea gained a new language (Sanskrit to
   Arabic to Latin), new users (scholars to merchants), and new purposes
   (astronomy to bookkeeping).
3. **Credit is complicated:** "Arabic numerals" began in India; "algebra"
   is an Arabic word for a system built on Indian and Greek foundations.
   Tracing the chain keeps us from giving one civilization the whole prize.

*The move to copy: when an idea moves, ask what stayed the same, what
changed, and who touched it along the way.*

**Common error:** "The Arabs invented algebra." More precise: Indian
mathematicians laid foundations; scholars like al-Khwarizmi in Baghdad
systematized them into the discipline we recognize. Ideas have family
trees, not single parents.

### Part B — The translation movement and the cities of learning

In Baghdad, the Abbasid caliphs founded the **House of Wisdom** (**Bayt
al-Hikma**) — part library, part translation bureau, part research
institute. Its project, the **translation movement**, rendered Greek,
Persian, and Indian works into Arabic: Aristotle and Ptolemy, Persian
astronomy, Indian mathematics and medicine.

What the movement produced:

- **Medicine:** **Ibn Sina** (Avicenna) wrote the *Canon of Medicine*, a
  systematic encyclopedia used in European universities for centuries.
- **Optics:** **Ibn al-Haytham** experimented with light, lenses, and the
  camera obscura, laying groundwork for the science of optics.
- **Astronomy:** scholars refined star tables and instruments — above all
  the **astrolabe** (AS-troh-layb), a handheld model of the heavens used
  to tell time, find direction, and calculate prayer times.
- **Paper:** the technique was learned from China — by tradition after the
  Battle of the **Talas River in 751** — and Baghdad had paper mills by the
  late **8th century**. Cheap paper meant cheap books, and cheap books let
  the translation movement circulate.

The cities showed what the civilization valued. Baghdad was built to a
**round-city plan** — a perfect circle with the caliph's palace at the
center, the geometry of power made visible. In Córdoba, the library's size
became legend: **chroniclers claimed it held hundreds of thousands of
volumes** — we cannot verify the number, and the boast may be part of the
story Córdoba wanted told, but even the claim shows a city competing in
books. Damascus carried Umayyad grandeur; Cairo, the Fatimid foundation of
969, grew into a rival capital of mosques and markets.

**Worked example 2 — evaluating one source: a merchant's letter.** Among
the most revealing leftovers of this world are the papers of the **Cairo
Geniza**: thousands of letters, contracts, and accounts kept by Jewish
merchants in Old Cairo from the **10th to the 13th centuries**, stored in
a synagogue storeroom rather than thrown away. Read this model letter of
that kind.

**(Original model text written for this unit — not a historical quotation.)**
*"In God's name. From Yusuf in Alexandria to his partner Ibrahim in
Fustat, greetings. The pepper you sent arrived, but the price has fallen —
the Sicilian ships brought more than the market can take. I have sold half
at a loss and hold the rest. Send no more pepper until you hear from me;
send flax instead, which the weavers want. Your servant's son is well. May
God keep you."*

Now evaluate it — what it shows and what it cannot show.

- **It shows:** a partnership across cities; real-time market intelligence
  (Sicilian competition driving prices down); flexible strategy (switch to
  flax); the human texture of trade — a note about a child inside a
  business letter.
- **It cannot show:** the whole economy. One merchant's pepper problem
  reveals nothing about total trade volume, or what Christian or Muslim
  merchants thought, or what the caliph's tax collectors recorded. It is a
  window, not a panorama. Yusuf writes to make money, so he emphasizes
  market facts and omits politics and faith.
- **The historian's question:** *this source proves X about one merchant's
  week — what would I need to claim X about the whole Mediterranean?*
  (Answer: many letters, plus tax records, plus port registers —
  corroboration across source kinds.)

*The move to copy: never stop at "what does this source say?" Ask what it
can show, what it cannot show, and what you would need to say more.*

**Common error:** treating one vivid letter as proof of a whole economy.
Vividness is not representativeness. One letter is evidence about its
writer; a pattern across many letters is evidence about a world.

## Guided practice (with hints)

*Do these with the adult. Hints are in italics.*

1. On your outline map, mark Mecca, Medina, Damascus, Baghdad (762),
   Córdoba, and Cairo (969). Draw arrows for the early expansion
   (632–750). What does the map suggest about why Damascus and Baghdad
   made sense as capitals?
   
   *Hint: look at what each capital sits between — routes, older imperial
   centers, the sea. Capitals are placed, not random.*

2. In your own words, explain how the **ulama** differed from Justinian's
   jurists in Lesson 01: who held the law in each society?
   
   *Hint: in Constantinople the emperor commissioned the code. In Islamic
   societies, who interpreted the Qur'an and hadith — the caliph's
   officials, or a separate class of scholars?*

3. A classmate says: "The House of Wisdom proves Muslims invented
   science." Using Worked example 1, write a two-sentence correction that
   is accurate and fair to the translators' real achievement.
   
   *Hint: what did they do with Greek, Persian, and Indian works — invent
   from nothing, or something else just as impressive?*

## Independent practice

4. Trace ONE of these across cultures as Worked example 1 traced numerals:
   **paper** (China → Baghdad → the Latin West) or the **astrolabe**
   (Greek foundations → Baghdad refinement → European navigation). Per
   stop: one thing that stayed the same, one that changed.
5. Re-read the model merchant letter. Make two columns: "What this letter
   proves" (three items, each tied to a specific line) and "What this
   letter cannot prove" (three items, each naming the *kind* of source
   that could prove it).
6. Córdoba's library: chroniclers claimed hundreds of thousands of
   volumes. In four sentences, explain why a historian should be careful
   with this number — and why the *claim itself* is still historical
   evidence (evidence of what?).

## Application

   Islam spread largely through merchants, not armies. Argue in five to

   faith and a legal tradition. Use at least two facts from the lesson.

8. In your own words: how did Islamic societies form (Muhammad, the Hijra,

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners compress the story into "Muhammad
founded an empire that conquered everything," missing the succession of
distinct caliphates and the merchant-led spread in Africa and Southeast
Asia. Keep the timeline visible: 622 (community), 632–661 (Rashidun),
661–750 (Umayyads), 762 (Baghdad), 969 (Cairo) — different centuries,
different capitals, different mechanisms. Also watch for flattening of
belief: students should describe the Five Pillars and sharia as what
Muslims believe and practice, not as museum facts. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L02.
