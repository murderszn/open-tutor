# Lesson 01 — The Byzantine State: Law, Faith, and Administration

**Unit:** [U02 — Byzantine, Islamic, and Mediterranean societies](../README.md) · **Week 7**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 1 of 16

## Goal

You will explain how law, faith, and administration combined to keep the
Byzantine state "Roman" for more than a thousand years, and you will
practice analyzing one event from two different perspectives.

## Materials

- Inquiry notebook and pencil
- A ruler or straightedge for the timeline you will draw

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer aloud or in the notebook:

1. In U01 you learned that historians read *leftovers*. Which three
   leftovers would best show how a medieval state was governed?
2. Rome's western half fell in the 400s CE. What must the eastern half
   have done to survive another thousand years?
3. What is the difference between what people call themselves and what
   later historians call them?

Question 3 opens today's lesson. The people we study called themselves
**Rhomaioi** — Romans. They spoke Greek and worshiped in Orthodox
churches, centered on a city on the Bosphorus strait. "Byzantine" is a
label historians began using in the **16th century**, long after the
empire fell. Useful for us — but remember who chose it, and who did not.

## Teaching

### Part A — An empire that called itself Rome

In 330 CE, Emperor **Constantine I** refounded the city of Byzantium as
his capital and named it for himself: Constantinople. It sat where Europe
meets Asia, on the strait linking the Mediterranean to the Black Sea — a
customs post, naval base, and grain route in one. Behind it rose the
Theodosian Walls, a triple ring of defenses that would turn back sieges
for a thousand years.

This was the Greek-speaking continuation of the Roman Empire. Its emperor
held the title **basileus** (bah-SIH-loos) and ruled as an **autocrat**,
with power concentrated in one person. Around him the Byzantines built a
governing machine:

- **The theme system.** From the **7th century**, the empire was divided
  into military provinces called **themes**, each ruled by a general who
  held *both* military and civilian authority — taxes, courts, and soldiers
  under one command. Soldiers received land for service, tying the army's
  loyalty to the soil it defended. Fusing the two offices meant a province
  could defend itself without waiting for orders from the capital.
- **A professional bureaucracy.** Departments kept registers, collected
  taxes in coin, and paid officials in coin. A tax base in money let the
  state plan years ahead.
- **The solidus.** The gold **solidus** (later called the nomisma) was
  struck at a steady weight and purity for centuries. Merchants from London
  to Baghdad trusted it — a stable currency is a government promise you
  can weigh in your hand.

Orthodox Christianity was the state's other pillar. The **patriarch of
Constantinople** was the empire's leading bishop, second only to the pope
in Rome — a ranking that would matter. Worship centered on the **icon**
(ee-KON): a painted image of Christ, Mary, or a saint, venerated as a
window onto the holy. In 726, Emperor Leo III banned venerating icons,
opening the **Iconoclasm controversy** (726–843): over a century of
emperors destroying icons and monks defending them, until the icons were
restored in 843. The quarrel was never just about art — it was about
whether the emperor or the church had the last word on faith.

That question split Christendom in **1054**, the East–West **Schism** — a
formal split in a religious body. That year, the pope's representatives
and Patriarch Michael I Cerularius of Constantinople excommunicated each
other. The stated causes were real: the **filioque clause** (whether the
Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father *and the Son*, as Latin Christians
say, or from the Father alone, as Orthodox Christians hold), the pope's
claim to authority over all Christians, and liturgical differences such as
married priests and leavened versus unleavened bread. The deeper cause was
centuries of growing apart — different languages, different laws, two
proud centers each convinced it was the true heir of the apostles.

Byzantine missionaries also carried the faith north. In the **9th
century**, the brothers **Cyril and Methodius** preached to the Slavs of
Moravia in the Slavic language and devised an alphabet for it, helping
bring Orthodox Christianity to the peoples who became the Russians,
Ukrainians, and Serbs.

The end came in two blows. In **1204**, the Fourth Crusade — called to
fight in the Holy Land — was diverted and sacked Constantinople itself.
The city was retaken in 1261 but never recovered. In **1453**, the Ottoman
sultan **Mehmed II** conquered Constantinople after a 53-day siege, ending
an empire that had endured, in one continuous line, for over eleven
centuries.

**Worked example 1 — multi-factor reasoning: why did Justinian's law code
matter?** The emperor **Justinian I** (r. **527–565**) had his officials,
under the jurist **Tribonian**, compile centuries of Roman law into the
**Corpus Juris Civilis** — the Body of Civil Law (**529–534**) —
organizing it, resolving contradictions, and cutting dead statutes. A
single-factor answer says *it preserved Roman law.* True, but thin. A
multi-factor answer names what EACH factor contributed:

- **Law:** one authoritative text replaced piles of contradictory rulings.
  Predictable courts make trade and contracts possible.
- **Administration:** written and distributed, the code let the emperor
  govern distant provinces through officials reading the *same book* —
  law as a tool of central control.
- **Faith:** Justinian presented the code as God's order made visible, and
  built the **Hagia Sophia** (completed **537**; architects **Anthemius of
  Tralles** and **Isidore of Miletus**) as its twin: one dome over the
  church, one book over the courts.
- **Geography:** Justinian reconquered North Africa, Italy, and parts of
  Spain — and the code traveled with the armies, carrying
  Constantinople's order into lands it had not ruled for a century.

*The move: never settle for one cause when the evidence shows four. Name
each factor and its contribution.*

**Common error:** "Justinian made Roman law." He did not make it; he
*organized* it. His achievement was the editing — choosing, arranging,
publishing. Precision about what someone did is a historian's habit.

**Worked example 2 — two views of 1054.** The same event looks different
depending on where you stand.

**(Original model text written for this unit — not a historical quotation.)**
*Text A — a Byzantine courtier writes to a friend, 1054:* "You ask what
happened in the Great Church this summer. The pope's men demanded our
patriarch submit to Rome and change the creed our fathers taught us.
Patriarch Michael refused, as any faithful Roman would. They laid their
curse upon the altar and fled. We did not break with them — they broke
with us, and with the true faith."

**(Original model text written for this unit — not a historical quotation.)**
*Text B — a Latin merchant in Constantinople writes home to Venice, 1054:*
"The Greeks quarreled with the Holy Father's legates, and now each side
curses the other. The priests argue over a word in the creed and over
bread, but it is about who commands. Trade goes on — the customs men still
take their due — but I would not leave my goods here long."

**The courtier** emphasizes: the pope's men demanded, the patriarch
defended, the *Latins* caused the break. His purpose is reassurance — our
side is the faithful one; he omits anything the Greek side did wrong.
**The merchant** emphasizes: both sides cursing, the real issue power
("who commands"), his practical worry — his goods. His purpose is business
intelligence; he omits theology because theology does not pay his bills.
Neither is exactly lying; each is *selecting* — and selection is the engine
of perspective. The historian's question (D2.His.4.6-8) is not "who was
right?" but "why does each account emphasize what it does?"

**Common error:** treating perspective as bias to discard. The merchant's
letter is not *worse* than the courtier's for being self-interested — it
is *different*, and that difference is itself evidence about how the schism
felt in the marketplace.

### Part B — What "Roman" meant in Constantinople

A person in Constantinople around the year 1000 would have said: I am a
Roman — meaning I live under the **basileus**, I am judged by Roman law, I
worship with **icons**, I pay taxes in **solidi**, and my city has stood on
these straits since Constantine. "Roman" was not a bloodline; it was a
package of law, faith, coinage, and geography. The theme system kept the
provinces loyal, the walls kept the city standing, the code kept the courts
consistent, the church kept the faith shared. When later historians reached
for a new name for this civilization, they admitted how far it had traveled
from the Rome of the Caesars — while its own people never stopped calling
it Rome.

## Guided practice (with hints)

*Do these with the adult. Hints are in italics.*

1. In two or three sentences, explain why the **theme system** combined
   military and civilian rule instead of keeping them separate.
   
   *Hint: think about speed. If an enemy attacks, what is faster — one
   commander who can raise troops AND spend tax money, or two officials who
   must write to each other first?*

2. A student writes: "The Byzantines were Greek, not Roman." Repair the
   sentence so it is historically accurate.
   
   *Hint: they spoke Greek — true — but what did they CALL themselves? And
   who invented the word "Byzantine"?*

3. For Text B (the merchant) above, answer: who made it, for whom, for
   what purpose — and name one thing it emphasizes and one it leaves out.
   
   *Hint: "for whom" is Venice — people who need to know whether trade is
   safe. What does a merchant emphasize that a courtier wouldn't?*

## Independent practice

4. Write a four-factor explanation (law, administration, faith, geography)
   of why Constantinople survived as a capital for over 1,100 years — one
   sentence per factor, each naming specific evidence from the lesson.
5. Imagine a third voice on 1054: a Jewish merchant in Constantinople who
   trades with both Latins and Greeks. Write six to eight lines in that
   voice, then add two sentences on how this perspective differs from BOTH
   Text A and Text B.
6. Draw a timeline from 330 to 1453 and place these on it:
   Constantinople's founding (330), Justinian's reign (527–565), Iconoclasm
   (726–843), Cyril and Methodius (9th century), the 1054 Schism, the 1204
   sack, the 1453 conquest. Circle the two events that changed the empire's
   direction most, with one sentence each explaining your choice.

## Application

7. **Whose label?** A museum plaque reads: "The Byzantine Empire
   (330–1453)." Rewrite the title line as a 12th-century Constantinopolitan
   would have wanted it, then defend your rewrite in three sentences: what
   does the people's own name tell us that the historians' label hides?

## Exit check

8. In your own words: name the four factors that made Constantinople
   "Roman" in its people's eyes, and explain why two accounts of the same
   event can both be useful to a historian even when they disagree.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners hear "Greek-speaking" and conclude the
Byzantines were simply Greeks who stopped being Roman — or that
"Byzantine" was the empire's own name. Return to the evidence: the
people's own word was Rhomaioi, and the coin, the law, and the emperor's
title all said "Roman." The 16th-century label is a historian's
convenience, not the people's identity. **Answer-key reference:**
