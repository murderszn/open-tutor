# Lesson 05 — Comparing Perspectives: Whose Story?

**Unit:** [U02 — Byzantine, Islamic, and Mediterranean societies](../README.md) · **Week 9**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 10 of 16

## Goal

You will apply the perspective/purpose/audience frame to two accounts of
the same event — the 1204 sack of Constantinople — compare where they
agree and disagree, and explain why disagreement alone does not tell you
which account is true.

## Materials

- Inquiry notebook and pencil
- Lessons 01–04 (the frame from Lesson 01, the routine from Lesson 04)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer aloud or in the notebook:

1. In Lesson 01, the Byzantine courtier and the Latin merchant described
   1054 differently. What did each emphasize, and what did each leave out?
2. Define **perspective** as a historian uses it — not just "opinion."
3. Two witnesses describe the same car crash differently. Does that
   *automatically* mean one is lying? What else could explain it?

Question 3 is today's whole lesson in miniature. Keep your answer — you
will need it at the exit check.

## Teaching

### Part A — The frame: six questions for any account

A **perspective** is the position someone writes from — role, interests,
loyalties, and what they could actually see. Analyze any account with six
questions (D2.His.4, 5, 6.6-8; D3.3, 3.4.6-8):

1. **Who** made it? (Role, loyalties, what they stood to gain or lose.)
2. **When?** (Memory fades; politics shift.)
3. **For whom?** (Audience shapes what gets emphasized and softened.)
4. **For what purpose?** (Inform? Justify? Warn? Grieve?)
5. **What does it emphasize?** (Selection is the engine of perspective.)
6. **What does it leave out?** (The shadows are as informative as the
   spotlight.)

Then the comparison move: lay two accounts of the SAME event side by side
and sort claims into **agreement** (both say it — stronger together) and
**disagreement** (they differ — ask *why the makers would differ*, not just
who is right). Then the closing rule, to practice until it is reflex:
**disagreement between accounts does not automatically tell you which is
true. You need corroboration** — evidence from a *third*, independent
source kind — before deciding.

**Worked example 1 — full perspective analysis of ONE account.** Our event:
the **1204 sack of Constantinople**, when the Fourth Crusade — called to
fight in the Holy Land — instead captured and looted the great Christian
city.

**(Original model text written for this unit — not a historical quotation.)**
*Text A — in the voice of a Byzantine chronicler-kind writer, soon after
1204:* "In the year of our Lord 1204, the Latins who had taken the cross
against the infidel turned their swords against fellow Christians. They
broke the holy icons, stripped the Great Church of its gold, and set fire
to the city our fathers built. The emperor fled by night. What the
barbarians of old could not do in a thousand years, these crusaders did in
three days — they murdered Rome."

The six questions:

1. **Who?** A Byzantine chronicler-kind writer — an educated man of the
   capital, loyal to emperor and Orthodox Church, writing from inside the
   catastrophe.
2. **When?** Soon after 1204 — fresh memory, no distance. Grief is still
   hot; that is data, not disqualification.
3. **For whom?** Fellow Byzantines — readers who share his faith and loss,
   who need the event explained in terms they already believe.
4. **For what purpose?** To mourn, record, and fix blame: a lament AND an
   indictment. "They murdered Rome" is a verdict dressed as a chronicle.
5. **What does it emphasize?** Sacrilege above all — broken icons,
   stripped churches, burned city. The religious violation outweighs the
   political collapse (the emperor's flight gets one line). Emphasis
   reveals values.
6. **What does it leave out?** Everything complicating the indictment: the
   Byzantine palace intrigues that invited the crusaders in, the unpaid
   debts they claimed, the Byzantines who fought beside Latins. No room
   for shared blame — and that absence is itself evidence of purpose.

*The move to copy: work the six questions in order, and weigh "what does
it leave out" equally with "what does it emphasize." Silences testify
too.*

**Common error:** stopping at "he was biased because he was Byzantine."
"Biased" is a dead-end word — it dismisses the source instead of reading
it. The six questions ask what the account *is*, not whether to trust it.
A lament is not a failed chronicle; it is a successful lament, and laments
are evidence about grief, values, and blame.

### Part B — Comparison and corroboration

**(Original model text written for this unit — not a historical quotation.)**
*Text B — in the voice of a Venetian merchant-kind writer, to his partners
soon after 1204:* "You will have heard the news from Constantinople. The
fleet took the city after the Greeks broke every promise — the young
emperor we raised to the throne was murdered by his own people, and our men
were owed money no one would pay. There was looting, I will not deny it;
every army loots. But the trade concessions are now ours: the customs
houses, the warehouses, the routes to the Black Sea. Our investment is
repaid a hundredfold. Write and tell me what goods to send east."

**Worked example 2 — modeled comparison.**

*Where they agree:* both say the crusaders took the city in 1204; both say
there was looting ("stripped the Great Church" / "there was looting, I
will not deny it"); both describe political collapse around the emperor.
**Agreement across hostile perspectives is strong evidence** — when a
grieving Byzantine and a profiting Venetian both say the city was looted,
the looting is as close to certain as history gets.

*Where they disagree:* the chronicler calls the crusaders oath-breaking
murderers of Rome; the merchant calls them creditors collecting a debt
("owed money no one would pay"). The chronicler centers sacrilege; the
merchant centers the balance sheet ("our investment is repaid a
hundredfold"). The chronicler omits Byzantine broken promises; the
merchant shrugs at the sacrilege ("every army loots").

*How perspective explains the difference:* each maker's position predicts
his selection. The chronicler writes as a *victim* — purpose: lament and
indictment — so Byzantine faults vanish and Latin crimes fill the frame.
The merchant writes as a *beneficiary* — purpose: business intelligence —
so the sack becomes a transaction: debts, concessions, return on
investment. Neither needs to be lying; each answers a different question.
The chronicler asks *"how could they do this to us?"* The merchant asks
*"what did we gain?"* Different questions, different spotlights, different
shadows.

*The closing rule:* the comparison does NOT settle who broke faith first.
The chronicler says the crusaders did; the merchant says the Greeks did.
Disagreement alone decides nothing. To decide, we need **corroboration**:
a third, independent source kind — a treaty text showing what was
promised, coin evidence of payments made or withheld, a neutral party's
chronicle. **Corroboration** (from U01) means independent confirmation:
the same claim, from a source with no reason to agree. Until you have it,
the honest sentence is: "The accounts disagree on who broke faith, and
the cause remains disputed."

*The move to copy: agree → disagree → explain-by-perspective →
corroborate-before-deciding. Four steps, in order, every comparison.*

**Common error:** treating comparison as a contest — "Text A wins, it has
more detail." Detail is not truth; a liar can be detailed and an honest
witness brief. Comparison sorts claims; only corroboration settles them.

## Guided practice (with hints)

*Do these with the adult. Hints are in italics.*

1. Run the six questions on Text B (the merchant) — one sentence per
   question.
   
   *Hint: "for whom" is stated — "his partners." For "purpose," look at
   the last line: what does he actually want from his readers?*

2. Find one claim where the texts AGREE and one where they DISAGREE. For
   the disagreement, write two sentences: what each says, and how each
   maker's perspective explains the difference.
   
   *Hint: agreement — try the looting. Disagreement — try who broke faith
   first. Explain BY the perspective (victim's lament vs. beneficiary's
   ledger); don't just pick a winner.*

3. The merchant writes "every army loots." A student says this proves the
   sack wasn't a big deal. Write a three-sentence rebuttal using the
   closing rule.
   
   *Hint: whose perspective minimizes the looting, and why would HE want
   it minimized? What third source kind could show how bad the looting
   really was?*

## Independent practice

4. Choose one Lesson 04 development (law, scholarship, city life, trade
   goods, the caliphate). Invent two perspectives on it — e.g., a Baghdad
   bookseller and a Toledo translator on the translation movement — four
   lines in each voice. Then run the four-step comparison: agree →
   disagree → explain-by-perspective → what corroboration would you need?
5. Defend in a paragraph: "The chronicler's account is *more useful* than
   the merchant's for understanding Byzantine values — and *less useful*
   for understanding Venetian motives." (Usefulness depends on the
   question: say so explicitly.)
6. Add the 1204 sack to your Lesson 01 timeline if it is not there. Then,
   in three sentences, describe how the sack looks through each of this
   unit's three lenses — Byzantine, Islamic (how might a Cairene
   chronicler report Christians sacking Christians?), and Latin Christian.

## Application

7. **Project preparation.** The unit project compares two societies using
   evidence. Draft your comparison plan: (a) the two societies and the
   question your comparison answers; (b) the four-step routine you will
   use; (c) two source kinds you will seek for each society, and why those
   kinds fit your question. Bring this plan to the next session.

## Exit check

8. In your own words: state the six perspective questions and the closing
   rule — then answer Readiness question 3 again with this lesson's
   vocabulary: when two witnesses to 1204 disagree, what are THREE
   possible explanations besides "one is lying"?

## Supports, alternate response modes, extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads both model texts aloud in
  distinct voices; the six questions stay posted as a checklist;
  **perspective** and **corroboration** stay on the word wall.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may sort claims into "agree" and
  "disagree" columns with sticky notes before writing; the six-question
  analysis may be dictated or drawn as a labeled diagram.
- **Extension:** research what real chroniclers wrote about 1204 — e.g.,
  the Byzantine Niketas Choniates or the crusader Geoffrey de
  Villehardouin — and compare one REAL account's perspective with the
  model texts': what did the model get right about how such a writer would
  see it?

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners hear "perspective" and conclude all
accounts are equally true — or equally false ("everyone's biased, so
nothing is knowable"). Fight both with the agreement finding: when hostile
witnesses agree (the looting), we know something solid. Perspective
analysis does not dissolve truth; it locates it — in corroborated
agreement, not in any single voice. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L05.
