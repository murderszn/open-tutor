# Lesson 04 — Change and Continuity Across the Era

**Unit:** [U02 — Byzantine, Islamic, and Mediterranean societies](../README.md) · **Week 8**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 8 of 16

## Goal

You will use the change-and-continuity routine to classify five
developments from this unit — citing evidence from at least two source
kinds for each — and you will repair a flawed classification.

## Teaching

### Part A — The routine, made explicit

Historians sort developments with three labels (D2.His.2.6-8):

- **CHANGE** — it became different. *The language of administration in
  Egypt shifted from Greek to Arabic under the Umayyads.*
- **CONTINUITY** — it stayed the same. *Constantinople's site kept its
  strategic value from 330 to 1453; geography does not renegotiate.*
- **BOTH** — each true *in a different respect*. *Paper (L03): continuity
  of the need to write; change in material, cost, and access.*

Three rules:

1. **Name the respect.** "Both" alone is a shrug — say *in what respect*
   it continued and *in what respect* it changed.
2. **Cite evidence from at least two source kinds.** A chronicle says what
   people *claimed*; a coin or building shows what people *did*. Two kinds
   agreeing is a classification standing on two legs.
3. **Respect the timeframe.** Change across 50 years and continuity across
   500 are different claims. Always say *when*.

**Worked example 1 — full modeled classification: the translation
movement as BOTH.** Run the routine on the translation movement (Greek,
Persian, and Indian works into Arabic, 8th–13th centuries; then Arabic
works into Latin, 12th century).

*Step 1 — the respects.* **Continuity in content:** the core texts
survived. Ptolemy's astronomy and Aristotle's logic, copied in Baghdad and
later in Toledo, stayed recognizably the same works — a 13th-century Latin
reader met substantially the same Aristotle a 9th-century Arabic reader
had. **Change in language, audience, and use:** Greek became Arabic became
Latin; readers changed from Abbasid court scholars to Toledo translators
to European university masters; *use* changed — astronomy that served
caliphal courts now served Christian universities, and algebra that
organized inheritance law now organized merchants' ledgers.

*Step 2 — two source kinds.* **Chronicle-kind:** court histories and
translators' prefaces *say* the translation happened — Gerard of Cremona's
Latin versions name their Arabic sources. **Artifact-kind:** the
manuscripts *show* it — the same diagram of Ptolemy's epicycles in a Greek
codex, an Arabic copy, and a Latin copy is physical proof that content
traveled while language changed. Claim plus object: two legs.

*Step 3 — timeframe.* Roughly 750–1300: the Arabic phase centered on the
8th–10th centuries, the Latin phase in the 12th. "Both" holds across the
whole span — itself a finding: five centuries of simultaneous continuity
and change.

*Verdict, in the frame:* "The translation movement was **continuity** in
content — the same Greek works surviving five centuries — and **change**
in language, audience, and use, because chronicles record the translations
and the manuscripts show the same diagrams in three languages, c.
750–1300."

*The move to copy: respects, two source kinds, timeframe — in that order,
every time. Skip one and the classification wobbles.*

**Common error:** "both" as a dodge — writing "it was both change and
continuity" and stopping. The grade is in the respects: *what* continued,
*what* changed, *proved by what*, *when*.

### Part B — Five developments, one routine

Apply the routine across the unit. The facts are supplied; the
classification is your job in practice.

**(a) Law: Roman law → Justinian's code → later legal systems.** Roman law
predated Justinian by centuries; his **Corpus Juris Civilis** (529–534)
organized it; later legal systems drew on the organized version.
Continuity of the legal tradition; change in form (scattered rulings → one
code) and reach (it outlived its empire by a millennium).

**(b) Scholarship: Greek learning preserved, extended, retransmitted.**
Continuity: the texts survived — Aristotle was not reinvented. Change:
Baghdad's scholars did not merely copy; Ibn al-Haytham's optics and
al-Khwarizmi's algebra *extended* Greek foundations, and the Latin
translations *retransmitted* them to new readers. Three respects — richly
BOTH.

**(c) City life: Constantinople, 330–1453.** Continuity: inhabited the
whole 1,100+ years, still the straits' tollbooth in 1453 as in 330.
Change: population and fortunes rose and collapsed — a great metropolis at
its height, a shadow after the 1204 sack. Same site, different city: BOTH
— *place* continuous, *scale and prosperity* changed.

**(d) Trade goods: silk and spices across the centuries.** Continuity: silk
and spices moved along Afro-Eurasian routes the whole era — the goods
barely changed. Change: *who carried them* shifted (Persian middlemen,
then Arab merchants, then Venetian galleys) as routes and treaties
shifted. The cargo stayed; the carriers turned over: BOTH.

**(e) Religious authority: the caliphate fragments.** Change: the single
**caliph** of the Rashidun and Umayyad eras gave way, by the 900s, to
rival claimants — Abbasids in Baghdad, Fatimids in Cairo, Umayyads in
Córdoba. Continuity: the *idea* of the caliphate survived fragmentation;
the title remained worth claiming. A title can outlive the power it named.

## Guided practice (with hints)

*Do these with the adult. Hints are in italics.*

1. **Error analysis — repair this classification.** A student wrote:
   
   *"The caliphate fragmenting was continuity because the caliphs were
   still Muslim."*
   
   Find the two flaws: (1) it ignores the obvious change (one caliph →
   rival caliphs); (2) "still Muslim" proves nothing, since it was true
   before, during, and after. Rewrite it properly: name the change
   respect, the continuity respect, and one real piece of evidence for
   each.
   
   *Hint: change = number and rivalry of claimants (Baghdad, Cairo,
   Córdoba — three capitals, three caliphs). Continuity = the title's
   survival as worth claiming. Evidence for change: the three capitals
   (chronicle-kind). Evidence for continuity: all three rulers stamped the
   SAME title on their coins (coin-kind).*

2. Classify development (c) — Constantinople's city life — in the full
   frame: respects, two source kinds, timeframe.
   
   *Hint: artifact-kind — the walls and buildings, still standing on the
   same site. Chronicle-kind — accounts of the 1204 sack and the shrunken
   15th-century city. Timeframe: 330–1453.*

3. Why does development (b) — scholarship — need THREE respects
   (preserved, extended, retransmitted) rather than two? What is lost if
   "extended" and "retransmitted" collapse into one?
   
   *Hint: extension = new knowledge (optics, algebra); retransmission = new
   readers (Latin Europe). One is a change in content, the other in
   audience — different respects, different evidence.*

## Independent practice

4. Classify development (a) — law — in the full frame: the continuity
   respect, the change respect(s), two source kinds (one artifact- or
   coin-kind), and the timeframe.
5. Classify development (d) — trade goods — in the full frame. Your two
   source kinds must include one "object" kind (artifact, coin) and one
   "words" kind (chronicle, traveler's account, or letter-kind).
6. **Find the flaw.** A student calls the translation movement pure
   CHANGE: "The language changed from Greek to Arabic to Latin, so nothing
   continued." Write a four-sentence rebuttal: name what this misses, cite
   the manuscript evidence from Worked example 1, and explain why "the
   words changed" does not mean "nothing continued."

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners treat "both" as the safe answer for
everything and stop at the label, or cite one vivid chronicle and call it
done. Enforce the discipline mechanically: no classification is accepted
until it names respects, cites two kinds, and states a timeframe — the
routine is the skill, not the label. **Answer-key reference:**
