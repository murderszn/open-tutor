## §L01 — Lesson 01

**Readiness:** 1. Law codes or tax records (documents), coins, and
buildings or other artifacts — leftovers that show how power was
organized. 2. It needed an army and tax base that survived, a defensible
capital, laws and administration that worked, and diplomacy with
neighbors. 3. People name themselves (Rhomaioi — Romans); later
historians invent labels (Byzantine, 16th century) for their own
convenience.

**Guided practice 1.** One commander holding both military and civilian
authority can raise troops *and* spend tax money immediately; two
separate officials must write to each other (and to the capital) first.
Speed in a crisis is the reason.

**Guided practice 2 (sample repair).** "The Byzantines spoke Greek, but
they called themselves Romans (Rhomaioi); 'Byzantine' is a label
historians invented in the 16th century." (Must contain: Greek-speaking =
true; self-name = Roman; "Byzantine" = later historians' label.)

**Guided practice 3.** Maker: a Latin merchant living in
Constantinople. Audience: people in Venice who need business
intelligence. Purpose: report whether trade is safe / what is happening.
Emphasizes: the power struggle ("who commands") and practical trade
worries. Leaves out: the theological substance of the dispute.

**Independent practice 4–7 / Application / Exit check.** Open. Criteria:
4 — one sentence per factor, each naming lesson evidence (law: Corpus
Juris Civilis; administration: themes/bureaucracy; faith: shared Orthodox
identity; geography: straits, walls, trade position). 5 — the Jewish
merchant's voice differs from both (trades with both sides; the schism is
a Christian dispute that complicates business, not a matter of faith for
him). 6 — dates placed correctly; two circled events each defended with
one reason. 7 — rewrite uses the people's own name (Romans/Rhomaioi);
defense explains what the self-name reveals (continuity claimed with
Rome) that "Byzantine" hides. **Exit check 8 (sample).** The four factors:
law, faith, administration/coinage, geography. Two disagreeing accounts
can both be useful because each reveals its maker's perspective —
position, purpose, audience; disagreement is data about perspectives, and
corroboration decides the facts.

## §L02 — Lesson 02

**Readiness:** 1. Governing institutions/law, a shared faith or identity,
and an administration that collects resources. 2. A trade town between
empires grows rich on transit — caravans, pilgrims, and goods must pass
through; it grows restless because tribes compete and new ideas arrive
with the caravans. 3. Counting Year 1 from the Hijra says the community
dates itself from its founding as a society, not from a birth or a
battle.

**Guided practice 1.** Damascus: between the Mediterranean and the desert
routes, near old Byzantine administrative centers — a capital placed to
rule the conquered Byzantine east. Baghdad: on the Tigris amid the
richest farmlands, at the crossroads of Persian and Arab worlds — a
capital placed at the empire's new eastern center of gravity. (Accept any
answer tying each site to routes, resources, or older centers.)

**Guided practice 2.** In Constantinople the emperor commissioned the
code — law flowed from the ruler's authority. In Islamic societies the
ulama, a class of scholars, interpreted the Qur'an and hadith — law
rested substantially in scholars' hands, not only in the caliph's
officials. (Who holds the law differs: ruler vs. scholars.)

**Guided practice 3 (sample).** "The House of Wisdom did not invent
science from nothing — it translated, preserved, and extended Greek,
Persian, and Indian works, then added original advances in optics,
algebra, and medicine. The achievement was transmission *plus* creation."

**Independent practice 4–7 / Exit check.** Open. Criteria: 4 — per stop,
one continuity and one change (paper: China → Baghdad → Latin West;
astrolabe: Greek foundations → Baghdad refinement → European
navigation). 5 — "proves" column: three items each tied to a specific
line (partnership across cities; pepper price fell on Sicilian
competition; switch-to-flax strategy); "cannot prove" column: three
items each naming the source kind that could (tax records for volume;
other merchants' letters for typicality; port registers for totals).
6 — the number is unverifiable and likely boasting; but the *claim* is
evidence of what Córdoba valued and wanted projected (a city competing
in books). 7 — five to six sentences using at least two lesson facts
(merchant networks carried Arabic and trust law; Sufi lodges along
routes; shared commercial law lowered risk; pilgrimage routes moved
people and ideas). **Exit check 8 (sample).** Formation order: Muhammad
and revelations → Hijra (622) → Rashidun → Umayyad → Abbasid. One
letter shows one merchant's week; it cannot show the whole economy —
that needs many letters plus tax and port records.

## §L03 — Lesson 03

**Readiness:** 1. Numerals: India → Baghdad → Latin West. Paper: China →
Baghdad → Latin West. 2. What people build — ships, treaties, ports,
warehouses — decides; the water is the same either way. 3. The Venetian
wants a safe harbor, predictable duties, and goods; the Alexandrian ruler
wants customs revenue, imported goods, and diplomatic leverage.

**Guided practice 0 — Whose eyes?** Open. Criteria: (a) three observed
details from the illustration or its text alternative (stalls, sacks,
ship, Hagia Sophia). (b) Customs official: notices tax stamps, dues,
volumes — his question is "what is owed to the emperor?" (c) Venetian
captain: notices prices, unloading speed, buyers — his question is "will
this voyage pay?" (d) Two things the picture can never tell us (the
year; what anyone thought; whether the scene was typical; what happened
next).

**Guided practice 1.** Four stops labeled with handlers: Alexandria
(broker + customs), the galley (Venetian merchant; risk changes hands),
the open sea (no hands; wages, wear, and bribes add cost), Venice
(weighers, tax men, onward sale).

**Guided practice 2.** Ruling: his kingdom held Greek, Arabic, and Latin
populations — addressing each in its own learned language bought
legitimacy. Knowledge: three scholarly traditions arguing in one room
produce new learning faster than one tradition alone.

**Guided practice 3.** Open. Sample: the compass makes voyages safer and
less season-bound → more voyages per year and new routes → the next
exchange is larger and cheaper.

**Independent practice 4–6 / Application 7 / Exit check.** Open. Criteria:
4 — four stops minimum, each naming handler and what changes hands
(money, tax, risk, work). 5 — numerals in the frame: continuity (the same
mathematical truths — place value, zero) and change (new users:
scholars → merchants; new language; new purpose: bookkeeping), each with
evidence from Lessons 02–03. 6 — the ledger line shows land and sea
routes, faith (pilgrims) and trade (silk), and state power (soldiers)
overlapping in one courtyard — the categories were not separate worlds.
7 — three clauses (safety for merchants; fixed, predictable duties; a
fondaco — warehouse, chapel, consul) each with a Venetian reason, plus
one sentence on the ruler's gain (revenue, goods, diplomacy). **Exit
check 8 (sample).** The sea connected them through trade, treaties, and
translation even where faith divided them — profit and curiosity crossed
lines that doctrine drew. "Change" means it became different; "both"
means continuity in one respect and change in another, each respect
backed by evidence.

## §L04 — Lesson 04

**Readiness:** 1. Paper: continuity of the need to write; change of
material, cost, and who could afford books. 2. Continuity of the site's
strategic value (330–1453); change of the city's scale and fortunes. 3.
One source kind carries one perspective; two independent kinds agreeing
makes the claim stand on two legs.

**Guided practice 1 (repair — sample).** Flaw 1: it ignores the change —
one caliph became rival claimants in Baghdad, Cairo, and Córdoba. Flaw
2: "still Muslim" proves nothing, since it was true before, during, and
after. **Rewrite:** "The caliphate's fragmentation was **change** in
that one office became three rival claimants (chronicles record the
Baghdad, Fatimid, and Córdoban caliphates), and **continuity** in that
the title 'caliph' remained worth claiming — all three stamped it on
their coins."

**Guided practice 2 (sample).** "Constantinople's city life was **both**:
**continuity** of place — the same straits-side site inhabited 330–1453
(artifact-kind: the walls and churches still on that site; the solidus
still minted there); **change** of scale — a great metropolis at its
height, a shadow after the 1204 sack (chronicle-kind: accounts of the
sack and the shrunken 15th-century city). Timeframe: 330–1453."

**Guided practice 3.** Three respects because preserved (texts survived),
extended (new knowledge: optics, algebra), and retransmitted (new
readers: Latin Europe) are different claims. Collapsing extended and
retransmitted loses the difference between a change in *content* (new
science) and a change in *audience* (new readers) — different respects,
different evidence.

**Independent practice 4–7 / Exit check.** Open. Criteria: 4 — law in the
full frame (continuity: the Roman legal tradition; change: scattered
rulings → one code, and reach beyond the empire's life; two source kinds
including an artifact/coin kind — e.g., law manuscripts + coins showing
imperial authority; timeframe). 5 — trade goods in the full frame with
one "object" kind and one "words" kind. 6 — four-sentence rebuttal:
names what "pure change" misses (the content survived — manuscript
evidence of the same diagrams in Greek, Arabic, Latin); explains that
changed words do not mean nothing continued. 7 — any uncovered
development, full frame (respects, two kinds, timeframe). **Exit check 8
(sample).** The three rules: name the respects; cite two source kinds;
state the timeframe. Task 1 failed rule 1 (no respects — "still Muslim"
is not a respect in which something continued *or* changed) and rule 2
(no evidence cited at all).

## §L05 — Lesson 05

**Readiness:** 1. The courtier emphasized Latin aggression and the
defense of the faith; the merchant emphasized the power struggle and
practical trade. Each left out the other's center. 2. Perspective is the
position someone writes from — role, interests, loyalties, what they
could see — not merely an opinion. 3. No: different positions, purposes,
audiences, access to facts, or memories can explain it.

**Guided practice 1 (sample).** 1. Who: a Venetian merchant, a
beneficiary of the sack. 2. When: soon after 1204 — fresh but
self-interested. 3. For whom: his business partners ("You will have
heard… Write and tell me"). 4. Purpose: business intelligence — what
goods to send east. 5. Emphasizes: debts owed, concessions won, returns
on investment ("repaid a hundredfold"). 6. Leaves out: the sacrilege and
the suffering — "every army loots" shrugs them away.

**Guided practice 2 (sample).** Agreement: both say the crusaders took
the city in 1204 and that looting and destruction followed. Disagreement:
who broke faith first — the chronicler says the crusaders turned on
fellow Christians; the merchant says the Greeks broke promises and owed
money. The victim's lament spotlights Latin crimes; the beneficiary's
ledger spotlights debts and returns — each selection follows from
position.

**Guided practice 3 (sample rebuttal).** "'Every army loots' is the
beneficiary's perspective minimizing what he gained from — he wants the
looting normal so the profit looks clean. One account cannot settle how
bad it was. Corroboration would need a third, independent kind — e.g.,
Byzantine records of specific losses, or archaeology of burn layers."

**Independent practice 4–7 / Exit check.** Open. Criteria: 4 — two
four-line voices, then the four steps (agree → disagree →
explain-by-perspective → corroboration needed). 5 — paragraph states the
usefulness rule explicitly: the chronicler serves questions about
Byzantine values; the merchant serves questions about Venetian motives;
usefulness depends on the question. 6 — 1204 added to the timeline; three
sentences, one per lens (Byzantine: catastrophe and betrayal; a Cairene
chronicler: Christians sacking Christians — evidence of Frankish
disunity; Latin: a detour turned profitable, or a scandal, depending on
the writer). 7 — project plan with societies, question, four-step
routine, and two source kinds per society with reasons. **Exit check 8
(sample).** The six questions: who, when, for whom, for what purpose,
what emphasized, what left out. The closing rule: disagreement alone does
not tell you which account is true — you need corroboration. Three
explanations besides lying: different positions (victim vs.
beneficiary); different purposes/audiences (lament for Byzantines vs.
business letter for partners); different access (the chronicler saw the
churches stripped; the merchant saw the warehouses filled).

## Project — "Two Societies, One Sea" (sample at the Strong level)

**Thesis (sample).** "Byzantium and the Abbasid world were most alike in
that both ran on written law administered by professionals — and most
different in *who* held the law: the emperor's code in Constantinople,
the scholars' interpretation in Baghdad. The difference reveals that
Byzantium located authority in the ruler's person, while Islamic
societies located it in a learned tradition no single ruler owned."

**Evidence rows (sample, 4 rows, 3 kinds).** 1. Claim: both states paid
officials in coin. Evidence: the solidus's stable weight across
centuries; Abbasid dinars stamped with caliphal authority. Kinds:
coins. 2. Claim: law was written and public. Evidence: the Corpus Juris
Civilis manuscripts; collections of legal opinions (fiqh) from Baghdad.
Kinds: chronicle/manuscript-kind. 3. Claim: each society's law-holders
differed. Evidence: Tribonian's commission under Justinian (imperial
order); the ulama's independent schools (biographical dictionaries of
scholars). Kinds: chronicle-kind + secondary interpretation. 4. Claim:
both used law to order trade. Evidence: Byzantine commercial regulations
in the law code; the Cairo Geniza-kind merchant letters showing
partnership law in practice. Kinds: manuscript-kind + letter-kind.

**Perspective paragraph (sample).** "My strongest evidence is the
Geniza-kind merchant letter: its creator is a working merchant writing to
his partner, so its purpose is profit, not posterity — which limits it to
one firm's view but strengthens it as proof that commercial law worked on
the ground, not just in jurists' books."

**Counterclaim (sample).** "One could argue the difference is overstated:
Byzantine emperors also deferred to patriarchs, and caliphs also issued
decrees. But the counterclaim is weaker because the *default* holder of
legal authority still differed — code from the throne versus
interpretation from the schools."
