## §L01 — Lesson 01

**Readiness:** 1. About 500–1700 CE. 2. Open — accept anything accurate.
3. The past is gone; historians work only from what it left behind, so the
questions they ask determine which leftovers they look for.

**Guided practice 1.**
a. **Supporting** — answerable: toys and household artifacts from Cairo
digs, texts describing family life. Both checks pass.
b. **Compelling** — "mattered most" is a judgment; arguable, no single
answer.
c. **Supporting** — answerable from manor account rolls (wage entries
before/after plague years) and chronicles describing labor shortages.
d. **Neither** — "smarter" judges across eras; no leftover can settle it.

**Guided practice 2 (sample).** Supporting Q1: *"What wages do manor rolls
record in the ten years before and after the plague?"* — kind: documents
(manor account rolls). Supporting Q2: *"What do chronicles say about
workers leaving manors for better pay?"* — kind: chronicles.

**Guided practice 3 (sample repair).** *"Which sea routes did medieval
ships sail, and what cargoes did they carry?"* — answerable from
shipwrecks (artifacts), port tax records (documents), and contemporary
maps. The repair replaces uncheckable feelings with trace-leaving facts.

**Independent practice 4–6 / Application 7.** Open. Criteria: the
compelling question is arguable (not merely big); each supporting question
passes both checks; the audit (task 7) names what makes the question
compelling and defends the strongest stepping stone.

**Exit check 8 (sample).** A compelling question is big, open, and worth
arguing about; a supporting question is smaller and answerable, and each
one climbs toward the compelling question. A supporting question must
pass two checks: *Can it be answered?* and *From what leftover (source
kind) would the answer come?*

## §L02 — Lesson 02

**Readiness:** 1. Maps, travelers' accounts, artifacts, coins, chronicles.
2. A traveler's account. 3. A map.

**Guided practice 1.**
a. **Map (primary).** CAN: show what its maker knew of coastlines and
places. CANNOT: show what he never heard of, or why he drew it this way.
b. **Artifact.** CAN: prove making techniques and materials of the time.
CANNOT: say who used it or what it meant to them.
c. **Secondary interpretation.** CAN: give an organized overview and point
to its sources. CANNOT: prove anything beyond those sources; it adds the
author's own argument.

**Guided practice 2 (sample).** Made in 2026; made from imagination (an AI
image generator), not from observation of the real past. It cannot answer,
for example, *"What did a 13th-century oasis market look like?"* — it
shows no real place at all.

**Guided practice 3 (sample).** The claim is about the real medieval
past, but the evidence is a 2026 reconstruction. It fails the
reconstruction test (made long after, made from imagination), so it
proves nothing about real markets.

**Independent practice 4 (sample pairs).** Map — well: where places were /
cannot: why people went there. Traveler's account — well: what a visitor
saw / cannot: what he never visited or exaggerated. Artifact — well: that
something was made and how / cannot: what it meant. Coin — well: a ruler
and date existed; it traveled / cannot: why or with whom. Chronicle —
well: sequence of events as the writer saw them / cannot: the other
side's story.

**Independent practice 5 (sample label).** *"Imagined market scene,
generated by AI in 2026. This is a modern illustration, not a historical
source: it shows no real place and proves nothing about the medieval
world. It may prompt questions — each must be answered from real
sources."*

**Application 6.** Criteria: three supporting questions from Lesson 01,
each with the single best starting kind and one honest limitation (e.g.,
"traveler's account — but one viewpoint, so I need corroboration").

**Exit check 7 (sample).** Ask: (1) *"When was this picture made?"* (2)
*"Was it made from observation of the real past, or from imagination?"*
It is disqualified if made long after the events and from imagination
rather than from the past's leftovers.

## §L03 — Lesson 03

**Readiness:** 1. C3. 2. The dashed red line is a road. 3. North is up,
per the compass rose.

**Guided practice 1.** (a) **E1**. (b) No road is drawn to the east gate,
so there is no road distance — the honest answer uses the scale bar on
the straight line: E1 (500,110) to well C1 (300,110) is 200 map units =
**2 km** straight-line. (c) Sample claim: *"The market is 2 km west of
the well by road"* (proved by grid + scale bar). Sample cannot-show:
*"The map cannot show who owned the market or what it sold — that would
take a document or an account."*

**Guided practice 2 (sample).** Constantinople sits on both the Silk Road
and Mediterranean bands. Meeting cities grew rich because every traveler
passing through paid for food, lodging, stabling, tolls, and exchange —
two corridors meant double the traffic.

**Guided practice 3 (sample).** The schematic's subtitle says distances
are NOT to scale — spacing is layout, like a subway map. "Halfway" on the
schematic means nothing about real distance.

**Independent practice 4.** Criteria: three claims each citing a map tool
(grid label, scale-bar measurement, key symbol, compass direction); two
cannot-show statements naming the gap (motives, sounds, smells, beliefs,
prices, people).

**Independent practice 5.** Longest single corridor: the **Silk Road** —
Chang'an, Dunhuang, Samarkand, Baghdad, Constantinople (5 cities).
Sample evidence sentence: *"A traveler's account describing the road
between two of these cities would prove someone really traveled it — the
schematic alone only proves the route existed as a connection."*

**Application 6.** Criteria: names one distortion, explains the merchant
benefit, and states what the map therefore can't be trusted for (e.g.,
distort size to fit the known world → can't be trusted for true country
sizes).

**Exit check 7 (sample).** The practice map is a *measured* map — built
for locating and measuring (grid + scale bar). The schematic is a
*connection* diagram — built to show which cities linked to which.
Measuring the schematic gives false distances because its spacing is
deliberate layout, not geography.

## §L04 — Lesson 04

**Readiness:** 1. When was it made? What was it made from? What can it do
(and not do)? 2. What the town was like, what people believed, or why the
coin came there. 3. Because one source can be mistaken, biased, or
incomplete — a second *independent* source corroborates the claim.

**Guided practice 1 (sample).** *Origin:* a town record-keeper, 1187, Qasr
Oasis (dated entry, local focus). *Authority:* high on town events (well
repair, market days); low on anything beyond the town. *Structure:* yearly
sequence, gifts recorded before politics — shaped by civic memory.
*Context:* a peaceful year ("raiders did not come") — prosperity may be
emphasized. *Corroborative value:* unknown alone — check against a
traveler's account or tax record. **Verdict:** trustworthy for what the
town recorded about itself in 1187; check its claims about outsiders
elsewhere; read its flattery ("the generous") as viewpoint, not fact.

**Guided practice 2.** (a) Late summer — stated. (b) Yes — *"cheaper than
in Chang'an by a third"* is a comparison only someone who knows Chang'an
prices could make. (c) He wants the reader to carry extra water and budget
for doubled water costs and a risen toll — it is a practical note, meant
to be *used*.

**Guided practice 3 (sample).** Anonymous does not mean useless: structure
(a practical list, with little motive to exaggerate), context (written
for use on the road, which rewards accuracy), and corroborative value
(its prices and tolls can be checked against other sources) can all be
judged without a name. Only origin is weakened.

**Independent practice 4–5 / Application 6.** Scored against the
criteria in the lesson; sample corroboration pairs for task 6: (1) a
second, independent traveler's account naming the same toll at the same
pass; (2) toll-station receipts or tax records (documents) showing the
collected amounts.

**Exit check 7 (sample).** A *limitation* is built into the kind — no coin
can ever say why it traveled, no matter how carefully read. A *failed
test* is about this source for this question — the merchant's note fails
the authority test on palace politics (he never went near a palace), even
though it passes on road conditions.

## §L05 — Lesson 05

**Readiness:** 1. Change = how something became different over time;
continuity = what stayed the same. 2. Sample: maps — show where, not why;
coins — prove a ruler/date, not motives. 3. Because a claim that merely
"sounds right" could be a guess — evidence is what separates history from
storytelling.

**Guided practice 1 (sample).** *Before:* sailors hugged coasts and
guessed at seasons. *After:* full sailing calendars timed to the monsoon —
leave Aden in summer, return in winter. *Evidence:* travelers' accounts
describing timed voyages; shipwreck artifacts with multi-port cargoes.
*Verdict:* **both** — the winds never changed (continuity of nature); what
changed was human knowledge and timing (change in practice).

**Guided practice 2 (sample).** *Verdict:* **both**, with continuity
answering the question. Rulers rose and fell (change), but the cities'
wealth, crowds, and stranger-filled markets persisted across dynasties
(continuity) — corridor position outlasted regimes. Evidence: maps (city
positions on corridors), coins (many mints in one market), chronicles
(tax records across dynasties).

**Guided practice 3 (sample).** The flaw: the brief is about the *form*
of travelers' writing, not how much they knew. The form — market notes,
road costs, surprises — stayed the same across five centuries, so the
verdict is **continuity**; "knew more" answers a different question.

**Independent practice 4–6 / Exit check 7.** Scored on the three steps
(precise name → before/after → two cited kinds → verdict). Exit sample:
*"'Both' is two claims, not a shrug — it requires stating what changed
AND what continued, with evidence for each side, which is exactly the
same work as arguing one side."*

## §UA — Unit assessment (25 points)

**Task 1.** a. Sample supporting questions: *"What did travelers write
about Timbuktu's schools?"* / *"What goods were traded there, and who
taxed them?"* / *"What buildings for learning still stand, and when were
they built?"* (3) b. Each with first-check kind + limitation, e.g.:
traveler's account — one viewpoint, needs corroboration; artifacts
(manuscripts/buildings) — prove making, not motives; coins — date rulers,
not scholars. (3)

**Task 2.** a. East gate E1 (500,110) → well C1 (300,110): 200 map units
= **2 km** straight-line (no road is drawn, so the honest answer is the
scale-bar straight line). (2) b. Two claims with tools cited (2). c. Two
cannot-shows, each paired with a source kind that could show it (2).

**Task 3.** a. Merchant's note: traveler's account (primary). Chronicle
entry: chronicle (primary). (1) b. Three tests on the chronicle, one
sentence each — see §L04 GP1 sample. (3) c. Sample: a second traveler's
account naming the same toll at the same pass; toll-station receipts or
tax records showing amounts collected. (2) d. The town itself / its
ruler — civic memory. Clue: local events (well, market, gifts) recorded
first, in a flattering tone. (1) e. Sample: *"It was generated in 2026 —
a reconstruction, not a leftover from the past."* (1)

**Task 4.** a. **Both**: the material changed (parchment/papyrus →
paper; books cheaper) while the human jobs continued (chronicles,
contracts, holy books still written). (2) b. Sample: artifacts (surviving
priced manuscripts — but survival is patchy); documents (mill or market
records — but records serve tax purposes, not price histories). (2)
c. Sample: *"Because most developments change something while something
else continues, and naming both is more truthful than picking one."* (1)
