# Lesson 05 — Change and Continuity

**Unit:** [U01 — Medieval Geography: Sources and Historical Inquiry](../README.md) · **Week 5**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 11 of 16

## Goal

You will **classify** medieval developments as **change**, **continuity**,
or **both** — citing evidence from at least two source kinds — and explain
your reasoning in an organized argument.

## Materials

- Inquiry notebook and pencil
- The three development briefs below (original summaries written for this
  lesson; each names the source kinds behind it)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. In one sentence each: what do *change* and *continuity* mean in
   history?
2. Name two source kinds and one limitation of each.
3. Why must a change-or-continuity claim cite evidence instead of just
   sounding right?

## Teaching

### Part A — The classification move

Historians constantly sort the past into what changed and what stayed the
same — and the honest answer is often **both**. The move has three steps:

1. **Name the development** precisely (not "trade" but "how paper reached
   the Mediterranean").
2. **Find the before and the after** — what was true earlier, what was
   true later.
3. **Cite the evidence** — at least two source kinds — and state the
   verdict: change, continuity, or both, with the reasoning shown.

**Worked example 1 — both (paper travels west).**
*Development:* by the 1100s, paper — invented in China centuries earlier —
was being made in Baghdad, Cairo, and Muslim Spain, and European
chronicles were written on it.
*Before:* Mediterranean writing ran on parchment (animal skin) and papyrus.
*After:* paper mills operated from Samarkand to Spain; books became
cheaper to make.
*Evidence:* artifacts (surviving paper manuscripts, dated), travelers'
accounts (descriptions of paper markets), chronicles (records of mill
foundings).
*Verdict:* **both.** The *material* changed (parchment → paper); the
*continuity* is deeper — people still wrote chronicles, contracts, and
holy books, still kept records of what mattered. New tool, same human
jobs.

**Worked example 2 — mostly change (the camel saddle transforms the
Sahara).**
*Development:* from around the early centuries CE, improved camel saddles
let caravans carry heavy loads across the Sahara — gold south-to-north,
salt north-to-south.
*Before:* the desert was a near-barrier; crossings were small and rare.
*After:* regular caravans of hundreds of camels linked Sijilmasa to
Timbuktu and Gao; desert-edge cities grew rich on the trade.
*Evidence:* artifacts (saddles, rock art of camels), travelers' accounts
(descriptions of caravans), coins (minted wealth in trading cities),
chronicles (kingdom records of trade revenue).
*Verdict:* **change** — the Sahara went from barrier to highway. The
continuity worth noting: trade itself is ancient; what changed was the
*scale* the desert could carry. A good historian states the continuity
even when the verdict is change.

Notice what both examples do: they never claim from one source kind
alone, and they never pretend a development is *only* change or *only*
continuity without checking.

### Part B — Development briefs (classify these)

**Brief 1 — The monsoon calendar.** Indian Ocean sailors learned that
seasonal winds blow one way for months, then reverse. By the medieval era,
whole sailing calendars were built around the monsoon: leave Aden in
summer, return in winter. *Source kinds: travelers' accounts describing
voyages; artifacts (shipwrecks with cargoes from many ports).*

**Brief 2 — Cities where corridors meet.** Constantinople, Alexandria,
Samarkand, and Timbuktu sat where routes joined. Across the medieval
centuries they stayed wealthy, crowded, and full of strangers — even as
the rulers above them rose and fell. *Source kinds: maps (city positions
on corridors); coins (many mints found in one market); chronicles (tax
records across dynasties).*

**Brief 3 — What travelers wrote home.** A 9th-century merchant and a
14th-century traveler both wrote the same kind of note: what the market
sold, what the road cost, what surprised them. Five hundred years apart,
the *form* of the traveler's account barely moved. *Source kinds:
travelers' accounts (two eras); secondary interpretations comparing them.*

## Guided practice (with hints)

*Do these with the adult. Hints are in italics.*

1. Classify Brief 1 (monsoon calendar): change, continuity, or both? Write
   the before/after in two sentences, cite the two source kinds, and give
   your verdict with one reason.
   
   *Hint: ask what changed — the wind did not change. What did humans
   change? (Their knowledge and timing.) Is that change or continuity?*

2. Classify Brief 2 (corridor cities). 
   
   *Hint: two things are true at once — the cities' wealth persisted
   (continuity) while their rulers changed (change). Your verdict should
   name both and say which answers the question "what happened to these
   cities?"*

3. A classmate classifies Brief 3 as "change, because travelers in 1400
   knew more than travelers in 900." Identify the flaw.
   
   *Hint: the brief is about the FORM of the account, not the amount of
   knowledge. What stayed the same about how travelers wrote?*

## Independent practice

4. Classify all three briefs in full: development named precisely,
   before/after, two source kinds cited, verdict with reasoning (3–4
   sentences each).
5. Write a "both" paragraph: choose one brief and argue the *minority*
   side — if your verdict was continuity, write the best two-sentence case
   for change (or vice versa). Historians strengthen claims by steelmanning
   the other side.

## Application

6. **Your own development.** Name one development from this unit's corridor
   work (e.g., "the rise of a city where two corridors meet," "the spread
   of a trade good," "a change in how people traveled"). Run the three
   steps: precise name, before/after, two source kinds, verdict. Four to
   six sentences.

## Exit check

7. Explain why "both" is often the most honest verdict — and why a
   historian who answers "both" still has to do the same evidence work as
   someone who answers "change."

## Supports, alternate response modes, extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the before/after frame stays on the word
  wall; the adult reads the briefs aloud.
- **Alternate response:** classifications may be presented as a three-column
  chart (change / continuity / both) with evidence bullets; the exit check
  may be oral.
- **Extension:** take one development and argue it from the viewpoint of
  two different people (a merchant vs. a farmer) — would they agree on
  what changed? Two paragraphs. (Perspective analysis returns in U02.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners equate "change" with "good" and
"continuity" with "boring" — or classify by vibes without before/after.
Insist on the three steps every time; the verdict word is worthless
without them. Also watch for single-source verdicts: two source kinds is
the floor, not the ceiling. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L05.
