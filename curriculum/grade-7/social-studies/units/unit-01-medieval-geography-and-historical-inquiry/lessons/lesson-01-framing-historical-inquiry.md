# Lesson 01 — Framing Historical Inquiry

**Unit:** [U01 — Medieval Geography: Sources and Historical Inquiry](../README.md) · **Week 3**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 1 of 16

## Goal

You will write a **compelling question** about the medieval world, break it
into **supporting questions** that history can actually answer, and match
each supporting question to the **source kinds** that could answer it.

## Materials

- Inquiry notebook and pencil
- This lesson's question cards (printed or copied by the adult from Guided
  practice below)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer aloud or in the notebook:

1. What years does "the medieval world" cover in this course? (About 500 to
   1700 CE — twelve centuries, most of Afro-Eurasia.)
2. Name one thing you already know about that time and place.
3. A historian wants to know what Timbuktu was like in 1400. Why can't she
   just go and look?

If question 3 is shaky, say it plainly now: *the past is gone; historians
work from what it left behind.* That is why the questions we ask matter so
much — a good question points to the right leftovers.

## Teaching

### Part A — Compelling vs. supporting questions

Historians start with a **compelling question**: a big, open question with
no single answer, worth arguing about. Then they break it into **supporting
questions**: smaller questions with findable answers, each one a stepping
stone toward the big one.

**Worked example 1 — building the ladder.**
Compelling question: *"Why did some medieval cities grow rich while others
faded?"*

Supporting questions that climb toward it:

1. *Which goods passed through Samarkand around 1300?* — answerable from
   travelers' accounts and market records.
2. *Where did the Silk Road run, and which cities sat on it?* — answerable
   from maps, old and new.
3. *What did a rich city spend its wealth on?* — answerable from buildings,
   coins, and chronicles that still exist.

Notice the pattern: each supporting question is **narrower** than the big
one, and each one **points at a source kind** — the type of leftover that
could answer it. A supporting question that points at no source is a dead
end: *"Were the merchants happy?"* sounds interesting, but no medieval
source recorded merchants' feelings in a way we can check. A historian
rewrites it: *"What did merchants buy, sell, and build?"* — that one leaves
traces.

**Worked example 2 — sorting question cards.**
Read each question. Decide: **compelling** (big, arguable), **supporting**
(small, answerable, points at a source), or **neither** (can't be answered
from what the past left behind).

1. *"Was the medieval world fair?"* → **Neither.** "Fair" is a judgment
   word; no source can settle it, and it smuggles in our own standards.
   Rewrite: *"Who could own land in medieval France, and who could not?"* —
   now it is supporting (answerable from law codes and land records).
2. *"How did people cross the Sahara before engines?"* → **Supporting.**
   Answerable: camel-saddle artifacts, caravan accounts, and the wells and
   way-stations still on the ground.
3. *"Why do we remember some medieval rulers and forget others?"* →
   **Compelling.** Arguable, no single answer — and it opens supporting
   questions about chronicles (who wrote them, and for whom?).

The test for "supporting" is always the same two checks: *Can it be
answered?* and *From what leftover?*

### Part B — Source kinds at a glance

You will meet five source kinds all year. Today, just learn their names —
Lesson 02 teaches what each one can and cannot tell us.

- **Maps** — old and new; show where things were.
- **Travelers' accounts** — what visitors wrote down.
- **Artifacts** — objects people made or used: pots, tools, buildings.
- **Coins** — small, datable, stamped with rulers' names; they traveled far.
- **Chronicles** — written records of events in time order.

Every supporting question you write this year should name at least one of
these.

## Guided practice (with hints)

*Do these with the adult. Hints are in italics.*

1. Sort these into compelling / supporting / neither, and say why:
   a. *"What did children play with in medieval Cairo?"*
   b. *"Which medieval invention mattered most?"*
   c. *"Did the Black Death change how much workers were paid?"*
   d. *"Were medieval people smarter than us?"*
   
   *Hint: ask the two checks — "Can it be answered?" and "From what
   leftover?" Judgment words like "mattered most" or "smarter" usually
   signal compelling or neither, never supporting.*

2. Take 1c above. Write two supporting questions under it and name a source
   kind for each.
   
   *Hint: wages leave traces — manor account rolls (documents), and
   chronicles describing shortages of workers.*

3. Repair this dead-end question: *"Did medieval sailors enjoy the sea?"*
   
   *Hint: feelings leave no checkable trace. What about a sailor's life
   DOES leave traces? (routes, ships, cargo, wrecks.)*

## Independent practice

4. Write one compelling question about the medieval world that genuinely
   interests you.
5. Write three supporting questions under it. For each, name the source
   kind(s) that could answer it and one sentence saying why that kind fits.
6. Trade with the adult: the adult tries to break one of your supporting
   questions by asking "but what leftover would prove the answer?" Fix any
   question that breaks.

## Application

7. **Question audit.** Look back at your compelling question from task 4.
   Write two sentences: (a) what makes it compelling rather than just big,
   and (b) which of your three supporting questions is the strongest
   stepping stone, and why.

## Exit check

8. In your own words: what is the difference between a compelling and a
   supporting question, and what two checks does a supporting question have
   to pass?

## Supports, alternate response modes, extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the worked examples aloud;
   key terms stay on a word wall all unit.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may dictate questions while the adult
  scribes; sorting may be done by pointing to three labeled boxes.
- **Extension:** write a *counter-question* — a question whose answer would
  weaken your compelling question's most likely answer — and explain what
  source kind could answer it. (Counter-questions return in U08.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners treat "compelling" as "any big
question," including unanswerable ones (*"Was it fair?"*). Keep pressing the
two checks; a compelling question must still be *approachable* through
supporting questions, not merely grand. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L01.
