# Lesson 03 — Reading and Building Maps

**Unit:** [U01 — Medieval Geography: Sources and Historical Inquiry](../README.md) · **Week 4**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 5 of 16

## Goal

You will read a map's **scale bar**, **grid**, **key**, **compass rose**,
and **symbols** to make evidence claims; locate the four medieval **trade
corridors** on a schematic; and explain what a map **shows and cannot
show** — including what every flat world map must distort.

## Materials

- Inquiry notebook, pencil, ruler
- The practice map
  ([oasis-town-practice-map.svg](../assets/oasis-town-practice-map.svg) —
  a deterministic diagram; every measurement in this lesson is checked
  against it)
- The corridor schematic
  ([trade-corridors-schematic.svg](../assets/trade-corridors-schematic.svg)
  — schematic: connections shown, distances NOT to scale)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer aloud, pointing at the practice map:

1. Read the grid label of the inn (caravanserai).
2. What does the dashed red line mean, according to the key?
3. Which way is north? How do you know?

## Teaching

### Part A — The six tools on every good map

A map is **evidence**: it can prove a claim about a place — but only when
you read it through its tools.

1. **Title** — what the map is about.
2. **Key (legend)** — what each symbol and color means.
3. **Compass rose** — which way is north (and therefore everything else).
4. **Scale bar** — what a length on the map equals in real distance. Here
   the bar runs 0 to 5 km, and each grid square equals 1 km.
5. **Grid** — lettered columns (A–E), numbered rows (1–4). Read column
   first, then row: the well is at **C1**.
6. **Symbols** — dots, squares, circles, triangles, and lines standing for
   real things.

**Worked example 1 — measure with the scale bar.**
Question: *"How far is it from the market to the well along the road?"*
*Thinking:* Find the market (black square, **A1**) and the well (open
circle, **C1**). The red dashed road runs straight from one to the other:
from column A to column C is 2 grid squares, and each square is 1 km, so
the road distance is **2 km**. Check with the scale bar: the segment from
A1 to C1 matches two 1-km segments of the bar. Claim: *the market is 2 km
west of the well by road.*

**Worked example 2 — road distance vs. straight-line distance.**
Question: *"How far is the market from the inn?"*
*Thinking:* The inn (black triangle) is at **C3**. Along the dashed road:
market (A1) → well (C1) is 2 km, well (C1) → inn (C3) is 2 km, so the road
trip is **4 km**. But the straight line from A1 to C3 crosses 2 squares
east and 2 squares south — about **2.8 km** as the crow flies (a diagonal
of a 2 km by 2 km square). Claim: *the road is longer than the straight
line — about 4 km by road versus about 2.8 km direct.* Maps show both
truths at once, and a traveler cares about the road, not the crow.

### Part B — The four corridors (schematic)

Open the corridor schematic. It is a **schematic diagram**: it shows which
cities connected to which, the way a subway map shows stations — distances,
directions, and coastlines are deliberately NOT to scale. Four corridors
carried goods, people, and ideas across Afro-Eurasia:

- **Silk Road (overland):** Chang'an → Dunhuang → Samarkand → Baghdad →
  Constantinople. Caravans of camels and horses; silk, paper, and ceramics
  went west, glass and gold went east.
- **Mediterranean (sea):** Venice → Constantinople → Alexandria. Galleys
  and cogs; grain, timber, and spices changed hands in port cities.
- **Indian Ocean (monsoon sea routes):** Aden → Hormuz → Calicut →
  Malacca. Seasonal winds reversed direction, so sailors timed voyages to
  the monsoon; spices, textiles, and porcelain moved by ship.
- **Trans-Saharan (desert caravan):** Sijilmasa → Taghaza → Timbuktu →
  Gao. Camel caravans carried salt south and gold north across the desert.

**Worked example 3 — reading the schematic as evidence.**
Question: *"Did Samarkand connect to the Mediterranean world?"*
*Thinking:* Trace the Silk Road band: Samarkand → Baghdad →
Constantinople. Constantinople also sits on the Mediterranean band. So the
schematic supports the claim that goods could travel from Samarkand to the
Mediterranean through connected routes. But the schematic **cannot** prove
how long the trip took, what it cost, or that any single merchant made the
whole journey — it shows connections, not experiences. That is a
**limitation** to state, not a flaw to hide.

### Part C — What every flat map must distort

The Earth is round; paper is flat. Flattening a sphere always distorts
something — shape, size, distance, or direction. That choice is called a
**projection**, and every world map picks its poison.

*Try it:* press an orange peel flat (adult supervises). It tears or
stretches — the sphere refuses to lie flat without damage. Medieval
mapmakers faced the same geometry: al-Idrisi's world map (1154, made for
Roger II of Sicily) put south at the top; the Catalan Atlas (1375, drawn
on Majorca) stretched Asia to fit a Christian worldview. Neither was
"wrong" — each served its maker's purpose. A historian always asks: *what
did this mapmaker choose to distort, and why?*

## Guided practice (with hints)

*Do these with the adult. Hints are in italics.*

1. On the practice map: (a) read the grid label of the east gate; (b)
   measure the road distance from the east gate to the well; (c) state one
   claim the map proves and one thing it cannot show.
   
   *Hint: the east gate is the diamond at E1. There is no road drawn to
   it — so (b) has no road answer; use the scale bar on the straight line
   instead, and say so. That honesty is the skill.*

2. On the schematic: name one city where two corridors meet, and explain
   in two sentences why a meeting city would grow rich.
   
   *Hint: Constantinople sits on two bands. Think about who passes through
   and what they pay for.*

3. A friend says the schematic proves Samarkand is exactly halfway between
   Chang'an and Constantinople. Explain the error.
   
   *Hint: re-read the schematic's subtitle.*

## Independent practice

4. Make three evidence claims from the practice map (each proved by grid,
   scale bar, key, or compass rose) and two "cannot show" statements.
5. On the schematic, trace the longest single-corridor journey (most
   cities). List the cities in order and write one sentence about what
   kind of evidence — not the schematic — could prove someone really made
   that trip. (A traveler's account, for example.)

## Application

6. **Mapmaker's choice.** You must draw a flat map of the whole medieval
   world for merchants. In four sentences, explain: what you would distort
   (choose one: shape, size, distance, or direction), why that choice helps
   merchants, and what your map therefore cannot be trusted for.

## Exit check

7. Explain the difference between the practice map and the corridor
   schematic: what is each one FOR, and what would go wrong if you used the
   schematic to measure a distance?

## Supports, alternate response modes, extensions

- **Reading/language access:** corridor names stay on the word wall with
  the goods each carried; the adult reads the schematic labels aloud.
- **Alternate response:** grid readings may be done by pointing; the exit
  check may be answered orally with the two diagrams in view.
- **Extension:** research how one real medieval map (adult previews; start
  with the British Museum collection or the resource pack) oriented its
  world, and write three sentences on what its orientation reveals about
  its maker.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners will measure distances on the schematic
despite the "NOT to scale" label — the subway-map analogy helps, but check
task 3 and the exit check carefully. Also watch for "the map proves it" as
a blanket claim; keep demanding *which tool* proves *which claim*.
