# Lesson 04 — Evaluating Sources

**Unit:** [U01 — Medieval Geography: Sources and Historical Inquiry](../README.md) · **Week 5**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 9 of 16

## Goal

You will **evaluate** a source's credibility using five tests — **origin,
authority, structure, context, corroborative value** — and **infer** a
plausible maker, date, place, or audience from clues inside the source.

## Materials

- Inquiry notebook and pencil
- The two model texts below (both are **original classroom model texts
  written for this lesson** — practice material, not quotations from real
  historical works)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. What is the reconstruction test from Lesson 02? (When made, made from
   what, what it can/can't do.)
2. A coin proves a ruler existed. What can it NOT prove?
3. Why do historians want a *second, independent* source before trusting a
   claim?

## Teaching

### Part A — The five evaluation tests

Classifying a source (Lesson 02) tells you what it is. Evaluating it tells
you how far to trust it. Run five tests:

1. **Origin** — who made it, when, where? A source close to the event in
   time and place starts stronger.
2. **Authority** — why would the maker know? An eyewitness merchant knows
   markets; he does not know palace politics.
3. **Structure** — how is it organized, and what does the shape reveal? A
   list of tolls paid is shaped by tax collection; a praise poem is shaped
   by flattery.
4. **Context** — what was happening around it? A chronicle written during a
   war reads its enemies unkindly.
5. **Corroborative value** — does an *independent* source agree? Two
   travelers who never met, describing the same market the same way, are
   worth more than one.

**Worked example 1 — running the five tests.**
Read Model Text A (below): a merchant's note from the road to Samarkand.

- **Origin:** a merchant, writing on the road, apparently soon after the
  events (present-tense details, named places).
- **Authority:** high on trade goods and road conditions — he handled them;
  low on anything inland he never visited.
- **Structure:** a practical note, probably to himself or a partner —
  lists goods and costs, no flattery, no storytelling. Practical writing
  has less motive to exaggerate.
- **Context:** written during travel; he wants the information to be
  *useful*, which rewards accuracy.
- **Corroborative value:** unknown from this text alone — we would check
  it against a second account or market records before trusting the
  prices.

Verdict: *trustworthy on what goods moved and what the road was like;
check the prices elsewhere; do not cite it for anything beyond its road.*

**Worked example 2 — inferring the unstated.**
Read Model Text B (below): from the Chronicle of Qasr Oasis, year 1187.
The text never says who wrote it — but clues do.

- It records the well's repair and the caravan's gifts *before* any other
  news: the writer cares about the town's water and its visitors.
- It calls the caravan master "the generous" — a writer flattering a
  patron, or hoping for the next gift.
- It is dated by year and written in tidy sequence: a chronicle keeper,
  likely attached to the town's ruler or its mosque.

Inference: *probably written by a town record-keeper close to the ruler,
in 1187, for the town's own memory — which means it is strong on local
events and soft on anything that would embarrass the ruler.* Every
inference above is tied to a clue; an inference without a cited clue is
just a guess.

### The model texts

**Model Text A — A merchant's note from the road to Samarkand.**
*Classroom model text — written for this lesson; not a quotation from any
real historical work.*

> Samarkand road, late summer. Bought paper in Dunhuang, twelve bundles,
> cheaper than in Chang'an by a third. The wells at the third station are
> dry; water costs double. Met a glass-seller from Baghdad going east. He
> says the pass beyond Samarkand is open but the toll has risen.

**Model Text B — From the Chronicle of Qasr Oasis, in the year 1187.**
*Classroom model text — written for this lesson; not a quotation from any
real historical work.*

> In the year 1187 the old well was repaired with new stone, and the
> generous caravan master of the Samarkand road gave oil and cloth for the
> work. In the same year the northern raiders did not come, and the market
> was full every seventh day.

## Guided practice (with hints)

*Do these with the adult. Hints are in italics.*

1. Run the five tests on Model Text B. For each test, write one sentence.
   End with a two-sentence verdict: what is it trustworthy for, and what
   must be checked elsewhere?
   
   *Hint: for Authority, ask what a town record-keeper would and would not
   know. For Context, notice what is missing — the raiders "did not come"
   gets one line; a threatened town might say more.*

2. From Model Text A, infer: (a) the season of writing, (b) whether the
   writer had been to Chang'an, (c) one thing the writer wants the reader
   to DO. Cite the clue for each.
   
   *Hint: "late summer" is stated — but (b) and (c) must be inferred. "Cheaper
   than in Chang'an by a third" is a comparison the writer could only make
   from knowledge of Chang'an prices.*

3. A classmate says Model Text A is useless because "we don't know the
   merchant's name." Explain why anonymous does not mean useless — in two
   sentences using the five tests.
   
   *Hint: which tests can you still run without a name? (Structure,
   context, and corroborative value do not need a name.)*

## Independent practice

4. Choose one model text. Write a "source passport": kind, origin
   (maker/date/place as best you can infer), authority rating (high /
   medium / low) with reason, one strength, one limitation, and what second
   source would corroborate its main claim.
5. Write three inferences about the *audience* of Model Text B (who was it
   written for?) with a clue cited for each. Then write one sentence on how
   that audience might have shaped what got recorded.

## Application

6. **Corroboration hunt.** Model Text A says the toll beyond Samarkand
   rose. List two *different, independent* source kinds that could confirm
   or challenge that claim, and for each explain exactly what you would
   look for. (Example: "a second traveler's account — the same toll named
   at the same pass.")

## Exit check

7. In your own words: what is the difference between a source's
   **limitation** (Lesson 02) and a failed **evaluation test** (this
   lesson)? Give one example of each using the model texts.

## Supports, alternate response modes, extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads both model texts aloud
  twice; the five test names stay on the word wall with one-line prompts.
- **Alternate response:** the five tests may be answered as a labeled
  table; the exit check may be answered orally.
- **Extension:** take one real medieval source description from the
  resource pack (adult selects) and run the five tests on the *description*
  — noting honestly which tests cannot be run without seeing the source
  itself.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners treat evaluation as "finding the
source's opinion and disagreeing." Redirect: the tests measure *fitness for
a question*, not agreeableness — a biased chronicle can still be excellent
evidence for what its writer wanted remembered. Also watch for
over-inference: every inference needs its cited clue, or it is a guess
wearing a uniform. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) §L04.
