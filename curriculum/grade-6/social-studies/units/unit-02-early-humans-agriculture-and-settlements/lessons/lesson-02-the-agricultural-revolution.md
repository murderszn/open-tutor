# Lesson 02 — The Agricultural Revolution: Causes and Trade-offs

**Unit:** [U02 — Early Humans: Agriculture and Settlements](../README.md) · **Week 8**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 5 of 16

## Goal

You will explain what caused the agricultural revolution and weigh its
trade-offs with a benefit/cost chart — then decide whether farming was a
good trade, using evidence instead of guesses.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- The four **scenario cards** below (the adult reads them aloud; the learner
  keeps them for the S7 debate)

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult reads two statements. You answer: **"cause of farming,"**
**"effect of farming,"** or **"neither."**

1. "The climate grew warmer and wetter after the last ice age, so wild
   grasses grew thicker." → *cause of farming* (it made planting possible)
2. "Villages stored grain in bins for the winter." → *effect of farming*
   (storage exists because there is a harvest)
3. "People invented the wheel." → *neither* — the wheel came thousands of
   years later; not every old invention belongs to this story.

## Teaching

### Part A — What caused farming to begin?

About 10,000 years ago, in several places around the world, people began to
**domesticate** plants and animals: saving the biggest seeds to plant,
penning the calmest young goats, breeding the animals that gave the most
milk. Archaeologists call this the **Neolithic Revolution** — "Neolithic"
means "new stone age," and "revolution" means it changed everything. It was
not one invention by one genius. It was a slow shift with several causes
working together:

1. **A warmer, steadier climate.** As the last ice age ended, longer warm
   seasons let wild grasses — wheat and barley in Southwest Asia — grow
   thick and reliable. Farming is a bet on next season; a steadier climate
   made the bet safer.
2. **Seed selection.** Foragers gathering wild wheat naturally dropped some
   grains near camp — and noticed the biggest seeds made the strongest
   plants. Saving and planting the best seeds, year after year, slowly bred
   bigger harvests. Nobody planned "agriculture"; the harvest improved one
   choice at a time.
3. **Domestication of animals.** Goats, sheep, cattle, and pigs were tamed
   and bred for meat, milk, and hides. A herd is a walking food store — one
   that reproduces itself.
4. **Population pressure (a debated cause).** Some archaeologists argue that
   growing groups needed more food than foraging could provide, so people
   farmed out of necessity. Others argue farming came first and populations
   grew after. The adult notes: when experts disagree, the honest answer is
   "we don't fully know yet."

**Worked example 1 — modeled benefit/cost chart.**

*Situation:* A forager band camps where wild barley ripens every autumn.
Should they plant some grains near camp on purpose?

*Thinking (benefits first):* A planted patch could give **more** grain than
gathering scattered wild stands (benefit 1: bigger, more reliable harvest).
Extra grain can be stored for winter (benefit 2: food in the lean season).
The band would not need to walk as far for food (benefit 3: less travel).

*Now the costs:* Someone must clear ground, plant, weed, and guard the crop
for months (cost 1: heavy, boring labor). If rains fail, the whole crop dies
and the work is wasted (cost 2: risk). Everyone eating mostly barley gets a
narrower diet than a forager eating dozens of wild foods (cost 3: less
varied food). Living close together around fields, people and animals share
germs more easily (cost 4: new diseases).

| Benefits (what we gain) | Costs (what we give up or risk) |
|---|---|
| Bigger, more reliable harvest | Months of hard labor |
| Stored food for winter | Crop can fail — work wasted |
| Less travel for food | Narrower diet |
| | New diseases from crowding |

*Verdict with reasoning:* The benefits are real, but so are the costs — and
notice the costs fall on **daily life** (work, health, food variety) while
the benefits are about **security** (reliability, storage). That split is
the whole debate of this unit: farming traded an easier daily life for a
more secure food supply. A good answer picks a side **and** names what was
sacrificed — anyone who lists only benefits has not finished thinking.

### Part B — Change and continuity: what farming changed, what stayed

Historians sort developments into **change** (what became different) and
**continuity** (what stayed the same). Farming changed a lot — but not
everything.

**Worked example 2 — sorting change vs. continuity.**

*Thinking:* Take six facts about the shift from foraging to farming. Sort
each:

1. "People ate a narrower diet — mostly wheat and barley." → **Change**
   (foragers ate dozens of wild foods).
2. "People still needed water every day." → **Continuity** (biology did not
   change).
3. "Groups stayed in one place year-round." → **Change** (mobility dropped).
4. "People still told stories and made tools." → **Continuity** (culture
   continued).
5. "Some people stopped farming and made pots or wove cloth full-time." →
   **Change** (new kinds of work — you will trace this in Lesson 03).
6. "Children still learned by watching adults." → **Continuity** (learning
   did not change).

*The pattern:* ask "was this true before farming too?" If yes, it is
continuity; if farming made it different, it is change. Most big events are
a mix — the skill is seeing **both**.

### The scenario cards (for S5 and the S7 debate)

*Fictional bands, realistic choices. Each card names the situation; the
learner builds the benefit/cost chart.*

**Card 1 — The Riverbend Band (Fertile Crescent, about 9,000 BCE).** Your
band camps where wild wheat and barley ripen thick every autumn, and wild
goats water at the river. A few elders remember a year when someone dropped
grains by the huts and a fat patch of wheat grew on its own. Do you clear
ground and plant on purpose this spring?

**Card 2 — The Dry Hills Band.** Your hills get little rain, but a spring
feeds a small valley where wild grasses grow. Your neighbors in the wet
lowlands have started planting and now stay in one village. Your hunters say
the goat herds are thinner than when your grandparents were young. Do you
start planting in the little valley, or keep following the herds?

**Card 3 — The Forest Edge Band.** Your forest gives mushrooms, nuts, deer,
and fish — more kinds of food than anyone can count. Travelers tell of
farming villages where people work from sunrise to sunset in the fields and
sometimes go hungry when the rains fail, but where the village holds a
hundred people. Do you keep the forest life or try the village life?

**Card 4 — The New Mothers' Question.** Two mothers in your band argue. One
says: "In the farming village downriver, children survive the winter because
grain is stored — I want that safety for my children." The other says: "In
our camp, children eat many foods and play while adults gather — in the
village, children work the fields. I want that childhood for my children."
Which mother convinces you? What evidence would change your mind?

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Build a benefit/cost chart for **Card 1** (the Riverbend Band). Name at
   least three benefits and three costs.
   - *Hint: steal the structure of Worked example 1, but change the details
     — Riverbend has goats and a remembered accident with dropped grains.*
2. Sort each as **change** or **continuity**, and defend your sort in one
   sentence:
   - *Hint: ask "was this true before farming too?"*
   a. "Farmers built houses of mud brick instead of branch huts."
   b. "People cared for their children."
   c. "Food could be stored for months."
   d. "People got sick."
3. Error analysis: a learner writes, "Farming was invented in 9000 BCE by a
   genius who got tired of hunting." Name two things wrong with this
   sentence and fix it.
   - *Hint: one mistake is the single inventor; the other is the date
     written as a fact.*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. In your own words, what does **domesticate** mean? Give one plant
   example and one animal example.
2. Name two causes of the agricultural revolution and explain each in one
   sentence.
3. Why did a warmer, steadier climate make farming more likely? (Two
   sentences.)
4. Build a benefit/cost chart for **Card 3** (the Forest Edge Band): at
   least two benefits and two costs of trying village life.
5. Sort as change or continuity, with a one-sentence defense each:
   a. "People began living close together in larger groups."
   b. "People used fire to cook food."
   c. "A few people became very rich while others had little."
6. A learner says, "Farming was obviously a great idea — that's why
   everyone did it." Write three sentences explaining what this argument
   misses, using the words **labor**, **diet**, and **disease**.
7. Which scenario card's choice is hardest for you, and why? (Two or three
   sentences. There is no wrong answer — only undefended ones.)
8. Write one question about the agricultural revolution that historians
   still debate. (Example starter: "Did people farm because they had to, or
   because...?")

## Application — the inquiry notebook

Under your essential-question page, add the heading "The farming trade."
Copy your best benefit/cost chart (from guided or independent practice) and
write one sentence underneath: *"Right now, I think farming was
________ because ________."* You may change this sentence later — that is
what evidence is for.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. Name one **cause** of the agricultural revolution and one **trade-off**
   it created.
2. Why is it wrong to say farming was "obviously a great idea"?

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** benefit/cost charts are drawn as a two-column
  T-chart with +/- headers; scenario cards read aloud; key terms defined in
  the margin of the notebook.
- **Alternate response mode:** benefits and costs may be sorted as printed
  cards into two piles before writing; the chart may be dictated; the
  change/continuity sort may use color tokens (green = change, yellow =
  continuity).
- **Extension:** read the adult-selected excerpt on the scholarly debate
  (was farming "the worst mistake in the history of the human race"? — the
  adult frames this as one provocative argument, not a verdict). Write a
  paragraph: which side has the stronger evidence, and what evidence would
  settle it for you?

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "A genius invented farming in a year" — press
the multi-cause, centuries-long reality; dates are "about." (2) Benefits
only — learners list gains and skip costs; require the chart's cost column
before any verdict. (3) "Everyone did it, so it was great" — survivorship
thinking; the debate (S7) exists to break it. (4) Card 4 upsets some
learners — both mothers love their children; the point is that good people
can weigh the same evidence differently. **Sensitive framing:** disease and
harder labor are discussed plainly, never graphically; skeletal evidence is
mentioned only as "bones show..." with no detail. **Answer key:**
