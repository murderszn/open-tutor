# Lesson 04 — Comparing Early Farming Regions

**Unit:** [U02 — Early Humans: Agriculture and Settlements](../README.md) · **Week 10**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 13 of 16

## Goal

You will compare early farming in two regions — the Fertile Crescent and
Mesoamerica — explain the multiple causes of the agricultural change, and
infer how a forager and a farmer would see that change differently.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- [agriculture-timeline.svg](../assets/agriculture-timeline.svg) — the
  deterministic timeline (positions computed from the stated approximate
  dates)

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult reads three pairs. You answer: **"same,"** **"different,"** or
**"can't tell."**

1. "Fertile Crescent farmers grew wheat; Mesoamerican farmers grew maize."
   → *different* (different starter crops)
2. "Both regions' farmers stored food for the lean season." → *same*
   (storage solved the same problem)
3. "Fertile Crescent farmers were happier." → *can't tell* — no dig
   measures happiness; this is the kind of claim evidence cannot support.

## Teaching

### Part A — Two regions, one revolution, twice

Farming was not invented once and copied everywhere. It began
**independently** in several regions, each working with local wild plants
and animals. This lesson compares two of them — the pair your track's audit
selected:

**The Fertile Crescent** (Southwest Asia — parts of present-day Turkey,
Syria, Iraq, Israel, Jordan, Lebanon).

- **Crops:** emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, peas, chickpeas, flax.
- **Animals:** goats, sheep, cattle, pigs — domesticated for meat, milk,
  and hides.
- **Environment:** river plains and rainy hills; wild wheat and barley grew
  thick after the ice age.
- **Timing:** cultivation begins about 9,600 BCE; goats and sheep
  domesticated about 8,500 BCE; towns like Jericho (about 8,000 BCE) and
  Çatalhöyük (about 7,100 BCE) follow.

**Mesoamerica** (Mexico and Central America — the comparison centers on the
Central Balsas River Valley in present-day Mexico).

- **Crops:** **maize** (bred over thousands of years from a wild grass
  called **teosinte**), plus beans and squash — the "three sisters,"
  planted together so each helps the others.
- **Animals:** far fewer large animals were domesticated — dogs, turkeys,
  and Muscovy ducks, but no cattle, sheep, or goats, and no animals to pull
  plows. Farming here was human-muscle work.
- **Environment:** tropical lowlands and highland valleys; seasonal rain
  rather than great river floods.
- **Timing:** maize domesticated from teosinte by about 8,700 years ago
  (about 6700 BCE) — researchers found the evidence in the Balsas River
  Valley, where wild teosinte still grows.

A note on the present tense: Maya peoples and other Indigenous communities
of Mexico and Central America are **living peoples**, and maize is still at
the center of their food cultures today. This is not a story about vanished
strangers — it is the deep history of living neighbors. (You will meet
Mesoamerican civilizations themselves in U07.)

**Worked example 1 — modeled comparison.**

*Thinking:* Build the comparison as a two-column chart, then read the
pattern:

| | Fertile Crescent | Mesoamerica |
|---|---|---|
| Starter crops | wheat, barley, lentils | maize, beans, squash |
| Starter animals | goats, sheep, cattle, pigs | dogs, turkeys, ducks (no draft animals) |
| Environment | river plains, rainy hills | tropical lowlands, highland valleys |
| Farming begins | about 9,600 BCE (cultivation) | about 8,700 years ago (maize) |

*Similarities:* both regions domesticated local plants, stored surpluses,
settled down, and developed specialists and towns. *Differences:* different
crops, different animals (draft animals vs. none), different environments,
different timing. *The big inference:* because the details differ so much,
farming was almost certainly **invented independently** in each place — not
copied. Same human problem (how to secure food), different local answers.
That is D2.His.1.6-8: connections among developments in broader contexts.

### Part B — Multiple causes: why farming, why there, why then?

Big changes never have one cause. Use the timeline to stack them:

**Worked example 2 — modeled multi-cause explanation.**

*Question:* Why did farming begin in the Fertile Crescent when it did?

*Thinking:* List every cause from Lessons 01–02, then sort by kind:

1. **Environmental cause:** the climate warmed after the ice age; wild
   wheat and barley grew thick and reliable (about 12,500–9,600 BCE).
2. **Biological cause:** the region happened to hold wild plants and
   animals that *could* be domesticated — big-seeded grasses, tamable
   goats and sheep. (Not every region was so lucky.)
3. **Human cause:** foragers already knew these plants intimately and
   already lived nearly settled (the Natufian culture); planting was a
   small step from tending wild stands.
4. **Debated cause:** population pressure may have forced the issue — or
   may have followed farming instead. Mark it "debated," not "proven."

*The explanation, assembled:* "Farming began in the Fertile Crescent about
10,000 years ago because a warming climate made wild grains reliable
(environmental), the region held domesticable plants and animals
(biological), local foragers already knew and tended those species (human),
and possibly because growing groups needed more food (debated). No single
cause was enough alone." That is D2.His.14.6-8: multiple causes and effects,
stated honestly.

### Part C — Two perspectives on the same change

A **perspective** is shaped by a person's situation (D2.His.4.6-8). The same
change looks different depending on where you stand:

**Worked example 3 — modeled perspectives.**

*The change:* a band settles by the river and plants wheat.

- **The farmer's view:** "Our grain bins are full. My children will eat
  this winter no matter what the herds do. Yes, my back aches from the
  harvest — but last winter nobody went hungry. The field is worth it."
  *Evidence used:* stored food, surviving winter, the memory of a hungry
  year.
- **The forager's view (a cousin who kept moving):** "You work from sunrise
  to dark and eat the same grain every meal. We ate forty foods this month
  and walked where we pleased. Your bins are full, but are you free?"
  *Evidence used:* varied diet, mobility, the ache of field labor.

*What the examples show:* both perspectives use **real evidence** — neither
is foolish. The farmer weighs security; the forager weighs freedom and
variety. Disagreeing about the farming trade does not mean one side is
wrong; it means they value different things. The adult guides this
discussion: keep both views respectful, and keep the evidence attached to
each claim.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Using the [agriculture-timeline.svg](../assets/agriculture-timeline.svg),
   answer: (a) How many years passed between the start of the Natufian
   culture (about 12,500 BCE) and the founding of Çatalhöyük (about
   7,100 BCE)? Show your subtraction. (b) Which came first — goat/sheep
   domestication or the tower of Jericho? By about how many years?
   - *Hint: BCE years count backward — 12,500 BCE is older (bigger number)
     than 7,100 BCE. Subtract smaller from larger.*
2. Build the two-column comparison chart for the two regions from memory,
   then check it against Part A. Fix any errors in a different color.
   - *Hint: the four rows are crops, animals, environment, timing.*
3. Error analysis: a learner writes, "Mesoamericans copied farming from the
   Fertile Crescent because farming was invented there first." Explain what
   is wrong, using two facts from Part A.
   - *Hint: check the crops and the timing — do they look copied?*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Name the two comparison regions and one starter crop from each.
2. What is **teosinte**, and why does it matter to this lesson?
3. List two similarities and two differences between farming in the two
   regions.
4. Write a multi-cause explanation (3–4 sentences) for why farming began in
   **Mesoamerica**, using at least three causes. Mark any debated cause as
   debated.
5. Using the timeline: about how many years passed between early
   cultivation in the Fertile Crescent (about 9,600 BCE) and maize
   domestication (about 8,700 years ago ≈ 6700 BCE)? Show your work. (Careful:
   convert "years ago" to BCE first: 8,700 years ago ≈ 6700 BCE.)
6. Write the **farmer's** perspective on settling down (3–4 sentences),
   using at least two pieces of evidence from the unit.
7. Write the **forager's** perspective on the same change (3–4 sentences),
   using at least two pieces of evidence from the unit.
8. A learner says, "The Fertile Crescent was better at farming because it
   started first." Write two sentences explaining why "better" is the wrong
   word here. (Hint: think about Readiness #3.)

## Application — the inquiry notebook

Return to your essential-question page and your sentence from Lesson 02:
*"Right now, I think farming was ________ because ________."* Below it,
write *"After comparing two regions, I now think ________"* — keep it,
change it, or complicate it, and give one new reason from this lesson.
Date the entry. Then add one new question the comparison raised for you.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. What is the strongest evidence that farming was invented independently
   in the two regions (not copied)?
2. Why can a farmer and a forager disagree about farming while both using
   real evidence?

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** the comparison chart is pre-drawn with row
  labels; the timeline is read left-to-right with the adult ("older this
  way"); key terms defined in the notebook margin.
- **Alternate response mode:** the chart may be built with picture cards
  (wheat stalk, maize ear, goat, river, sun); perspectives may be spoken as
  a dialogue with the adult playing one role; answers may be dictated.
- **Extension:** investigate a third independent center of agriculture
  (the adult selects the source — e.g., the Yellow River valley's millet
  farmers or New Guinea's highland gardeners). Add a third column to your
  chart and write a paragraph: does the new evidence strengthen or weaken
  the independent-invention inference? Why?

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Copied from the Fertile Crescent" — press
the crop/animal/timing differences; independent invention is the inference
to earn, not assume. (2) BCE subtraction errors — re-teach: bigger BCE
number = older; always subtract smaller from larger; "8,700 years ago"
must be converted before subtracting from a BCE date. (3) "Better/worse"
regions — redirect to evidence: different, not ranked. (4) Perspectives
drift into caricature ("farmers dumb, foragers smart") — keep evidence
attached to each view; the adult models respectful disagreement.
**Sensitive framing:** Indigenous peoples of the Americas in the present
tense, always; maize's living food culture named explicitly.
