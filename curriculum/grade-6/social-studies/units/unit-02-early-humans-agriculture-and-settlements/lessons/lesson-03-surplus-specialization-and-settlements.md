# Lesson 03 — Surplus, Specialization, and Settlements

**Unit:** [U02 — Early Humans: Agriculture and Settlements](../README.md) · **Week 9**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 9 of 16

## Goal

You will trace how a farming surplus led to specialists and growing
settlements, describe two innovations that raised farm productivity, and
find the surplus at work in a farming-village illustration.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- [early-farming-village.png](../assets/early-farming-village.png) — the
  unit's generated illustration (AI illustration of a fictional village —
  not evidence of any real place)
- [surplus-flow.svg](../assets/surplus-flow.svg) — the deterministic flow
  diagram (a teaching sketch of the idea, not data)

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult reads three statements. You answer: **"surplus,"** **"not a
surplus,"** or **"it depends."**

1. "A family grows enough wheat to eat all year, with ten extra baskets."
   → *surplus* (extra beyond need)
2. "A family grows exactly enough wheat to eat all year." → *not a
   surplus* (nothing extra)
3. "A family grows extra wheat, but it rots before winter." → *it depends*
   — extra grain only counts as a surplus if it can be **stored**; rotten
   grain feeds no one. Storage is half the story.

## Teaching

### Part A — The surplus chain: from extra grain to new jobs

A **surplus** is food beyond what the growers need. A forager band rarely
has one — wild food spoils or must be carried. Farming changed the math:
grain can be dried, stored in bins and baskets, and eaten months later.
That storable extra is the hinge of this whole lesson.

Follow the chain on [surplus-flow.svg](../assets/surplus-flow.svg):

1. **Reliable harvests** — planting, weeding, and guarding produce more
   food than gathering the same ground wild.
2. **Surplus stored** — grain bins, baskets, and granaries hold the extra
   through winter and bad years.
3. **Some people freed** — when ten farmers grow food for fifteen people,
   five people do not have to farm.
4. **Specialists** — those freed people become potters, weavers, builders,
   toolmakers, traders: a **specialization of labor**. They get very good
   at one thing because they do it all day.
5. **Towns grow** — specialists need customers; customers need food; more
   people means more jobs, more building, more trade.

**Worked example 1 — modeled production chain.**

*Thinking:* Trace one product from field to town, naming each worker:

- A farmer grows emmer wheat and harvests a surplus.
- The surplus grain is stored in the village granary (a storekeeper guards
  and measures it).
- A miller grinds grain into flour with heavy grinding stones (a
  specialist's tool, made by a stoneworker).
- A baker bakes the flour into bread in a clay oven (built by a builder,
  fired with wood cut by a woodcutter).
- A trader carries loaves to a neighboring village, returning with
  obsidian for new blades.

*What the chain shows:* one harvest feeds at least six kinds of workers,
and none of them farmed that day. The surplus did not just feed more
mouths — it **invented new jobs**. Every step also needs tools, which is
why toolmakers were among the first specialists.

### Part B — Innovation: better tools, water on demand

An **innovation** is a new tool or method that changes how work is done.
Two innovations multiplied what one farmer could grow:

- **Better tools.** Polished stone sickles with wooden handles cut grain
  faster than tearing it by hand; heavy grinding stones turned grain into
  flour; digging sticks loosened soil for planting. Each tool is small, but
  across a whole village and a whole season, small savings become a bigger
  harvest — more food per worker. (That "more per worker" idea is
  D2.Eco.13.6-8: as productivity improves, societies can support more
  people and more kinds of work.)
- **Irrigation.** Digging channels to carry river water to fields meant
  crops no longer depended only on rain. A village with irrigation could
  farm drier land, plant more ground, and survive a dry year. But channels
  must be dug, cleaned, and shared fairly — which meant villages needed
  **organization**: who digs, who gets water first, who fixes a break.
  Technology created a social job.

**Worked example 2 — modeled reasoning: the surplus test.**

*Thinking:* Look at [early-farming-village.png](../assets/early-farming-village.png).
Ask of each feature: "Could this exist if every person spent all day
finding their own food?" If no, it exists because of a surplus:

1. **The big grain-storage building with baskets of grain** — no. Storing
   months of food requires a harvest bigger than immediate need: the
   surplus itself, made visible.
2. **The pottery workshop with rows of pots and a kiln** — no. A potter
   shaping dozens of pots is not farming that day; someone else's surplus
   feeds them. Specialization, made visible.
3. **The walled garden plots in neat rows** — no. Walls and straight rows
   mean planned, controlled growing — agriculture, not gathering.
4. **The irrigation channel from the river** — no. Digging and maintaining
   a channel is organized group labor, only possible when food is secure.
5. **The fenced pen of goats and sheep** — no. Keeping animals alive
   year-round means feeding them from stored food in winter: domestication
   plus surplus.
6. **The large mud-brick houses** — no. Permanent, heavy houses mean people
   stay put year-round: sedentism, which the surplus makes possible.

*The pattern:* the question is always "who is freed, and what feeds them?"
Every feature that needs full-time workers points back to stored extra
food. (A text-only description of the same village follows the activity for
anyone not using the image.)

### Part C — A real place: Çatalhöyük

About 9,000 years ago — around 7100 BCE — people built a town in what is
now Turkey called **Çatalhöyük** (cha-tal-HOO-yuk). At its height several
thousand people lived there — bigger than any village before it. The houses
were mud brick, packed wall-to-wall with **no streets**: people walked
across the rooftops and climbed down ladders into their homes. Families
buried their dead under the house floors — archaeologists treat these
burials with respect, as the remains of real people, not as treasure. The
town had wall paintings, obsidian tools traded from far away, and — as far
as archaeologists can tell — no kings and no palaces. Ordinary houses, side
by side, for centuries.

*Why it matters here:* Çatalhöyük is what the surplus chain builds when it
runs for a thousand years: specialists (obsidian workers, painters),
trade, organization — and still no kings. Towns did not automatically
produce rulers. (The adult can open the Çatalhöyük Digital Kit from the
[resource pack](../resource-pack.md) for the virtual house tour.)

> **Text-only alternative to the village illustration.** The picture shows
> a fictional early farming village from a slight elevated angle: flat-roofed
> mud-brick houses; a large open-fronted grain-storage building holding
> baskets and bins of grain; walled garden plots with neat rows of green
> crops; a narrow irrigation channel running from a river to the fields; a
> pottery corner with a dome-shaped kiln and rows of unfired clay pots; a
> wooden fenced pen holding goats and sheep; simple wooden tools (digging
> sticks, hoes) leaning by a field; hills and trees beyond. Every question
> asked about the image can be answered from this description.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Using the surplus-flow diagram, explain in your own words what happens
   between "surplus stored" and "specialists." Why can't specialists exist
   without the step before?
   - *Hint: a potter still eats lunch. Where does it come from?*
2. Name the two innovations from Part B. For each, explain how it raised
   farm productivity (more food per worker) in one sentence.
   - *Hint: productivity = output divided by workers; what does each
     innovation change?*
3. Error analysis: a learner writes, "Çatalhöyük proves that as soon as
   people farmed, kings took over." Explain what is wrong, using two facts
   from Part C.
   - *Hint: what did archaeologists NOT find there?*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Define **surplus** in your own words, and explain why storage matters to
   the definition.
2. Copy the five-step surplus chain (harvests → stored → freed → specialists
   → towns) and write one sentence explaining each arrow — *why* does each
   step lead to the next?
3. Trace a production chain for a clay pot: name at least four workers from
   clay pit to finished pot, and say what each one does.
4. Explain how irrigation changed **both** farming and village organization.
   (Two or three sentences.)
5. Look at the village illustration (or its text alternative). List three
   features that exist only because of a surplus, and give the reason for
   each — use the "who is freed, and what feeds them?" test.
6. Why did specialists get **better** at their crafts than a forager who
   made their own pots? (Two sentences.)
7. A learner says, "Towns grew because people liked living close together."
   Write two sentences explaining the deeper cause, using the word
   **surplus**.
8. Çatalhöyük had no streets and no palaces. What does each of those two
   facts suggest about how the town was organized? (One sentence each; mark
   any answer that is a guess as a guess.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Surplus = extra" without storage — press
Readiness #3: unstored extra is waste, not surplus. (2) Learners think
specialists were "invented" by a decision — stress emergence: nobody voted;
the math of extra food made it possible. (3) "Towns grew because people
liked company" — redirect to the chain: jobs and food came first.
(4) Learners may read the illustration as a real place — repeat the caption:
AI illustration of a **fictional** village, not evidence. **Sensitive
framing:** burials are "the remains of real people, treated with respect";
no detail beyond "buried under house floors." **Answer key:**
