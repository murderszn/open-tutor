# Lesson 01 — Environments Shaped Early Human Life

**Unit:** [U02 — Early Humans: Agriculture and Settlements](../README.md) · **Week 7**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 1 of 16

## Goal

You will explain how environments — water, wild food, shelter, and climate —
shaped the lives of early human foragers, and decide where a forager group
would camp or move using environmental evidence.

## Materials

- Notebook (your **inquiry notebook**) and pencil
- The unit's essential question, copied at the top of a fresh page: *"Was
  learning to farm a good trade? How did growing food change what it meant
  to be human?"*

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult reads three descriptions. You answer: **"forager"** or **"farmer."**

1. "A group follows wild goats through the hills, gathering berries and
   nuts along the way. They carry everything they own." → *forager* (they
   move to find wild food)
2. "A family plants wheat seeds in a field by the river, then waits months
   for the harvest." → *farmer* (they grow food in one place)
3. "A group stays near a lake all year because fish, birds, and wild grains
   are always nearby." → *forager* — tricky! They barely move, but they
   still live on **wild** food, not planted food. Staying put does not make
   someone a farmer; *growing* food does.

If #3 felt tricky, that is the lesson's key idea: for thousands of years,
some foragers lived almost in one place. Farming was not the first time
humans settled down — it was the first time they **controlled** their food.

## Teaching

### Part A — Foraging: the original human way of life

For roughly 300,000 years — nearly all of human history — people lived by
**foraging**: hunting wild animals and gathering wild plants. There were no
grocery stores, no farms, no planted fields. Everything edible had to be
found, caught, or collected.

Foragers needed four things from their environment, every single day:

- **Water** — people, like all animals, must drink daily. Camps stayed near
  rivers, lakes, springs, or reliable rain.
- **Wild food** — game animals to hunt, fish to catch, fruits, nuts, roots,
  and wild grains to gather. Different seasons offered different foods.
- **Shelter** — caves, rock overhangs, or simple huts of branches and hides
  protected people from weather and predators.
- **Safety** — high ground to watch for danger, distance from floods, and
  enough people in the group to share the work and the risk.

Because wild food moves and seasons change, most forager groups had high
**mobility**: they packed up and followed the food. A group might spend
spring by the river (fish spawning), summer in the hills (berries ripening),
and winter in a sheltered valley. The environment was not background scenery
— it was the schedule, the menu, and the map all at once.

**Worked example 1 — modeled reasoning: would a forager group camp here?**

*The adult describes two fictional places; think along before reading the
verdict.*

- **Place A — Willow Bend:** a slow river with fish, flat banks with wild
  barley grasses, a limestone cliff with dry caves, and hills behind that
  block the cold north wind.
- **Place B — Dry Ridge:** a rocky hilltop far from any stream, thin grass,
  no trees, strong winds, but a fine view.

*Thinking:* Check the four needs. Willow Bend has water (river), wild food
(fish, wild barley), shelter (dry caves), and safety (hills block wind, and
the cliff gives high ground). Every need is met, so a forager group would
very likely camp here — perhaps for a whole season. Dry Ridge has only the
view: no water, little food, no shelter from wind. A forager group would not
camp there; they might cross it while traveling, but they would not stay.
*The reasoning pattern:* name each need, check it against the evidence, and
only then decide. Never decide from one feature alone — a pretty view is
not drinking water.

**Worked example 2 — matching environments to ways of life.**

*Thinking:* Three fictional forager groups each describe their home. Match
the group to how the environment shapes their year.

1. **The Lake People** live where a great lake never freezes and reeds hide
   nesting birds year-round. → They can stay nearly all year (low mobility):
   the environment provides steady food, so they do not need to chase it.
   They still forage — nothing is planted.
2. **The Hill Walkers** live where summers are rich with berries but winters
   bury everything in snow. → They must move (high mobility): summer camps
   in the berry hills, winter camps in a sheltered valley. The climate sets
   their calendar.
3. **The Coast Gatherers** live where tides expose shellfish twice a day but
   storms can flood the beach. → They time their days by the tide and keep
   camp above the flood line. The environment sets both the clock and the
   campsite.

*What the examples show:* the same human needs produce **different ways of
life** in different environments. Geography does not dictate everything —
people make choices — but it sets the options on the table. That is
D2.Geo.4.6-8: cultural patterns and daily life shaped by environments.

### Part B — Constraints: what the environment would not allow

A **constraint** is something the environment simply does not offer. A
forager group in a desert cannot decide to eat river fish; a group on a
treeless plain cannot build wooden houses. Constraints are not failures of
imagination — they are the edges of the possible.

This matters for the whole unit: farming did not appear everywhere at once,
because the constraints were different everywhere. Wild wheat and barley
grew in the Fertile Crescent, so farming started there with wheat and
barley. Wild teosinte grew in Mexico, so farming started there with maize.
Nobody chose their starter crops from a catalog — the local environment
dealt the hand, and people played it brilliantly.

*Common mix-up to avoid:* "early humans were primitive." Foragers knew their
environments deeply — which plants were safe, where animals watered, how
weather changed. Their knowledge was enormous; it was just stored in memory
and stories instead of books. Respect the expertise.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. For each fictional place, name which of the four needs (water, wild food,
   shelter, safety) are met and which are missing. Then decide: camp, pass
   through, or avoid?
   - *Hint: check all four needs before deciding; one missing need can be
     enough to move on.*
   a. **Reed Marsh:** shallow lake, ducks and reeds, no dry ground, mosquitoes
      thick in summer.
   b. **Pine Hollow:** a spring, pine forest with deer and nuts, a dry cave,
      a ridge above the flood line.
   c. **Salt Flat:** no water, no plants, no animals, flat and open for miles.
2. The Hill Walkers (from Worked example 2) find a valley with wild wheat
   grasses that ripen every autumn without fail. Explain in two sentences
   how this environmental fact could change their mobility.
   - *Hint: if food comes to you reliably, what happens to the need to move?*
3. Error analysis: a learner writes, "Foragers moved all the time because
   they were too primitive to build houses." Explain the two mistakes in
   this sentence and fix it.
   - *Hint: one mistake is about why they moved; the other is the word
     "primitive."*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. List the four daily needs of a forager group, in your own words.
2. A forager group camps where a river meets the sea. Name one advantage
   and one constraint of this spot.
3. Explain why most forager groups had high mobility. Use the word
   **seasons** in your answer.
4. The Lake People (Worked example 2) barely move. Does that make them
   farmers? Explain why or why not in one or two sentences.
5. A group lives at the edge of a grassland where wild goats graze each
   spring. Predict two ways this environment shapes their year.
6. Name one constraint a forager group would face in each place: (a) a
   desert with one small spring, (b) a dense forest with heavy rain.
7. A learner says, "Foragers just wandered randomly." Write two sentences
   explaining why this is wrong, using evidence from this lesson.
8. Draw and label a simple map of an ideal forager camp: show water, a wild
   food source, shelter, and one safety feature. Add a one-sentence caption
   explaining your camp's best feature. (Stick figures and simple shapes are
   perfect.)

## Application — the inquiry notebook

Open your inquiry notebook to the essential-question page. Under it, write
the heading "Forager life — what I know." Add three bullet points: one thing
that surprised you, one environmental constraint you had not considered, and
one question you still have about how foragers lived. The adult dates the
page. You will answer your own question (or revise it) as the unit goes on.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. In your own words, what is **foraging**, and how is it different from
   farming?
2. Name two environmental facts that could make a forager group move camp.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** the four needs are posted as icons + words
  (💧 water, 🌾 wild food, 🏕️ shelter, 🛡️ safety); the adult reads practice
  items aloud on request; home-language drafts welcome.
- **Alternate response mode:** camp decisions may be shown by placing tokens
  on a printed landscape (blue = water, green = food, brown = shelter);
  answers may be dictated for the adult to scribe; the camp map may be built
  with cut paper shapes instead of drawn.
- **Extension:** research one real forager society (the adult selects the
  source — e.g., the San peoples of southern Africa or Indigenous Australian
  groups, in present-tense, respectful sources). Write three sentences on
  how their environment shapes their year, and note that these are living
  peoples, not people of the past.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners equate "not moving" with "farming" —
press the definition: farming = *growing* food, not staying put. (2) "They
moved because they were primitive" — reframe mobility as expertise: moving
was the smart response to seasonal food. (3) Learners treat the four needs
as a checklist to memorize rather than a reasoning tool — in guided
practice, always ask "so would they stay?" to force the verdict.
