## Lesson 01 — Environments shaped early human life

**Readiness:** 1 → forager (moves to find wild food). 2 → farmer (grows
food in one place). 3 → forager — staying put does not make a farmer;
*planting* does.

**Guided practice.**
1. a. **Reed Marsh:** water ✓ (lake), wild food ✓ (ducks, reeds), shelter ✗
   (no dry ground), safety ~ (mosquitoes; no high ground named). **Decision:
   pass through** (or a short cool-season camp) — two needs met, but no dry
   shelter makes a long stay unwise; defend either with the four-need check.
   b. **Pine Hollow:** water ✓ (spring), wild food ✓ (deer, nuts), shelter ✓
   (dry cave), safety ✓ (ridge above flood line). **Decision: camp** — all
   four needs met. c. **Salt Flat:** water ✗, wild food ✗, shelter ✗,
   safety ~ (open, visible, but exposed). **Decision: avoid** — cross only if
   necessary; nothing here supports life.
2. Accept: "If wild wheat ripens reliably every autumn, the band doesn't
   need to travel for food that season, so mobility drops — they can stay
   longer, perhaps nearly year-round. The environment, not preference, sets
   the schedule."
3. Two mistakes: (1) foragers moved to *follow seasonal food*, not from
   inability to build — many built solid shelters; (2) "primitive" insults
   deep expertise. Fixed: "Forager groups moved with the seasons to follow
   wild foods; their mobility was a skilled strategy, and some groups, like
   the Lake People, stayed nearly put where food was steady."

**Independent practice.**
1. Four needs in own words: water (drink daily), wild food (animals to
   hunt, plants to gather), shelter (protection from weather/danger), safety
   (safe ground, group strength).
2. Advantage: fresh river water plus two food sources (river fish and sea
   fish/shellfish). Constraint: floods and storms can swamp the camp (or:
   the sea end is salt — drinking water must come from the river side).
3. Wild foods are seasonal and patchy — herds migrate, plants fruit in
   different places at different times. A group that stayed put year-round
   would go hungry in the lean season, so most groups moved with the food.
4. No. Farming means *growing* food (planting crops, raising domesticated
   animals). The Lake People live on wild food — staying put is sedentism,
   not farming.
5. Any two genuine seasonal effects, e.g.: "They time a spring hunt when
   the goats pass, and move to a sheltered valley camp in winter when the
   herds leave and grasses die." / "Spring is for hunting and drying meat;
   autumn is for gathering nuts before the snow."
6. a. Desert with one spring: the group must stay near the single water
   source, and its size and stay are limited by how much water it gives.
   b. Dense rainy forest: keeping dry is hard — shelter must shed constant
   rain, food spoils quickly, and floods can ruin a camp.
7. Accept: "Forager movements followed known seasonal patterns — the same
   berry hills each summer, the same fishing spots each spring — planned
   from generations of knowledge. That is the opposite of random."
8. Check: water, wild food, shelter, and one safety feature all labeled;
   caption names the camp's best feature (e.g., "the cave stays dry and the
   ridge keeps us above floods").

**Exit check.** 1. Foraging = living by hunting wild animals and gathering
wild plants; farming = planting crops and raising domesticated animals —
finding food vs. growing it. 2. Any two: the herd migrated; winter snow
buried the valley; the stream dried up; the berry season ended; a flood
ruined the camp.

## Lesson 02 — The agricultural revolution

**Readiness:** 1 → cause of farming. 2 → effect of farming. 3 → neither
(the wheel is millennia later).

**Guided practice.**
1. Card 1 chart — accept any 3+ genuine benefits and 3+ genuine costs, e.g.
   Benefits: bigger, more reliable wheat harvest; stored grain for winter;
   less travel for food; goats penned nearby for meat and milk. Costs:
   months of clearing, planting, weeding labor; crop failure wastes the
   work; narrower diet; new diseases from crowding with animals; someone
   must stay to guard the crop.
2. a. **Change** — branch huts came before mud brick. b. **Continuity** —
   caring for children predates farming. c. **Change** — foragers could not
   store months of food. d. **Continuity** — illness existed before farming
   (note: crowding brought *new* diseases, a change in kind — accept this
   nuance as strong).
3. Two mistakes: (1) no single genius invented farming — it was a slow,
   multi-cause shift over centuries by many people; (2) "9000 BCE" stated as
   exact fact — dates are approximate ("about"). Fixed: "Farming emerged
   gradually about 10,000 years ago as many people made small planting and
   herding choices over generations."

**Independent practice.**
1. **Domesticate:** to tame and breed a wild plant or animal so it becomes
   more useful to people. Plant: wheat (or barley). Animal: goats (or
   sheep, cattle, pigs).
2. Any two, each explained in a sentence: warmer steadier climate (longer
   growing seasons made planting a safer bet); seed selection (saving the
   biggest seeds bred bigger harvests); animal domestication (herds = food
   that reproduces itself); population pressure (debated — mark it so).
3. Warmer, steadier weather meant wild grasses grew thicker and more
   reliably, and longer warm seasons gave planted seeds time to ripen.
   Farming is a bet on next season — a steadier climate made the bet safer.
4. Card 3 chart — accept 2+ per side, e.g. Benefits: stored food for
   winter; a village can hold a hundred people; less daily searching.
   Costs: sunrise-to-sunset field labor; a failed rain means hunger; a
   narrower diet; new diseases from village crowding.
5. a. **Change** — forager bands were small and mobile. b. **Continuity** —
   fire cooking predates farming by hundreds of thousands of years.
   c. **Change** — stored surplus let some accumulate much more than others.
6. Accept three sentences using **labor**, **diet**, **disease**, e.g.:
   "It misses that farming demanded far harder daily labor — hours in the
   fields. It misses that diets narrowed to a few crops, and that living
   close with animals spread new diseases. And 'everyone did it' ignores
   that many foragers kept thriving without farming for thousands of years."
7. Open — accept any defended choice (e.g., "Card 4, because both mothers
   love their children and both have real evidence, so there is no clean
   answer").
8. Open — accept any genuine debated question (e.g., "Did people farm
   because they had to, or because the harvests tempted them?").

**Exit check.** 1. One cause (e.g., warmer climate / seed selection /
domestication) and one trade-off (e.g., stored food gained, harder labor
paid). 2. Because it ignores the real costs — harder labor, narrower
diets, new diseases — and assumes that because farming spread it must have
been purely good; historians genuinely debate this.

## Lesson 03 — Surplus, specialization, and settlements

**Readiness:** 1 → surplus (extra beyond need). 2 → not a surplus (nothing
extra). 3 → it depends — rotted grain is not storable, so it is not a
surplus.

**Guided practice.**
1. Stored surplus feeds people who do not farm: ten farmers growing food
   for fifteen means five people are freed for other work. Without stored
   extra, everyone must find their own food daily — so no full-time potter.
   The potter's lunch comes from someone else's stored grain.
2. (1) Better tools — sickles, grinding stones, digging sticks let one
   worker cut, grind, and plant faster, so each worker produces more food.
   (2) Irrigation — channels water fields regardless of rain, so more land
   is farmed and dry years are survived: more food per worker.
3. Wrong because Çatalhöyük shows the opposite: archaeologists found **no
   palaces and no kings** — ordinary houses side by side for centuries.
   Towns did not automatically produce rulers; organization without kings
   was possible.

**Independent practice.**
1. A **surplus** is food beyond what the growers need *that can be stored*.
   Storage matters because unstorable extra rots — only stored surplus can
   feed non-farmers later or carry a village through winter.
2. Five steps with arrow logic: reliable harvests → (more food than
   gathering) → surplus stored → (bins/baskets hold it for months) → some
   people freed → (their food comes from others' stored grain) →
   specialists → (full-time crafts, better tools, trade) → towns grow.
3. Clay-pot chain (4+ workers), e.g.: (1) clay digger digs and carries clay;
   (2) potter shapes the pots; (3) woodcutter gathers fuel and the kiln
   tender fires the kiln; (4) trader carries finished pots to neighboring
   villages for grain.
4. Irrigation raised output: fields watered regardless of rain, drier land
   farmed, dry years survived. It changed organization: channels must be
   dug, cleaned, and shared — creating decisions about who digs, who gets
   water first, and who repairs breaks.
5. Any three with the test applied, e.g.: the grain-storage building (needs
   a harvest bigger than immediate need); the pottery workshop (the potter
   is freed — others' stored grain feeds them); the irrigation channel
   (organized group labor, only possible with secure food).
6. No surplus. Surplus means *beyond* need — food for exactly 100 people
   with 100 mouths is zero extra.
7. Wrong: nobody ordered it — specialization **emerged** from the surplus
   math, and Çatalhöyük (no palaces, no kings found) contradicts
   chief-ordered craft. Fixed: "As stored surpluses grew, some people could
   spend their days on crafts instead of food-finding; over generations
   these became full-time specialists — the extra food made it possible,
   not a decree."
8. Accept three evidence-based sentences, e.g.: "Farm labor was not easy —
   the lesson describes sunrise-to-sunset work in the fields. Diets
   narrowed and new diseases spread in crowded villages. Towns grew because
   the surplus could feed more people and new kinds of workers, not because
   daily life got easier."

**Exit check.** 1. A stored farming surplus feeds people who do not farm,
so one person can spend full days making pots — the potter's food comes
from others' stored grain. 2. One innovation (e.g., irrigation: watered
fields regardless of rain → more land farmed → more food per worker; or
sickles/grinding stones: faster harvest and processing → more food per
worker).

## Lesson 04 — Comparing early farming regions

**Readiness:** 1 → different (wheat vs. maize). 2 → same (storage solved
the same problem). 3 → can't tell — no evidence measures happiness.

**Guided practice.**
1. a. 12,500 − 7,100 = **5,400 years**. (BCE: bigger number is older;
   subtract smaller from larger.) b. Goat/sheep domestication (about
   8,500 BCE) came **before** the tower of Jericho (about 8,000 BCE), by
   about **500 years** (8,500 − 8,000).
2. Chart check against: crops — wheat/barley/lentils vs. maize/beans/squash;
   animals — goats/sheep/cattle/pigs vs. dogs/turkeys/ducks (no draft
   animals); environment — river plains/rainy hills vs. tropical
   lowlands/highland valleys; timing — about 9,600 BCE cultivation vs.
   about 8,700 years ago (≈ 6700 BCE).
3. Two mistakes: (1) Mesoamerica started with **maize from teosinte**, not
   wheat — a completely different plant; (2) the different crops, animals,
   environments, and separate timing show **independent invention**, not
   copying. Fixed: "Farming began independently in Mesoamerica with local
   plants like maize; the different crops and timing show it was not copied
   from the Fertile Crescent."

**Independent practice.**
1. Fertile Crescent — wheat (or barley). Mesoamerica — maize.
2. Similarities (any two): both domesticated local plants; both stored
   surpluses; both settled down; both developed specialists and towns.
   Differences (any two): different starter crops; different animals (draft
   animals vs. none); different environments; different timing.
3. Multi-cause for Mesoamerica (3–4 sentences, 3+ causes, debated marked),
   e.g.: "Farming began in Mesoamerica because the warm climate and
   seasonal rains supported wild teosinte (environmental); teosinte could be
   bred generation by generation into maize (biological); local foragers
   knew the plant intimately and selected bigger kernels (human); and
   possibly because growing groups needed more reliable food (debated)."
4. Convert first: 8,700 years ago ≈ 2026 − 8,700 = −6,674 → about **6,700
   BCE**. Then 9,600 − 6,700 = **2,900 years**. (Work must show the
   conversion step — that is the skill being tested.)
5. Farmer's view (3–4 sentences, 2+ evidence), e.g.: "Our bins are full and
   my children will eat this winter whatever the herds do — stored grain
   beat a hungry winter (evidence: storage, Lesson 02). My back aches, but
   last winter nobody went hungry, and the village holds a hundred people
   who help each other (evidence: larger groups, Lesson 03)."
6. Forager's view (3–4 sentences, 2+ evidence), e.g.: "You work sunrise to
   dark for the same grain every meal while we ate forty foods this month
   and walked where we pleased (evidence: varied diet, mobility, Lesson
   01). Your bins are full, but crowding brought new sicknesses, and you
   cannot leave the fields (evidence: disease, labor costs, Lesson 02)."
7. Accept two sentences rejecting "better," e.g.: "'Better' ranks what the
   evidence can't rank — each region worked brilliantly with the plants,
   animals, and climate it had. Different starter crops and different timing
   show different answers to the same problem, not a contest."

**Exit check.** 1. Strongest evidence: completely different starter crops
(wheat/barley vs. maize from teosinte), different animals, different
environments — too different to be copied; independent invention is the
better explanation. 2. Both use real evidence but **value different things**
— the farmer weighs security, the forager weighs freedom and variety;
evidence informs values but doesn't dictate them.

## Practice Set A — Environments and forager life

1. **Foraging:** living by hunting wild animals and gathering wild plants.
   **Mobility:** how much a group moves (high = moves often, low = stays put).
2. a. **High** — the herds migrate hundreds of miles; the band must follow
   its food. b. **Low** — fish and wild grains are always available; no
   need to chase food. c. **High** — deep snow buries the hills; the band
   must move to a sheltered winter camp.
3. **Cedar Cove:** water ✓ (stream), wild food ✓ (acorns; deer likely),
   shelter ✓ (dry rock overhang), safety ~ (ridge above floods ✓, but wolf
   dens nearby in spring ✗). **Decision: seasonal camp — autumn/winter,
   avoid spring** (or "camp with caution"). Defense: three needs fully met,
   but the spring wolf dens make it unsafe for part of the year — a good
   camp in one season can be a bad camp in another.
4. Accept: "The same four needs produce different routines because
   environments offer different foods at different times — a lake gives
   steady food year-round while snowy hills force seasonal moves. Geography
   sets the options; each group's calendar answers its own landscape."
5. a. Desert spring: the group must stay near the single source — group
   size and length of stay are limited by its water. b. Rainy forest:
   staying dry is the constraint — shelter must shed constant rain, stored
   food spoils, and floods can ruin a camp.
6. Mistake: groups moved to *follow seasonal food*, not from inability —
   and many built solid shelters. Rewrite: "Forager groups moved with the
   seasons to follow wild foods; where food was steady year-round, like the
   Lake People, they stayed — mobility was strategy, not failure."
7. Check: water, one wild food source, shelter, and one safety feature all
   labeled; caption names the best feature.
8. Accept three respectful sentences with one specific example, e.g.:
   "Foragers tracked dozens of plant and animal cycles from memory — knowing
   exactly when berries ripened or herds passed. They made tools, controlled
   fire, and read weather and animal signs most modern people couldn't. That
   knowledge was stored in memory and story instead of books, which is
   different from ignorance."

## Practice Set B — Causes, trade-offs, change and continuity

1. Three causes, debated marked: warmer steadier climate; seed selection;
   animal domestication; population pressure **(debated)**.
2. Accept: "Gatherers noticed dropped grains grew into strong plants, so
   they began saving the biggest seeds to plant. Repeated every generation,
   this slowly bred larger, more reliable harvests — farming emerging one
   choice at a time, not one invention."
3. Card 2 chart (3+ per side), e.g. Benefits: food even as herds thin;
   staying in the valley year-round (less travel); stored food for lean
   times; learning from the lowland neighbors. Costs: hard labor to clear
   and plant; little rain — the crop may fail; narrower diet; someone must
   stay to tend and guard; new diseases from settling.
4. a. **Change** — taming and keeping animals is new. b. **Continuity** —
   ancestor stories predate farming. c. **Change** — field labor lengthened
   the workday. d. **Continuity** — the need for water is biological and
   unchanged.
5. Missing: the costs. A benefits-only chart is a sales pitch, not a
   decision — the costs are where the trade-offs live, and no honest
   verdict is possible without weighing both sides.
6. "About 10,000 years ago" is honest because farming emerged gradually,
   over centuries, in different places — there was no single year it
   began. "8000 BCE" pretends a precision the evidence cannot support.
7. Exemplar — Mother 1's evidence: "Stored grain means children survive
   winters that would have starved a foraging camp; a bigger village means
   more help." Mother 2's evidence: "Camp children eat dozens of wild foods
   and play while adults gather; village children work the fields from a
   young age." Personal judgment (open): "The stored-food evidence matters
   more to me because a hungry winter is the worst thing I can imagine —
   but I admit I'm trading away the childhood evidence."
8. Verdict paragraph exemplar: "For the Riverbend Band, farming is worth
   trying but not obviously good. The benefits are real: a bigger, storable
   harvest and less travel for food. The costs are real too: months of hard
   labor, the risk of a failed crop wasting it all, and a narrower diet.
   What they sacrifice is the varied diet and freedom of the moving life —
   and they should only pay it if the stored grain truly carries them
   through a bad year."

## Project exemplar — "The Settlement Council" (what Strong looks like)

*An exemplar, not a model answer to copy. The learner's own band reasoning
scores higher than a memorized exemplar.*

**Recommendation (claim + 3 reasons):** "The Riverbend band should clear
ground and plant this spring. (1) The wild wheat already ripens thick here
every autumn (Lesson 01: the environment offers the chance). (2) Stored
grain would carry our children through the winter that starved two elders
three years ago (Lesson 02: security benefit). (3) Goats water at our river
daily — penning a few gives meat and milk without the long hunts (Lesson
02: domestication)."

**Sketch map:** title ("Riverbend — proposed farm village"), key, compass
rose, scale bar; river (water), wheat fields (food), mud-brick houses
(shelter), granary bins (storage), and the cliff caves kept as storm
shelter (safety) — all four needs visibly met.

**Benefit/cost table:** Benefits — reliable harvest; winter food stored;
less travel. Costs — months of hard field labor; a failed rain wastes the
season; narrower diet; new sicknesses from crowding.

**Counterclaim:** "The best argument against my advice is the forest-edge
