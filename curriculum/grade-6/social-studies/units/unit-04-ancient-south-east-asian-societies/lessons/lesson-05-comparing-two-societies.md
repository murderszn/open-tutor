# Lesson 05 — Comparing Two Societies: What Each Did With Its Environment

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can compare two ancient
Asian societies, explain how their physical and human
characteristics connected to their cultures, and build a
multi-cause explanation of one change in each society.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson,
[asia-map.svg](../assets/asia-map.svg),
[asia-timeline.svg](../assets/asia-timeline.svg), a pencil,
your inquiry notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Name one physical feature and
one human feature of any society from this unit (for example,
"monsoon rains" and "terraced fields"). If you can name both,
you are ready.

## Comparison is not a contest

Comparing two societies does not mean picking a winner. It
means holding the same questions up to both and noticing where
the answers match and where they differ — and then asking why.
The questions for this lesson are the same for both societies:

1. What environmental problems did people face?
2. What did they build or organize to solve them?
3. Who did the work, and who benefited most?
4. What held the society together across distance — writing,
   officials, beliefs, trade, or armies?
5. What changed, what stayed the same, and whose perspective
   dominates the surviving evidence?

Use those five questions every time you compare, and your
comparisons will stay fair. A comparison that asks different
questions of each society ("Was India spiritual? Was China
organized?") smuggles in a ranking. Ask the same questions,
and let the differences speak.

## Worked example A — Indus cities and Han China, side by side

| Question | Indus cities (about 2600–1900 BCE) | Han China (about 206 BCE–220 CE) |
|---|---|---|
| Environmental problem | Indus floods; need for drainage and clean water in dense cities | Yellow River floods and silt; need for irrigation, canals, and flood control across a huge empire |
| Built/organized answer | Grid streets, covered drains, Great Bath, standardized bricks and weights | Canals, roads, granaries, standardized script and currency, appointed officials |
| Who worked / who benefited | Artisans, farmers, merchants; benefits shared through trade, but hierarchy's details are unknown (script undeciphered) | Farming families paid grain tax and labor; officials and merchants benefited from standards and roads; laborers bore building costs |
| What held it together | Shared planning standards and trade networks; no readable royal records survive | Bureaucracy, one script, Confucian ideals of official conduct, armies, and Silk Road trade |
| Change / continuity / perspective | Decline about 1900 BCE from several causes (river shifts, rainfall change, trade weakening); evidence favors planners' artifacts over any ruler's voice — because there is no readable ruler's voice at all | Han kept Qin's centralized system but softened Legalist harshness; evidence favors officials' records over farmers' daily experience |

Notice what the table does not do: it does not say the Indus
cities were "better" for lacking palaces, or that Han China was
"better" for lasting longer. Longevity is not virtue, and
missing evidence is not proof of equality. The table's last row
is the most honest one: it names what we cannot know.

## Worked example B — One change, two societies, different causes

Change: *A new idea spreads across a large area.*

In South Asia, Buddhism spread largely through monks,
merchants, and royal support (Ashoka's emissaries and edicts),
along trade routes, without an empire forcing conversion.
In China, standardized writing spread largely through
government action: the Qin imposed one script, and the Han kept
it, because officials needed to read orders everywhere.

Same pattern — an idea crossing distance — different main
cause: in one case, traveling teachers and traders plus royal
patronage; in the other, state standardization. A multi-cause
explanation names both the carriers and the conditions: routes
existed, patrons supported the carriers, and the idea itself
answered a need (a path out of suffering; a readable order).
If you name only one cause, your explanation is too thin. If
you name five causes but cannot say which mattered more, your
explanation is a list, not an argument. This lesson asks you
to rank causes and defend the ranking — that is what
"evaluating relative influence" means in practice.

## Guided practice

1. Copy the five comparison questions into your notebook and
   answer question 1 and 2 for Mauryan India.
2. Using the table in Worked example A, state one similarity
   between Indus cities and Han China in how they solved a
   water problem, and one difference in what held them
   together.
3. For Buddhism's spread, name two carriers and one condition
   (a route, a patron, or a need) that helped it spread.
4. Rank these three causes of Qin standardization's success —
   (a) one conquering army, (b) appointed officials, (c) traders
   wanting one currency — from strongest to weakest, with one
   reason for your top choice.

## Independent practice

5. Compare Gupta India and Han China on question 4 (what held
   the society together): give one similarity and one
   difference, each with evidence.
6. Compute: about how many years passed between Ashoka's reign
   (about 268 BCE) and the start of the Gupta dynasty (about
   320 CE)? Remember: across the BCE/CE boundary, subtract 1
   for no year 0. Show your work.
7. A classmate writes, "Han China was more advanced than Indus
   cities because it lasted longer." Explain in three sentences
   why longevity alone does not prove superiority, using one
   Indus achievement.
8. **Explain your reasoning:** Which physical feature shaped
   its society more — the Himalayas for South Asia, or the
   Yellow River for China? Defend your choice with two
   concrete effects.

## Application

Choose two societies from this unit (one South Asian, one East
Asian, or two from different periods). Write a five-paragraph
comparison using the five questions: one paragraph per
question. End with a sixth paragraph: your synthesis — one
change that happened in both societies, the strongest cause in
each case, and one sentence on whose perspective the evidence
favors. This draft becomes the core of your project exhibit.

## Exit check

In two sentences: what is one fair comparison you can make
between two societies from this unit, and what evidence
supports it?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** The adult reads the worked
  example table row by row; the learner covers one column and
  predicts its contents before revealing it.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may build the
  comparison as a two-column poster with drawings for each
  row and explain it aloud instead of writing the five
  paragraphs.
- **Extension:** Add a third society from an earlier unit
  (Mesopotamia or Egypt) to the table for one row only, and
  write two sentences on what that row reveals about
  river-valley societies in general.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: comparison as ranking; longevity as
superiority; missing evidence as proof (for example, "no
palaces means no rulers" — it means no palace evidence);
single-cause explanations. Watch for present-day stereotyping
("Asian societies are...") and redirect to specific evidence
about specific societies at specific dates. See the answer key
for full solutions.
