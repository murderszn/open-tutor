# Lesson 04 — Early China: Dynasties, Bureaucracy, and Innovation

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can explain dynastic
change and continuity in early China (Shang, Zhou, Qin, Han),
how bureaucracy and standardization held a large empire
together, and how a farmer, an official, and a merchant might
have seen the same tax differently.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson,
[asia-timeline.svg](../assets/asia-timeline.svg),
[asia-map.svg](../assets/asia-map.svg), a pencil, your
inquiry notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Name one thing that stayed the
same and one thing that changed between two units you have
already studied (for example, from Unit 02 farming villages to
Unit 03 cities). If you can name both, you are ready.

## Four dynasties, one long story

Chinese history is often told dynasty by dynasty, and that can
make it sound like history simply swapped one royal family for
another. The useful question is different: what changed when a
dynasty changed, and what stayed the same?

**Shang (about 1600–1046 BCE).** The Shang ruled from cities in
the Yellow River valley. They cast beautiful bronze ritual
vessels, built walled cities, and wrote on **oracle bones** —
tortoise shells and ox bones inscribed with questions to
ancestors ("Will the harvest be good? Should the king hunt?"),
then cracked by heat so priests could "read" the cracks. Oracle
bones are precious evidence: they are the earliest known
Chinese writing, and they show a society where rulers consulted
ancestors before big decisions.

**Zhou (about 1046–256 BCE).** The Zhou overthrew the Shang
and justified it with a new idea: the **Mandate of Heaven**.
Heaven, they said, grants the right to rule to a just ruler and
withdraws it from an unjust one — so a rebellion against a bad
king could be legitimate, not just treason. That idea outlived
the Zhou by thousands of years: later rebels and new dynasties
kept claiming the Mandate had passed to them. In the later Zhou
centuries, central power broke down into rival states that
fought each other for generations (the Warring States period).
It was in that disorder that Confucius, Laozi, and the Legalists
offered their competing answers (Lesson 03).

**Qin (221–207 BCE).** The state of Qin conquered the other
states in 221 BCE, and its ruler took a new title: **Shi
Huangdi**, the First Emperor. In just over a decade, the Qin
standardized the script (small seal script), currency, weights,
measures, and even the width of cart axles, so carts could run
in the ruts of imperial roads. They built roads and canals,
joined earlier northern walls into longer defenses, and ran
the empire through appointed officials and strict Legalist laws.
The cost was high: heavy taxes, forced labor on building
projects, and harsh punishments — including, in 213 BCE, the
burning of books the regime saw as threatening. The Qin fell
within four years of the First Emperor's death, but its
standards survived: later dynasties kept one script, one
currency system, and centralized administration.

**Han (about 206 BCE–220 CE).** The Han kept Qin's centralized
system and softened its harshness. Officials were chosen by
recommendation and testing for ability and learning (an early
form of civil service), taxes were still collected in grain and
labor, and the empire expanded trade westward along the Silk
Roads. Han workshops produced silk, lacquerware, and iron tools;
by tradition, paper was made at the imperial court about
105 CE, which eventually made record-keeping and learning far
cheaper than bamboo or silk writing surfaces. The Han lasted
more than four centuries — long enough that the Chinese people
later called themselves "people of Han."

Continuity across all four: writing as a tool of rule,
irrigation and flood control as a central government job,
farming families paying taxes in grain and labor, and the
belief that a ruler's legitimacy depended on keeping order and
feeding people. Change across all four: who ruled, how strictly
laws were enforced, how officials were chosen, and how far
trade reached.

## Worked example A — Change and continuity in one table

Question: What changed and what stayed the same from Zhou to
Qin?

*Change:* From many rival states with their own scripts,
currencies, and measures to one empire with one script, one
currency, and one set of measures. From Mandate-of-Heaven
justification debated by philosophers to Legalist rule by
strict, uniform law.
*Continuity:* Farming families still grew the grain, paid taxes
in grain and labor, and depended on irrigation and flood
control. Writing was still the government's tool — only now
the writing itself was standardized.
*Check:* Is "the emperor became more powerful" a change or a
continuity? It is a change in degree — Zhou kings had already
claimed broad authority, but Qin's centralized bureaucracy
reached further into daily life (tax counts, road widths,
script forms). Good answers note degree, not just direction.

## Worked example B — Three views of one tax (fictional practice statements)

A Han county announces a grain tax: each farming household
must deliver a fixed amount of grain to the county granary and
provide labor days for canal repair. The three statements below
are **fictional practice statements written for this lesson** —
not quotations from ancient people.

*Farmer:* "The tax takes grain my family grew. If the flood
was bad, the fixed amount hurts twice — less harvest, same
tax. But the canal work keeps water coming to my field."
*Official:* "The granary feeds soldiers, officials, and
workers, and stores grain against famine. Without the tax,
there is no road, no canal, no defense — and no record of who
paid what."
*Merchant:* "One script, one currency, and good roads mean I
can sell grain in three counties without changing coins or
re-learning measures. The tax costs me too, but the standards
make trade possible."

*Modeled reasoning:* All three describe the same tax, and all
three can be partly right: the tax burdens the farmer, funds
the state, and — through the standards it supports — helps
the merchant. Whose view do the surviving records favor? The
official's: tax records, edicts, and histories were written by
or for the government. The farmer's daily experience survives
mostly in archaeology (tools, houses, bones) and in later
stories, not in tax receipts. That is a perspective limit, and
good historians name it.

## Guided practice

1. Put Shang, Zhou, Qin, and Han in order, with one achievement
   or idea for each.
2. Explain the Mandate of Heaven in two sentences, and give one
   example of how a later ruler could use it.
3. Name three things the Qin standardized, and explain in one
   sentence why standardization helped a merchant.
4. From Worked example B, state one cost of the grain tax for
   the farmer and one benefit of the system for the merchant.

## Independent practice

5. Classify each as change or continuity from Shang to Han,
   with a one-sentence reason: (a) rulers consult writing for
   decisions; (b) one standard script across China; (c) farmers
   pay taxes in grain; (d) officials chosen by testing.
6. Compute: about how many years passed between the start of
   the Shang (about 1600 BCE) and Qin's unification (about
   221 BCE)? Show your work.
7. A classmate says, "The Qin fell because its ideas were all
   bad." Explain why that is too simple, using one Qin
   achievement that outlived the dynasty.
8. **Explain your reasoning:** Which mattered more for holding
   the Han empire together — the army or the bureaucracy?
   Defend your choice in three sentences, naming one job
   officials did that soldiers could not.

## Application

You are a Han official writing a report (three paragraphs) on
whether to lower the grain tax after a bad flood year. Paragraph
1: what the farmer would say. Paragraph 2: what the granary
records would say. Paragraph 3: your recommendation, with one
reason that answers the farmer's concern and one reason that
answers the state's need. Label the report as a classroom
reconstruction, not an ancient document.

## Exit check

Name one change and one continuity from Zhou to Han, and state
in one sentence whose perspective is hardest to recover from
the surviving records.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** The adult reads the four-dynasty
  section aloud while the learner tracks each dynasty on the
  timeline; "standardize" is taught as "make the same everywhere."
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may act out the
  three tax perspectives in a short adult-guided role play and
  then state each perspective in one sentence, instead of
  writing practice answer 4.
- **Extension:** With adult-selected sources only, find one
  Han innovation not covered here (for example, the seismoscope
  or improved iron plows) and add a two-sentence note on what
  problem it solved.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: dynasties as clean breaks (stress
continuity); the Great Wall as a single Qin construction (Qin
joined and extended earlier walls; the famous brick wall is
mostly much later); paper as instantly replacing bamboo/silk
(it spread gradually); Legalism as a religion (it is a
governing philosophy). Sensitive content: forced labor and
book burning are named plainly, briefly, without graphic
detail. See the answer key for full solutions.
