# Lesson 02 — Indus Cities and Later South Asian Kingdoms

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can explain how Indus
cities were planned and organized, how later South Asian
kingdoms governed large populations, and what the surviving
evidence cannot tell us.

your inquiry notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Name one feature of a planned

## Cities before kings we can name

About 2600 BCE — roughly 4,600 years ago — large cities were
already flourishing beside the Indus River, in what is now
Pakistan and northwest India. The two best known are
**Mohenjo-daro** and **Harappa**. Their streets run in a grid,
like a checkerboard. Houses are built of baked bricks made to
standard sizes. Covered drains carry wastewater away from
streets and houses. Mohenjo-daro has a large watertight tank
called the **Great Bath**, which scholars think may have been
used for ritual bathing, though no text explains it — that
"may have been" matters, and you will meet it again below.
Merchants used carved stone **seals** to mark goods, and
standardized weights to trade fairly across long distances.
Seals and weights show up as far away as Mesopotamia, so Indus
traders were part of a Bronze Age world network.

Here is the strange and honest part: we cannot name an Indus
king, read an Indus law, or retell an Indus story. The Indus
people wrote — hundreds of short inscriptions survive on seals
— but their **script is undeciphered**: no one today can read
it, because no bilingual text (like Egypt's Rosetta Stone) has
been found. Archaeologists also find no grand palaces or royal
tombs at Mohenjo-daro, unlike in Egypt or Mesopotamia. So
historians describe Indus cities as highly organized — the
grid, drains, and standard weights prove planning — without
claiming to know exactly who ruled them or how. That is good
historical honesty: the evidence shows organization, not a
king's name, and this lesson will not invent one.

Indus cities declined after about 1900 BCE. River channels
shifted, rainfall patterns changed, and trade networks weakened.
Historians debate which cause mattered most, and the honest
answer is probably several causes together — a multi-cause
explanation, not a single dramatic event. People did not vanish;
farming villages and towns continued across the region, and
later South Asian societies grew out of that long history.

## Later kingdoms: Maurya and Gupta

Centuries later, much larger kingdoms rose on the Ganges plain.
The **Maurya Empire** (about 322–185 BCE) was founded by
**Chandragupta Maurya** and reached its greatest extent under
his grandson **Ashoka** (reigned about 268–232 BCE). After a
bloody war against Kalinga (about 261 BCE), Ashoka publicly
turned toward a policy of moral rule. He had orders carved on
rocks and polished stone pillars across his empire, proclaiming
**dhamma**: respect for parents and elders, kindness to
servants and animals, and respect between religious groups.
These edicts are real evidence — we can read them (in the
Brahmi script, deciphered in the 1800s) — and they tell us what
a ruler wanted his subjects to hear. They do not tell us whether
everyone obeyed. A ruler's public message is evidence about the
ruler's goals, not proof of daily life. Keep that distinction.

The **Gupta Empire** (about 320–550 CE) ruled much of northern
India centuries after the Mauryas. Gupta-era scholars made
advances students still use today: mathematicians in India
developed a decimal place-value system with a symbol for zero —
the system that, carried westward over centuries, became the
numbers you use now. The astronomer Aryabhata (about 476–550 CE)
calculated the length of the year and explained eclipses
without blaming gods or demons. The great learning center of
Nalanda, founded in the Gupta period, drew students from across
Asia. Gupta art, literature, and temple building flourished —
but remember the cost question from Unit 03: prosperity for
some rested on the labor of farmers, artisans, and laborers
whose names rarely survive, and on a social hierarchy that
ranked people by birth (see the vocabulary note on varna and
jati). Describing that hierarchy plainly is not the same as
approving of it; India's modern constitution bans
discrimination based on caste.

## Worked example A — What a city plan proves, and what it does not

Evidence card (simplified from excavation summaries): "Streets
cross at right angles. Bricks measure the same size in house
after house. A covered drain runs beneath the main street. A
large tank is lined with waterproof bitumen. A seal shows an
animal and five script signs."

*Observation:* The streets, bricks, and drain are uniform.
*Inference:* Someone planned the city and enforced standards —
uniform bricks do not happen by accident.
*Limit:* The seal's five signs cannot be read. We cannot say
whose seal it was or what the signs mean. Any story about an
Indus king ordering this city would be invented, so we do not
tell one.
*Comparison:* In Unit 03, a cuneiform tablet could be read and
named its writer's business. Indus evidence is strong on
planning and weak on words — the opposite strength from
Mesopotamia.

## Worked example B — Reading an edict as evidence

Paraphrase of the kind of message Ashoka's edicts carry (not a
quotation): officials should treat people fairly, respect
elders and servants, protect animals, and honor all religious
groups rather than praising only one's own.

*What this proves:* Ashoka wanted these ideas spread publicly,
across a large empire, in writing meant to be seen.
*What this does not prove:* That officials always obeyed, or
that ordinary people agreed. A carved order is a wish from the
top, not a report from the bottom.
*Perspective check:* Whose voice is missing? The farmer who
paid taxes, the servant the edict says to treat kindly, and
anyone who disagreed with the king. Good historians ask for
those voices even when they have not survived.

## Guided practice

1. List three features of Mohenjo-daro's plan, and for each,
   state what it suggests about organization.
2. Why can't historians retell an Indus king's story? Give two
   reasons from the lesson.
3. In one sentence each, explain what Ashoka's edicts prove and
   what they do not prove.
4. Name one Gupta-era achievement and explain, in two
   sentences, why it still matters to a student today.

## Independent practice

5. A classmate says, "Indus cities had no government because
   they had no king's palace." Explain why that argument is
   weak, using two pieces of evidence.
6. Compare Mauryan and Gupta rule: give one similarity (how
   they held a large area together) and one difference (a
   tool or achievement each is known for).
7. Compute: about how many years passed between the flourishing
   of Indus cities (about 2600 BCE) and Qin's unification of
   China (about 221 BCE)? Show your subtraction.
8. **Explain your reasoning:** Which is stronger evidence that
   Indus merchants traded widely — standardized weights, or
   the Great Bath? Defend your choice in three sentences.

## Application

Choose one object for a museum label: an Indus seal, an Ashoka
pillar, or a Gupta-era number chart. Write the label (five
sentences): what the object is, what it shows, what it cannot
show, and one question a visitor should ask about it.

## Exit check

Name one thing Indus evidence tells us confidently, and one
thing it cannot tell us. (Two sentences.)

  before the lesson; the learner may listen to the lesson text
  while following the timeline.

  cards into "proves planning," "proves trade," and "cannot
  prove" piles and explain two placements aloud instead of
  writing practice answers 5–6.

  find one more fact about Nalanda (who studied there, or what
  subjects were taught) and add one sentence to the museum
  label — with the source named.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "undeciphered" means "no writing"
(it means writing we cannot yet read); edicts as proof of
behavior (they are proof of policy); Gupta achievements as
one person's work (they were a scholarly tradition).
Sensitive content: name the varna/jati hierarchy and caste
discrimination plainly, briefly, and without graphic detail;
note the modern constitutional ban. See the answer key for
full solutions.
