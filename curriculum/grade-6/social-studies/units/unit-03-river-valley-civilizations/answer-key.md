# Answer Key — Unit 03: River-Valley Civilizations

**Adult only.** Every solution below was solved independently from
the student tasks and reconciled with the lessons. Accept any
answer that matches the reasoning and evidence here, even if the
wording differs. Do not hand this file to the learner.

## §L1 — Lesson 01

**Guided practice.** 1. Mesopotamia — Tigris and Euphrates; Egypt —
Nile; Indus Valley — Indus River; Yellow River valley — Yellow
River (Huang He). 2. Model: "The Nile flooded each year (about
June–September), leaving water and silt. Farmers trapped water with
basin irrigation (or lifted it with a shaduf), then planted wheat
and barley in the damp soil. A good year left surplus grain to
store, tax, or trade." 3. Mesopotamian floods were violent and
hard to predict (too early, too late, too high, or absent), so
farmers needed larger canal and levee systems and more organized
labor to control water; Egypt's flood was fairly predictable and
gentler. 4. Observations (any two): people carry grain baskets;
a canal with wooden gates runs beside the fields; mud-brick houses
and grain piles stand near the river; a stepped ziggurat rises
behind the houses; a reed boat floats on the river. Inference:
farmers controlled water with gates, or stored/moved surplus grain.
Cannot show: how often floods were bad, who owned the grain, or
whether carriers were paid, free, or forced workers. 5. Very high
flood: drowned crops, washed-away houses and canals. Very low
flood: too little water and silt, failed crops, possible famine.

**Independent practice.** 6. Egypt's Nile flood was the most
predictable: it came at a fairly regular time each year, so farmers
could plan planting, labor, and storage instead of guessing.
7. Model sentences: "The Indus Valley grew beside the Indus River,
and its city Mohenjo-daro had planned grid streets and covered
drains." / "The Yellow River valley in northern China is named for
the yellow loess silt the river carries, and its floods could be
devastating." 8. Errors: (a) floods were *not* the same — the Nile
was fairly predictable while the Tigris–Euphrates floods were
violent and unpredictable, and the Indus and Yellow Rivers had
their own patterns; (b) cities were *not* the same — Egypt tended
toward one kingdom while Mesopotamia had independent city-states,
and Mohenjo-daro's planned city shows yet another pattern.
9. Diagram check: river → bank/dike holding water → field with
soaked soil; farmer controls the bank/gate and timing; farmer
cannot control flood size, timing, or rainfall.

**Applied task (model).** "I recommend Site A. Its benefit is
predictable floodwater and silt on flat land, giving reliable
harvests; its risk is flood damage in an extreme year. Site B's
benefit is safety from floods; its risk is the long carry of water
and lower yields without irrigation. In its first year Site A needs
canals, levees, and field banks, plus a grain store. One question
remains: how high was the worst flood in living memory, and what
did it destroy?" Any well-defended choice (including Site B with
safety-first reasoning) earns credit if it names a benefit and a
risk for each site, first-year irrigation work, and an open
question.

**Exit check.** (a) Mesopotamia, Egypt, Indus Valley, Yellow River
valley. (b) Irrigation is moving water to fields on purpose through
canals, ditches, dikes, and lifting devices. (c) Helped: water and
silt produced surplus food. Threatened: violent floods or drought
could destroy crops and homes.

## §L2 — Lesson 02

**Guided practice.** 1. Surplus = more food than growers need right
away. Specialization = different people doing different jobs.
Example: surplus barley feeds a potter who makes storage jars full
time. 2. Cloth chain (any valid chain): farmer/herder supplies
wool or flax → weaver makes cloth → merchant carries cloth to
market (scribe records the sale). 3. Mesopotamia: temple
storehouses collected grain and paid workers. Egypt: officials
measured fields and collected grain taxes; temples/palaces
organized building projects. 4. Scribes were needed because
(1) taxes, trade deals, and grain stores had to be recorded
accurately across years and places, and (2) laws and building
orders had to be written down and consulted — tasks memory alone
could not do reliably at city scale. 5. Model: "In part, yes —
specialists made pots, tools, and records everyone used. In part,
no — farmers still did the hardest physical work, and rulers,
priests, and officials controlled stored grain, so benefits were
unequal; some workers were enslaved or forced."

**Independent practice.** 6. Model (4 sentences): "Both had cities
built on surplus grain and specialist jobs. Mesopotamia was mostly
independent city-states, while Egypt tended toward one kingdom.
One reason is geography: Egypt's single long Nile valley favored
unification, while Mesopotamia's separate river cities stayed
apart and often competed." 7. Error: temples were economic
organizations, not only worship places — they collected grain,
stored it, paid workers, and organized building and trade.
8. Grows/makes: farmer, potter, weaver. Moves/sells: merchant.
Records/rules: scribe, tax official. Serves temple/palace: priest,
soldier. Dual placements accepted with reasons: scribe and tax
official also serve temple/palace; merchant may serve temple trade;
soldier may serve a ruler's government. 9. Model: "Grid streets and
covered drains show planning and organized labor, so Mohenjo-daro
was a coordinated city. But without a palace, royal tomb, or
readable writing, historians cannot claim what kind of king or
government it had — the evidence for rulers is missing, not proof
there were none."

**Applied task (model).** Jobs board (8+ jobs, 3+ groups, arrows):
farmer → potter (grain paid for jars) → scribe (records jars and
grain) → merchant (carries jars/grain by boat); plus weaver,
priest, soldier, tax official. Paragraph model: "Record-keepers are
needed because the city must remember who paid tax, how much grain
is stored, and what workers are owed; without records, disputes
cannot be settled and stores cannot be checked." Honest note model:
"Farmers and builders do the hardest physical work, while rulers
and temple officials decide how stored grain is used."

**Exit check.** (a) Surplus makes specialist jobs and cities
possible. (b) Any three from the lesson with correct job
descriptions (for example: potter makes jars; scribe keeps records;
merchant moves goods). (c) Any one: collecting/storing grain,
paying workers, organizing building or trade.

## §L3 — Lesson 03

**Guided practice.** 1. Model: "A city of 5,000 must remember taxes
owed, grain stored, trade deals, and laws across years; memory and
word of mouth cannot track that much, for that long, across that
many people." 2. Balance: 60 − 40 = 20 measures. Entry 1 = income
received; entry 2 = spending on workers; entry 3 = balance still
owed/stored. None of the entries tells us whether canal workers
thought 40 measures was fair, or what their lives were like.
3. Common: both were durable records made by trained scribes for
tax, trade, and law. Difference: cuneiform was pressed into clay
with a reed stylus; hieroglyphs were carved on stone or written in
ink on papyrus. 4. Scribe power: (1) only scribes could read and
write the records, so temples, palaces, and merchants depended on
them; (2) scribes controlled what was recorded and could check —
knowledge others could not verify. 5. Order: cuneiform (about
3300 BCE), Egypt unified (about 3100 BCE), Mohenjo-daro flourishing
(about 2500 BCE), Hammurabi's Code (about 1754 BCE). Elapsed:
3300 − 1754 = 1546 years.

**Independent practice.** 6. Errors: (a) not everyone could read —
writing took years of training and scribes were few; (b) laws and
records were therefore not directly usable by ordinary people, who
needed scribes or officials to explain them. 7. Written records
help trade by (1) proving a deal's terms far from home and
(2) recording amounts, dates, and parties so disputes can be
checked. Risk remains: floods, theft, dishonest measuring, or a
buyer who refuses to pay. 8. Model: "Because the Indus script
cannot be read, historians cannot learn Indus rulers' names, laws,
or religious texts from writing. They also cannot read Indus trade
contracts or literature. City remains still show planning (grid
streets), sanitation (covered drains), and trade/standards
(seals, standardized weights)." 9. Advantage of picture signs:
signs can be recognized without an alphabet and are durable in
clay/stone. Difficulty: hundreds of signs must be learned, and
abstract ideas are hard to draw; an alphabet needs fewer signs.

**Applied task (model).** "CLASSROOM MODEL — Tablet of the North
Canal village: Received: 90 measures of barley. Paid to canal
workers: 55 measures. Balance stored: 35 measures. (90 − 55 = 35.)
Scribe's note: 'This record matters because the temple can check
what arrived, what workers were paid, and what remains.' Missing
view: 'The canal workers' own view — whether 55 measures felt fair
— is not in this tablet.'"

**Exit check.** (a) Cuneiform — Mesopotamia; hieroglyphs — Egypt.
(b) Any two: recording taxes, proving trade deals, writing laws,
preserving stories/knowledge. (c) Writing gave scribes power
because only they could read and write the records everyone else
depended on.

## §L4 — Lesson 04

**Guided practice.** 1. Model (3 sentences): "Hammurabi, king of
Babylon from about 1792 to 1750 BCE, had 282 laws carved in
cuneiform on a tall diorite stele around 1754 BCE. The stele was
found at Susa in 1901 and is now in the Louvre Museum in Paris."
2. Law 196 reveals the retaliation principle (punishment matching
the injury) written in fixed form, and a society that recorded
exact consequences for injury. It does not reveal whether the law
was enforced equally, or how often such injuries happened.
3. Law 53 addresses canal neglect flooding a neighbor's field; it
holds the dike keeper responsible for the lost grain. Law 229
addresses faulty building; it holds the builder responsible for a
collapse. Both show responsibility assigned by written rule.
4. More consistent: (1) the same written text could be consulted
by different judges; (2) consequences were fixed in advance rather
than improvised. Limits: (1) most people could not read the text;
(2) punishments varied by rank, so the same rule did not treat
everyone equally. 5. Model judgment: "Strength: written laws gave
everyone a fixed, consultable rule, which is fairer than unwritten
custom that varies by judge. Limit: punishments differed by social
rank and most people could not read the laws. Judgment: the Code
was an advance in consistency, but not equal justice."

**Independent practice.** 6. Written code helps a dispute by
(1) giving a fixed rule both sides can be told and (2) letting a
judge compare the case with the written consequence. The farmer
may still be disadvantaged because he likely cannot read the code,
may rank below the merchant, and may lack the means to press a
claim. 7. Errors: (a) most people could not read, so carving did
not mean universal knowledge; (b) knowing a law does not mean
following it — enforcement depended on officials, evidence, and
power. 8. The Shamash carving presented Hammurabi's laws as given
by a god of justice, lending them divine authority and legitimacy;
historians learn Hammurabi wanted his laws seen as righteous and
god-backed, not merely as royal orders. 9. Law 53 policy model:
"Problem: neglected dikes flood neighbors' fields. Benefit of
writing it down: every dike keeper knows the same consequence in
advance. Drawback: the fine comes after the damage and does not
repair the dike or feed the flooded farmer at once. Overall
judgment: a reasonable solution for its time, because it assigned
clear responsibility, though it could not prevent floods."

**Applied task (models).** Case 1 (broken dike): Law 53 fits; the
dike keeper pays for the grain lost; missing fact: whether the
keeper was warned or too poor to repair the dike, or whether a
storm (not neglect) broke it; fairness: fair in assigning
responsibility, possibly harsh if the keeper could not afford
repairs. Case 2 (damaged boatload): no Lesson 04 law fits exactly —
nearest is the principle of liability for goods in one's care
(compare Law 53's responsibility logic); the adult should accept
"no exact law in this lesson" as a correct, careful answer, with
the learner stating what fact (contract terms, fault) is missing
and judging fairness as undecidable without it. Any second case
with a named law, a decision, a missing fact, and a reasoned
judgment earns credit.

**Exit check.** (a) About 1754 BCE; 282 laws. (b) Any one law with
a correct reveal (for example, Law 53: canal upkeep was a legal
duty). (c) Strength: fixed, consultable rules. Limit: unequal by
rank, or unreadable by most people.

## §L5 — Lesson 05

**Guided practice.** 1. Egypt chain in order: predictable Nile
flood → basin irrigation/canals and shaduf control → surplus grain
→ specialists, scribes, and officials → one kingdom held together
by records and government. Most-important choice accepted with a
sequence reason (for example: "the predictable flood mattered most
first, because without it there is no reliable surplus to organize").
2. Mesopotamia change: villages became cities with specialist jobs
and rulers/officials making decisions. Continuity: farming grain
beside rivers remained the basis of food and life. 3. A scribe's
tablet gives the temple/organizer's perspective (amounts received
and owed); missing is the farmer's or worker's perspective on
whether taxes and pay were fair. 4. "Writing caused civilization"
reverses the chain: surplus farming and specialization came first
and made full-time scribes possible. Corrected sentence: "Surplus
and specialization made scribes possible, and writing then let
organizations grow larger and last longer." 5. (a) 3300 − 1754 =
1546 years. (b) 3100 − 2500 = 600 years. (c) 2500 − 1754 = 746
years.

**Independent practice.** 6. Model explanation (Egypt, 10
sentences): "Egypt became a large kingdom because the Nile flooded
predictably, bringing water and silt. Farmers controlled that
water with basin irrigation and later the shaduf. Reliable harvests
produced surplus grain. Surplus fed scribes, builders, soldiers,
and craftspeople who did not farm. Officials measured fields and
collected grain taxes. Scribes recorded taxes, trade, and building
in hieroglyphs. The pharaoh's government tied the long valley
together under one rule. This changed settlement from villages to
cities and decisions from households to kings and officials. But
farming wheat and barley in flood-fed soil stayed the same
throughout. Most surviving records come from kings and temples, so
ordinary farmers' views of taxes and labor are mostly missing."
7. Errors: (a) single cause — rivers alone do not build cities;
canals, surplus, specialists, records, and government were also
needed; (b) equal benefit — surplus control was unequal, and some
people were enslaved or forced to work. 8. Model paragraph: "Both
explanations use the same chain structure: water control, surplus,
specialists, records. They differ in emphasis: the Egypt
explanation stresses the predictable flood and one kingdom's
government, while the Mesopotamia explanation stresses canal
organization under unreliable floods and independent city-states —
because geography shaped different political answers to the same
surplus problem." 9. Salinization model: "Irrigation leaves salts
in the soil over many years, so later harvests fall even though the
farmers who chose irrigation are gone or the fields have new
owners. The cost is paid later, by later farmers, not only by those
who benefited first. Responses include resting fields (fallowing),
draining salty water away, or opening new fields — each with its
own cost."

**Applied task (model, Mesopotamia).** Claim: "Mesopotamia's cities
grew because unreliable rivers forced organized water control,
which produced surplus, specialists, and written rule."
Chain: unpredictable floods → canals/levees organized by
city-states → surplus grain → potters, merchants, scribes →
cuneiform records and Hammurabi's laws. Change: villages became
cities with specialist jobs and kings. Continuity: grain farming
beside rivers stayed the food base. Perspective note: "This story
comes from palace and temple records; farmers' and forced workers'
views are missing — a worker's account or a village record would
help, but few survive." Asterisk-marked revision: any one sentence
improved after peer questioning (for example, adding the
salinization cost after a partner asked, "Did irrigation ever
hurt?").

**Exit check.** (a) Any two causes in order (for example: water
control, then surplus). (b) Change: cities/specialist jobs;
continuity: grain farming. (c) Farmers', builders', and enslaved
workers' perspectives.

## Practice Sets — Solutions

### Set A

A1. Mesopotamia — Tigris and Euphrates; Egypt — Nile; Indus Valley
— Indus; Yellow River valley — Yellow River (Huang He).
A2. Floodplain = flat land beside a river that floods regularly;
levee = a raised bank (natural or built) that holds back river
water. Model sentence: "The village farmed the floodplain and built
a levee to keep ordinary floods off its fields."
A3. The Nile flood came at a fairly regular time each year and was
gentler, so farmers could plan planting and storage; the
Tigris–Euphrates floods were violent and unpredictable in timing
and height.
A4. Any two: canals (carry water to fields), levees/dikes (hold
water back), basin banks (trap water on a field), shaduf (lifts
water to higher fields).
A5. Silt is fine, fertile soil carried by floodwater and left on
fields; it renewed the soil so crops could grow year after year
without exhausting it.
A6. Farthest west: Egypt (Nile). Farthest east: Yellow River
valley. Seas: Egypt — Mediterranean Sea; Mesopotamia — Persian
Gulf (via the rivers' lower course); Indus — Arabian Sea;
Yellow River — Yellow Sea/Bohai Sea. Any two correct pairings earn
credit.
A7. Disagree (model): "With no floods, fields get no fresh silt and
no natural watering, so farmers must irrigate everything by hand
and fertilize the soil themselves; a gentle, predictable flood is
better than no flood at all."
A8. Chain: Nile flood (June–September, water + silt) → basin
irrigation/shaduf control → plant wheat and barley in damp soil →
surplus stored, taxed, or traded.

### Set B

B1. Surplus = extra food beyond growers' immediate needs.
Specialization = different jobs. Surplus feeds non-farmers, so
potters, scribes, and merchants can work full time.
B2. With two failed harvests there is little surplus, so potters
and scribes cannot be paid in grain; they must farm, trade less,
or leave — the city shrinks toward subsistence.
B3. Mesopotamia: temple storehouses collected grain and paid
workers. Egypt: officials measured fields and collected grain
taxes; temples/palaces organized building.
B4. Geography reason: Egypt is one long, narrow Nile valley, easy
to unify; Mesopotamia is a broad plain of separate river cities.
River reason: the Nile's single predictable system favored one
administration, while Mesopotamia's two rivers and many cities
favored independent city-states.
B5. Can conclude: Mohenjo-daro was planned and organized (grid
streets, drains, standard bricks). Must not conclude: that it had
no rulers at all, or what kind of rulers it had — absence of a
palace is not evidence of absence of government.
B6. Tax official → records/rules (collects and records taxes);
second group: serves temple/palace (if collecting for a temple).
Boat crew → moves/sells (carries goods); second group: none
required, but "grows/makes" is not accepted — crews move, not make.
B7. Correction: "In ancient cities, most people still farmed;
specialists were a minority fed by the farmers' surplus."
B8. Rulers, priests, and their officials controlled stored grain.
That mattered because grain was pay, food security, and power:
whoever held the storehouse decided who ate in a bad year and who
worked on building projects.

### Set C

C1. Order: cuneiform begins (about 3300 BCE), Egypt unified (about
3100 BCE), Mohenjo-daro flourishing (about 2500 BCE), Hammurabi's
Code (about 1754 BCE).
C2. (a) 3300 − 3100 = 200 years. (b) 3100 − 1754 = 1346 years.
(c) 2500 − 1754 = 746 years.
C3. Cuneiform: clay, reed stylus. Hieroglyphs: stone (chisel) or
papyrus (reed pen and ink).
C4. Trade: a tablet proves a deal's terms and amounts far from
home; a sealed record travels with goods. Taxation: officials
record field measures and amounts owed, then check amounts
received.
C5. Benefit: temples/palaces and merchants (provable accounts).
Possibly harmed: farmers or debtors whose obligations are now
fixed in writing and hard to dispute, especially if they cannot
read.
C6. Agree in part: writing is durable and checkable. Disagree in
part: records carry their maker's point of view (a temple's tax
list is not a farmer's story), and spoken accounts can preserve
views writing leaves out.
C7. Cannot learn from Indus writing: rulers' names and decrees,
laws, religious texts. City remains can still show: planning and
sanitation (streets, drains), trade and standards (seals, weights),
and public water use (Great Bath).
C8. Any three entries balancing, for example: received 120, paid
75, balance 45 (120 − 75 = 45).

### Set D

D1. Chain in order: canals and levees built → surplus grain stored
→ scribes keep records → cities grow. Labels: water control makes
surplus possible; surplus feeds specialists; records let
organizations grow; all together support cities. (Cities also
reinforce the chain — accept a noted feedback arrow.)
D2. Egypt change: villages became cities with kings, officials,
and specialists. Continuity: wheat and barley farming in
flood-fed soil.
D3. The flood mattered first in sequence: predictable water made
reliable surplus possible, and surplus made officials and
government affordable; government then organized water and taxes
in return. (The reverse argument — government made irrigation
work — is acceptable if tied to Mesopotamia's larger canal
systems, but for Egypt the flood-first sequence is the expected
answer.)
D4. Reasons not to take it whole: (1) inscriptions praise the king
and may exaggerate or omit failures; (2) they do not record
workers' experience, costs, or whether the canal worked well.
D5. Correction: "Irrigation also hurt over time: salts built up in
irrigated soil (salinization) and reduced harvests, so farmers had
to rest fields, drain land, or open new fields."
D6. Cause = something that helps bring about a development (for
example, canal building causing surplus). Continuity = something
staying the same while other things change (for example, grain
farming continuing after cities grew).
D7. Similarity: both built cities on river surplus and specialist
jobs. Differences: (1) Egypt tended to one kingdom, Mesopotamia to
city-states; (2) Nile floods were fairly predictable, while
Tigris–Euphrates floods were violent and unpredictable.
D8. Any four-sentence explanation containing a cause, a change, a
continuity, and a perspective note (see §L5 model).

## §Project — Exemplar (Mesopotamia dossier, 20/20)

1. **Map page:** Schematic map labels the Tigris and Euphrates in
   Mesopotamia ("land between the rivers"), notes violent,
   unpredictable floods, and states "classroom schematic — not to
   scale."
2. **Chain page:** Flood (unpredictable) → canals and levees built
   and maintained by organized labor → fields watered → barley
   surplus stored in temple storehouses. Flood risk: a high flood
   breaks dikes and drowns fields; a low flood leaves too little
   water.
3. **Jobs board:** Farmer, potter, weaver, metalworker, merchant,
   boat crew, scribe, tax official, priest, soldier (10 jobs;
   groups: grows/makes, moves/sells, records/rules, serves

   potter → merchant (jars carried to market); merchant → scribe
   (sale recorded). Honest note: "Farmers and builders do the

   90, paid 55, balance 35 (90 − 55 = 35), labeled "classroom model."
   Law analysis (Law 53): reveals canal upkeep as a legal duty and
   neighbors' dependence on each other's dikes; limit: the law
   states intent, not whether fines were collected or poor farmers

5. **Rise explanation:** As in §L5 applied-task model, with claim,
   three-cause chain, labeled change and continuity, perspective

earns full marks on the same rubric if it meets each row's
