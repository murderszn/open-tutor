# Lesson 02 — Cities, Specialists, and Belief

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can explain how surplus food
made specialist jobs, cities, and organized temples and palaces
possible, using two river-valley civilizations as evidence.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, your inquiry
notebook, the city-jobs cards from Session 7 (prepared by the adult).

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** In one sentence, what is a surplus?
If you cannot answer, re-read Lesson 01's "A river is a bargain"
section first.

## Surplus changes who works at what

In a farming village where everyone grows food, there is little time
for anything else. A surplus changes the arithmetic. If ten farming
families grow enough food for twelve families, two families' worth of
food can feed people who do other work all year: a potter who makes
storage jars, a weaver who makes cloth, a metalworker who makes
tools, a merchant who carries goods to other towns, a scribe who
keeps records, a priest who runs temple rituals, a soldier who
guards the city, and a ruler's officials who organize canals, taxes,
and building projects. That division of work is specialization, and
it is the engine of the city. Specialists depend on farmers, farmers
depend on specialists' pots, tools, and records, and everyone depends
on the organizers who keep water, grain, and trade moving.

Cities also concentrate power. Grain stored in a temple or palace
storehouse can feed workers, pay soldiers, or be traded — but it can
also be kept from people who need it. Social order in river-valley
cities was steep: at the top, rulers and high priests; below them,
officials and scribes; then merchants and artisans; then the great
majority, farmers; and at the bottom, people who were enslaved or
forced to work — people whose labor was taken, not chosen. Naming
that plainly matters: a city's beautiful temple and its forced labor
belong in the same honest picture. Temples and palaces were not just
religious or royal buildings; they were organizations that collected
grain, paid workers, organized building, and kept records. In
Mesopotamia the stepped ziggurat was a temple platform at the heart
of a city, and temple workshops and storehouses helped run the
city's economy. In Egypt the pharaoh — the king — stood at the head
of government, temples, and large building projects, and officials
and scribes measured fields, collected taxes in grain, and recorded
what came in and went out.

## Worked example A — Follow one pot of grain

Follow a harvest through three specialist jobs in Mesopotamia.

1. **Farmer** grows barley beside a canal and delivers part of the
   harvest as tax to the temple storehouse.
2. **Potter** (paid in grain) makes sealed storage jars; the jars
   keep grain dry and measurable through the year.
3. **Scribe** presses cuneiform marks into a clay tablet: how much
   grain arrived, from which village, and what was paid out to
   workers building a canal.

Notice what each step adds. The farmer produces; the potter
preserves; the scribe remembers. Without the scribe's record, the
temple cannot prove what it received or owes. Without the potter's
jars, the grain spoils. Without the farmer's surplus, neither the
potter nor the scribe eats. A good explanation of specialization
names the chain and the dependency, not just a list of jobs.

## Worked example B — The same pattern in Egypt, with differences

In Egypt, follow a harvest through a different chain:

1. **Farmer** plants wheat and barley after the Nile flood falls.
2. **Official/scribe** measures the field after the flood (boundary
   markers wash away, so measuring matters) and records the tax due
   in grain.
3. **Merchant/boat crew** loads surplus grain onto a Nile boat and
   carries it to a city market or a temple storehouse, returning with
   stone, timber, or craft goods Egypt lacked.

The pattern — surplus, specialists, organization — matches
Mesopotamia. The differences matter too: Egypt's single great river
and long, narrow valley helped one kingdom hold together, while
Mesopotamia's separate river cities often stayed independent
city-states that traded and fought with each other. Same engine,
different political shape. A strong comparison says what is shared
*and* what differs.

## Guided practice

1. Define *surplus* and *specialization* in your own words, then give
   one example of each from this lesson.
2. Trace a woven cloth (not grain) through three specialist jobs in a
   river-valley city: who grows or supplies the raw material, who
   makes it, and who moves or records it? Name the job at each step.
3. Explain two jobs temples or palaces did besides worship or royal
   ceremony. Use one Mesopotamian and one Egyptian example.
4. Why did cities need scribes? Give two reasons, each tied to a
   concrete task (tax, trade, building, or law).
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** A classmate says, "Specialists meant
   everyone had an easier life." Agree in part and disagree in part,
   using evidence about who did extra work and who controlled stored
   grain.

## Independent practice

6. Compare Mesopotamia and Egypt in four sentences: one shared
   feature of their cities, one difference in political shape
   (city-states vs. one kingdom), and one reason for that difference
   linked to geography.
7. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Temples were only places
   to pray, so they had nothing to do with the economy." Identify
   the error and correct it with two temple economic jobs.
8. Sort these jobs into "grows/makes," "moves/sells,"
   "records/rules," or "serves temple/palace" — and note any job that
   could fit two groups, with a reason: farmer, potter, merchant,
   scribe, priest, soldier, tax official, weaver.
9. Mohenjo-daro (Indus Valley, about 2500 BCE) had grid streets,
   covered drains, and a Great Bath, but no giant palace or royal
   tomb has been found there. In two or three sentences, explain why
   historians are careful about claiming what kind of rulers
   Mohenjo-daro had. (Hint: what evidence is missing?)

## Applied task

Design a "city jobs board" for an imagined river-valley city of about
5,000 people (this is a classroom model, not a real city). Your board
must list at least eight jobs across at least three groups from
Question 8, show with arrows how food or goods flow between three of
the jobs, and include one honest note: who does the hardest physical
work, and who decides how stored grain is used? Write one paragraph
explaining why your city needs record-keepers even though they grow
no food.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) What does surplus make possible?
(b) Name three specialist jobs and what each one does.
(c) Name one economic job of a temple or palace.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** Job names are paired with a one-line job
  description on the Session 7 cards; the adult reads any card aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** The jobs board may be drawn, built
  with cut-out cards and arrows, or dictated; the assessed skill is
  the job-flow reasoning, not handwriting or drawing quality.
- **Vocabulary support:** Keep the unit vocabulary list open; new
  words (*artisan*, *merchant*, *scribe*) are defined where they
  first appear.

## Extension

Compare a modern city job you know (for example, a warehouse
inventory clerk) with an ancient scribe: what is similar about
recording what comes in and goes out, and what is different about
tools and training? Write four sentences. Do not assume the ancient
scribe's life was easier or harder — argue from the tasks.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: "everyone in a city stopped farming" (most
people still farmed); "temples were only religious" (they were also
economic organizations); "specialization helped everyone equally"
(surplus control created inequality and forced labor). Solutions are
in [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), §L2. Handle forced labor
plainly and briefly; see [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).
