# Lesson 03 — Writing That Keeps Records

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can explain writing as an
innovation: what cuneiform and hieroglyphs recorded, who could write,
and how writing changed trade, taxation, law, and memory.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson,
[river-valley-timeline.svg](../assets/river-valley-timeline.svg),
a lump of modeling clay and a blunt stylus (a thick pencil or craft
stick) if available, your inquiry notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Name two things a city must
remember from one year to the next. (If you named taxes, grain
stores, trade deals, or laws, you already see the problem writing
solves.)

## Why cities needed writing

Memory works for a village. It fails for a city. A temple that
receives grain from fifty villages, pays hundreds of workers, trades
with distant cities, and settles disputes needs records that outlast
one person's memory and travel farther than one person's voice.
Writing began as that tool — not as stories first, but as lists:
how much grain, from whom, owed to whom. Over time the same tool
recorded laws, religious texts, letters, and literature, including
the Epic of Gilgamesh in Mesopotamia.

**Cuneiform** began in southern Mesopotamia around 3300 BCE.
Early signs were pictures drawn on clay; gradually they became
wedge-shaped marks pressed into soft clay with a reed stylus — the
word *cuneiform* comes from the Latin word for "wedge." The script
was later adapted to write at least fifteen different languages.
**Hieroglyphs** were the Egyptian picture-sign system, carved on
stone and written in ink on papyrus, the paper-like material made
from a Nile reed plant. Egyptian scribes also used a faster everyday
handwriting for daily records. In both civilizations, learning to
write took years of school, so scribes were few, respected, and
powerful: they worked for temples, palaces, and merchants, and
ordinary farmers usually could not read what was written about
them. That is both writing's power and its limit — it made big
organizations possible, and it put the record in few hands.

Writing changed four things. **Trade:** a merchant's tablet or
sealed record could prove a deal made far from home. **Taxation:**
officials could measure fields, record what was owed, and check what
arrived. **Law:** rules written down could be consulted and quoted
instead of remembered differently by each judge — the subject of
Lesson 04. **Memory:** stories, rituals, and knowledge could survive
their authors by centuries. Writing did not make societies fair;
it made them larger, more organized, and more checkable — for those
who could read.

## Worked example A — A scribe's tablet, decoded

Imagine a clay tablet (a classroom model, not a real artifact) with
three entries:

1. "Village of the North Canal: 60 measures of barley received."
2. "Paid to canal workers: 40 measures."
3. "Still owed to the temple storehouse: 20 measures."

*Reasoning:* Entry 1 is income, entry 2 is spending, entry 3 is the
balance — the same three ideas in a modern account book. A reader
centuries later can check whether the temple was paid, whether
workers were paid, and whether the numbers add up (60 − 40 = 20).
*Inference:* Because the tablet survives, the temple's side of the
story survives. The canal workers left no tablet of their own, so
their view — whether 40 measures felt fair — is missing. Records
are evidence, and every record has a point of view.

## Worked example B — Two writings, two materials, one job

Compare cuneiform and hieroglyphs as tools, not as art:

| Question | Cuneiform (Mesopotamia) | Hieroglyphs (Egypt) |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Soft clay, dried or baked | Stone, and papyrus reed sheets |
| Tool | Reed stylus, pressed marks | Reed pen and ink, or chisel |
| Early use | Lists, accounts, trade records | Royal and temple inscriptions; daily records in a faster hand |
| Who wrote | Trained scribes for temples, palaces, merchants | Trained scribes for pharaoh, temples, officials |

*Modeled conclusion:* The materials differ, but the job is the same:
make agreements, taxes, and decisions durable and checkable. When a
question asks you to compare two innovations, compare their *jobs*
first and their looks second.

## Guided practice

1. Explain why a city of 5,000 people needs writing but a village of
   50 might manage without it. Use the words *record*, *tax*, and
   *trade* in your answer.
2. Using Worked example A, compute the balance yourself and explain
   what each of the three entries tells a historian — and what none
   of the three entries can tell us about the canal workers' view.
3. Name two things cuneiform and hieroglyphs have in common as tools
   and one difference in material or use.
4. Why did scribes have power even though they were not rulers?
   Give two reasons tied to what only they could do.
5. Place these events in order on the timeline (use the SVG):
   cuneiform begins (about 3300 BCE), Egypt unified (about
   3100 BCE), Mohenjo-daro flourishing (about 2500 BCE),
   Hammurabi's Code (about 1754 BCE). Then compute: how many years
   passed between cuneiform's beginning and Hammurabi's Code?

## Independent practice

6. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Once people invented
   writing, everyone could read the laws." Identify two errors and
   correct them.
7. A merchant sends grain by boat to a distant city. Explain two
   ways a written record helps this trade and one way it does not
   remove risk (think: floods, theft, dishonest measurers).
8. The Indus script has never been deciphered. Explain in three
   sentences why that limits what historians can claim about Indus
   Valley rulers, religion, and trade — and what other evidence
   (city plans, drains, seals, weights) can still tell us.
9. Try it (adult-supervised): press three wedge-shaped marks into
   clay or draw them, then write what each mark stands for in your
   notebook (for example: grain, water, house — your own classroom
   code). In two sentences, explain one advantage and one difficulty
   of a picture-sign system compared with an alphabet.

## Applied task

You are a temple scribe for one day (a classroom role, not a real
job). Write a short tablet record (5–8 lines) for a grain delivery:
the village name (invented, labeled as invented), the amount
received, the amount paid to workers, and the balance owed or stored.
Then write a two-sentence note from the scribe's point of view
explaining why this record matters — and a one-sentence note naming
whose point of view is missing from your tablet. Label the whole
piece "classroom model" at the top.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) Name the two writing systems and where
each was used. (b) Name two jobs writing made possible.
(c) In one sentence, why did writing give scribes power?

## Supports

- **Reading access:** The timeline SVG labels every event in text;
  the adult reads the comparison table aloud on request.
- **Alternate response mode:** The clay activity may be replaced by
  drawing wedge marks on paper; the applied task may be dictated.
- **Motor support:** If pressing clay is difficult, the learner
  directs the adult's hand and narrates each mark.

## Extension

Find out how scholars deciphered hieroglyphs (the Rosetta Stone,
with the same text in hieroglyphs, everyday Egyptian script, and
Greek). Write three sentences: why did having the same text in a
known language unlock the unknown one? The adult previews any
source used.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: "writing started with stories" (it started
with lists and accounts); "everyone could read" (scribes were few
and training took years); "a written record is automatically true"
(records carry their maker's point of view). Solutions are in
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), §L3. The tablet in Worked
example A and the applied task are classroom models — label them
as such and never present them as real artifacts.
