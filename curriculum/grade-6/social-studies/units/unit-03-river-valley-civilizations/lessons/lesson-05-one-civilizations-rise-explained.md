# Lesson 05 — One Civilization's Rise, Explained

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can build a sequenced,
multi-cause explanation of one civilization's rise, classify its
developments as change and/or continuity, and note how a scribe's
or ruler's perspective shaped what survives.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson,
[river-valley-timeline.svg](../assets/river-valley-timeline.svg),
your inquiry notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Write the unit chain from memory:
river water → ______ → ______ → ______ → ______. If you can fill
at least three blanks, you are ready; if not, re-read Lesson 01.

## No single cause ever explains a civilization

It is tempting to write, "The Nile made Egypt." It is also wrong —
or rather, incomplete. A river alone does not build a city. People
build canals, organize labor, store grain, keep records, and enforce
rules — and those choices interact. A strong historical explanation
does three things: it names **multiple causes**, it puts them in a
**sequence** (what made what possible, in what order), and it weighs
them (which cause mattered most, and why). It also separates
**change** (what became different) from **continuity** (what stayed
the same), because most big developments are both at once: farming
continued, but where people lived, what jobs they did, and who made
decisions all changed.

A final caution comes from Unit 01's source work: the surviving
record leans toward rulers, priests, and scribes, because they were
the people who commissioned carvings, kept tablets, and built
temples that lasted. Farmers, builders, and enslaved workers rarely
wrote their own accounts. When we explain a civilization's rise from
palace and temple sources, we are largely hearing the organizers'
story. A careful explanation says so: "According to temple and
palace records …" and "What farmers thought about the taxes is
mostly missing from the record."

## Worked example A — Egypt's rise, in sequence

**Claim:** Egypt became a large, organized kingdom because the
Nile's predictable flood, organized water control, surplus grain,
and record-keeping officials worked together — no one of them alone
is enough.

1. **Cause 1 — predictable water:** The Nile's annual flood
   brought water and silt on a fairly regular calendar, so farmers
   could plan planting with unusual confidence.
2. **Cause 2 — organized control:** Basin irrigation, canals, and
   later the shaduf turned floodwater into farmland beyond the
   river's edge, and officials measured fields and collected grain
   taxes after floods washed away boundary markers.
3. **Cause 3 — surplus and specialists:** Stored grain fed scribes,
   builders, soldiers, and craftspeople who did not farm, so
   temples, cities, and building projects could grow.
4. **Cause 4 — writing and rule:** Hieroglyphs and scribal records
   let one kingdom track taxes, trade, and building across a long
   valley, and the pharaoh's government tied the valley together.

*Change:* settlement grew from villages to cities; jobs split into
specialties; decisions moved toward kings and officials.
*Continuity:* farming itself — planting wheat and barley in
flood-fed soil — stayed the backbone of life throughout.
*Perspective note:* This story comes mostly from royal and temple
sources; the daily experience of the farmers who paid the grain tax
is barely recorded. The explanation is strong about organization
and weak about ordinary people's views — and it should say so.

## Worked example B — Weighing causes: which mattered most?

Two learners argue about Mesopotamia. One says, "Canals mattered
most — without them, no surplus." The other says, "Writing
mattered most — without it, no large organization." Both name real
causes. Here is how to weigh them:

- **Test 1 — remove it:** Without canals, Mesopotamia's unreliable
  floods make large harvests unlikely, so cities shrink. Without
  writing, cities could still exist for a time, but tracking taxes
  and trade across many city-states becomes much harder.
- **Test 2 — order:** Canals come first in the chain: water control
  makes surplus possible; surplus makes specialists possible;
  specialists include scribes. Writing therefore *depends* on the
  earlier steps.
- **Judgment:** Canals and water control mattered most *first*,
  because they made the rest possible; writing mattered most for
  *scale*, because it let organizations grow large and last. A
  good answer can rank causes differently for different questions —
  as long as it gives a reason tied to sequence and evidence.

## Guided practice

1. Write the Egypt chain from Worked example A in your own words,
   in order, using all four causes. Then underline the cause you
   judge most important and give one reason.
2. For Mesopotamia, name one change and one continuity between a
   farming village and a Sumerian city, and explain each in one
   sentence.
3. A scribe's tablet records grain taxes paid to a temple. Explain
   in two sentences what perspective this source gives us and whose
   perspective is missing.
4. **Explain-your-reasoning:** A classmate writes, "Writing caused
   civilization." Explain why that sentence reverses the chain, and
   rewrite it correctly in one sentence.
5. Compute elapsed time (show work; remember there is no year 0,
   but for two BCE dates simply subtract): (a) about 3300 BCE to
   about 1754 BCE; (b) about 3100 BCE to about 2500 BCE; (c) about
   2500 BCE to about 1754 BCE.

## Independent practice

6. Choose Mesopotamia or Egypt. Write a sequenced, multi-cause
   explanation (about 10–12 sentences) of its rise with at least
   three causes in order, one change, one continuity, and one
   sentence noting whose perspective is missing from the sources.
7. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The river made cities,
   and everyone benefited equally." Identify two errors and correct
   each with evidence from this unit.
8. Compare the two worked examples: in one paragraph, state one way
   the Egypt and Mesopotamia explanations are similar (chain
   structure) and one way they differ (which cause is stressed and
   why).
9. Salinization: over many years of irrigation, salt can build up
   in fields and reduce harvests. Explain in three sentences how
   this is an *external cost* of irrigation — a cost paid later,
   partly by people other than those who chose the irrigation —
   and name one response farmers or rulers could try.

## Applied task

Write a one-page "rise explanation" for the civilization you will
feature in your City Dossier (project): a claim sentence, a
sequenced chain of at least three causes, one labeled change and
one labeled continuity, and a perspective note naming one group
whose view is missing and what source kind might supply it. Trade
explanations with a partner (or your adult) and revise one sentence
based on their question. Keep the revised sentence marked with an
asterisk so the adult can see the revision.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) Name two causes of a civilization's rise
and put them in order. (b) Give one example of change and one of
continuity from village to city. (c) Whose perspective is most
likely missing from palace and temple records?

## Supports

- **Reading access:** Sentence starters are available: "First …,
  which made it possible to …, and then …"; "This changed …, but
  … stayed the same."
- **Alternate response mode:** The applied task may be presented as
  a labeled chain diagram with a spoken explanation, or dictated.
- **Arithmetic support:** BCE subtraction is modeled in Worked
  example B's companion note in the answer key; the adult checks
  subtraction before the learner moves on.

## Extension

Research question for the adult to approve: historians debate why
some civilizations declined. Choose one river-valley civilization
and find one proposed cause of change or decline (for example,
shifting rivers, salinization, conflict, or trade disruption).
Write three sentences: the proposal, the evidence cited, and why
historians treat it as a proposal rather than a certainty.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: single-cause explanations ("the river did
it"); change-or-continuity thinking (most developments are both);
treating palace sources as the whole story. Solutions are in
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), §L5. BCE arithmetic: for two
BCE dates, subtract directly (no year-0 adjustment); the year-0
adjustment applies only when crossing from BCE to CE.
