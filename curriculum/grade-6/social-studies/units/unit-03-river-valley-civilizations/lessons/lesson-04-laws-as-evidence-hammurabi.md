# Lesson 04 — Laws as Evidence: Hammurabi's Code

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can examine Hammurabi's Code
as historical evidence — its origins, purposes, and impact — and
assess whether writing laws down was a good way to address a public
problem.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, the law-case
cards (prepared by the adult), your inquiry notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** In one sentence, why would a king
want laws written down instead of only spoken? Keep your sentence;
you will test it against the evidence below.

## A king, a stone, and 282 laws

Hammurabi was king of Babylon, in Mesopotamia, from about 1792 to
1750 BCE. Around 1754 BCE his laws were carved in the Akkadian
language, in cuneiform script, onto a tall diorite stone slab called
a stele — about 2.25 meters (7.4 feet) tall. The stele was found at
Susa (in present-day Iran) in 1901 and is now in the Louvre Museum
in Paris. At its top, a carved relief shows Hammurabi receiving
authority from Shamash, the sun god associated with justice. The
Code contains 282 laws covering trade, property, labor, family
matters, injury, and building work. Most laws follow an "if … then …"
pattern: if something happens, then this is the consequence.

The Code's own prologue states its purpose: Hammurabi was called,
in the public-domain L. W. King translation (1910), "to bring about
the rule of righteousness in the land, to destroy the wicked and
the evil-doers; so that the strong should not harm the weak." That
is a *claim about purpose* made by the king himself — strong
evidence about what Hammurabi wanted people to believe, and weaker
evidence about how fairly the laws worked in practice. Historians
always ask who made a source and why before trusting its claims.

Four age-appropriate laws, paraphrased from the King translation
with one brief quotation, show what the Code reveals:

- **Law 53 (canals):** If a person neglects to strengthen a dike and
  a break floods a neighbor's field, that person must pay for the
  grain that was lost. *Reveals:* canal care was a shared duty, and
  neglect had a price — water management was law, not just custom.
- **Law 196 (injury):** "If a man put out the eye of another man,
  his eye shall be put out." *Reveals:* the retaliation principle —
  punishment matching the injury — and a society that wrote down
  exact consequences instead of leaving revenge open-ended.
- **Law 197 (injury):** If a person breaks another person's bone,
  that person's bone shall be broken. *Reveals:* the same principle
  applied to a lesser injury, in the same fixed form.
- **Law 229 (building):** If a builder builds a house badly and it
  falls and kills the owner, the builder faces death. *Reveals:*
  builders were held responsible for their work — an early form of
  liability — though the punishment was severe by modern standards.

Two limits matter as much as the laws themselves. First,
punishments often depended on social rank: the same injury could
bring different consequences depending on whether the people
involved were free, common, or enslaved. The Code was not equal
justice in the modern sense. Second, most Babylonians could not
read. A carved stele in a public place symbolized that laws existed
and were fixed, but ordinary people learned the laws mostly by
hearing them, not by reading them. A written code is evidence of
organization and intent — not proof that everyone was treated
fairly.

## Worked example A — What one law reveals

Take Law 53 (canals). Step by step:

1. **What it says:** Neglect a dike, flood a field, pay for the
   lost grain.
2. **What it reveals about the society:** Canals mattered so much
   that their upkeep was written into law; farming neighbors
   depended on each other's dike work; grain was the measure of
   loss.
3. **What it does not reveal:** Whether the fine was actually
   collected, whether poor farmers could pay it, or how often dikes
   broke. A law tells us what rulers *wanted* to happen, not a
   report of what happened every time.
4. **Assessment (D2.Civ.12.6-8):** As a way to address the problem
   of neglected canals, writing the rule down helped in one way —
   everyone could be told the same consequence — and fell short in
   another — it did not repair dikes by itself, and it punished
   after the damage was done.

## Worked example B — Judging a law fairly

A learner says, "Hammurabi's Code was fair because it was written
down." Test that claim against three questions:

1. **Same rule for all?** No — punishments varied by rank, so the
   same injury did not always bring the same consequence.
2. **Could people use it?** Partly — the stele was public and fixed,
   but most people could not read it and needed others to explain it.
3. **Did it solve the problem?** Partly — fixed consequences may
   have discouraged some harm and settled some disputes, but a
   written rule alone cannot prevent floods, dishonesty, or
   accidents.

*Modeled conclusion:* "Written laws were an improvement in
consistency over unwritten custom, but they were not equal justice,
because rank changed punishments and most people could not read
them." A fair judgment names both a strength and a limit, with
evidence for each.

## Guided practice

1. In your own words, state the Code's origin (who, when, where it
   was carved, where it is now) in three sentences or fewer.
2. For Law 196, explain what it reveals about Babylonian society in
   two sentences, and name one thing it does not reveal.
3. Compare Law 53 and Law 229: what problem does each law address,
   and what does each one show about who was held responsible?
4. Explain two reasons a written code can be *more* consistent than
   unwritten custom, and two limits of a written code for people who
   cannot read.
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** Was Hammurabi's Code fair? Give a
   two-sided answer: one strength with evidence, one limit with
   evidence, and a final judgment sentence.

## Independent practice

6. A merchant and a farmer dispute a grain delivery. Using this
   lesson, explain two ways a written code could help settle the
   dispute and one way it could still leave the farmer at a
   disadvantage.
7. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Because the laws were
   carved in stone, everyone in Babylon knew and followed them."
   Identify two errors and correct them.
8. The stele's top carving shows Hammurabi receiving authority from
   Shamash. Explain in two sentences why a king would want that
   picture on a law stone, and what it tells historians about how
   Hammurabi wanted his laws to be seen.
9. Assess Law 53 as a public policy (D2.Civ.12.6-8): state the
   problem it addresses, one benefit of writing it down, one
   drawback, and whether you judge it a good solution overall — with
   a reason.

## Applied task

Law-case cards (adult-prepared): each card gives a short, invented
dispute set in Babylon — a broken dike, a damaged boatload of
grain, a builder's faulty wall, a disputed sale. Choose two cards.
For each, (a) state which Code law from this lesson best fits the
case, (b) explain what the law would decide, (c) name one fact
missing from the card that could change the decision, and
(d) judge whether the law's outcome seems fair, with a reason.
Label your work "classroom case work — the cards are invented;
the laws are paraphrased from the King translation."

## Exit check

Without looking back: (a) About when was the Code made, and how
many laws does it contain? (b) Name one law and what it reveals.
(c) Name one strength and one limit of written laws as evidence.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** The adult reads the four laws aloud; the
  paraphrases are short and the two quotations are marked as
  quotations.
- **Alternate response mode:** Law-case work may be answered orally
  or as a two-column chart (law / judgment); the assessed skill is
  the reasoning, not the length of writing.
- **Sensitive content:** Punishments in the Code were severe,
  including death for some offenses. They are stated plainly here
  without graphic detail; the adult may skip Law 229's consequence
  wording for a learner who finds it distressing and use Law 53
  instead — the assessment skill is unchanged.

## Extension

Compare Hammurabi's Code with a classroom or school rule you know:
what problem does the school rule address, is it written down, who
can read it, and does it apply the same way to everyone? Write five
sentences. Do not claim the school rule and the ancient code are
"the same" — compare their *jobs* as rules.
