**Adult only.** Do not hand this key to the learner. Every solution

All grain prices are **fictional classroom-model numbers**, not real

## §L1 — Lesson 01: Greek city-states and citizenship

**Guided practice**
1. The Aegean Sea; any two of Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Piraeus,
   Delos (also Byzantium, Sicily, Egypt).
2. Mountains made travel slow and hard, so each valley and coastal
   plain grew into its own community; the sea connected them, so
   ships, trade, and colonies linked the separate cities.
3. The farmland fed the city: grain, olives, grapes, and pasture.
   Without it the city could not eat, so the polis had to include
   both the walled city and the land that fed it.
4. Accept any two observations from the image (speaker on a stone
   platform; merchant with a balance scale; clay pots, grain
   baskets, oil jars; columned buildings; temple on a hill; sailing
   ship in the harbor) and one inference (trade and politics shared
   one place; trade was measured and argued over). One thing the
   picture cannot tell: whether the speaker is addressing voters or
   calling out prices; whether the woman and boy could vote; who
   owned the grain.
5. (a) Yes — a 20-year-old Athenian-born man is an adult male
   citizen (after enrollment in his deme). (b) No — she is
   enslaved, so she has no rights at all. (c) No — he is a free
   trader from Egypt living in Athens, a metic; metics pay taxes
   and serve but cannot vote.

**Independent practice**
6. 30,000 ÷ 300,000 = 1/10; 40,000 ÷ 250,000 = 4/25 ≈ 1/6. So
   about one person in ten to about one in six — roughly one in
   eight. It is an estimate because no census survives and scholars
   disagree on the population figures.
7. The Council of 500 (chosen by lot, 50 per tribe) prepared
   proposals and set the agenda; the assembly (all citizens,
   meeting about 40 times a year) debated and voted by show of
   hands. Both were needed so that proposals were prepared by a
   small working body and decided by the whole citizen body —
   preparation and decision were separate jobs.
8. Error 1: not everyone in Athens voted — only adult male
   citizens (about one person in eight). Error 2: women, enslaved
   people, and metics lived in Athens and had no vote at all.
9. Fairer: a random draw cannot be bought and gives poor men the
   same chance as rich men; it stops one family from keeping power.
   Worse: a draw can pick someone with no skill or knowledge for a
   hard job (which is why Athens elected its generals).

**Applied task (sample):** A letter from a metic oil merchant that
names who he is (ten years in Athens, pays taxes, son serves in the
army, sells oil in the agora), explains the tax's effect on his
stall, names the rule that keeps him from voting (only Athenian-born
adult men are citizens), and asks politely but firmly to speak at
one meeting. Full marks need all four elements and the "classroom
model" label.

**Exit check:** (a) A polis is a city plus its surrounding farmland,
ruling itself with its own government and laws. (b) Women, enslaved
people, and metics. (c) The Council of 500 set the agenda.

## §L2 — Lesson 02: Athens, Sparta, and systems of government

**Guided practice**
1. Solon, about 594 BCE; Cleisthenes, about 508 BCE; Pericles,
   about 461–429 BCE.
2. Ostracism was a yearly vote to exile a citizen judged too
   powerful for 10 years; citizens wrote the name on a potsherd
   (*ostrakon*). Athens used it to stop one man from becoming a
   tyrant before he seized power.
3. Two kings (shared power, led the army, religious duties); the
   gerousia (28 elders over 60 plus the two kings, proposed laws,
   judged serious cases); the assembly (male citizens, voted yes or
   no but could not debate or propose); the five ephors (elected
   yearly, watched the kings, could fine or arrest a king).
4. Choosing by lot stopped rich families from keeping power and
   made bribery pointless — you cannot buy an office a random draw
   gives away. One problem: a draw can pick someone unskilled for
   a hard job.
5. The Athenian assembly had more real power: it could debate and
   vote on anything, while the Spartan assembly could only vote yes
   or no on proposals written by others. (Either answer earns marks
   if defended with that difference.)

**Independent practice**
6. (a) 594 − 508 = 86 years. (b) 431 − 404 = 27 years. (c) 594 −
   322 = 272 years.
7. Widened: pay for jurors (and later council members) so poor
   citizens could afford to serve; or the expansion of participation
   under his leadership. Narrowed: the citizenship law of 451 BCE
   required both parents to be Athenian, so fewer people counted as
   citizens.
8. Error 1: Sparta was an oligarchy, not a democracy — rule by a
   few, built around its army. Error 2: its assembly could only vote
   yes or no; it could not debate or propose, so voting did not mean
   ruling.
9. Chart, one honest detail per cell. Example: Who decides —
   Athens: all citizens in the assembly; Sparta: two kings, elders,
   and ephors. Who is left out — Athens: women, enslaved people,
   metics; Sparta: helots, women, non-citizens (and most Spartans
   from real debate). Good at — Athens: debate, new ideas, broad
   participation; Sparta: discipline, stability, military strength.

**Applied task (sample):** A speech for triremes: the Persians may
return; ships protect the grain route; without ships the temples
are undefended anyway; the objection (temples honor the gods and
the dead) answered by "a city that cannot defend itself cannot
keep its temples." Or for temples: the gods protected Athens at
Salamis; rebuilding honors the dead and the city; the objection
(the Persians may return) answered by "walls and the existing fleet
already guard the harbor." Full marks need a choice, two reasons,
one named disagreeing group with its reason, and an answer to the
objection.

**Exit check:** (a) Cleisthenes, about 508 BCE. (b) 10 years.
(c) Any one: two kings (led the army); gerousia (proposed laws);
ephors (watched the kings); assembly (voted yes or no).

## §L3 — Lesson 03: Mediterranean exchange

**Guided practice**
1. Grain crossed the Aegean Sea, passed through the Hellespont and
   the Bosporus, and arrived at Piraeus, Athens' harbor.
2. Imports: grain (food), timber (ships and buildings), metals
   (tools, weapons, coins). Exports: olive oil and wine (made from
   crops that grow on rocky soil), pottery, silver (from Laurium).
   The land grew olives and grapes well but not enough grain.
3. The Long Walls joined Athens to its harbor at Piraeus, making
   city and harbor one fortified unit. In war they mattered most
   because grain could keep arriving by sea even when an enemy army
   held the land around Athens.
4. The League began as a defensive alliance with its treasury on
   Delos. After 454 BCE the treasury sat in Athens, and Athens used
   the allies' money for its own ships, walls, and temples — the
   alliance became an Athenian empire.
5. The price rises: fewer grain ships mean less supply while the
   same number of people need bread, so buyers compete and sellers
   raise the price.

**Independent practice**
6. (a) The war year, at 14 drachmas — the fewest ships (30) meant
   the least supply. (b) Below 5 drachmas: 120 ships is more supply
   than the good-harvest year's 100 ships at 5 drachmas, so with
   the same demand the price would fall.
7. A trireme carried no cargo, but it protected trade: it cleared
   pirates from the sea lanes, and it enforced Athenian power over
   the League and the grain route, so merchant ships could sail
   safely.
8. Error 1: Athens *imported* grain — it did not have enough of
   its own. Error 2: Athens *sold* olive oil, wine, pottery, and
   silver; grain was what it bought, not what it sold.
9. Example: the Black Sea farmer gains a buyer and silver for his
   grain, but risks a bad harvest or a war closing the route; the
   Athenian potter gains a market and silver for his pots, but risks
   his ship being lost at sea or his pots not selling. Any gainer
   and loser with a reason earns marks.

**Applied task (sample):** For the cap: poor families cannot afford
bread at double the price, so a cap keeps the city fed; the cost is
that merchants earn less and may stop bringing grain, so the city
must watch the supply. Against the cap: a cap below the market
price makes merchants sell elsewhere, so less grain arrives and the
shortage gets worse; the cost is that poor families pay more now.
Full marks need a side, a supply-and-demand reason, and both a
gainer and a loser named.

**Exit check:** (a) Import: grain (or timber, metals). Export:
olive oil (or wine, pottery, silver). (b) Tribute — money or
ships. (c) The price rises.

## §L4 — Lesson 04: How ideas traveled

**Guided practice**
1. Ships (alphabet, coins, building styles, stories); colonies
   (language, gods, laws, building styles — and learning from
   neighbors); roads and gathering places (festivals, games,
   markets — people meeting and carrying ideas home).
2. The Phoenician alphabet had signs for consonants only; the
   Greeks added letters for vowels, so the alphabet could spell any
   Greek word — which made writing useful for laws, poems, and
   records, not just merchants' lists.
3. A route east to west: a Phoenician port (for example Tyre) →
   Cyprus or Crete → a Greek port → a Greek colony in Sicily (for
   example Syracuse). Any two sensible stopping places earn marks.
4. The games brought athletes and crowds from all over the Greek
   world to one place every four years, and they shared a calendar
   (years counted by Olympiads) — so people met, talked, and
   carried ideas home even when their cities were at war.
5. (1) Yes — it changed how people lived: allies paid tribute,
   Athens built ships and temples, and the League's navy kept the
   seas safer. (2) Yes — its effects lasted: it built the Athenian
   empire, shaped the Peloponnesian War, and left a model of
   alliance-turned-empire historians still study.

**Independent practice**
6. A stamped coin is easier to trade with because (1) the stamp
   guarantees its weight and purity, so a merchant trusts it without
   weighing and testing it; (2) everyone accepts it, so it can be
   spent again anywhere — a lump of silver must be weighed and
   argued over every time.
7. Buildings show what a city spent money on, not how it was
   governed or how strong its army was: Sparta's plain buildings
   hid a feared army, and Athens' grand temples were paid for with
   allies' tribute, not with Athenian strength alone. A city can
   look powerful and be weak, or look plain and be strong.
8. Error 1: most Greek ideas spread through trade, colonies, and
   festivals, not conquest — Athens never conquered the whole Greek
   world. Error 2: diffusion ran both ways — colonies learned from
   their neighbors (Etruscans, Scythians), and the spread was slow
   copying over centuries, not forced copying by an army.
9. Example (alphabet): it started with Phoenician traders, whose
   alphabet had signs for consonant sounds; Greek traders borrowed
   it and added vowels, so it could spell any Greek word.

**Applied task (sample):** A case for Cleisthenes: (1) he changed
how people lived — ordinary citizens could serve on the Council of
500 and vote in the assembly, and the old rich families lost their
grip; (2) his effects lasted — the democracy ran nearly two
centuries and gave later peoples the word and the idea of
democracy. Limit: it did not change life for women, enslaved
people, or metics, who still had no vote. Judgment: significant.

**Exit check:** (a) Ships, colonies, or roads/gathering places.
(b) Letters for vowels. (c) Did it change how people lived? Did
its effects last?

## §L5 — Lesson 05: Weighing the legacy

**Guided practice**
1. Sentence 1: Athens invented rule by the people — citizens
   voting on laws and wars, juries, office by lot. Sentence 2:
   Athens kept it narrow — women, enslaved people, and metics had
   no vote, and allies paid for it.
2. Gainer: Athens — tribute paid for ships, walls, and temples.
   Loser: the allies — they paid and lost independence; when one
   tried to leave, Athens used force. The same payment is Athens'
   income and the ally's loss.
3. Lifeline: grain from the Black Sea, Egypt, and Sicily fed the
   city. Weapon: cutting off a rival's grain route could starve it
   into surrender — the same ships that fed Athens could blockade
   another city.
4. Judgment: "Athens' assembly was its best institution."
   Evidence: every citizen could speak and vote, so laws were
   decided by the people they governed. Limit: only adult male
   citizens could take part, and the assembly could be swayed by a
   single powerful speech.
5. Two reasons: enslaved people, women, and allies left almost no
   written records of their own; the records that survive were
   written by Athenian men, who had every reason to praise their
   own system.

**Independent practice**
6. Example: Sparta was better at stability — its system lasted
   without the faction fights that tore Athens apart, and its army
   was feared. It was worse at open debate — its assembly could not
   debate or propose, and its citizens produced little art, trade,
   or new thinking. Either pairing earns marks with evidence.
7. An Athenian might call the Parthenon a triumph: it honored
   Athena, showed the city's greatness, and was voted for by the
   citizens. An ally might call it a theft: it was built with the
   tribute allies paid for defense, not for Athenian temples. Both
   can be right because the building is both a temple and a use of
   other people's money.
8. Error 1: Athenian democracy was not perfect — it excluded most
   of the people who lived there. Error 2: not everyone admired
   it — Sparta chose the opposite system, allies resented the
   empire, and philosophers like Plato criticized rule by the
   people.
9. Worth keeping: ordinary people voting on the laws that govern
   them — it gives people a share in rule. Leave behind: limiting
   the vote to one group — it makes "rule by the people" a slogan
   for the few. Any pair with a reason earns marks.

**Applied task (sample):** "Yes, but." Yes, because Athens was
where ordinary citizens first voted on laws themselves, and the
word and idea come from there. But the label leaves out women,
enslaved people, and metics — most of the people who lived there.
A fairer label would be "the birthplace of democracy for some" —
or "the first democracy, and an unfinished one."

**Exit check:** (a) Gainer: Athenian citizens (a voice, a jury, a
share of rule). Loser: enslaved people (forced labor, no voice),
women, metics, or allies. (b) Judgment, evidence, and a limit.
(c) The record comes mostly from Athenian men, who praised their
own system; women, enslaved people, and allies left almost no
written record.

## Project exemplar — "The Grain Vote" (20 points)

A full-marks dossier contains all five parts, each citing its
evidence and labeling invented elements "classroom model":

1. **Map page:** Athens, Piraeus, Delos, and the Black Sea grain
   route marked and labeled on the schematic map; two sentences
   explaining that Attica's rocky soil could not grow enough grain,
   so Athens bought grain from the Black Sea, Egypt, and Sicily;
   the "classroom schematic, not to scale" note.
2. **Deliberation record:** the proposal (ten more triremes vs. a
   new storehouse at Piraeus); two speeches for and two against,
   each labeled "classroom model"; a vote count (for example 14 for
   triremes, 9 for the storehouse, classroom model); and the
   procedure note — the council narrowed the choice to two options,
   so the assembly could not vote for a third option it preferred.
3. **Trade ledger:** cargo out — olive oil in amphorae, Piraeus →
   Black Sea, barrier: storms or pirates; cargo in — grain, Black
   Sea → Piraeus through the Hellespont and Bosporus, barrier: a
   toll or a war closing the straits. Good-year price 4 drachmas
   (120 ships) and bad-year price 9 drachmas (50 ships), explained:
   less supply with the same demand raises the price.
4. **Who gained / who lost:** trade — grain merchants gained
   profits, poor buyers lost when prices rose; League — Athens
   gained ships and temples, allies lost independence and money.
   Silent group: enslaved people (or women, or allies) — "the
   record does not say" what they thought.
5. **Legacy judgment:** "yes, but" — Athens was where ordinary
   citizens first voted on laws themselves, with evidence (assembly
   votes, lotteries, juries); but the label leaves out women,
   enslaved people, and metics; closing sentence: the label should
   mean "the first democracy, and an unfinished one."
