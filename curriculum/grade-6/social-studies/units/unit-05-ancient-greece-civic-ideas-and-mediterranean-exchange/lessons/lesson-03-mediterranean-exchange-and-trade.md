# Lesson 03 — Mediterranean Exchange: Goods, Ships, and Markets

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can explain what Athens
imported and exported, how ships, harbors, colonies, and the Delian
League moved goods, and how supply and demand changed the price of
grain.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson,
[aegean-trade-map.svg](../assets/aegean-trade-map.svg), a pencil,
your inquiry notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Name one thing Athens could make
well from its rocky soil (olives, grapes) and one thing it could not
grow enough of (grain). If you can name both, you are ready; if
not, re-read the "Why Athens had to trade" section below first.

## Why Athens had to trade

Attica — the land around Athens — is rocky and dry. It grows olives
and grapes well, but it cannot grow enough grain to feed a big city.
By the 400s BCE, Athens was a large city, and much of its grain had
to be bought from abroad. Scholars debate exactly how large that
share was — one careful study argues that even in the 300s BCE
Athens never had to find food for more than half its people from
imports — but all agree the imports mattered, and by the mid-400s
BCE the grain route shaped Athenian policy. At least half of the
grain Athens imported came from around the Black Sea; the rest came
from Egypt, Sicily, and other grain-growing lands. Grain was not a luxury. It was the
food that kept the city alive, and keeping it coming was Athens'
most important job — more important than temples, more important
than wars.

What did Athens sell to pay for it? What its land *could* produce:
olive oil and wine, beautifully painted pottery, and silver from the
mines at Laurium. Athenian coins — silver pieces stamped with an
owl, the symbol of Athena — were trusted all around the
Mediterranean, and trusted money makes trade easier: a merchant in
Egypt would accept an Athenian owl because he knew he could spend
it again. This is the trade pattern to remember: **grain, timber,
and metals in; olive oil, wine, pottery, and silver out.**

## Ships, harbors, and colonies

Trade moved by sea, and sea trade needed three things. First,
**ships**: merchant sailing ships carried the cargo, and fast
warships called **triremes** — with three banks of oars — protected
them and enforced Athenian power. Second, **harbors**: Athens'
harbor was **Piraeus**, a few miles from the city, joined to Athens
by the **Long Walls** — two long walls that made the city and its
harbor into one fortified unit, so grain could keep arriving even
during a war. Third, **colonies and allies**: Greeks had founded
cities all around the Mediterranean and the Black Sea, and those
cities traded with each other and with their mother cities. A colony
was not a possession; it was a new polis, with its own government,
that stayed in touch and traded.

The **Delian League** tied much of this together. Formed in 478 BCE
after the Persian Wars, it was an alliance of more than 170 Greek
city-states, led by Athens, to defend against Persia and to stop
piracy. Each member paid money or ships — **tribute** — and the
treasury sat on the island of Delos. In 454 BCE Athens moved the
treasury to Athens itself, and the League slowly became an Athenian
empire: allies paid, Athens decided, and the money built Athenian
ships, walls, and temples — including the Parthenon. Trade and power
were the same story: the navy that protected the grain route was
paid for by the allies it was supposed to protect.

## Supply and demand at the grain market

A market price is a tug-of-war between **supply** (how much is
available) and **demand** (how much people want). When supply falls
and demand stays the same, buyers compete and the price rises. When
supply rises, the price falls. The grain price in the agora followed
that rule, and three forces kept moving the supply:

- **Harvests.** A good harvest in the Black Sea meant more grain
  and lower prices; a bad harvest meant less grain and higher
  prices. Athens could not control the weather in another land.
- **War and piracy.** A war that closed the sea route, or pirates
  who seized grain ships, cut the supply — and the price jumped.
  That is one reason Athens built its navy and its League: to keep
  the sea lanes open.
- **Barriers.** Mountains made land transport slow and expensive,
  so most grain came by sea. Narrow straits — the Hellespont and
  the Bosporus, the passages into the Black Sea — let the city that
  controlled them tax or stop the ships passing through. A toll at
  a strait was a cost every grain buyer in Athens paid for, in the
  price of bread.

Solon understood this long before the League: his law banned the
export of most Athenian produce except olive oil, so that grain and
other food stayed home. Trade policy was food policy, and food
policy was survival policy.

## Worked example A — Tracing one cargo

Follow one cargo on the map, step by step.

*Out from Athens:* A merchant ship leaves Piraeus loaded with
amphorae — tall clay jars — of olive oil and wine, plus painted
pottery. It sails east across the Aegean, past Delos, through the
Hellespont, and into the Black Sea.
*The exchange:* In a Black Sea port, the merchant sells the oil,
wine, and pottery, and buys grain — wheat and barley — with the
silver he earned. The grain is loaded into the ship's hold.
*Back to Athens:* The ship sails home through the Bosporus and the
Hellespont, past Delos, and into Piraeus. The grain is weighed,
taxed, and sold in the agora. The merchant keeps his profit; the
city eats.
*What the map shows:* the route is a loop — goods out, food in.
*What the map cannot show:* the price of the grain, the danger of
the voyage, or who did the loading. The route is a line; the trade
is a story.

## Worked example B — Why did the price rise? Two cases

Use the rule: **less supply (same demand) → higher price; more
supply (same demand) → lower price.**

*Case 1 — a bad harvest in the Black Sea.* The Black Sea cities
grow less grain, so fewer grain ships reach Piraeus. The same
number of Athenians still need bread every day. Buyers compete for
the smaller supply, and sellers raise the price. The price rises
because supply fell.
*Case 2 — the Delian League clears pirates from the Aegean.* More
grain ships arrive safely. The supply grows, buyers have more to
choose from, and the price falls. The price falls because supply
rose — and because Athens' navy made the sea safer.
*Check your reasoning:* in both cases, ask first, "Did the amount
of grain arriving change, or did the number of hungry people
change?" If the grain changed and the people did not, the price
moved with the supply.

## Guided practice

Work with a partner or your adult. Answer in full sentences.

1. Using the schematic map, trace the grain route: name the sea the
   grain crossed, the two narrow straits it passed through, and the
   harbor where it arrived in Athens.
2. List two things Athens imported and two things it exported, and
   explain why the land around Athens made that pattern necessary.
3. Explain what the Long Walls did, and why they mattered most
   during a war.
4. The Delian League began as an alliance for defense. Explain in
   two or three sentences how it became an Athenian empire — name
   the treasury move and what the money was spent on.
5. A storm sinks ten grain ships in one week. Predict what happens
   to the price of grain in the agora, and explain your prediction
   with supply and demand.

## Applied task

You are an Athenian grain merchant. The Black Sea harvest was bad
this year, and the price of grain in the agora has doubled. The
assembly is debating a law to cap the price of grain. Write a short
argument (about 8–10 sentences) EITHER for the price cap OR against
it. If you argue for the cap, explain who it helps and who pays the
cost. If you argue against it, explain what happens to merchants
and to future grain shipments. Use supply and demand in your
argument. Label your argument "classroom model."

## Exit check

Without looking back, answer: (a) Name one import and one export
of Athens. (b) What did the Delian League's members pay Athens?
(c) If grain supply falls and demand stays the same, does the price
rise or fall?

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: "Athens grew its own grain" (it imported
most of it — that is the point of the lesson); "the Delian League
was always an empire" (it began as a defensive alliance and became
an empire over time, marked by the treasury move in 454 BCE);
"triremes carried cargo" (they were warships that protected cargo
ships); "a price cap fixes a shortage" (it can help buyers today
and hurt supply tomorrow — accept either side of the applied task
if it names both a gainer and a loser). The grain-price numbers are
fictional classroom-model data; do not present them as ancient
records. Solutions are in [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), §L3.
