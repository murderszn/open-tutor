# Lesson 05 — Weighing Athens: What Greek Civic Ideas Left Behind

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can build a claim with
evidence about what Greek civic ideas are worth keeping, name who
was excluded and who paid the costs, and note the limits of the
evidence.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, your
inquiry notebook, your work from Lessons 01–04.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Name one thing Athens invented
that you think is worth keeping (voting, juries, open debate) and
one group that was excluded from it. If you can name both, you are
ready; if not, re-read the "Two honest sentences" section below
first.

## Two honest sentences

Any fair judgment of Athens needs two sentences, held together:

1. **Athens invented something remarkable:** ordinary citizens
   voting on laws and wars themselves, serving on juries, holding
   office by lot, and debating in public — ideas no earlier
   government had tried at this scale, and ideas later peoples
   borrowed, argued about, and built on.
2. **Athens kept that invention narrow and paid for it with other
   people's freedom:** women, enslaved people, and metics had no
   vote; the Delian League's allies paid for the buildings and
   ships; and the enslaved labor that freed citizens for politics
   was forced, not chosen.

A judgment that says only the first sentence is praise, not
history. A judgment that says only the second sentence is blame,
not history either. The work of this lesson is to hold both
sentences and decide, with reasons, what is worth keeping — and
what must be left behind.

## Who gained, who lost

Weighing a legacy means asking, for each change, who gained and
who lost. Use the trade and the empire as the two test cases.

**The grain trade.** Athens gained food and survival; its people
ate. Grain merchants gained profits, and the city's poor gained
bread at a price the assembly watched. But Black Sea farmers sold
at prices Athens' navy and market set, not prices they chose; and
when Athens went to war, the grain route became a weapon — a city
that could cut off another city's food could starve it into
surrender. The same ships that fed Athens could also blockade a
rival.

**The Delian League.** Athens gained ships, walls, temples, and
power. Its citizens gained pay for jury service and a city that
looked — as Thucydides warned — twice as powerful as it was. The
allies lost independence: their tribute paid for Athens' buildings,
and when an ally tried to leave, Athens used force to keep it in.
The empire's gains and the allies' losses are the same coins,
counted from different sides.

**The democracy itself.** Citizens gained a voice, a jury, and a
share of rule. Enslaved people lost the most: their labor made the
citizens' leisure possible, and they had no voice at all. Women and
metics kept the city running — weaving, trading, raising children,
serving in the army — and had no vote. When you weigh Athens, count
the people who are not in the assembly. They are the majority.

## Building a claim with evidence

A claim is a judgment you can defend. A strong claim has three
parts: the judgment, the evidence, and the limit.

- **Judgment:** "Athenian democracy's most valuable idea was
  that ordinary people should vote on the laws that govern them."
- **Evidence:** citizens voted directly in the assembly; officials
  were chosen by lot so that poor men could serve; juries of
  ordinary citizens decided trials; the idea lasted and spread.
- **Limit:** only adult male citizens could take part, so the idea
  was incomplete — and the evidence comes mostly from Athenian men
  themselves, who had every reason to praise their own system.
  Women, enslaved people, and allies left almost no written
  record of what they thought of it.

Notice the limit: the people with the most reason to criticize
Athens left the fewest records. That does not mean they had no
opinions; it means the record is one-sided, and a careful historian
says so. A claim without a limit is a slogan. A claim with a limit
is an argument.

## Worked example A — A claim, built step by step

*Question:* Was Athens' use of the lot (random draw) a good idea?

**Judgment:** Choosing most officials by lot was a good idea for
Athens, because it stopped rich families from keeping power.
**Evidence 1:** the Council of 500 was drawn by lot, 50 from each
tribe, so ordinary men — potters, farmers, traders — took turns
running the city. **Evidence 2:** lotteries made bribery pointless:
you cannot buy an office that a random draw gives away.
**Counter-evidence:** lotteries could pick someone with no skill
for a hard job, which is why Athens *elected* its generals instead
of drawing lots for them.
**Limit:** we know the lot worked for the council because Athenian
writers say so; we have almost no record of how the poorest
citizens felt about serving, or whether they could afford the time
away from work before pay was introduced.
*Revised claim:* the lot was a good idea for routine offices, a bad
idea for jobs needing skill, and its fairness depended on pay that
arrived later. The revision is stronger than the first draft,
because it answers its own objection.

## Worked example B — What the evidence cannot tell us

*Question:* Did enslaved people in Athens want the democracy to
end?

*What we know:* enslaved people did the labor that freed citizens
for politics; they had no vote, no voice in the assembly, and no
legal standing.
*What we do not know:* what most enslaved people thought about
Athenian democracy, because they left almost no written records,
and the records we have were written by their owners.
*The honest answer:* we can say the system harmed them — forced
labor is harm — but we cannot say what they hoped for, because the
evidence does not survive. A good historian names that gap instead
of filling it with a guess. When your project and assessment ask
about enslaved people, women, or allies, the right answer often
includes the words "the record does not say."

## Guided practice

Work with a partner or your adult. Answer in full sentences.

1. Write the two honest sentences about Athens in your own words:
   one for what it invented, one for who it excluded.
2. Name one gainer and one loser from the Delian League, and
   explain how the same tribute payment is a gain for one and a
   loss for the other.
3. Explain why the grain trade was both a lifeline and a weapon.
   Give one example of each.
4. A claim needs a judgment, evidence, and a limit. Take this
   judgment — "Athens' assembly was its best institution" — and
   add one piece of evidence and one limit.
5. Why do we know so little about what enslaved people, women, and
   allies thought of Athenian democracy? Give two reasons.

## Independent practice

Answer on your own. Use the vocabulary words *significance*,
*tribute*, and *democracy* correctly at least once each across your
answers.

6. Build a claim of your own: "Sparta's system was better than
   Athens' for one thing, and worse for another." State the one
   thing Sparta did better, with evidence, and the one thing it
   did worse, with evidence. (Hint: think about stability and
   about who could speak.)
7. The Parthenon was built with Delian League tribute money.
   Explain in two or three sentences why one Athenian might call
   it a triumph and one ally might call it a theft — and why both
   could be right about the same building.
8. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Athenian democracy was
   perfect, and everyone in the ancient world admired it." Explain
   two errors in that sentence.
9. Name one idea from this unit that you think is worth keeping
   today, and one idea that should be left behind. For each, give
   one reason.

## Applied task

Write a short judgment (about 8–10 sentences) answering this
question: *Should we call Athens "the birthplace of democracy"?*
In your answer: state your judgment (yes, no, or "yes, but"),
give two pieces of evidence for it, name one group the label
leaves out, and end with one sentence about what the label should
really mean. Label your judgment "classroom model."

## Exit check

Without looking back, answer: (a) Name one gainer and one loser
from Athenian democracy. (b) What are the three parts of a strong
claim? (c) Why is the record of Athens one-sided?

## Extension

Research question for the adult to approve: the word *democracy*
is still used today, but most modern democracies are
*representative* — citizens elect people to vote for them. Find
one modern example of direct democracy (a referendum, a town
meeting, a jury) and explain in one or two sentences how it is
like Athens and how it is different. Bring it to the next session.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: "weighing a legacy means finding a single
verdict" (the point is to hold the invention and the exclusion
together, not to pick one); "the allies liked the League" (many
resented it, and Athens used force to keep allies in — name that
plainly); "no record means no opinion" (the absence of records
from enslaved people, women, and allies is a gap in the evidence,
not proof they agreed); "a claim with a limit is a weak claim"
(the opposite: naming the limit is what makes the claim
trustworthy). If a learner is distressed by the slavery content,
stay factual, keep it brief, and move to the claim-building task —
do not debate whether slavery existed. Solutions are in
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), §L5.
