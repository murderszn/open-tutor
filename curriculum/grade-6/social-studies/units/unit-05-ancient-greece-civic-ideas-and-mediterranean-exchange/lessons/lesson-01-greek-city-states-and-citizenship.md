## A city and its farmland

Ancient Greece was not one country. It was hundreds of independent
city-states, each called a **polis** (plural: *poleis*). A polis was
a city plus the farmland around it, ruling itself with its own
government, its own laws, its own army, and often its own coins.
Athens was a polis. So was Sparta, Corinth, and Thebes — and hundreds
of smaller ones most people have never heard of. The Greeks shared a
language, gods, and customs, but no single king or government ruled
them all. When two poleis disagreed, they negotiated, traded, or
fought, the way separate countries do today.

Why so many separate states? Look at the land. Greece is a
mountainous peninsula with deep valleys, rocky hills, and a long,
jagged coastline. Mountains made travel slow and hard, so each
valley and coastal plain grew into its own community. The sea did
the opposite job: it connected those communities. Greek ships sailed
between islands, along coasts, and across the Mediterranean to
colonies — new Greek cities founded overseas — in Sicily, southern
Italy, and around the Black Sea. So Greece was divided by its
mountains and joined by its sea, all at the same time.

Every polis had a public square called the **agora**. The agora was
the marketplace and the civic center in one place: stalls sold grain,
olive oil, pots, and cloth; citizens met to talk, argue, and vote;
law courts sat; and the council's buildings stood nearby. In Athens
the agora sat below the Acropolis, the rocky hill with the city's
great temples. The picture in this lesson shows an imagined agora —
use it to practice observation and inference, the way you did with
the riverside city in Unit 03.

## Who counted as a citizen?

Here is the question that will follow us all unit: who got to decide?
In Athens, a **citizen** was a free adult man born to Athenian
parents. Citizens could attend the assembly, speak, vote on laws,
serve on juries, and hold office. After a law passed in 451 BCE
under the leader Pericles, *both* parents had to be Athenian for a
son to count as a citizen.

That sounds like "rule by the people" — the Greek word
*demokratia* means exactly that. But count the people. Athens had
about 30,000 to 40,000 adult male citizens, in a city of about
250,000 to 300,000 people. (Scholars disagree on the exact numbers,
so these are estimates, not a census.) That means roughly one person
in eight could vote. Who was left out?

- **Women** — even women born to citizen families — could not vote,
  speak in the assembly, or hold office.
- **Enslaved people** — perhaps a third of the people in Athens —
  had no rights at all. They worked in households, mines, workshops,
  and fields, and their labor freed citizens to spend time on
  politics.
- **Metics** — free foreign residents who lived in Athens for years,
  paid taxes, and served in the army — could not vote or hold office.

So Athenian democracy was real and it was narrow: the citizens who
voted genuinely decided laws and wars themselves, and most of the
people who kept the city running had no say. Keep both facts in your
head at once. This unit will not let you forget either one.

## How a law was made

Athens had two bodies that worked together. The **Council of 500**
(*boule*) — 500 citizens over 30, chosen by lot (random draw), 50
from each of 10 tribes, serving one-year terms — set the agenda: it
prepared proposals and ran the city's daily business. Then the
**Assembly** (*ekklesia*) — every citizen, meeting about 40 times a
year on the Pnyx hill — debated and voted, by a show of hands, on
laws, war and peace, and treaties. Some votes needed a quorum of
6,000 citizens present. The council proposed; the assembly decided.
No king, no parliament, no president — the citizens themselves, in
person.

## Worked example A — Reading the agora picture

Look at [agora-civic-scene.png](../assets/agora-civic-scene.png).
It is a modern AI illustration of an *imagined* Athenian agora — a
picture to think with, not a photograph of the past and not evidence
about any real place or person.

*Observation:* In the foreground, a man stands on a low stone
platform speaking to a small group — a woman holding a bowl of
olives, a bearded man, and a boy. To the right, a merchant weighs
goods on a balance scale beside clay pots, grain baskets, and olive
oil jars. Behind them are market stalls, columned public buildings,
and a temple on a hill; a sailing ship is visible in the distant
harbor.
*Inference:* The agora mixed buying and selling with public
speaking — trade and politics happened in the same place.
*Second inference:* The balance scale and the grain baskets suggest
that trade here was measured and argued over, not just handed from
hand to hand.
*What the picture cannot show:* whether the speaker is a citizen
addressing voters or a merchant calling out prices; whether the
woman and the boy could vote (they could not); or who owned the
grain. A picture can start a question; it cannot answer it alone.

**Text-only alternative (same information, no picture needed):** A
sunny open square. A man in a white tunic stands on a low stone
platform, gesturing as he speaks to a woman with a bowl of olives,
a bearded man, and a boy. To the right, a merchant weighs goods on
a balance scale at a stall with clay pots, baskets of grain, and
jars of olive oil. Behind the square stand columned public
buildings and a temple on a hill. A sailing ship is visible in the
distant harbor. Market stalls with striped awnings line the square.

## Worked example B — Who can vote? Three cases

Use one rule for each case: **only a free adult man born to
Athenian parents is a citizen with a vote.**

1. **Nikias, 35, born in Athens to Athenian parents, a potter.**
   He is a citizen. He can attend the assembly, vote, and serve on
   a jury — even though he is not rich. His job does not matter;
   his birth and sex do.
2. **Eirene, 30, born in Athens to Athenian parents, a weaver.**
   She is Athenian by birth, but she is a woman, so she cannot
   vote or speak in the assembly. Being born in Athens is not
   enough; the rule also requires being a man.
3. **Dion, 40, a free merchant from Corinth who has lived in Athens
   for 15 years, pays taxes, and served in the army.** He is a
   metic. He can trade and he must pay taxes, but he cannot vote
   or hold office. Living in Athens for years is not enough; the
   rule requires Athenian parents.

Notice what the rule leaves out: wealth, length of residence, and
loyalty. Nikias the potter votes; Eirene the Athenian-born weaver
and Dion the loyal taxpayer do not. That is the point of the
example: Athenian citizenship was about birth and sex, not about
who contributed most.

## Guided practice

Work with a partner or your adult. Answer in full sentences.

1. Using the schematic map, name the sea that separates mainland
   Greece from Asia Minor, and name two Greek cities marked on it.
2. Explain in your own words why mountains helped Greece stay
   divided into many city-states, and why the sea pushed the other
   way.
3. A polis is "a city plus the farmland around it." Explain why the
   farmland mattered as much as the city walls.
4. Look again at the agora picture. Name two observations and one
   inference, and name one thing the picture cannot tell us.
5. For each person, say whether they could vote in Athens and why:
   (a) a 20-year-old Athenian-born man; (b) a 45-year-old enslaved
   woman who cooks in a citizen's house; (c) a free trader from
   Egypt living in Athens.

## Independent practice

Answer on your own. Use the vocabulary words *polis*, *citizen*,
*assembly*, and *metic* correctly at least once each across your
answers.

6. Athens had about 30,000–40,000 adult male citizens in a city of
   about 250,000–300,000 people. About what fraction of the people
   could vote? Show your thinking. (An estimate is fine — say why
   it is an estimate.)
7. Explain the two-step path of an Athenian law: what the Council
   of 500 did, and what the assembly did. Why do you think Athens
   needed both bodies instead of just one?
8. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "In Athenian democracy,
   everyone in Athens voted on the laws." Explain two errors in
   that sentence.
9. The council was chosen by lot — a random draw — instead of by
   election. Give one reason random choice might be fairer than an
   election, and one reason it might be worse.

## Applied task

You are a metic merchant who has lived in Athens for ten years. You
pay taxes, your son serves in the army, and you sell olive oil in
the agora. Write a short letter (about 8–10 sentences) to the
assembly asking for the right to speak at one meeting about a new
tax on olive oil. In your letter: state who you are and what you
contribute, explain why the tax affects you, name the rule that
keeps you from voting, and make your request politely but firmly.
You may invent your name and your stall's location; label the
letter "classroom model."

## Exit check

Without looking back, answer: (a) What is a polis? (b) Name the
three groups who lived in Athens but could not vote. (c) Which body
set the assembly's agenda — the Council of 500 or the assembly
itself?

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: "everyone in Athens voted" (only adult male
citizens — about one person in eight); "a polis is just a city"
(it is a city *plus* its farmland, ruling itself); "the council
made the laws" (the council proposed; the assembly decided);
"metics were enslaved" (metics were free residents without
political rights — keep the two groups distinct). Solutions are in
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), §L1. The picture is an AI
reconstruction — never present it as evidence of a real place or
