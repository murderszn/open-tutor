# Lesson 02 — Athens, Sparta, and Systems of Government

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson you can compare the Athenian
assembly and council with Sparta's kings, council of elders, and
assembly, and explain how each system made decisions, who could
take part, and what each was good and bad at.
**Time:** about 40 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson,
[greek-timeline.svg](../assets/greek-timeline.svg), a pencil, your
inquiry notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Name the two Athenian bodies from
Lesson 01 (the Council of 500 and the assembly) and say in one
sentence what each one did. If you cannot, re-read the "How a law
was made" section of Lesson 01 first.

## How Athens built its democracy, step by step

Athenian democracy was not born in a day. Three reformers, about a
century apart, built it piece by piece. Use the timeline to follow
them.

**Solon, about 594 BCE.** Athens was in trouble: poor farmers fell
into debt, and some were enslaved for debts they could not pay.
Solon ended the enslavement of citizens for debt and divided
citizens into four property classes by how much their land produced.
The richest could hold the highest offices; the poorest could attend
the assembly but not hold high office. It was not democracy yet —
wealth still decided who ruled — but it was the first step: no
Athenian citizen could be enslaved for debt again, and ordinary
citizens got a voice in the assembly.

**Cleisthenes, about 508 BCE.** Cleisthenes is often called the
father of Athenian democracy. He broke the power of the old rich
families by mixing citizens into 10 new tribes, each made of people
from different parts of Attica — city, coast, and countryside — so
no tribe belonged to one family or one place. He created the
Council of 500 (50 citizens drawn by lot from each tribe) and gave
every tribe a share of the work. He also introduced **ostracism**:
once a year, citizens could vote to exile a man they thought was
getting too powerful. If enough voted yes, each citizen wrote a name
on a potsherd — an *ostrakon* — and the man with the most votes
left Athens for 10 years. It was democracy's safety valve: a way to
stop a tyrant before he seized power.

**Pericles, about 461–429 BCE.** Under Pericles, Athens was at its
most democratic — and its most powerful. He arranged pay for
jurors and later for council members, so poor citizens could afford
to serve. But in 451 BCE he also passed the citizenship law that
narrowed who counted: both parents now had to be Athenian. More
poor men could take part, and fewer people counted as Athenians at
all. That is the pattern of this unit: every expansion came with a
new exclusion.

Two other Athenian institutions matter. The **law courts** used
huge juries of ordinary citizens — hundreds at a time, chosen by
lot — instead of professional judges. And **generals** (ten of
them, one per tribe) were elected, not chosen by lot, because Athens
wanted skill in war. Most offices were filled by lot so that no
small group could keep power; the few elected offices were the ones
where a mistake could lose a war.

## Sparta: the deliberate contrast

Sparta, Athens' great rival, chose the opposite answer to "who
decides?" Sparta was an **oligarchy** — rule by a few — built
around its army.

- **Two kings** shared power, led the army, and handled religious
  duties. Two kings meant neither could rule alone.
- The **gerousia**, a council of 28 elders over 60 plus the two
  kings, proposed laws and judged serious cases.
- The **assembly** of male Spartan citizens could vote yes or no
  on proposals — but it could not debate them or propose its own.
- Five **ephors**, elected yearly, watched over the kings and the
  state, and could fine or even arrest a king.

Spartan citizens were full-time soldiers. Who farmed? The
**helots** — enslaved people, mostly Messenians conquered by Sparta
— worked the land and gave half their produce to their Spartan
masters. Spartan citizens did not farm, trade, or make things; they
trained for war. Sparta was strong, stable, and feared — and it was
built on forced labor at a scale even larger than Athens'. When the
two cities went to war (the Peloponnesian War, 431–404 BCE), Sparta
won. The system that let ordinary men vote lost to the system that
made every citizen a soldier.

## Which system for which problem?

Neither system was "better" at everything. Athens was good at
debate, new ideas, and letting ordinary men take part — but its
assembly could be swayed by a loud speaker, and it excluded most of
the people who lived there. Sparta was good at discipline and
staying power — but its citizens could not debate, its helots lived
under constant threat, and it produced little art, trade, or new
thinking. The honest comparison names what each system did well
*and* what each system cost.

## Worked example A — Reading the timeline

Use [greek-timeline.svg](../assets/greek-timeline.svg). All dates
are approximate, and BCE years count *down* toward the present: 594
BCE is earlier than 508 BCE.

*Question:* How many years passed between Solon's reforms (about
594 BCE) and Cleisthenes' reforms (about 508 BCE)?
*Working:* 594 − 508 = 86 years. About three generations.
*Question:* How long did Athenian democracy last, from Cleisthenes
(about 508 BCE) to its end (about 322 BCE)?
*Working:* 508 − 322 = 186 years — nearly two centuries.
*What the timeline cannot show:* why each change happened, or
whether ordinary people welcomed it. Dates order events; they do
not explain them.

**Text-only alternative (same information, no diagram needed):**
From earliest to latest: Solon reforms about 594 BCE; Cleisthenes
about 508 BCE; Marathon about 490 BCE; Salamis about 480 BCE;
Delian League about 478 BCE; Peloponnesian War begins about 431
BCE; Athens defeated about 404 BCE; democracy ends about 322 BCE.
The treasury moved from Delos to Athens about 454 BCE.

## Worked example B — Comparing two decisions

The same problem, two systems. **Problem:** A neighboring city has
seized a border village. Should we go to war?

*In Athens:* the Council of 500 debates and prepares a proposal.
The assembly meets on the Pnyx; any citizen may speak; the citizens
vote by show of hands. The decision is fast, public, and final —
and it can be swayed by a single powerful speech.
*In Sparta:* the two kings and the gerousia consider the problem;
the ephors weigh in; the assembly votes yes or no on a proposal it
did not write and cannot amend. The decision is slower and narrower
— and the men who will fight it had little say in shaping it.
*The comparison:* Athens lets more people shape the decision but
risks a hasty one; Sparta limits debate but keeps decisions in
experienced hands — and both systems send men to die for a decision
made by people who will not all fight.

## Guided practice

Work with a partner or your adult. Answer in full sentences.

1. Put these in order, earliest first: Pericles' leadership,
   Cleisthenes' reforms, Solon's reforms. Give the approximate
   date of each.
2. Explain what ostracism was, and why Athens thought it needed
   such a rule. (Hint: think about tyrants.)
3. Name the three parts of Sparta's government that limited the
   kings, and say what each one did.
4. Why did Athens choose most officials by lot instead of by
   election? Give the reason from the lesson, then give one
   problem lotteries could cause.
5. A Spartan assembly could vote yes or no but could not debate or
   propose. An Athenian assembly could debate and vote on anything.
   Which assembly had more real power? Defend your answer with one
   reason.

## Applied task

You are an Athenian citizen in 480 BCE, the year of the Battle of
Salamis. The assembly must decide: spend the city's silver on
building more triremes (warships), or spend it on rebuilding the
temples the Persians destroyed. Write a short speech (about 8–10
sentences) arguing for ONE side. In your speech: state your choice,
give two reasons, name one group that would disagree with you and
why, and answer their objection. Label the speech "classroom model"
— it is your argument, not a real ancient speech.

## Exit check

Without looking back, answer: (a) Who created the Council of 500,
and about when? (b) How long was an ostracized person exiled?
(c) Name one Spartan institution and what it did.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: "Cleisthenes invented democracy in one
day" (it was built over a century by Solon, Cleisthenes, and
Pericles); "ostracism was a punishment for a crime" (it was a
political safety valve against too much power, not a criminal
sentence); "Sparta had no assembly" (it had one, but it could not
debate or propose); "lotteries mean anyone could be general"
(generals were elected — Athens drew lots for most offices but not
for war leadership). The helot system must be named plainly as
slavery at scale; do not soften it into "workers." Solutions are
in [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), §L2.
