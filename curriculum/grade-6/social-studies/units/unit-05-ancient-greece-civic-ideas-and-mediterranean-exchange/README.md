# Unit 05 — Ancient Greece: Civic Ideas and Mediterranean Exchange

**Grade:** 6 · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#33](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/33)
**Status:** validated draft — delivered 2026-11-07; not yet educator-reviewed or merged.
**Weeks:** 20–23 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, about 40 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

Ancient Greece was not one country. It was hundreds of independent
city-states — each a city and its surrounding farmland, each with its
own government, laws, and customs — scattered across a mountainous
peninsula, Aegean islands, and colonies around the Mediterranean and
Black Sea. This unit asks the two questions that made Greece matter:
how did ordinary citizens in Athens come to vote on laws and wars
themselves, and how did ships, markets, and colonies tie the whole
Mediterranean together? Athens carries the detailed civic work; Sparta
is the deliberate contrast; the grain trade carries the economic work.
The through-line is a pair of honest tensions the learner keeps
returning to: Athens invented rule by the people and still excluded
most of the people who lived there, and the trade that fed Athens
also made it an empire over its allies.

The unit's through-line is a chain the learner builds and tests:
**rocky soil → olives, wine, and silver → ships and markets →
grain imports → a fed city that could afford democracy — and a navy
that could collect tribute**. Every lesson asks who the chain helped
and who it left out: women, enslaved people, and resident foreigners
had no vote; allies paid for the buildings democracy voted to build;
and the same ships that carried grain also carried war.

## Prerequisites (re-taught here as needed)

- Reading a map with title, key/legend, compass rose, scale bar, and
  grid (Unit 01, Lesson 03); this unit reads maps at Mediterranean
  scale and traces routes on a schematic map.
- Telling an observation apart from an inference, and classifying a
  source as an artifact, document, image, or oral account (Unit 01,
  Lessons 02 and 04).
- Placing dates on a BCE timeline and computing elapsed time across
  centuries (Unit 01, Lesson 05; this unit uses
  [greek-timeline.svg](assets/greek-timeline.svg), about 600–300 BCE).
- Explaining how environments shaped settlement and how trade moved
  goods between specialists (Units 02–03); this unit applies
  supply-and-demand reasoning to grain (Unit 03's chain: surplus →
  specialists → cities → writing and laws).
- Comparing two societies in their own contexts without ranking them
  (Unit 04).
- Safety: the adult supervises the deliberation lab and the
  trade-model investigation; no real money, food, or ships are
  involved — all trade uses classroom-model cargo cards.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (mapped to the track
objectives in parentheses):

1. **Explain** the Greek city-state (*polis*): what it was, why
   Greece had hundreds of them, and who counted as a citizen in
   Athens — and who did not — naming women, enslaved people, and
   resident foreigners (*metics*) plainly. (Track objective 6;
   D2.Civ.2, 5.6-8; D2.His.4.6-8)
2. **Compare** the Athenian assembly and council with Sparta's
   kings, council of elders, and assembly, and **explain** how each
   system made decisions, who could take part, and what each system
   was good and bad at. (Track objective 6; D2.Civ.4, 5, 9.6-8)
3. **Explain** Mediterranean exchange: what Athens imported (grain,
   timber, metals) and exported (olive oil, wine, pottery, silver),
   how ships, harbors, colonies, and the Delian League moved goods,
   and how supply and demand changed grain prices. (Track objective
   6; D2.Eco.3, 6, 14, 15.6-8; D2.Geo.7, 11.6-8)
4. **Trace** how one idea or practice traveled the Mediterranean
   (the alphabet, coinage, or the Olympic games), **explain** what
   ships, roads, and colonies had to do with it, and **judge** why
   one Greek person or development is seen as historically
   significant. (Track objective 6; D2.Geo.7.6-8; D2.His.1, 3.6-8)
5. **Weigh** Athens' civic legacy honestly: **build** a claim with
   evidence about what Greek civic ideas are worth keeping, **name**
   who was excluded and who paid the costs, and **note** the limits
   of the evidence. (Track objectives 6, 9, 10; D2.Civ.7, 10.6-8;
   D2.His.14.6-8; D4.2, 4.8.6-8)

## Essential question

*Athens let ordinary citizens vote on laws and wars — so why did
most of the people who lived there have no vote at all, and what did
the trade that fed the city cost everyone else?*

## Vocabulary

**Polis** (plural: *poleis*) — a Greek city-state: a city plus the
farmland around it, ruling itself with its own government and laws.
· **Citizen** — in Athens, a free adult man born to Athenian
parents who could vote, speak in the assembly, serve on juries, and
hold office. · **Democracy** — from the Greek *demokratia*,
"rule by the people": in Athens, a direct democracy in which
citizens voted on laws themselves instead of electing
representatives. · **Direct democracy** — citizens vote on every
major decision themselves. · **Assembly** (*ekklesia*) — the
meeting of all Athenian citizens, held about 40 times a year on the
Pnyx hill, which voted on laws, war and peace, and treaties. ·
**Council of 500** (*boule*) — 500 citizens over 30, chosen by lot
(50 from each of 10 tribes) for one-year terms, who set the
assembly's agenda and ran daily business. · **Lot** — choosing by
random draw, the way Athens picked most officials and jurors. ·
**Ostracism** — a yearly vote that could exile a citizen judged too
powerful for 10 years; voters wrote the name on a potsherd called
an *ostrakon*. · **Metics** — free foreign residents of Athens
who paid taxes and served in the army but could not vote or hold
office. · **Helots** — enslaved people in Sparta, mostly
Messenians, forced to farm for Spartan citizens. · **Gerousia** —
Sparta's council of elders: 28 men over 60 plus the two kings. ·
**Ephors** — five Spartan overseers, elected yearly, who watched
over the kings and the state. · **Oligarchy** — rule by a few
people. · **Tyranny** — rule by one person who seized power. ·
**Agora** — the public square of a Greek city: marketplace, civic
center, and meeting place in one. · **Piraeus** — Athens'
harbor, joined to the city by the Long Walls. · **Trireme** — a
fast warship with three banks of oars, which protected Greek trade
and enforced Athenian power. · **Delian League** — an alliance of
Greek city-states formed in 478 BCE to defend against Persia, led
by Athens; it became an Athenian empire. · **Tribute** — money or
ships allies paid to Athens as their League contribution. ·
**Colony** — a new Greek city founded overseas by settlers from an
older city, trading and staying in touch with its mother city. ·
**Supply and demand** — when a good is scarce, buyers compete and
its price rises; when it is plentiful, its price falls. ·
**Diffusion** — the spread of an idea, practice, or object from one
place to another. · **Significance** — why a person, event, or
development matters enough for historians to study it. ·
Supporting terms used in the unit: *acropolis, deme, tribe,
prytany, jury, archon, stoa, amphora, tetradrachm, Hellespont,
Bosporus, Long Walls, Peloponnesian War, Persians, tribute list.*

- This unit teaches each indicator at the **grade-6 entry-band level**:
  sources are adult-curated, inquiry scaffolds are heavy, and
  evaluation is observation-level.
- Unit indicators: D2.Civ.2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10.6-8 (roles of
  citizens; origins/purposes/impact of laws; powers and limits of
  government in other countries; origins/functions/structure of
  government; civic virtues and democratic principles; deliberative
  processes; personal interests and perspectives in civic life);
  D2.Eco.3, 5, 6, 14, 15.6-8 (buyers and sellers; money easing
  exchange; supply and demand; barriers to trade; benefits and costs
  of trade policies); D2.Geo.1, 7, 11.6-8 (maps as evidence;
  transportation and communication changing spatial connections;
  environment, production, and world trade); D2.His.1, 2, 3, 4,
  14.6-8 (connections in broader contexts; change and/or continuity;
  historical significance; multiple factors shaping perspectives;
  multiple causes and effects); D1.3, 1.5.6-8 (points of agreement
  about supporting questions; kinds of sources that answer
  questions); D4.2, 4.8.6-8 (sequenced explanations with noted
  strengths and weaknesses; deliberative and democratic procedures).
- Dates in this unit are **approximate ("about")** unless a source
  gives an exact year; BCE arithmetic is subtraction across the BCE
  number line with no year 0 (see Lesson 02 and the answer key).
  Population figures for Athens are **estimates that scholars
  disagree about**: the unit uses "about 30,000–40,000 adult male
  citizens in a city of about 250,000–300,000 people" and names the
  uncertainty in the teacher guide; the Smarthistory reference gives
  a wider range (30,000–60,000 citizens of 150,000–400,000 people).
- All investigation datasets are **fictional practice data** (the
  grain-price table, the cargo cards, the deliberation role cards,
  the tribute-list figures) or labeled museum/library references in
  the Resource Pack. The generated agora image is an **AI
  illustration of an imagined scene — not evidence** of any real
  place or event. No ancient text is quoted at length; the one
  short Thucydides paraphrase in Lesson 04 is labeled as a
  paraphrase, not a quotation.
- Sensitive-content framing: slavery and exclusion from citizenship
  are named plainly and briefly (who was forced, who decided, who
  was left out) with no graphic detail and no glorification;
  Athens is praised for its invention and criticized for its
  limits in the same lesson; belief systems are **described, never
  evaluated**; Greek peoples are presented as real societies, not
  as curiosities; the Peloponnesian War and the Delian League's
  turn into empire are taught as choices with costs, not as
  inevitable glory.

## 16-session pacing (4 × ~40 min per week)

Each session names its activity and what "done well" looks like.

| Week | Sessions | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 1–4 | **Greek city-states, citizenship, and assemblies.** S1: Lesson 01 — what a polis is, why Greece had hundreds, who counted as a citizen and who did not; the generated agora illustration as an observation task. *Success: learner defines a polis, names three groups excluded from Athenian citizenship, and explains one reason Greece stayed divided into city-states.* S2: citizenship practice set ([practice.md](practice.md), Set A) + map work with [aegean-trade-map.svg](assets/aegean-trade-map.svg). *Success: ≥80% correct; the citizenship-chart answer names who could and could not vote, with a reason for each.* S3: investigation — **the agora observation lab**: sort 12 agora role cards into "could vote," "could not vote," and "could trade but not vote," then defend two placements. *Success: all cards placed with a reason; two defenses name the rule that decides each case.* S4: review + week check — polis, citizen, assembly, council. *Success: learner explains, without notes, how an Athenian law was made (council proposes, assembly votes).* |
| 21 | 5–8 | **Comparing systems of government: Athens, Sparta, and beyond.** S5: Lesson 02 — Athens vs. Sparta: assemblies, councils, kings, ephors, ostracism, juries; Solon, Cleisthenes, Pericles; timeline work with [greek-timeline.svg](assets/greek-timeline.svg). *Success: learner compares the two systems on who decides, who is left out, and what each is good at — with one concrete detail per system.* S6: systems practice set ([practice.md](practice.md), Set B). *Success: ≥80% correct; the timeline arithmetic shows work.* S7: investigation — **deliberation lab**: the class runs a mini-assembly and a mini-council on the same proposal (build a new harbor storehouse or not), using Athenian procedure — council sets the agenda, assembly debates and votes by show of hands. *Success: both bodies follow their procedure; the learner states one way the two procedures changed the outcome.* S8: review + week check. *Success: learner explains ostracism in two sentences and names one limit of Athenian democracy.* |
| 22 | 9–12 | **Mediterranean exchange: goods, ideas, and trade networks.** S9: Lesson 03 — why Athens needed grain; what it sold (olive oil, wine, pottery, silver); ships, harbors, colonies, the Delian League; supply and demand in the grain trade. *Success: learner traces one cargo (grain in, oil out) across the map and explains one price change with supply and demand.* S10: trade practice set ([practice.md](practice.md), Set C) + grain-price table work. *Success: ≥80% correct; every price answer names the supply or demand change behind it.* S11: investigation — **the grain-vote simulation**: three fictional harvest years (good, bad, war) change the grain supply; teams set the agora grain price, predict who gains and who loses, and check against the price table. *Success: price set for each year with a supply/demand reason; one winner and one loser named per year.* S12: review + spiral check — one citizenship fact, one systems fact, one trade fact, from memory; quiz format previewed. *Success: learner names all three facts without notes.* |
| 23 | 13–16 | **Diffusion and weighing the legacy.** S13: Lesson 04 — how ideas traveled: alphabet, coinage, games, building styles; ships, roads, and colonies as carriers; the significance question. *Success: learner traces one idea's journey on the map and states why it mattered to the people who received it.* S14: diffusion practice set ([practice.md](practice.md), Set D) + **formative quiz** ([assessments/week-23_quiz.md](assessments/week-23_quiz.md)). *Success: ≥80% on the quiz; every explanation answer cites evidence.* S15: Lesson 05 + **project work session** — "The Grain Vote" ([project.md](project.md)): citizenship chart, deliberation record, trade map and ledger, who-gained/who-lost analysis, legacy reflection. *Success: project draft has all five parts; each part cites its evidence.* S16: unit review + **unit assessment** ([assessments/unit-assessment.md](assessments/unit-assessment.md)); project due. *Success: assessment complete; project submitted with rubric.* |

The project is launched in Week 21 S7 (the deliberation lab doubles as
the project warm-up) and completed through S15; it is due at S16.
