# Teacher Guide — Unit 05: Ancient Greece

**Adult only.** This guide contains session notes, misconception
alerts, and reteach moves. Solutions to every student question are
in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md) — do not hand the answer key to
the learner.

## Session-by-session notes

- **S1 (Lesson 01):** Open with the readiness check; learners who
  cannot name a city need and a decision method should re-read the
  "city and its farmland" paragraph before the map work. Show the
  agora image *after* asking what a marketplace and a meeting place
  would need — the observation task works better as a check than as
  a reveal. Stress the caption: AI illustration, not evidence. The
  citizenship rule is the unit's load-bearing idea; write it on a
  card and keep it visible all unit: "only a free adult man born to
  Athenian parents."
- **S2 (Set A + map):** Check Set A the same day. The common miss
  is A5 (why "about"): accept any answer naming the lack of a
  census and scholarly disagreement. For A7, insist on two reasons;
  one reason is not enough to earn full marks.
- **S3 (agora role-card investigation):** Pre-make 12 cards:
  citizen potter, citizen farmer, citizen merchant, woman weaver,
  enslaved cook, enslaved miner, metic trader, metic teacher,
  Spartan visitor, Black Sea grain merchant, ship captain, jury
  member. The trap cards are the Spartan visitor (not Athenian, so
  no vote) and the ship captain (vote only if he is an Athenian
  citizen — the card does not say, so the learner must ask). Praise
  learners who refuse to place a card without enough information.
- **S4 (Week 20 review):** Rebuild the law-making chain chorally
  (council proposes → assembly debates → assembly votes). Any
  learner who cannot order the chain needs S1–S3 re-teaching, not
  more new content.
- **S5 (Lesson 02):** The timeline is the lesson's tool: have the
  learner point to each date before computing. The common arithmetic
  slip is subtracting BCE dates the wrong way round (508 − 594
  instead of 594 − 508); remind them the earlier date is the larger
  number. The helot system must be named plainly as slavery at
  scale; do not soften it into "workers" or "servants."
- **S6 (Set B):** B6 is the unit's main arithmetic check; require
  shown work. B8 needs one strength and one weakness *with a
  concrete detail each* — a vague "Sparta was strong" earns no
  marks.
- **S7 (deliberation lab):** Run the mini-council first (5–6
  learners, one-year "term," agenda set by vote), then the
  mini-assembly (everyone, show of hands). The learning is in the
  *difference*: the council narrows the options, the assembly
  decides among them. Debrief with the question "Did the council's
  shortlist change what the assembly could choose?" If the assembly
  simply rubber-stamps the council, run it again with a second
  proposal the council rejected — the assembly cannot vote on what
  the council never proposed.
- **S8 (Week 21 review):** Re-check ostracism: 10-year exile, voted
  on a potsherd, aimed at too much power — not a criminal
  punishment. If a learner calls it a punishment for a crime,
  re-teach with Lesson 02's worked example B.
- **S9 (Lesson 03):** The grain-price table is fictional
  classroom-model data — say so out loud before the lesson, and
  again before the simulation. The Garnsey nuance matters: do not
  let the learner claim Athens was "totally dependent" on imported
  grain; the honest sentence is "much of its grain, and scholars
  debate how much." The Long Walls are the lesson's best image:
  city + harbor as one fort.
- **S10 (Set C):** C5 is the price-table check; every price answer
  must name the supply or demand change behind it. C7 is a trap
  (Athens imported grain, so banning exports would not lower the
  price) — praise learners who spot the trap.
- **S11 (grain-vote simulation):** Three rounds (good harvest, bad
  harvest, war). Each team sets the agora grain price from the
  fictional table, names one winner and one loser, and writes the
  supply/demand reason on a card. The adult checks the reason
  before the next round. Keep the numbers fictional and labeled.
- **S12 (Week 22 review):** Spiral check — one citizenship fact,
  one systems fact, one trade fact, from memory. Preview the quiz
  format (12 items, full sentences, about 20 minutes).
- **S13 (Lesson 04):** The alphabet's journey is the lesson's
  spine: Phoenicia → Greece → colonies → Italy → Rome → us. Have
  the learner trace it on the map with a finger before writing.
  The Thucydides passage is a labeled paraphrase — do not present
  it as a quotation, and do not let the learner quote it as one.
- **S14 (Set D + quiz):** Check Set D, then give the formative
  quiz ([assessments/week-23_quiz.md](assessments/week-23_quiz.md)).
  Score the same day; any item below 80% on the quiz gets a
  reteach note in the margin, not just a mark.
- **S15 (Lesson 05 + project work session):** Lesson 05 is the
  unit's moral center: hold the invention and the exclusion
  together. If a learner offers a single-sentence verdict ("Athens
  was good" or "Athens was bad"), ask for the second sentence.
  Then run the full project work session — the dossier's five parts
  should be drafted by the end of S15.
- **S16 (unit review + assessment + project due):** Give the unit
  assessment ([assessments/unit-assessment.md](assessments/unit-assessment.md)),
  collect the project, and close with the essential question:
  "Why did most of the people who lived in Athens have no vote,
  and what did the trade that fed the city cost everyone else?"
  A good closing answer names one excluded group and one cost.

## Misconception alerts

- "Everyone in Athens voted" — only adult male citizens, about
  one person in eight. Re-teach with Lesson 01, Worked example B.
- "A polis is just a city" — it is a city *plus* its farmland,
  ruling itself. Re-teach with the Lesson 01 map work.
- "The council made the laws" — the council proposed; the assembly
  decided. Re-teach with the S4 choral chain.
- "Ostracism was a punishment for a crime" — it was a political
  safety valve against too much power. Re-teach with Lesson 02.
- "Sparta had no assembly" — it had one, but it could not debate
  or propose. Re-teach with Lesson 02's comparison.
- "Athens grew its own grain" — it imported much of it; that is
  the point of Lesson 03. Re-teach with the cargo trace.
- "The Delian League was always an empire" — it began as a
  defensive alliance and became an empire; the treasury move in
  454 BCE is the marker. Re-teach with Lesson 03.
- "Triremes carried cargo" — they were warships that protected
  cargo ships. Re-teach with Lesson 03, Worked example A.
- "The Greeks invented the alphabet" — they adapted the Phoenician
  alphabet and added vowels. Re-teach with Lesson 04.
- "Famous means significant" — use the two-question test (changed
  how people lived? effects lasted?). Re-teach with Lesson 04,
  Worked example B.
- "A price cap fixes a shortage" — it can help buyers today and
  hurt supply tomorrow; accept either side of the Lesson 03 applied
  task if it names both a gainer and a loser.
- "No record means no opinion" — the absence of records from
  enslaved people, women, and allies is a gap in the evidence, not
  proof they agreed. Re-teach with Lesson 05, Worked example B.

## Reteach moves

- **Citizenship rule not secure:** return to Lesson 01, Worked
  example B; run three new cases (a 17-year-old Athenian-born boy,
  a freed enslaved man, a metic's Athenian-born son) and sort them
  together. Note: the 17-year-old cannot vote yet (must be an
  adult); the freed man and the metic's son are the hard cases —
  the honest answer is that the sources are unclear and the adult
  should say so, not guess.
- **BCE arithmetic not secure:** return to Unit 01, Lesson 05 and
  the timeline; count intervals on the number line with a finger
  before subtracting; remind the learner that the earlier date is
  the larger number.
- **Supply and demand not secure:** return to Lesson 03, Worked
  example B; run the two cases again with the learner supplying
  the "supply changed or demand changed?" question before the
  answer.
- **Claim-building not secure:** return to Lesson 05, Worked
  example A; build the claim together on paper (judgment →
  evidence → counter-evidence → limit → revised claim), then have
  the learner build one alone on a new question.
- **Quiz below 80%:** reteach the missed strand (citizenship,
  systems, trade, or diffusion) the same week; do not move on to
  the next unit until the strand is secure.

## Sensitive-content guidance

- Slavery and exclusion are named plainly and briefly in every
  lesson where they belong: who was forced, who decided, who was
  left out. No graphic detail, no glorification, no debate about
  whether slavery existed.
- If a learner is distressed, stay factual, keep it brief, and move
  to the next task (usually the claim-building or map task) — do not
  linger on the distressing content and do not ask the learner to
  imagine themselves as an enslaved person.
- Belief systems are described, never evaluated; the Olympic games
  and temples are taught as civic and religious practice, not as
  beliefs to judge.
- The Delian League's turn into empire and the Peloponnesian War
  are taught as choices with costs, not as inevitable glory.
  Athens is praised for its invention and criticized for its limits
  in the same lesson — keep both sentences visible.
