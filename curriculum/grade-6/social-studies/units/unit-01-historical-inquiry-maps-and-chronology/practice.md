# Unit 01 — Practice Sets

**Unit:** [U01 — Historical Inquiry: Archaeology, Maps, and Chronology](README.md) · **Grade 6**

These sets serve the S2/S6/S10/S14 practice sessions. Work guided items
with the adult first, then independent items alone. Every answer needs a
reason — a cited question type, map feature, source clue, or computation.

**Dataset note:** Set B uses
[`resources/un_countries.csv`](../../../../../resources/un_countries.csv).
Use only these columns: `name_common`, `cca2`, `cca3`, `region`,
`subregion`, `capital`, `area_km2`. The `population`, `lat`, and `lng`
columns are **empty** — never use them, and never guess their values.

---

## Set A — Turning curiosity into questions (Week 3, S2)

*Lesson 01. Guided: 1–3. Independent: 4–10.*

1. **(Guided)** Sort: compelling, supporting, or not a history question?
   a. "Why did writing develop in some societies but not others?"
   b. "How many people lived in ancient Rome?"
   c. "What was the coolest ancient building?"
2. **(Guided)** "What happened in ancient Egypt?" — explain why this fails
   as a compelling question, then fix it.
3. **(Guided)** For the compelling question "How did trade change what
   ancient people believed?", name two supporting questions and the source
   kind that could answer each.
4. Label each: compelling (C), supporting (S), or not a history question
   (N). a. "Why did some cities last for centuries?" b. "Which river is
   the longest in Africa?" c. "Did ancient kids like school?"
5. Reword into a question history can answer: "Which ancient god was the
   strongest?"
6. Write one compelling question about the ancient world that you genuinely
   wonder about.
7. Write three supporting questions for it.
8. For one supporting question, name the source kind that would answer it
   (map, dig, document, object, oral account).
9. Explain in 2–3 sentences how your supporting questions help answer your
   compelling question.
10. Error analysis: "My supporting questions are: Why did Rome fall? Why
    did empires end? What causes collapse?" What is wrong, and how would
    you fix one of them into a true supporting question?

## Set B — Measuring and locating on maps (Week 4, S6)

*Lesson 03. Use the [practice map](assets/map-grid-scale.svg) for 1–7 and
the CSV for 8–10. Guided: 1–3. Independent: 4–10.*

1. **(Guided)** Locate on the practice map: the town, the well, the market,
   the mountain. Say each grid label aloud (letter first, then number).
2. **(Guided)** Measure the town-to-well road with the scale bar. How many
   1-km segments does it span? What is the distance?
3. **(Guided)** Make an evidence claim about the river using the key and
   the grid, pointing to the features that prove it.
4. Measure the straight-line town-to-market distance with the scale bar.
   Show your segment count.
5. Estimate the straight-line market-to-mountain distance. (Count whole
   kilometers, then estimate the leftover. A straight line is shorter than
   any winding path.)
6. Name two things the practice map **cannot** show, and explain why each
   is a limit for a historian.
7. A learner says the map proves the mountain is the most important
   feature because its symbol is biggest. Explain the error.
8. Open `un_countries.csv`. List every country whose `subregion` is
   exactly `Northern Africa`. (Count them.)
9. In the CSV, find the `capital` of Japan, Egypt, Peru, and Norway.
10. In the CSV, which has the larger `area_km2`: Argentina or India? By
    about how many square kilometers do they differ? (Show the
    subtraction.)

## Set C — Classifying sources and judging credibility (Week 5, S10)

*Lesson 04. Guided: 1–3. Independent: 4–10.*

1. **(Guided)** Classify as artifact, document, image, or oral account, and
   name one limit of each: a stone arrowhead; a king's decree carved on a
   wall; a 1920s street photograph; an elder's harvest story.
2. **(Guided)** Question: "What did children play with in the ancient
   town?" Rank by credibility *for this question*: a clay toy horse, a tax
   list, a grandmother's childhood-games story. One reason per rank.
3. **(Guided)** Mystery source: a painted pot shows dancers around a fire —
   no signature, no date. Infer a plausible maker and audience, one
   in-source clue each. Use "probably."
4. Classify: a bronze coin with a ruler's face; a sailor's diary; a cave
   painting of a hunt; a folk song about a flood.
5. For the bronze coin: one thing it *can* tell a historian, one thing it
   *cannot*.
6. Question: "Was the harvest good this year?" Which is more credible — a
   farmer's diary entry or a royal monument reading "the king brings
   endless plenty"? Use **relevance** and **intended use**.
7. A short letter begins "Dear Mother" and describes a sea voyage. Infer
   maker and audience with one clue each.
8. A stone tablet lists laws ending "...so that the strong do not harm the
   weak." Infer the intended audience and what the tablet was made for.
9. All surviving sources about a battle are victory songs of the winners.
   Name two limits of this record and one source kind that could fill a
   gap.
10. Error analysis: "This source is 3,000 years old, so it must be true."
    Explain the error using **intended use**.

## Set D — Sequencing and dating (Week 6, S14)

*Lesson 05. Guided: 1–3. Independent: 4–10. Use the
[BCE/CE timeline](assets/bce-ce-timeline.svg) and the
[dig illustration](assets/fictional-dig-trench.png) (or its text-only
alternative in Lesson 05).*

1. **(Guided)** Order oldest to youngest: 3000 BCE, 1500 BCE, 776 BCE,
   1 CE, 2026 CE. Explain the BCE direction rule.
2. **(Guided)** Compute 2500 BCE → 1500 BCE. Show the subtraction and name
   the rule.
3. **(Guided)** Compute 44 BCE → 44 CE. Show both steps and explain why
   the naive add is wrong.
4. Order oldest to youngest: 2026 CE, 2000 BCE, 500 CE, 1200 BCE, 1 BCE.
5. Compute: 3000 BCE → 1000 BCE. Show your work.
6. Compute: 2000 BCE → 1000 CE. Show both steps.
7. A learner says "1500 BCE was 1500 years ago." Explain the error and give
   the correct number of years ago (as of 2026 CE).
8. From the dig illustration: list the five layers top to bottom, then
   order the bone, the coin-like disk, and the pottery shard oldest to
   youngest, citing the layer rule.
9. Could the coin-like disk be younger than the pottery shard even though
   it lies deeper? Describe one realistic way.
10. Write three sentences: what layers tell you for sure (order), what they
    cannot tell you (exact dates), and what **context** adds beyond the
    object alone.
