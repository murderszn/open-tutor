# Lesson 01 — Compelling Questions and Supporting Questions

**Unit:** [U01 — Historical Inquiry: Archaeology, Maps, and Chronology](../README.md) · **Week 3**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 1 of 16

## Goal

You will write a **compelling question** about the past, list
**supporting questions** that help answer it, and explain how the supporting
questions and the compelling question help each other.

## Materials

- Notebook (your **inquiry notebook** — you will keep this all year) and pencil
- One curiosity: something about the ancient world you genuinely wonder about

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult reads three questions. You answer aloud: "big and arguable,"
"small and answerable," or "not a history question."

1. "Why did some ancient peoples build big cities while others kept
   moving?" → *big and arguable* (people could disagree; it needs evidence)
2. "In what year was the first Olympic games traditionally held?" → *small
   and answerable* (one fact answers it)
3. "What is the best ancient civilization?" → *not a history question*
   ("best" is an opinion word — history cannot measure it)

If #3 felt tricky, that is exactly what this lesson teaches: opinions are
not questions history can answer.

## Teaching

### Part A — Two kinds of historical questions

Historians start with curiosity, but not every curiosity makes a good
historical question. There are two kinds that work together.

A **compelling question** is big, open, and worth arguing about. It has no
single correct answer. People can disagree about it, and the disagreement is
settled with evidence — not with shouting. Compelling questions are about
*key ideas* in history: why things changed, what mattered most, how people
saw the world.

A **supporting question** is smaller and answerable. One fact, one map, or
one source can answer it. Supporting questions do the *legwork*: each answer
is a stepping stone toward the big question.

Here is the key relationship: **supporting questions and compelling
questions help each other.** The compelling question decides which supporting
questions are worth asking (you do not research everything — only what serves
the big question). And the supporting questions make the compelling question
answerable (without facts, the big question is just an opinion). Neither
works alone.

**Worked example 1 — one compelling question, three supporting questions.**

Compelling question: *"Why did some ancient peoples build cities while
others kept moving?"*

*Thinking:* This is compelling because it is big (it is about a turning
point in human history), open (there is no single answer — climate, food,
and safety all matter), and arguable (two historians could weigh the causes
differently and both use evidence). It represents a key idea: how
environments and choices shape ways of life.

Supporting questions:
1. "Where did the earliest cities appear?" — *Answerable:* maps of river
   valleys (Tigris–Euphrates, Nile, Indus, Yellow River) show the places.
2. "What did a city need that a moving camp did not?" — *Answerable:*
   farming surplus, stored food, and defense; sources on early farming give
   the list.
3. "What did people give up when they settled down?" — *Answerable:* time
   for heavy labor, exposure to new diseases, less varied diets; the answers
   come from skeletons and tools archaeologists found.

*How they help each other:* the compelling question keeps the research
focused (we ask about *cities vs. moving*, not about everything ancient).
Each supporting answer becomes one piece of the big answer: *where* cities
arose, *what* made them possible, *what* they cost. Facts without the big
question are trivia; the big question without facts is just a guess.

**Worked example 2 — fixing a bad question.**

Question: *"What is the best ancient civilization?"*

*Thinking:* "Best" is an opinion word — no map, dig, or document measures
"best." This is not a question history can answer. But the curiosity behind
it is real: the learner wonders what made some civilizations powerful or
long-lasting. Keep the curiosity; change the question.

Compelling reword: *"What made some ancient cities last for centuries while
others faded quickly?"*

*Thinking:* Now it is big (about change over time), open (many possible
causes), and arguable — and it can be answered with evidence. Supporting
questions follow naturally: "Which cities lasted longest?" (answerable from
timelines), "What did long-lasting cities have in common?" (answerable from
maps and ruins), "What happened when a city faded?" (answerable from layers
in a dig).

### Part B — How experts use questions

Historians do not all ask different questions about the same topic — they
usually **agree on which questions are worth asking**, even when they
disagree on the answers. When experts agree that "why did cities arise where
they did?" is a key question, that agreement tells you the question
represents a **key idea in the field** — not just one person's curiosity.
Your compelling questions should aim at those key ideas: change, causes,
perspectives, and evidence.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Sort each question: **compelling**, **supporting**, or **not a history
   question**.
   - *Hint: compelling = big, open, arguable; supporting = small, one fact
     answers it; "best/worst/favorite" = opinion, not history.*
   a. "How did the Nile River shape life in ancient Egypt?"
   b. "What year did the Roman Empire reach its largest size?"
   c. "Which ancient food tasted the yummiest?"
   d. "Why did writing develop in some societies but not others?"
2. Take question 1d above. Name **two supporting questions** whose answers
   would help answer it, and say what kind of source could answer each one.
   - *Hint: start with "where" and "what" questions — they are the easiest
     supporting questions to find sources for.*
3. Error analysis: a learner writes, "My compelling question is: What
   happened in ancient Egypt?" Explain what is wrong and fix it.
   - *Hint: "what happened" is too big to be useful — it is not a question,
     it is a topic. A compelling question needs a direction: why, how, or
     what mattered.*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Label each: compelling, supporting, or not a history question.
   a. "Why did trade networks change what people believed?"
   b. "How many wonders of the ancient world were there?"
   c. "Were the pyramids cooler than the Great Wall?"
2. Write one **compelling question** about the ancient world that you
   genuinely wonder about.
3. Write **three supporting questions** for your compelling question.
4. For one of your supporting questions, name the kind of source that could
   answer it (a map? a dig? a written record? an object?).
5. Explain in two or three sentences: how do your supporting questions help
   answer your compelling question?
6. Reword this into a question history can answer: "What was the most
   awesome ancient invention?"
7. A learner asks, "Did ancient people have pets?" Is this compelling,
   supporting, or neither? Explain in one sentence.
8. Error analysis: "My supporting questions are: Why did Rome fall? Why did
   the Maya cities empty? Why did empires end?" What is wrong here, and what
   would make them better supporting questions?

## Application — the inquiry notebook

Open your **inquiry notebook** to a fresh page and title it "Unit 01 —
Questions." Copy your compelling question and your three supporting
questions from independent practice onto this page, and add one sentence for
each supporting question: *what source kind would answer it?* The adult
dates the page. You will add to this notebook in every lesson of this unit —
questions are the thread that ties the whole unit together.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. In your own words, what is the difference between a compelling question
   and a supporting question?
2. Why can't a compelling question be answered without supporting
   questions?

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** question words are color-coded if helpful
  (compelling = red, supporting = blue); the adult reads practice items
  aloud on request; home-language question drafts welcome in the notebook.
- **Alternate response mode:** sorts may be done by placing printed question
  cards into three labeled piles; answers may be dictated for the adult to
  scribe; drawings may stand in for one supporting question (e.g., a map
  sketch for a "where" question).
- **Extension:** take your compelling question and find the *key idea* it
  represents — is it about change over time, about causes, about different
  perspectives, or about evidence? Write one sentence defending your choice.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners label any question with "why" as
compelling — press for *arguability*: could two people with evidence
disagree? If yes, it is compelling. (2) "Best/worst/favorite" questions keep
returning — treat the reword as the skill, and praise keeping the curiosity
while changing the question. (3) Learners write supporting questions that are
actually second compelling questions (see independent practice #8) — the fix
is the "one fact answers it" test. **Answer key:**
