# Lesson 04 — Sources and Their Limits

**Unit:** [U01 — Historical Inquiry: Archaeology, Maps, and Chronology](../README.md) · **Week 5**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 9 of 16

## Goal

You will sort historical sources into **four kinds**, judge a source's
**credibility** from its relevance and intended use, and **infer** a source's
maker, date, or audience from clues inside it.

## Materials

- Notebook (inquiry notebook) and pencil
- Four "mystery source" cards (the adult prepares these from the worked
  examples, or prints the descriptions below)

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

Answer aloud:

1. Name the two sources from Lesson 02 (the sketch and the note). Which one
   is a map, and which one is a written record?
2. "A source with no named creator is useless." Agree or disagree — one

If #2 split the room, good: this lesson is about exactly that question.

## Teaching

### Part A — Four kinds of historical sources

Historians sort sources into kinds because each kind can answer different
questions — and each kind has built-in limits.

1. **Artifacts** — made or used objects: a clay pot, a stone tool, a coin,
   a bone needle. *Can tell:* what people made, what materials they had,
   how skilled their craft was. *Cannot tell:* people's names, what they
   believed, or why they did things — objects do not talk.
2. **Documents** — written records: a letter, a tax list, a law, a diary.
   *Can tell:* what someone chose to write down, in their own words.
   *Cannot tell:* what was never written down — and the writer may have had
   reasons to bend the truth.
3. **Images** — pictures of the past: a cave painting, a carving, a
   photograph. *Can tell:* what something looked like, what the maker chose
   to show. *Cannot tell:* what happened just before or after, or what the
   maker left out of the frame.
4. **Oral accounts** — history passed down by speaking: family stories,
   elders' memories, recorded interviews. *Can tell:* how people remember
   and explain their own past. *Cannot tell (alone):* exact dates or
   details — memory shifts over time, and each teller shapes the story.

No kind is "best." A strong historical claim uses **more than one kind**,
so the limits of one are covered by the strengths of another.

**Worked example 1 — classify and name the limits.**
Four sources about a fictional ancient marketplace:
a. A broken clay scale weight stamped with a mark.
b. A merchant's letter complaining that "the new tax is unfair."
c. A wall carving showing the market full of happy traders.
d. A grandmother's story about her grandfather selling grain there.

*Thinking:* (a) is an **artifact** — it proves people weighed goods here,
but not what they thought about taxes. (b) is a **document** — it gives one
merchant's view in his own words, but he is angry about a tax, so his
purpose may color his claims. (c) is an **image** — it shows what the
carver wanted viewers to see (a thriving market — perhaps carved to please
the ruler who paid for it). (d) is an **oral account** — it carries family
memory across generations, but details may have shifted. Each one alone is
thin; together they start to tell a fuller story.

### Part B — Credibility: relevance and intended use

A source is not simply "reliable" or "unreliable" — it is **credible for a
particular question**. Credibility comes from two checks:

- **Relevance:** does this source actually speak to *my* question? A tax
  list is highly relevant to "what did people buy?" and nearly useless for
  "what did people believe?"
- **Intended use:** what was this source *made for*? A law carved in stone
  was made to be obeyed and admired — it tells you what rulers wanted, not
  necessarily what people did. A shopping list was made to remember items —
  it is plain, private, and less likely to exaggerate.

**Worked example 2 — judge credibility for a question.**
Question: *"Did ordinary people in the fictional market town resent the new
tax?"*
Source A: the merchant's angry letter. Source B: the royal tax law carved in
stone. Source C: the grandmother's story.
*Thinking:* For *this* question, the letter (A) is the most relevant and
credible: it is an ordinary person's own words about the tax. The carved law
(B) is relevant to what the tax *was*, but its intended use was to display
royal power — it cannot tell us how people *felt*. The story (C) is relevant
but weaker: it passed through generations of retelling. So: A > C > B *for
this question*. For a different question — "what did the law say?" — the
order flips, and B wins. Credibility is always *for a question*, never in
general.

### Part C — Inferring maker, date, and audience from clues

Often a source arrives with no label: no signature, no date. Historians then
play detective **using other sources** — comparing the mystery item against
things already known to infer a plausible maker, date, place of origin, or
intended audience.

**Worked example 3 — the mystery receipt.**
A clay tablet lists "3 baskets of grain, 2 jars of oil" in wedge-shaped
marks. No name, no date.
*Thinking:* Clues: the wedge-shaped marks match the writing system of
ancient Mesopotamia (known from other tablets). Clay tablets with grain
lists are typical of temple or palace storehouses — the **maker** was
probably a record-keeping scribe, not the farmer. The **audience** was
probably the storehouse overseer who checked deliveries — this was an
internal note, not a public monument. The **date** is a range, not a year:
"whenever this writing system and this kind of record-keeping were in use"
— other dated tablets narrow it down. Notice what we did *not* do: we did
not guess a year, and we said "probably" — inferences from clues are
plausible, not certain.

### Part D — Detecting limits in the historical record

Now the hard question: what is **missing**? If every surviving source about
the market town was made by the palace — tax lists, royal carvings, the
king's letters — then the whole record has one perspective. The farmers, the
porters, the enslaved workers: their voices are absent **not because they
had nothing to say**, but because nobody with power wrote their words down
(or their words were written on materials that rotted away).

**Worked example 4 — spot the missing voices.**
The record: three palace tax lists and one royal carving of a thriving
market.
*Thinking:* Kinds present: documents (the lists) and one image (the
carving). Kinds missing: artifacts of daily life, oral accounts. Makers:
all palace-connected — the intended audience was the court. Limits: we can
say what the palace *collected* and what it *wanted shown*; we cannot say
how traders felt, whether the poor ate, or what the market sounded like.
Next inquiry step: look for a different kind — a midden (ancient trash pit)
full of broken pots and bones (artifacts), or a traveler's letter (a
document from outside the palace). One perspective is a clue about power,
not a picture of the whole town.

*Revisit:* the Lesson 02 source note had the same problem in miniature —
two requests, both from people who write to councils. Whose voices were
missing there? (The path's other users — children, elders, workers who
never wrote in.)

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Classify each as **artifact, document, image, or oral account**, and name
   one thing it *cannot* tell us:
   a. A stone arrowhead found in a field.
   b. A king's decree carved on a wall.
   c. A photograph of a 1920s street.
   d. An elder describing how her village celebrated harvests.
   - *Hint: the limit is the flip side of the kind — what does this kind
     never include?*
2. Question: "What did children play with in the ancient town?" Rank these
   three sources by credibility *for this question* and give one reason per
   rank: (i) a small clay toy horse, (ii) a tax list, (iii) a grandmother's
   story about childhood games.
   - *Hint: relevance first — which source actually touches children or
     play?*
3. Mystery source: a painted pot shows people dancing around a fire. No
   signature, no date. Infer a plausible **maker** and **audience** with one
   in-source clue for each.
   - *Hint: who makes painted pots? Who would see the painting — where
     would the pot be used? Say "probably" and give your clue.*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Classify: a bronze coin with a ruler's face; a sailor's diary; a cave
   painting of a hunt; a folk song about a flood. (Four labels.)
2. For the bronze coin, name one thing it *can* tell a historian and one
   thing it *cannot*.
3. Question: "Was the harvest good this year in the ancient village?" Which
   is more credible: a farmer's diary entry, or a royal monument claiming
   "the king brings endless plenty"? Explain using **relevance** and
   **intended use**.
4. A source note says: "The council received two requests." (Lesson 02.)
   Classify the note as a document — then name one limit that comes from its
   **intended use** (what was it made for?).
5. Mystery source: a short letter begins "Dear Mother" and describes a long
   sea voyage. Infer the **maker** and **audience** with one clue each.
6. Mystery source: a stone tablet lists laws and ends "...so that the
   strong do not harm the weak." Infer the **intended audience** and explain
   what the tablet was *made for*.
7. The surviving sources about a battle are all victory songs sung by the
   winning side. Name **two limits** of this record and **one source kind**
   that could fill a gap.
8. Inquiry notebook: pick your Lesson 01 compelling question. List the
   **kinds of sources** that would be most helpful in answering it, and name
   one perspective the sources might miss. (D1.5.6-8 and D3.1.6-8: you are
   planning an inquiry and guiding your source selection.)

## Application — source-set audit

The adult gives you a set of 4–6 real or fictional sources on one topic
(the [Resource Pack](../resource-pack.md) lists where to find
age-appropriate source sets, or the adult assembles one). Build a table:

| Source | Kind | What it can tell us | One limit | Credible for which question? |

Then write three sentences: which perspective dominates the set, whose voice
is missing, and what source kind you would seek next. Bring the table to the
next session.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. Name the four source kinds and one limit of each.
2. Why do we judge a source's credibility *for a particular question*
   instead of calling it reliable or unreliable in general?
3. Give one example of a "missing voice" in a historical record and explain
   why it might be missing.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "Old = true" — learners trust ancient
sources more; press on *intended use* (a 3,000-year-old royal boast is still
a boast). (2) Credibility as a fixed trait — keep returning to "*for which
question?*"; the same source tops the ranking for one question and sinks
for another. (3) "No signature = useless" — redirect to clue-hunting; the
mystery receipt shows how much can be inferred, and "probably" is honest
historian language. **Answer key:**
