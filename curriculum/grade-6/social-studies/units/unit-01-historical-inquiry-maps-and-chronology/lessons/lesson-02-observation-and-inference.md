# Lesson 02 — Observation, Inference, and What a Source Cannot Tell You

**Unit:** [U01 — Historical Inquiry: Archaeology, Maps, and Chronology](../README.md) · **Week 3**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 3 of 16

> This lesson keeps and reuses the track's starter lesson ("Ask what a map
> and source can show"): the fictional town sketch and source-note activities
> below are the starter's core. The starter's "Answer checks" have been moved
> to the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and [answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Goal

You will study a fictional map and a fictional source note, separate
**observations** from **inferences**, and name what each one **cannot**
tell you.

## Materials

- Notebook (your inquiry notebook) and pencil
- The fictional town sketch and source note below (printed or read aloud —
  no internet, no real addresses, nothing political)

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

Answer aloud. The adult shows you the town sketch.

1. Point to the clinic on the sketch. What is southwest of the library?
2. "The sketch proves the walk from the school to the market is short."
   Observation or inference — and how do you know?

If #2 felt hard, this lesson is exactly about that trap.

## Teaching

### Part A — The fictional town sketch and source note

Study these. They are **fictional** — a made-up town for practicing
historian skills, not a real place.

```text
Fictional town sketch — not to scale
N ↑
┌────────────────────┐
│ School     Library  │
│     Main Road       │
│ Clinic     Market   │
└────────────────────┘
```

Fictional source note: *"The town council received two requests to improve
the path near the clinic. The note gives no information about who made the
requests, how often the path is used, or what an improvement would cost."*

Every **source** — a map, a note, a photo, an object — has three things to
ask about:

1. **Creator:** who made it? (Sometimes the source does not say.)
2. **Purpose:** why was it made? What was it *for*?
3. **Limits:** what can it not tell you?

A map can show **relative location** — what is near what, and in which
direction. But *this* sketch does not show **scale** (how far things really
are), **path conditions** (is the path paved? muddy? lit?), or **travel
time**. An **inference** — an idea about what something *means* — must be
tied to an actual clue. A guess floating free of clues is not an inference;
it is just a guess.

**Worked example 1 — observation vs. inference on the sketch.**
Observation: *"The clinic is southwest of the library in the sketch."* —
the sketch directly shows this: clinic is below-left, library is
above-right, and the N arrow sets the directions.
Inference: *"The clinic may be busy."* — the sketch gives **no evidence**
about how many people visit. Nothing on the sketch counts visitors. This
inference has no clue behind it, so a historian would not make it.

**Worked example 2 — observation vs. inference on the note.**
The note directly **states** that two requests were received — that is an
observation; it is right there in the text. But the note does **not** say
who made the requests, so *"the town council wrote the note"* would be an
unsupported inference (the council is *mentioned*, but the creator is not
named — do not assume). And the note does **not** establish that *only* two
people care, or that the proposed improvement will work. "Everyone wants a
path change" goes far beyond what the note states.

### Part B — What the sources cannot tell you

Read the note's limits sentence again: it gives no information about *who*
made the requests, *how often* the path is used, or *what an improvement
would cost*. Those are the note's **limits**. Good historians name limits
out loud, because limits decide what you may claim — and what you still need
to find out.

The sketch's limits: no scale, no distances, no path surface, no traffic,
no travel time. So "the map proves the clinic path is short" is wrong —
the map cannot prove anything about length, because length was never drawn
to a scale.

When you know a source's limits, you know your **next inquiry step**: what
evidence to gather next, and whose perspective to ask. Useful evidence here
would include a count of path users, a safety and accessibility check of the
path, a cost estimate — and, importantly, perspectives from *different*
users of the path, not just the two who wrote in. Never assume a group
speaks with one voice.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Is the creator of the source note named? Identify **one detail** the note
   directly states.
   - *Hint: "directly states" means the words are right there — you do not
     have to guess.*
2. Name **two details** the sketch does not provide.
   - *Hint: think scale, conditions, and time — what would you need to plan
     a real walk?*
3. A learner says the map proves the clinic path is short. Identify the
   missing map information that makes this claim fail.
   - *Hint: what three things about distance did the sketch leave out?*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Label each as **observation** or **inference**, and name the clue (or the
   missing clue):
   a. "Two requests were received."
   b. "Everyone wants a path change."
   c. "The market is east of the clinic in the sketch."
   d. "The path near the clinic is dangerous."
2. The note mentions the town council. Does that mean the town council wrote
   the note? Explain in one sentence.
3. List **two limits** of the source note (two things it cannot tell you).
4. List **two limits** of the sketch (two things it cannot show).
5. Suggest **two pieces of evidence** to gather before the council decides
   about the path.
6. Name **one perspective** (whose voice) the council should hear besides
   the two people who wrote requests, and say why.
7. Error analysis: "The sketch shows the school is north of Main Road, so
   students must walk far to get to school." What is right and what is wrong
   in this sentence?
8. Add to your inquiry notebook: write one **supporting question** about the
   town's path that a *count* could answer, and one that an *interview*
   could answer. (D1.5.6-8: you are deciding which *kinds of sources* would
   answer your questions.)

## Application — the one-page recommendation

Make a **one-page recommendation** for the town council: should they
improve the path, wait, or study more first? Your page must include:
(1) one observation from the sketch, (2) one observation from the note,
(3) one limit of each source, and (4) two pieces of evidence you would
gather next, including whose perspective you would ask. There is no single
correct recommendation — your evidence and reasoning are what count. The
adult reviews it with the 2-1-0 rubric below.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. Give one **observation** and one **inference** about the sketch or the
   note, and say which is which.
2. Name one **limit** of either source.
3. What is your **next inquiry step** — what would you find out before
   deciding about the path?

## Review rubric (adult use)

| Criterion | 2 — shows skill | 1 — developing | 0 — not yet observed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Observation/inference | Separates direct information from a conclusion | Labels with support | Model and relabel one statement |
| Source limits | Identifies missing context | Names one limit | Prompt with "what don't we know?" |
| Inquiry | Gathers evidence from more than one relevant perspective | Suggests one useful check | Use who/what/how question stems |

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** the adult may read the sketch description and
  note aloud; enlarge the sketch or build a tactile layout (raised lines for
  roads, textured shapes for buildings); home-language labels welcome.
- **Alternate response mode:** all practice may be answered orally, by
  pointing at the sketch, or by sorting statement cards into
  "observation"/"inference" piles; the adult scribes dictated answers; the
  recommendation may be a labeled drawing or a spoken presentation.
- **Extension:** rewrite the source note so that it *does* answer two of its
  current limits — then explain how the new note changes what the council can
  decide. What did you have to invent, and why does that matter?

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "The council is mentioned, so the council
wrote it" — press for what the text *directly states* vs. what is assumed;
the creator is unnamed. (2) Learners treat the sketch as scaled evidence —
the "not to scale" label is the whole point; ask "where is the scale bar?"
until the habit sticks. (3) "Everyone" language — any claim about a whole
group from two requests is unsupported; this is the seed of the
representativeness idea used all year. **Answer key:**
