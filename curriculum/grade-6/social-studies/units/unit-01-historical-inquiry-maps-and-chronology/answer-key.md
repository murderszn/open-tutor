# Unit 01 — Answer Key

**For the guiding adult only.** Every solution below was solved independently
and reconciled with the student-facing questions (same numbers and labels).
For open responses, sample reasoning and acceptable variation are given —
accept any answer that meets the stated criterion.

## Lesson 01 — Compelling Questions and Supporting Questions

### Guided practice

1. a. **Compelling** — big, open, arguable; many factors could be weighed
   with evidence. b. **Supporting** — one fact (a year) answers it.
   c. **Not a history question** — "yummiest" is an opinion word.
   d. **Compelling** — big, open, arguable across societies.
2. Sample: "Where did the earliest writing systems appear?" (answerable from
   maps of finds — source kind: map/artifact record); "What did the earliest
   writing record — lists, stories, laws?" (source kind: documents/tablets).
   Accept any two small, answerable questions with a plausible source kind.
3. "What happened in ancient Egypt?" is a **topic**, not a directed question
   — too big to guide inquiry. Fix, e.g.: "How did the Nile River shape life
   in ancient Egypt?" or "Why did Egyptian civilization last for thousands
   of years?"

### Independent practice

1. a. **Compelling** (big, open, arguable). b. **Supporting** (one fact).
   c. **Not a history question** ("cooler" is opinion).
2. Open. Accept any big, open, arguable question about the ancient world
   (e.g., "Why did some cities last for centuries?").
3. Open. Each must be smaller and answerable by one fact or source.
4. Open. The named kind must plausibly answer that supporting question.
5. Sample: "My supporting questions break the big question into pieces I can
   actually research. Each answer becomes part of the big answer — without
   them, my compelling question is just a guess."
6. Acceptable reword, e.g.: "Which ancient invention spread the most
   widely, and what is the evidence?" — any reword replacing "awesome" with
   something evidence can answer.
7. **Supporting** — it is small and answerable (burials with animals, art,
   and texts give evidence); it is not big/arguable.
8. All three are **compelling** questions, not supporting — each is big and
   arguable. Fix one, e.g.: "Why did Rome fall?" → "In what year did the
   western Roman Empire traditionally end?" (answerable: 476 CE) or "Name
   three problems Rome faced in the 400s CE."

### Exit check

1. A compelling question is big, open, and arguable with no single answer;
   a supporting question is smaller and answerable — one fact or source
   answers it.
2. Without supporting questions there are no facts to build on — the
   compelling question would be only an opinion or a guess.

   words.)

### Independent practice

   (unsupported)** — the note says two requests, not "everyone." c.
   **Observation** — the sketch shows the market east (right) of the clinic

   safety data.
2. No. The council is *mentioned*, but the creator is not named — do not

3. Any two: who made the requests; how often the path is used; what an

   traffic.
5. Any two sensible: a count of daily path users; a safety/accessibility

6. Open. Sample: children walking to school (or elders, clinic patients,

7. Right: the school is north of Main Road (the sketch shows this). Wrong:

### Exit check

   the sketch." Inference: "The path may be muddy when it rains" (no clue

2. Open. Sample: "The note cannot tell us who made the requests."

## Lesson 03 — Maps as Evidence

### Guided practice

1. a. Town: **A1**. b. Market: **A3**. c. Mountain: **E4**. (Letter first,
   then number.)
2. The road spans **4** one-kilometer segments → **4 km**. (200 px at
   50 px/km; it also runs 2 grid columns east, and each column is 2 km.)
3. Open. Sample: "The river passes through grid squares B4 and D3" — the
   key says the blue line is the river; the grid names the squares. Accept
   any two squares the river crosses (A4, B4, B3, C3, D3, E3).
4. Every projection must distort shape, size, distance, or direction. The
   Mercator projection kept compass directions straight for sailors by
   stretching areas near the poles — so Greenland *looks* as big as Africa
   while Africa is actually about 14 times larger in land area
   (~30.4M km² vs ~2.2M km²).

### Independent practice

1. From A1, walk **east** two grid squares: A1 → B1 → C1.
2. Town (A1) to market (A3): straight south, **4** segments → **4 km**.
3. Tie: the **town–well** and **town–market** pairs are both 200 px = 4 km
   apart — the closest pairs on the map. (Well–market is ~5.7 km;
   market–mountain ~8.2 km.) Accept either pair with the measurement.
4. Open. Sample: "The mountain (triangle, per the key) is at E4, in the
   southeast corner of the map."
5. Any three: people; weather; sounds; what happened there (history);
   what places look like at ground level.
6. Symbol size is a **drawing choice**, not a measure of importance. The key
   defines what a symbol *means*; "most important" is a judgment, not a map
   reading.
7. The Earth is round. Flattening it must stretch something — shape, size,
   distance, or direction cannot all be preserved at once.
8. Open. Sample: "Where were the earliest cities?" — needs the scale bar
   (distances), the grid (locations), and symbols (city marks).

### Exit check

1. Title (what the map is about); key/legend (what symbols mean); compass
   rose (direction); scale bar (real distances); grid (exact locating);
   symbols (stand for real things).
2. The Earth is round, so every flat world map must stretch shape, size,
   distance, or direction — a projection is always a trade.

## Lesson 04 — Sources and Their Limits

### Guided practice

1. a. **Artifact** — cannot tell names, beliefs, or reasons. b. **Document**
   — cannot tell what was never written; the writer may bend the truth.
   c. **Image** — cannot tell what happened before/after or what was left
   out of the frame. d. **Oral account** — cannot alone give exact dates;
   memory shifts over retellings.
2. For "what did children play with": **1st — clay toy horse** (most
   relevant: an actual toy); **2nd — grandmother's story** (relevant to
   games, but passed through retellings); **3rd — tax list** (irrelevant to
   play). Relevance decides the order.
3. Open. Sample — maker: *probably* a potter (painted pots are skilled craft;
   the painting is the clue). Audience: *probably* the household or guests
   who would see the pot where it was used (pots are seen in daily life —
   the visible painting is the clue). Accept any plausible pair with
   in-source clues and "probably."

### Independent practice

1. Bronze coin: **artifact**. Sailor's diary: **document**. Cave painting:
   **image**. Folk song: **oral account**.
2. Can tell: whose face/rule it names, what metal people had, craft skill.
   Cannot tell: what people believed, why events happened, ordinary people's
   names.
3. The **farmer's diary** is more credible *for this question*: it is
   relevant (it is about the harvest) and its intended use was private
   record-keeping — little reason to exaggerate. The monument's intended
   use was to glorify the king ("endless plenty" is a boast), so its
   relevance to the actual harvest is low and its purpose distorts.
4. The note was made to **pass along the requests** (a brief administrative
   relay), not to investigate them — so it omits who/usage/cost. Limits
   follow from intended use.
5. Maker: *probably* the traveler (the letter is written by the one on the
   voyage — "describes a long sea voyage" is the clue). Audience: the
   mother ("Dear Mother" names her).
6. Audience: the people subject to the laws (the public). Made for public
   display and obedience — and to legitimize the ruler, per its own closing
   purpose ("so that the strong do not harm the weak").
7. Two limits: only the winners' perspective survives; victory songs are
   made to celebrate/remember, not to record accurately. One filling kind:
   losing-side documents, battlefield artifacts, or non-combatant oral
   accounts.
8. Open. Sample — compelling question: "Why did trade change what ancient
   people believed?" Source kinds: maps (trade routes), artifacts (traded
   goods), documents (travelers' accounts). Possibly missed: enslaved
   laborers' perspectives.

### Exit check

1. Artifacts (objects; cannot tell thoughts/motives), documents (writings;
   miss the unwritten and may be slanted), images (pictures; miss
   before/after and the excluded), oral accounts (spoken memory; shift over
   time, weak on exact dates).
2. Relevance and intended use change with the question — the same source can
   be the best evidence for one question and useless for another.
3. Open. Sample: "The losing side in the victory songs — missing because
   only the winners' songs were sung, remembered, and recorded."

## Lesson 05 — Chronology and Archaeology: Reading Time in Layers

### Guided practice

1. **1500 BCE is younger** than 2500 BCE — BCE counts *down* toward year 1,
   so the bigger BCE number is older. **500 BCE is older** than 500 CE —
   every BCE year comes before every CE year.
2. a. 3000 − 1500 = **2000 years** (same side: subtract). b. 44 + 44 − 1 =
   **87 years** (across eras: add, then subtract 1 — there is no year 0).
3. Top to bottom: (1) dark topsoil with roots and small pebbles; (2) dark
   brown layer with a small pottery piece; (3) reddish layer with the large
   broken pottery shard; (4) pale sandy layer with the coin-like disk;
   (5) gray layer with the bone fragment. Oldest to youngest: **bone →
   coin-like disk → pottery shard**. Reason: deeper layers are older —
   new soil settles on top of old.
4. Right: the shard is **younger** than the bone (higher layer = buried
   later). Wrong: "more advanced" — a layer gives *order*, not a ranking of
   advancement; that inference needs far more evidence.

### Independent practice

1. Oldest to youngest: **2000 BCE, 1200 BCE, 1 BCE, 500 CE, 2026 CE.**
2. 2000 − 1000 = **1000 years**.
3. 2000 + 1000 − 1 = **2999 years** (add, then subtract 1).
4. They forgot there is **no year 0**. Correct: 250 + 250 − 1 = **499
   years**.
5. The **bone** — it lies in the deepest (gray) layer, and deeper = older.
6. Yes. One realistic way: someone later dug a pit down through the layers
   and dropped (or lost) the coin in it; or an animal burrow shifted it;
   or construction disturbed the layers. Any plausible disturbance
   mechanism.
7. Sample: "Layers tell me the *order* — what came before what. They cannot
   tell me *exact dates* or how many years passed. **Context** — the exact
   layer and surroundings where a find lay — is what turns an object into
   evidence; without it, the object loses its story."
8. Open. The compelling question must be big/arguable; one supporting
   question answerable by the layers (e.g., "Which find is oldest?"), one
   needing another kind (e.g., "What did the pottery people eat?" — needs
   bones/seeds analysis or documents).

### Exit check

1. The calendar jumps from 1 BCE straight to 1 CE. So a span crossing the
   boundary must be (add, then subtract 1) — the naive add counts a phantom
   year 0.
2. New soil and debris settle on top of old, burying earlier material
   deeper. It can be wrong when later activity — dug pits, burrows,
   construction — mixes the layers.
3. **Context** is where an artifact was found: its layer and what surrounded
   it. Lifting an object without recording its context destroys the evidence
   of its age and associations.

## Project — The Fictional Tell (rubric exemplar)

**Stratigraphy table (ordering):** Layer 5 (gray clay) = 1 (oldest);
Layer 4 (sandy) = 2; Layer 3 (reddish) = 3; Layer 2 (brown) = 4;
Layer 1 (surface) = 5 (youngest). Rule: deeper = older.

**Classifications:** bottle cap — artifact; "2019" coin — artifact; glazed
shard — artifact; iron nail — artifact; clay tablet with wedge marks —
**document** (it is written); unglazed shard — artifact; bronze pin —
artifact; stone blade — artifact; bone fragment — artifact; charcoal flecks
— natural material (accept "artifact"); painted pebble — artifact.

**Find ordering:** stone blade (Layer 4) → bronze pin (Layer 3) → iron nail
(Layer 2), cited by layer.

**Sample inference (4-pt level):** "The tell's people *probably* changed how
they made things over time: the deepest tool layer has chipped stone, then a
bronze pin appears, then an iron nail — and pottery goes from rough
unglazed to glazed. This suggests new crafts or materials arriving (or being
invented) between those layers. A supporting find would be a kiln or more
glazed shards in Layer 2; a weakening find would be a glazed shard down in
Layer 4, which would break the sequence."

**Limits (sample):** no exact dates — layers give order only; no names or
beliefs; the "2019" coin dates only the fictional surface.

**Next question (sample):** "What did the tell's people eat?" — best
answered by artifacts: animal bones and charred seeds from the layers
(D3.1.6-8: choosing source kinds by relevance).
