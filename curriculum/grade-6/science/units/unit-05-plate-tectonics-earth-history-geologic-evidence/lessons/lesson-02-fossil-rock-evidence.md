# Lesson 02 — Fossils and Rocks That Match Across Oceans

**Student goal:** I can use fossil and rock evidence from continents
now separated by an ocean to argue that the continents were once
joined, and I can say what that evidence cannot prove alone.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S5 (plus S6 puzzle)
**Materials:** the evidence cards E1–E6 in the [unit README](../README.md);
paper continents for the fit puzzle (Session S6); a notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Hold your hands palms-down, fingers
together. Now pull them slowly apart. Imagine the gap between them is
an ocean that did not exist a minute ago. What would you expect to find
on the two facing edges? Keep that prediction — the lesson tests it.

## Teaching

In 1912 a German scientist named **Alfred Wegener** proposed a strange
idea: the continents move. In his time most geologists believed the
continents were fixed in place forever. Wegener's idea was called
**continental drift**, and it was rejected for decades — not because his
evidence was weak, but because he could not explain what force could
possibly push a continent. The force question was answered later, by
the seafloor evidence in Lesson 03. This lesson is about Wegener's
evidence, which was strong enough to be worth keeping.

Wegener built his case from four independent kinds of evidence. No
single one proves the continents moved. Together, they are hard to
explain any other way.

**1. The fit.** South America and Africa fit together like two pieces
of a torn page — and the fit gets better when you match the edges of
their **continental shelves**, the shallow underwater edges of the
continents, rather than just the coastlines. A torn page fits because
it was once whole.

**2. The fossils.** The same land fossils appear on continents now
separated by wide oceans — and these were land and freshwater
organisms, not ocean swimmers:

- ***Glossopteris***, a seed plant with tongue-shaped leaves, left
  fossils in southern Africa, South America, Australia, Antarctica,
  and India. Its seeds were too heavy to blow or float across an ocean.
- ***Mesosaurus***, a small freshwater reptile, left fossils only in
  southern Africa and eastern South America. A freshwater animal
  cannot swim across the Atlantic.
- ***Cynognathus***, a mammal-like reptile, left fossils in South
  Africa and South America.
- ***Lystrosaurus***, a stocky land animal, left fossils in Antarctica,
  India, and South Africa — including Antarctica, where it could not
  live in today's cold.

**3. The rocks.** The geologist Alexander du Toit found the same stack
of rock layers on the west coast of Africa and the east coast of South
America, in the same order: basalt at the bottom, then shale with
fossil reptiles, then coal layers with *Glossopteris*, then rocks with
*Mesosaurus* fossils, then shale on top. Two coasts, thousands of
kilometers apart, with the same five-layer sequence. A matching
mountain range tells the same story: a range in Argentina lines up
with an ancient range in South Africa when the two continents are
fitted back together.

**4. The ancient climates.** Wegener also found grooves scratched by
long-gone glaciers across southern Africa, South America, Australia,
Antarctica, and India — places that are warm today. Fitted together,
the grooves form one continuous ice sheet spreading outward, as if
those lands were once joined near the South Pole. (This unit uses the
glacial grooves as supporting color; the assessed evidence is fossils,
rocks, and fit.)

Wegener's conclusion was that the southern continents were once joined
in a supercontinent — later named **Pangaea**, "all lands" — which
began breaking apart about 225–200 million years ago. The evidence
cards E1–E6 in the unit README carry the exact distributions to use.

### Worked example 1 — Why land fossils, not sea fossils?

**Question:** Why does Wegener's case rest on land and freshwater
fossils? Why not use fossils of ocean animals?

**Reasoning:** An ocean animal can swim, drift, or float across an
ocean, so finding the same ocean fossil on two shores proves nothing
about the shores being joined. A freshwater reptile like
*Mesosaurus* cannot cross salt water, and a land plant's heavy seeds
cannot cross an ocean. When the *same* land fossil appears on two
shores now separated by an ocean, there are only two explanations:
the shores were once joined, or the organism crossed an ocean it
could not cross. The first explanation is the reasonable one. The
lesson: **evidence is only as strong as the alternative explanations
it rules out.**

### Worked example 2 — One card is not a case

**Question:** A learner finds a *Glossopteris* fossil in South
America and says, "This proves the continents moved." What is wrong
with that sentence?

**Reasoning:** One fossil in one place proves that *Glossopteris*
lived in South America. It says nothing about Africa until you check
whether the fossil is also there — and nothing about motion until you
also check the rocks, the fit, and the seafloor. A single observation
is a clue, not a proof. The correct sentence is: "*Glossopteris* in
South America **and** southern Africa, **plus** matching rock layers
**plus** the continental fit, together support the claim that the two
continents were once joined." Science argues from a **convergence of
evidence**, not from one exhibit.

## Fit-the-continents puzzle (Session S6, 20 minutes)

Work with the adult. Cut out (or trace) simple paper shapes of South
America and Africa — the adult can print or sketch them from any atlas;
accuracy of the outline matters less than the exercise, because the
fit is judged by the coastline shapes, not by measurements. Then:

1. Fit the two shapes together along their facing coasts. Notice
   where they match and where they bulge.
2. Place the evidence cards on the joined map: E1 (*Glossopteris*)
   on both sides of the join; E2 (*Mesosaurus*) on the facing coasts;
   E5 (the five-layer rock stack) as a strip crossing the join.
3. Answer: when the continents are fitted together, do the fossil
   bands form one continuous band or two separate patches? What does
   that tell you?
4. Now pull the shapes apart to today's positions. The fossil bands
   are split by an ocean. Write the two-sentence argument: what was
   once joined, and what evidence says so?

**Text-only alternative:** the puzzle can be done entirely from the
evidence cards and the distribution table in the README — list each
fossil's continents, then list which continents share each fossil.
The shared-fossil pattern is the same argument without cutting paper.

## Guided practice

1. For each fossil (E1–E4), write the continents where it is found
   and one reason it could not have crossed an ocean.
2. Card E5 describes five rock layers in the same order on two coasts.
   Explain in two sentences why *the order* matters, not just the
   rock types.
3. A learner says, "The continents fit, so they must have been
   joined." What is missing from that argument? Name two other
   evidence cards that would strengthen it.

## Independent practice

4. Write a four-sentence case for Pangaea using one fossil card, one
   rock card, and the fit card. End with one sentence about what
   your case still cannot explain. (Hint: it cannot explain *what
   moved the continents* — that is Lesson 03.)
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Mesosaurus swam from
   Africa to South America, which proves the continents were joined."
   Find the two errors and rewrite the sentence correctly.

## Applied task

Imagine you are Wegener in 1912, writing to a skeptical geologist.
Write a short paragraph (5–6 sentences) presenting your three
strongest pieces of evidence and naming the one objection you cannot
yet answer (the missing force). Be honest about the gap — scientists
are.

## Exit check

Name the four fossils in cards E1–E4 and, for one of them, say why a
land or freshwater organism is stronger evidence than an ocean
organism.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** fossil names are hard to pronounce — the adult
  says each name aloud first, and the learner may write "G-plant"
  or "M-reptile" as a shorthand in notes (full names on assessed work).
- **Alternate response mode:** the guided-practice table may be
  completed by sorting the evidence cards into piles by continent
  instead of writing lists.
- **Extension:** research (with the adult) one more matching pair —
  the Appalachian Mountains of North America and the Caledonian
  Mountains of Scotland and Scandinavia, which line up when the
  Atlantic is closed. Add it as card E6b on your board.

## Teacher note

Two traps recur here. First, learners treat one fossil as proof;
worked example 2 is the antidote — always ask "what else would you
need?" Second, learners may think Wegener was ignored because he was
wrong; he was ignored because his mechanism was missing, and the
evidence was right. That distinction — evidence versus mechanism —
is the bridge to Lesson 03. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 02.
