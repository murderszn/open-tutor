# Lesson 05 — Deep Time: Strata and the Geologic Time Scale

**Student goal:** I can order events by relative age from rock layers,
and I can explain how the geologic time scale organizes Earth's
4.6-billion-year history on a timeline I can hold.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S11 (plus S12 timeline work)
**Materials:** the strata diagram below; a 5-meter rope or paper strip
and tape for the timeline; the event list in this lesson; a notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Stack three books on a table, one at
a time. Which book was placed first — the bottom one or the top one?
Now imagine the books are rock layers. What rule did you just use?
Keep it in mind: it is the oldest rule in geology.

## Teaching

Earth is about **4.6 billion years old**. That number is too big to
feel. The geologic time scale is how scientists make it usable: a
calendar that divides Earth's history into named blocks — eons, eras,
periods, epochs — from oldest to youngest. The blocks were drawn where
the rocks show big changes, most often in the fossils. This lesson
teaches you to read the calendar from the rocks themselves. You will
not be asked to memorize period or epoch names — **NGSS MS-ESS1-4's
assessment boundary excludes recalling the names of specific periods
or epochs** — and the unit never tests them. You will use the big
divisions and the order of events.

**Superposition.** Sediment settles in layers, and new layers pile on
top of old ones. In an undisturbed stack of **strata** — layers of
sedimentary rock — the oldest layer is on the bottom and the youngest
is on the top. That is the rule from the readiness check, and it is
called **superposition**. It gives **relative age**: which layer is
older or younger than its neighbor, without any number of years. A
layer is not dated by its color or its thickness; it is dated by its
position and by what is inside it.

**Index fossils.** Some organisms lived for a short time but spread
widely. Their fossils are **index fossils**: if you find the same
index fossil in rock layers in two far-apart places, those layers
formed at about the same time. An index fossil must meet two tests —
short-lived and widespread. A fossil of an animal that lived for
hundreds of millions of years is useless as a marker, because it
could sit in layers of very different ages. A fossil found in only
one valley is useless too, because it cannot connect two places.

**The big divisions.** From oldest to youngest, Earth's history is
organized as:

| Division | When | What it holds |
|---|---|---|
| **Precambrian** | 4.6 billion to 539 million years ago | Earth's formation, first life; about 88% of all Earth's history |
| **Paleozoic** ("old life") | 539 to 252 million years ago | First fish, first insects, first land animals |
| **Mesozoic** ("middle life") | 252 to 66 million years ago | First dinosaurs, first mammals, first birds; ends with the dinosaurs' extinction |
| **Cenozoic** ("new life") | 66 million years ago to today | Mammals spread; humans appear at the very end |

The boundaries are not guesses. Geologists matched strata and index
fossils around the world to put the blocks in order, and later,
radioactive dating of volcanic rock added ages in years. The scale is
still updated as new rocks are dated.

### Worked example 1 — Reading a stack

**Question:** A cliff shows five layers, bottom to top: A (sandstone
with shell fossils), B (shale), C (a thin ash layer), D (sandstone
with leaf fossils), E (soil). A fault cuts through layers A, B, and C
but not D or E. Order the layers oldest to youngest, and say whether
the fault is older or younger than layer D.

**Reasoning:** By superposition, the bottom is oldest: A, then B, then
C, then D, then E. The fault cuts A, B, and C, so it must have formed
after those layers existed — but it does not cut D, so D was laid down
after the fault moved. The fault is younger than C and older than D.
The rule: **a fault or intrusion is younger than the rock it cuts and
older than the rock that covers it.** The ash layer C is a bonus: a
volcanic ash layer falls everywhere at once, so the same ash in two
cliffs marks the same moment — a natural time stamp.

### Worked example 2 — The calendar-year squeeze

**Question:** Squeeze Earth's 4.6 billion years into one calendar
year. On what date did the first dinosaurs appear (240 million years
ago)?

**Reasoning:** 4.6 billion years = 365 days, so one day stands for
4,600 ÷ 365 ≈ **12.6 million years**. Dinosaurs appeared 240 million
years ago: 240 ÷ 12.6 ≈ 19 days before December 31 — **December 12**.
Check the same way: the dinosaurs' extinction (66 million years ago)
is 66 ÷ 12.6 ≈ 5 days before December 31 — **December 26**. And the
first humans (300,000 years ago) are 0.3 ÷ 12.6 ≈ 0.024 days, about
**34 minutes before midnight on December 31**. The calendar makes the
point the numbers cannot: dinosaurs had the planet for the last three
weeks of the year, and humans arrived in the last half hour.

**Activity — Order the stack (10 minutes).** Using the diagram:

1. List the layers A–E from oldest to youngest, with one reason for
   each position.
2. Is the fault older or younger than layer D? Give the evidence
   from the diagram.
3. The shell fossil is in A and the leaf fossil is in D. Which
   organism lived first, and how do you know?
4. **Limit check:** the diagram makes layer B look thin and layer D
   look thick. Does that mean D took longer to form than B? Why not?
   (Thickness depends on how fast sediment piled up, not only on
   time — a thick layer can form quickly in a flood, and a thin layer
   can take ages. Never read time off thickness.)

**Text-only alternative:** the diagram shows five layers, bottom to
top: A (sandstone, shell fossil), B (shale), C (thin ash layer),
D (sandstone, leaf fossil), E (soil); a fault cuts A, B, and C but
stops below D. Every question above can be answered from this list
without seeing the picture.

## Rope timeline build (Sessions S11–S12, 20 minutes)

Work with the adult. Lay a 5-meter rope (or taped paper strips) on a
clear floor. Mark **today** 20 cm from one end and **Earth's
formation** 4.6 meters away. The scale is **1 meter = 1 billion
years**. Place these events (values from the Purdue geologic time
lab, checked 2026-11-08):

| Event | Years ago | Distance from "today" |
|---|---|---|
| Earth's formation | 4.6 billion | 4.6 m (460 cm) |
| First cellular life | 3.3 billion | 3.3 m (330 cm) |
| First fish | 530 million | 53 cm |
| First insects | 480 million | 48 cm |
| First dinosaurs | 240 million | 24 cm |
| First mammals | 220 million | 22 cm |
| First birds | 150 million | 15 cm |
| First humans | 300,000 | 0.3 cm (3 mm) |

Then answer: how much of the rope is Precambrian (4.6 meters minus
the last 53.9 cm — about 4.06 meters, or 88%)? And where on the rope
do humans sit — can you even see the mark without looking closely?
That invisibility is the lesson: nearly everything that ever lived
came and went before the last three millimeters.

## Guided practice

1. A stack has three layers: bottom (trilobite fossils), middle
   (fern fossils), top (dinosaur bone). Which lived first, and which
   rule tells you?
2. Two cliffs 500 km apart both contain the same index fossil in a
   shale layer. What can you conclude about the two shale layers, and
   what can you not conclude? (Same age, approximately. You cannot
   conclude they formed in the same environment or from the same
   sediment.)
3. Place these in order oldest to youngest: first birds, first fish,
   Earth's formation, first humans, first dinosaurs. Then give the
   time-scale division each one falls in.

## Independent practice

4. A fossil species lived for 200 million years and spread across
   three continents. Is it a good index fossil? Explain using both
   tests.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Layer D is thicker than
   layer B, so D is older than B." Find the two errors (thickness is
   not age; in a stack, lower is older) and rewrite the sentence
   correctly.

## Applied task

Write a short paragraph (4–5 sentences) explaining the geologic time
scale to a friend who thinks Earth is 6,000 years old. Use the rope
timeline: what would 6,000 years measure on the rope? (6,000 years ÷
1 billion years per meter = 0.000006 meters — six millionths of a
meter, far thinner than a hair. The rocks themselves — 4.6 meters of
them — are the evidence for the longer history.) Be respectful and
stick to the evidence.

## Exit check

(a) State superposition in one sentence. (b) What two tests must a
fossil pass to be an index fossil? (c) Name the four big divisions in
order, oldest to youngest.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the divisions table aloud; the
  learner points to each row's time span as it is read.
- **Alternate response mode:** the stack-ordering exercises may be
  done by arranging five labeled cards in a column instead of writing.
- **Extension:** compute one more calendar-year date yourself: first
  fish, 530 million years ago. (530 ÷ 12.6 ≈ 42 days before
  December 31 — November 19.) Add it to the calendar table in your
  notebook.

## Teacher note

Two ideas need protecting. First, **relative age is not a number of
years**: learners who say "layer A is 500 million years old" from
position alone have missed the point — superposition gives order,
and numbers come from radioactive dating, which this unit names but
does not teach. Second, **period and epoch names are out of scope by
design** (MS-ESS1-4 boundary): if a learner brings up "Jurassic" or
"Cretaceous," acknowledge it and steer back to the four big
divisions. The applied task touches a belief some families hold; keep
it on the evidence (the rope, the rocks) and never on the learner's
family. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 05.
