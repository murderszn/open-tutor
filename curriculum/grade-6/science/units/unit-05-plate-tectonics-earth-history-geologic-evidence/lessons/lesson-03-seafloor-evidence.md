# Lesson 03 — The Seafloor Tells the Story

**Student goal:** I can explain how the age pattern of the ocean floor
— youngest at the ridges, older toward the trenches — supports
seafloor spreading and past plate motion.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S7 (plus S8 graphing)
**Materials:** evidence cards E7–E8 in the [unit README](../README.md);
graph paper or a notebook; a ruler.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** If a continent split apart and an
ocean opened between the halves, where would you expect the ocean
floor to be youngest — in the middle of the ocean, or at the edges
near the continents? Write your guess and one reason. The lesson
checks it.

## Teaching

Wegener had the fossils, the rocks, and the fit — but no force, and no
way to watch a continent move. The missing piece came from the ocean
floor, and it arrived in the 1960s, when ships began mapping the
seabed properly for the first time.

Three discoveries turned continental drift into **plate tectonics**,
the theory that Earth's rigid outer layer — the **lithosphere** — is
broken into a dozen or more large and small **plates** that move
relative to one another over hotter, weaker material below.

**1. Mid-ocean ridges.** Down the middle of every ocean runs a long
underwater mountain chain — a **mid-ocean ridge**. The Mid-Atlantic
Ridge runs down the center of the Atlantic; the East Pacific Rise runs
along the eastern Pacific. At a ridge, two plates pull apart and hot
material rises to fill the gap, cooling into new ocean floor. This is
**seafloor spreading**: new crust forms at the ridge and moves outward
as the plates separate.

**2. The age pattern.** The ocean floor is youngest at the ridges and
gets older as you move away from them, toward the continents and the
trenches. The oldest seafloor anywhere is under 300 million years old
— while some continental rocks are more than 4 billion years old. The
ocean floor is young because it is constantly being made at ridges
and destroyed at trenches; the continents are old because they are
not destroyed the same way. If the seafloor were not spreading, there
would be no reason for its age to line up so neatly with distance
from the ridge.

**3. Trenches.** Where the seafloor gets old and cold, it sinks. At an
ocean **trench** — a deep, narrow valley in the ocean floor — one
plate dives beneath another in a process called **subduction**. The
Mariana Trench, the deepest place on Earth at about 11 kilometers
down, is where the Pacific plate subducts. Creation at the ridges and
destruction at the trenches balance: Earth is not growing, so new
seafloor must be matched by old seafloor sinking somewhere else.

There is a fourth discovery worth one sentence and no more: scientists
also found stripes of matching magnetic patterns on either side of the
ridges. The stripes support spreading too — but **NGSS MS-ESS2-3's
assessment boundary states that paleomagnetic anomalies are not
assessed**, so this unit mentions them only here, never tests them,
and never uses them as evidence in an assessed task.

Together, the ridges, the age pattern, and the trenches supplied what
Wegener lacked: a mechanism. Plates move because new seafloor forms
at ridges and old seafloor sinks at trenches. The continents ride
along on the plates like passengers. On average the plates move about
2 centimeters a year — too slow to feel, fast enough to open an ocean
in 200 million years. (Check: 2 centimeters a year for 200 million
years is 4,000 kilometers — roughly the width of the Atlantic at its
widest. The arithmetic closes.)

### Worked example 1 — Reading the age table

**Question:** Card E7 gives seafloor ages at four distances from a
ridge: 0 km → 0 million years; 500 km → 25; 1,000 km → 50;
1,500 km → 75. What is the spreading rate, and how do you know?

**Reasoning:** Take any two rows. From 0 km to 500 km the age grows
by 25 million years, so the floor moved 500 km in 25 million years:
500 ÷ 25 = 20 km per million years. Convert: 20 km per million years
is 20,000 meters per 1,000,000 years, which is 0.02 meters a year —
**2 centimeters a year**. Check with another pair: 1,000 km in 50
million years is also 20 km per million years. Two pairs agreeing
means the rate is steady, not a coincidence. A rate that stays the
same across the whole table is exactly what a spreading seafloor
predicts.

### Worked example 2 — Why the old floor is missing

**Question:** The oldest continental rocks are over 4 billion years
old, but the oldest seafloor is under 300 million years old. If the
seafloor were just sitting still, what would you expect — and what
does the difference tell you?

**Reasoning:** If the seafloor sat still like the continents, it
should hold rocks as old as the continents. It does not. The only
way to have a young ocean floor on a 4.6-billion-year-old planet is
for the floor to be **recycled**: made at ridges, carried outward,
and destroyed at trenches. The missing old seafloor is evidence of
destruction, and the age pattern (young at ridges, old at trenches)
is evidence of the conveyor belt that does it.

## Seafloor-age graphing activity (Session S8, 15 minutes)

Plot card E7 on graph paper: distance from the ridge (km) on the
horizontal axis, age (millions of years) on the vertical axis. Plot
all four points, draw the best straight line through them, and answer:

1. Is the line straight? What does a straight line mean here?
2. Predict the age of seafloor 2,000 km from the ridge, using your
   line. Show the calculation (2,000 km ÷ 20 km per million years).
3. The Atlantic is about 4,000 km wide at its widest, measured from
   the Mid-Atlantic Ridge to a continent on one side. How old would
   you expect that seafloor to be? (About 200 million years — which
   matches the real age of the Atlantic's oldest floor.)
4. Write one sentence: what does your graph prove, and what does it
   assume? (It proves the age grows steadily with distance *if* the
   spreading rate stayed steady — the straight line is the evidence,
   and the steady rate is the assumption.)

## Guided practice

1. In your own words, explain seafloor spreading in three sentences:
   what happens at the ridge, what happens to the new floor, and what
   happens at the trench.
2. Use card E8: why is the oldest seafloor so much younger than the
   oldest continental rock? Give the two-step answer (made at ridges;
   destroyed at trenches).
3. A learner says, "The ocean floor is old because the ocean is old."
   Explain the error with one number from this lesson.

## Independent practice

4. The Pacific is wider than the Atlantic, but its oldest seafloor is
   not much older than the Atlantic's. What does that suggest about
   how fast the Pacific floor is destroyed? (Hint: a wide ocean with
   young floor needs fast destruction — many trenches around its rim.)
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Trenches make new seafloor
   and ridges destroy it." Swap the two words that are wrong and
   explain why the corrected sentence matches the age pattern.

## Applied task

Write a short paragraph (4–5 sentences) answering Wegener's missing
question: *what moves the continents?* Use the ridge, the age pattern,
and the trench. End with the sentence: "The continents do not plow
through the ocean floor; they ___." (Fill in the blank correctly.)

## Exit check

(a) Where is the ocean floor youngest, and why? (b) Where does old
ocean floor go? (c) How fast do plates move on average, and what
everyday growth is that like?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the three discoveries aloud;
  the learner points to each one on the evidence cards as it is named.
- **Alternate response mode:** the graph may be built with a strip of
  paper and four marked dots instead of drawn axes.
- **Extension:** find the Mariana Trench's depth (about 11 km) and
  compare it with the height of Mount Everest (about 8.8 km above sea
  level). How much deeper is the trench than Everest is tall? Write
  the subtraction.

## Teacher note

The arithmetic in worked example 1 is the lesson's hinge: 20 km per
million years = 2 cm per year must be shown, not asserted, because the
same rate returns in the project and the assessment. Watch for the
reversal error in independent practice 5 (ridges make, trenches
destroy) — it is the most common confusion in this unit. Do not let
magnetic stripes creep into assessed answers; if a learner brings them
up, praise the knowledge and redirect to the ridge/trench/age
evidence. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 03.
