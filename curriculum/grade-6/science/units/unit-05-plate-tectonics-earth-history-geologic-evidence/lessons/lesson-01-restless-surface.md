# Lesson 01 — A Restless Surface

**Student goal:** I can explain that Earth's surface changes at very
different speeds and sizes, and I can read the pattern in where
earthquakes and volcanoes happen.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S1 (plus S2 map activity)
**Materials:** the historic earthquake and volcano lists below; a
notebook; a pencil.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Write down two ways Earth's surface
can change. Put a **F** next to any change that can happen in a single
day and an **S** next to any change that takes longer than a human
lifetime. Keep the list — the lesson will sort it properly.

## Teaching

Your street, your school, the hill behind it — they all look finished,
as if Earth were done building. It is not. Earth's surface is being
rebuilt all the time, and the rebuilding happens at wildly different
speeds. Some changes are **fast and small**: an earthquake can move the
ground in seconds; a volcano can bury a valley in a day; a landslide
can strip a hillside in minutes. Some changes are **slow and huge**: a
continent creeps a few centimeters a year — about as fast as your
fingernails grow — and over millions of years that creep opens an ocean
or raises a mountain range thousands of meters high. A single process
can be both: most of the time a plate boundary creeps gradually, and
then, suddenly, it slips and an earthquake happens. Geologists describe
this as **gradual most of the time, punctuated by sudden events** — a
long quiet stretch, interrupted by a violent moment.

That range of speeds is the first big idea of this unit, and it is what
NGSS MS-ESS2-2 asks you to explain: **geoscience processes have changed
Earth's surface at varying time and spatial scales**. "Spatial scale"
means size: a landslide changes one hillside; plate motion rearranges
whole continents. "Time scale" means duration: seconds for an
earthquake, millions of years for a mountain range. A good explanation
names the process, its speed, and its size — never just "Earth changes."

The second big idea is that the fast, violent events are not scattered
at random. Earthquakes and volcanoes cluster in lines. The most famous
line is the **Ring of Fire**: the rim of the Pacific Ocean, where
volcanoes and earthquakes ring the ocean from South America to Alaska
to Japan to Indonesia. If you plotted every earthquake of the last
century on a map, you would see the edges of the plates drawn for you
in dots. That is the map activity in Session S2: you will read real
lists and find the lines yourself.

### Worked example 1 — Fast, slow, or both?

**Question:** A mountain range rises 5 millimeters in one year. Is
that fast or slow?

**Reasoning:** 5 millimeters in a year is almost nothing — thinner
than a pencil eraser. A person would never notice it. But multiply:
5 millimeters a year for 1 million years is 5,000 meters — higher than
most mountains on Earth. So the honest answer is **both**: slow on a
human timescale, enormous on a geologic timescale. The mistake is to
judge a geologic process by human patience. Always ask "how long has
it been doing this?" before calling something slow.

### Worked example 2 — Reading the cluster

**Question:** Six historic earthquakes are listed below. Four of them
happened around the Pacific Ocean. What does that suggest, and what
does it not prove?

**Reasoning:** Four out of six landing on one ocean's rim is a
pattern, not a coincidence — it suggests something about the Pacific
rim makes earthquakes more likely there (we will learn in Lesson 04
that the rim is ringed by plate boundaries). But six earthquakes do
not prove the pattern: a small list can mislead, and this list was
chosen because these quakes are famous, not because it is a fair
sample. The pattern is a clue to investigate, not a conclusion. That
caution — pattern first, claim second — is how the whole unit works.

## Historic earthquakes (real events; magnitudes are USGS values)

| Event | Year | Place | Magnitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valdivia, Chile | 1960 | West coast of South America (Pacific rim) | 9.5 |
| Alaska (Prince William Sound) | 1964 | Alaska (Pacific rim) | 9.2 |
| Indian Ocean (Sumatra) | 2004 | Indonesia (Pacific/Indian Ocean rim) | 9.1 |
| Tohoku, Japan | 2011 | Japan (Pacific rim) | 9.1 |
| San Francisco, California | 1906 | California coast (Pacific rim) | 7.9 |
| Port-au-Prince, Haiti | 2010 | Caribbean island (Atlantic side) | 7.0 |

Five of the six sit on or near the Pacific rim. Haiti is the exception
that keeps the learner honest: earthquakes also happen away from the
Pacific, along other plate boundaries (Haiti sits on a boundary
between the Caribbean and North American plates).

## Well-known volcanoes (real eruptions of record)

| Volcano | Place | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Mount St. Helens | Washington, USA (Pacific rim) | Erupted violently in 1980 |
| Kilauea | Hawaii, USA (middle of the Pacific) | Erupts often; built by a hotspot, not a boundary — see Lesson 04 |
| Mount Etna | Sicily, Italy (Mediterranean) | Erupts often; near a plate boundary |
| Eyjafjallajokull | Iceland (mid-Atlantic) | Erupted in 2010; Iceland sits astride a ridge |

## Map activity — Find the lines (Session S2, 15 minutes)

Work with the adult. For each earthquake and volcano above, say
whether it sits on the Pacific rim, on another known plate edge
(the mid-Atlantic ridge through Iceland, the Mediterranean, the
Caribbean), or somewhere that surprises you. Then answer:

1. How many of the six earthquakes are on the Pacific rim? (Five.)
2. Name one earthquake and one volcano that are NOT on the Pacific
   rim, and say where they are instead.
3. Kilauea sits in the middle of the Pacific, far from any plate
   edge. What does that single fact tell you about the claim
   "volcanoes only happen at plate boundaries"? (It tells you the
   claim is false as stated — at least one volcano has another
   cause. Keep the exception in mind for Lesson 04.)
4. Write one sentence: what pattern do you see, and what would you
   need to check before calling it a rule?

**Text-only note:** every question above can be answered from the two
tables; no picture is required.

## Guided practice

1. Sort your readiness-check list into three columns: **fast and
   small** (seconds to days; one place), **slow and large** (thousands
   to millions of years; a region or a continent), and **both**
   (gradual most of the time, sudden sometimes). Give one example in
   each column that is not in the lesson.
2. An earthquake shakes a town for 30 seconds. A mountain range rises
   for 50 million years. Which one changed Earth's surface more in
   total? Explain why "more" needs a time and a place attached.
3. A learner says, "Earthquakes happen everywhere, so their locations
   tell us nothing." Use the earthquake table to answer: how many of
   the six are on the Pacific rim, and what does that number suggest?

## Independent practice

4. Write a two-sentence explanation of MS-ESS2-2 in your own words:
   one sentence about time scales, one about spatial scales. Use one
   example from this lesson in each sentence.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Mountains form quickly
   because earthquakes are quick." Find the error in the reasoning
   and rewrite the sentence correctly.

## Applied task

Choose one event from the tables and write a four-sentence report:
where it happened, when, how big it was (magnitude or note), and
whether it fits the Pacific-rim pattern or is an exception — and why
the exception matters.

## Exit check

In one sentence each: (a) name a fast, small-scale surface change;
(b) name a slow, large-scale surface change; (c) say what the
earthquake table suggests about where big earthquakes happen.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the tables aloud on first pass;
  the learner points to each row as it is read.
- **Alternate response mode:** the sort in guided practice may be done
  by moving word cards into columns instead of writing.
- **Extension:** look up one more historic earthquake (with the adult)
  and add it to the table. Does it strengthen or weaken the Pacific-rim
  pattern? Say which, with the numbers.

## Teacher note

The common misconception is that "slow" means "unimportant." Worked
example 1 exists to break it: 5 millimeters a year becomes 5,000
meters over a million years. The other trap is treating six famous
earthquakes as a fair sample — name it explicitly (worked example 2),
because the same caution returns in Lessons 02 and 03. Answer-key
reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 01.
