# Lesson 04 — Three Boundaries, Three Fates

**Student goal:** I can describe the three plate boundary types and
predict the surface features each one produces, using a real place as
the example for each.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S9 (plus S10 activity)
**Materials:** the boundary illustration below; the boundary sort
cards in Session S10; a notebook.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Two toy cars drive toward each
other, two drive away from each other, and two drive side by side in
the same direction, scraping doors. Which pair makes a crash, which
pair opens a gap, and which pair just scrapes? Plates do the same
three things — the lesson names them.

## Teaching

A **plate boundary** is the edge where two plates meet, and what
happens there depends on one thing: which way the plates are moving
relative to each other. There are exactly three possibilities.

**Divergent boundary — plates move apart.** The plates pull away
from each other, a gap opens, and hot material rises to fill it,
cooling into new crust. On the ocean floor this builds a
**mid-ocean ridge** (Lesson 03). On land it can split a continent:
the East African Rift Valley is a continent pulling apart today, a
long valley where the land is dropping and splitting. Features to
expect: ridges, new crust, rift valleys, volcanoes, and shallow
earthquakes. Real example: the **Mid-Atlantic Ridge**, running down
the center of the Atlantic Ocean — and in Iceland, the same ridge
rises above the sea, so you can stand on it.

**Convergent boundary — plates move together.** The plates collide,
and something has to give. What gives depends on what is colliding:

- **Ocean plate meets continent:** the denser ocean plate dives
  beneath the continent (**subduction**). The sinking plate melts
  as it descends, feeding volcanoes on the overriding plate, and the
  seafloor bends down into a deep **trench**. Expect: trenches,
  volcanoes, and large earthquakes. Real example: the **Mariana
  Trench** and the volcanoes of Japan — the Pacific plate subducting
  beneath Japan.
- **Continent meets continent:** neither plate will sink easily, so
  the crust crumples upward into mountains. Expect: the highest
  mountain ranges on Earth and large earthquakes. Real example: the
  **Himalayas**, still rising a few millimeters a year as India
  pushes into Asia.
- **Ocean plate meets ocean plate:** one subducts and the volcanoes
  that grow above it form an island arc. Expect: trenches and island
  volcanoes. Real example: the islands of Indonesia.

**Transform boundary — plates slide past each other.** The plates
grind sideways along a **fault**. Nothing is created and nothing is
destroyed; the crust is simply sheared. The plates stick, stress
builds, and then they slip — an earthquake. Expect: faults and
earthquakes, but no volcanoes from the boundary itself. Real example:
the **San Andreas Fault** in California, where the Pacific plate
slides past the North American plate.

Notice what this explains about Lesson 01: earthquakes and volcanoes
cluster in lines because plate boundaries are lines. The Ring of Fire
is mostly convergent boundaries around the Pacific. Iceland sits on a
divergent boundary. And Kilauea, the exception from Lesson 01, is a
**hotspot**: a plume of hot material rising from deep in the mantle,
far from any boundary, that builds a volcano in the middle of a plate.
Hotspots are real and important — Hawaii exists because of one — but
they are not a boundary type, and this unit does not assess them.

### Worked example 1 — Predict from the motion

**Question:** Two plates are moving toward each other. One carries
ocean floor, the other carries a continent. Predict three features
you expect to find at this boundary, and give the real place that
matches.

**Reasoning:** Toward each other means convergent. Ocean plate plus
continent means the ocean plate subducts: expect (1) a deep ocean
**trench** where the plate bends down, (2) **volcanoes** on the
continent above the sinking plate, and (3) **large earthquakes**
from the plates sticking and slipping. The real match is Japan and
the Japan Trench, where the Pacific plate subducts. The method is
always the same: name the motion, name the plates, then read off the
features.

### Worked example 2 — What a boundary cannot do

**Question:** A learner finds a volcano and says, "This must be a
convergent boundary." What is wrong with that reasoning?

**Reasoning:** Volcanoes happen at divergent boundaries too (the
Mid-Atlantic Ridge) and at hotspots (Hawaii). A volcano alone cannot
tell you which boundary you are on. You need a second feature: a
trench points to convergent, a ridge points to divergent, and a
volcano with neither points you toward a hotspot. **One feature is a
clue; two features that agree are an argument.** This is the same
rule as Lesson 02's fossils.

**Activity — Name the boundary (10 minutes).** For each of the three
parts of the picture, write which boundary type it shows and two
features that prove it:

1. **Left (ocean, ridge, rising lava):** which boundary? Which two
   features?
2. **Middle (deep trench, sinking plate, volcano, mountains):** which
   boundary? Which two features?
3. **Right (straight crack through the crust, ground offset):** which
   boundary? Which two features?
4. **Limit check:** the picture shows the ridge lava glowing above
   the water like a surface volcano. In reality, ridge lava erupts
   underwater and cools the moment it meets the sea. Write one
   sentence saying what the picture gets wrong and why it is still
   useful for learning the three motions.

**Text-only alternative:** the picture shows, left to right: an ocean
with a jagged ridge and orange lava rising between two plates; a deep
blue trench where one plate dives under another, with a smoking
volcano and mountains above; and a straight dark fault line cutting
through layered crust with the ground offset on either side. Every
question above can be answered from this list without seeing the
picture.

## Boundary sort (Session S10, 15 minutes)

Work with the adult. Sort these six features into the three boundary
columns — divergent, convergent, transform. A feature may belong in
more than one column, but you must say why:

mid-ocean ridge · deep ocean trench · San Andreas Fault · Himalayas ·
rift valley · island volcanoes (Indonesia)

Then answer: which feature could sit in two columns, and what extra
information would settle it? (Island volcanoes: convergent if they sit
above a subducting plate, divergent if they sit on a ridge like
Iceland — you need the trench or the ridge to decide.)

## Guided practice

1. For each boundary type, write the plate motion in one phrase
   (apart / together / past each other) and two features it produces.
2. A new island appears in the middle of the Atlantic. Which boundary
   is the most likely cause, and what would you look for to confirm
   it? (A ridge — look for a long underwater mountain chain and young
   seafloor on both sides.)
3. Why do transform boundaries produce earthquakes but not volcanoes
   from the boundary itself? (Nothing is created or destroyed; the
   plates only shear, so there is no new magma source from the
   boundary motion.)

## Independent practice

4. Match each place to its boundary and one feature: Mid-Atlantic
   Ridge; Himalayas; San Andreas Fault; Mariana Trench. Write each
   as a full sentence.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The Himalayas formed
   because India subducted under Asia." Find the error and rewrite
   the sentence correctly. (Continents do not subduct easily; they
   crumple upward into mountains.)

## Applied task

A town council asks you: "Our town sits near a plate boundary. What
should we prepare for?" You do not know which boundary type it is.
Write a short paragraph explaining what you would need to find out
first (the motion, the plate types, the features nearby) and why the
answer changes the preparation (volcanoes and tsunamis vs. shaking
alone).

## Exit check

(a) Name the three boundary types and the motion of each. (b) Name
one real place for each type. (c) Which boundary type builds the
highest mountains, and why?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads each boundary section aloud
  while the learner traces the motion with two hands (apart,
  together, sliding past).
- **Alternate response mode:** the boundary sort may be done with
  feature cards placed on three labeled mats.
- **Extension:** with the adult, find the Ring of Fire on a world map
  or globe and name three countries on it. For each, say whether its
  volcanoes and earthquakes come from a convergent boundary — and
  name the exception (Hawaii's hotspot) if it comes up.

## Teacher note

The handed-motion trick (two hands apart / together / sliding past)
is worth doing every time a boundary is named — learners who can show
the motion rarely confuse the types. The Himalaya error in
independent practice 5 is deliberate: "subduction" is the word
learners overuse after Lesson 03, and continent–continent collision
is the case that breaks it. The illustration's ridge lava is
stylized; the limit check in the activity exists so the picture is
never mistaken for an observation. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
