## plate-boundaries-cross-section.png (generated raster image)

- **Prompt:** "A clean educational science illustration, cross-section
  view through Earth's crust and upper mantle, showing three kinds of
  plate boundary side by side in one continuous landscape: on the
  left, two plates pulling apart at a mid-ocean ridge with new crust
  rising up between them under the ocean; in the middle, one ocean
  plate sliding down beneath a continent at a deep ocean trench, with
  a volcano and a mountain range on the overriding plate; on the right,
  two plates sliding past each other along a straight fault line with a
  small offset in the ground. Flat watercolor textbook style, muted
  earth tones, plain light background, no text, no labels, no letters,
  no numbers, no people."
- **Tool:** Muse built-in image generation, 2026-11-08.
- **Learning purpose:** Lesson 04 activity — learners identify each
  of the three boundary types in the picture and name two features
  that prove each one (ridge and rising lava; trench, sinking plate,
  volcano, mountains; straight fault with offset ground), then run the
  limit check on the stylized ridge lava.
- **Factual review (inspected 2026-11-08):** the picture shows, left
  to right: an ocean with a jagged mid-ocean ridge and orange lava
  rising between two plates; a deep blue trench where one plate dives
  beneath another, with a smoking volcano and mountains above; and a
  straight dark fault line cutting through layered crust with the
  ground offset on either side. No text, no labels, no people, no
  numbers. The three motions are shown correctly: apart, together
  (one plate sinking), and sliding past.
- **Caption:** "An AI-generated illustration of the three plate
  boundary types in one stylized landscape. It is a drawing, not a
  photograph, not a map, and not a measurement."
- **Alt text:** "Watercolor-style cross-section illustration of
  Earth's crust showing three plate boundaries side by side: on the
  left, two plates pulling apart at a mid-ocean ridge with orange lava
  rising between them under the ocean; in the middle, a deep ocean
  trench where one plate dives beneath another, with a smoking volcano
  and mountains above; on the right, a straight fault line cutting
  through the crust where two plates slide past each other. No labels
  or text appear in the picture."
- **Text-only alternative:** in Lesson 04, directly below the image.
- **Known limitations:** the ridge lava is drawn glowing above the
  water like a surface volcano — in reality, ridge lava erupts
  underwater and cools the moment it meets the sea (the Lesson 04
  activity's limit check exists for this). The transform fault is
  drawn as a crack through the cross-section rather than as a
  map-view offset. The picture has no scale: the trench, the
  mountains, and the ridge are not in true proportion. The
  illustration must never be presented as an observation, a map, or
  evidence for any answer.

## geologic-time-scale-bar.svg (deterministic diagram)

- **Source:** drawn by hand for this unit from the Lesson 05 division
  table (Precambrian 4.6 billion–539 million years ago; Paleozoic
  539–252 million; Mesozoic 252–66 million; Cenozoic 66 million–
  today). No generation involved. Segment widths are proportional to
  duration: 920 px = 4,600 million years (1 px ≈ 5 million years).
- **Learning purpose:** Lesson 05's shared timeline model — the bar
  makes visible that the Precambrian is about 88% of Earth's history
  while the Cenozoic is about 1%.
- **Factual review:** widths check out (812 + 57 + 37 + 14 = 920 px;
  4,061 + 287 + 186 + 66 = 4,600 million years). Period and epoch
  names are deliberately absent, per the MS-ESS1-4 assessment
  boundary.
- **Known limitations:** the bar is a summary, not a clock — it shows
  divisions, not individual events; the Cenozoic segment is too
  narrow to label inside the bar, so its description sits below it.

## strata-relative-age-diagram.svg (deterministic diagram)

- **Source:** drawn by hand for this unit from the Lesson 05 stack
  (layers A–E, bottom to top; ash layer C; shell fossil in A; leaf
  fossil in D; a fault cutting A, B, and C and stopping below D). No
  generation involved.
- **Learning purpose:** Lesson 05's shared reference for the
  superposition exercises — every ordering question in the lesson,
  practice Set E, and the assessment refers to this diagram.
- **Factual review:** layer order matches the lesson text (A oldest,
  E youngest); the fault stops below D as stated; the footer states
  that thicknesses are not timescales.
- **Known limitations:** layer thicknesses carry no time meaning by
  design; the fossils are symbols, not specimens; the fault is drawn
  as a single straight line, while real faults branch and bend.
