# Unit 01 — Answer Key

**For the guiding adult only.** Every solution below was solved independently
and reconciled with the student-facing questions (same numbers and labels).
For open responses, sample reasoning and acceptable variation are given —
accept any answer that meets the stated criterion.

## Lesson 01 — Testable Questions, Observations, and Inferences

### Guided practice

1. a. **Testable** — measure the table's length with a tape measure (cm).
   b. **Not testable** — "greatest" is an opinion; reword: "Which food has the
   most calories per serving?" (measurable). c. **Testable** — grow the same
   kind of plant in a sunny window and in a dark corner for two weeks and
   measure each plant's height in centimeters. d. **Not testable** — stars
   do not think; reword: "How do cloud shapes change over one hour?" (observe
   and sketch/photograph).
2. Sample: observations — "It feels bumpy," "It is smaller than my hand";
   inference — "I think it is a pinecone"; test — "I could smell it / weigh
   it." Accept any two sense-based observations and one guess clearly labeled
   as an inference, plus a follow-up observation.
3. Inferences: it rained / someone spilled water (or the fountain leaks). A
   test for "it rained": check whether other nearby sidewalks are wet too —
   rain would wet them all; a spill would not.

### Independent practice

1. Testable — measure the volume in milliliters (fill with water, pour into a
   graduated cylinder).
2. Not testable ("better" is opinion). Acceptable reword, e.g.: "Do more
   people in our family choose rainy-day or sunny-day activities?" or "Which
   has more hours of daylight?" — any countable reword.
3. Testable — drop the same ball from the same height onto tile and onto
   carpet and measure the bounce height in centimeters (or time how long it
   stays in the air in seconds) for each surface.
4. Not testable ("mysterious" is an opinion/feeling word). Acceptable
   reword, e.g.: "How many named storms formed in the Atlantic in each of
   the last 10 years?" or "How many minutes does sunset last?" — any
   countable reword.
5. Observation (felt directly).
6. Inference (an idea about what droopiness *means*; the observation would be
   "the leaves droop").
7. Observation (a tool reading).
8. "The sidewalk is hot" — observation (felt or measured); "because the sun
   shone on it all morning" — inference (the cause was not directly observed;
   a hot pipe underneath could also explain it).

### Exit check

1. A question is testable when you can name what you would observe, count,
   measure, or time to answer it — and someone else could repeat the test.
2. Open. Sample: observation — "Clouds cover most of the sky"; inference —
   "It might rain this afternoon."

## Lesson 02 — Variables, Fair Tests, and Repeated Trials

### Guided practice

1. Changed variable: amount of sunlight (sunny window vs. dark corner).
   Measured variable: plant height after two weeks (cm). Controlled
   variables (any two): same kind of plant, same amount of water, same
   pot/soil, same two-week period.
2. Unfair: stirring was changed along with temperature (warm cup stirred,
   cold cup not). Fix: stir both cups the same way — or stir neither.
3. 132 cm is the suspicious trial (118 and 119 agree; 132 does not). Honest
   move: mark it, note the likely misread, and re-measure.

### Independent practice

1. Changed: ramp height. Measured: roll distance.
2. Any three: the same car, the same board, the car released from the top
   every time with no push, the same table surface, the same ruler.
3. Changed: bob mass. Measured: swing time (time per swing, s).
4. Any two: the same string length, the same release angle, the same timing
   method, no friction/consistent setup.
5. Unfair: different water amounts (50 mL vs. 100 mL) — a second changed
   variable. Fix: pour the same amount (e.g., 50 mL) on each brand.
6. One trial cannot catch mistakes — a slip, a bump, or a misread ruler
   becomes the whole answer. Repeated trials let a weird result stand out and
   show the consistent pattern.
7. Yes, consistent — all within 1 °C of each other (61, 62, 61). The
   consistency suggests a steady measurement with no large error.
8. Flaw: two things changed (height AND car), so the cause of the longer roll
   is unknown — the one-variable rule was broken. Honest rewrite: "In this
   test the car rolled farther, but because I changed both the ramp height
   and the car, I cannot tell which change caused it."

### Exit check

1. Because if two things change at once, you cannot tell which one caused the
   effect you measured.
2. Repeated trials protect against mistakes and flukes — an odd result stands
   out, and the pattern across trials is what you trust.

## Lesson 03 — Measuring with Standard Tools and the Metric System

### Guided practice

1. 45 mm = **4.5 cm** (mm→cm: ÷10, decimal one place left). 1.2 km =
   **1,200 m** (km→m: ×1,000, decimal three places right).
2. 750 mL = **0.75 L** (mL→L: ÷1,000). 0.5 kg = **500 g** (kg→g: ×1,000).
3. "14" is missing its unit — 14 mm, 14 cm, and 14 m describe completely
   different objects. Correct, e.g.: "The eraser is 14 mm long" (with whatever
   unit fits the object).

### Independent practice

1. 7.5 cm = **75 mm**.
2. 620 mm = **62 cm**.
3. 1.5 m = **150 cm**.
4. 2,000 mL = **2 L**.
5. 0.75 L = **750 mL**.
6. 500 g = **0.5 kg**.
7. The header names the quantity but not the unit. Fix: **"Temperature (°C)"**.
8. Two problems: (a) no unit — 61 mm vs. 61 cm vs. 61 m are wildly different;
   (b) no precision/tool context. Rewrite: "The board is 61 cm long (measured
   with a meter stick)."

### Exit check

1. The meter is a bigger unit than the centimeter, so fewer meters fit into
   the same length — the physical length never changed, only the size of the
   counting unit.
2. Every column header must name the quantity being recorded AND its unit.

## Lesson 04 — Data Tables and Line Graphs

### Guided practice

1. **64 °C** — find 6 on the time axis, go straight up to the line, then
   straight left to the temperature axis.
2. Drops: 80→74 = 6; 74→69 = 5; 69→64 = 5; 64→60 = 4; 60→57 = 3 °C. The
   **least** drop was the last interval (8–10 minutes): **3 °C**.
3. Sample correction: "In this sample — one cup, in this room, on this day —
   the water cooled this way. One test cannot prove anything about all
   liquids."

### Independent practice

1. A line graph shows the *shape* of change over time (faster early, slower
   later); a list of numbers hides that pattern.
2. Ramp height (the changed variable) goes on the horizontal axis — it is what
   was set, and the trend then reads left to right like a story.
3. The unit is missing. Corrected label: **"Roll distance (cm)"** (or
   "Distance (cm)").
4. 80 − 57 = **23 °C** total drop.
5. Sample: "In this sample, the water temperature fell from 80 °C to 57 °C
   over 10 minutes." Accept any honest trend sentence with "in this sample."
6. Faster at the start: the first 2 minutes dropped 6 °C (80 → 74) while the
   last 2 minutes dropped only 3 °C (60 → 57).
7. The straight ruler line pretends the cooling rate was constant, but the
   real points show it *slowing down* — connecting only the endpoints hides
   the true shape of the data.
8. Extending the trend assumes the pattern continues, but the water cannot
   cool below room temperature — beyond the measured 10 minutes the line is a
   guess, not data.

### Exit check

1. Horizontal axis: the changed variable (often time), labeled with its unit.
   Vertical axis: the measured variable, labeled with its unit.
2. Accept any honest completion, e.g.: "In this sample, the water temperature
   fell from 80 °C to 57 °C over 10 minutes."

## Lesson 05 — Bar Graphs, Patterns, and Models vs. Reality

### Guided practice

1. **Bar graph** — rock types are categories; there is no "in-between" rock
   and no time order.
2. **Line graph** — months have a time order, and the line between months
   shows the trend across the half year.
3. Open. Sample includes: the board, the book-stack height, the car, the
   ruler/landing zone. Sample leaves-out: friction, air resistance, the exact
   release, table bumps.

### Independent practice

1. **Bar graph** — project topics are categories with no in-between values.
2. **Line graph** — temperature over time is a time series; the line shows
   the cooling trend.
3. Four separated bars (Ramp 9, Pendulum 7, Cooling water 5, Shadows 4);
   horizontal axis "Project topic," vertical axis "Number of learners." Winner
   *in this sample*: **Ramp (9)**.
4. Mean: (1.4 + 1.5 + 1.4) = 4.3; 4.3 ÷ 3 = **1.43 s** (rounded). Range: 1.5 −
   1.4 = **0.1 s**. Sample sentence: "In this sample, the swing time averaged
   about 1.43 s, with trials varying by only 0.1 s — a steady measurement."
5. Touching bars imply a continuous scale where in-between values exist;
   categories have nothing between them, so separated bars tell the truth
   about the data's structure.
6. Without units the numbers are meaningless — 80 could be degrees,
   centimeters, or seconds — so no one can interpret, check, or repeat the
   work.
7. Open. Accept any side-view sketch with ≥4 correct labels (board,
   book-stack/height, car, release point, ruler/landing zone) plus two
   includes (what the drawing shows) and two leaves-out (friction, air
   resistance, release wobble, timing error).
8. Flaw: *every* model leaves things out — that is what makes it a model
   rather than the thing itself. The drawing shows the plan and the variables
   clearly, but it cannot show friction, the exact release, or timing error,
   so it cannot show "exactly" what happened.

### Exit check

1. Time order or a continuous scale → line graph; categories with nothing
   in-between → bar graph. The test: would in-between values make sense?
2. Open. Sample: includes — the board and the book-stack height; leaves out
   — friction between the car and the board.

- **P10.** (91 + 88 + 90) = 269; 269 ÷ 3 = **89.7 cm** (rounded; 89.67 cm

### Part C — Investigation design critiques

- **P15.** Two things changed: sunlight AND watering — breaks the
  one-variable-at-a-time rule. Fix: water both plants identically (same
  amount, same schedule); change only the sunlight.
- **P16.** **Nothing is wrong** — this is a clean plan: one variable
  changed, same car, same release, three trials per height. (If the learner
  "fixes" it, check they did not break it — a common error is adding an
  unnecessary change.)
- **P17.** Two things changed: bob mass AND string length — breaks the
  one-variable rule. Fix: choose one question — e.g., keep the 30-cm string
  for both bobs to test mass, or use one bob on both lengths to test length.
- **P18.** Only one trial per brand — breaks the repeated-trials rule. Fix:
  test each brand at least three times so a fluke cannot decide the winner.
- **P19.** Two problems: "Height" and "Time" name no units; "Notes" is too
  vague. Corrected headers, e.g.: **"String length (cm)"**, **"Time for 10
  swings (s)"**, **"Trial notes (what happened)"**.
- **P20.** Sample correction: "This model shows the board, the books, the
  car, and the ruler — the investigation plan. It leaves out friction and air
  resistance, so it cannot show exactly what happened; no model shows
  everything." Requires: one includes, one leaves-out, and the humility
  frame.

## Unit Assessment

- **A1.** Three flaws required (any three, each with the rule and fix):
  1. **Two variables changed** (ramp height AND the car) — breaks
     one-variable-at-a-time. Fix: use the *same* car for both heights.
  2. **One trial each** — breaks the repeated-trials rule. Fix: run at least
     three trials per height.
  3. **No real measurement plan** ("which height won" is not data) — breaks
     the labeled-data rule. Fix: record each roll distance in centimeters in
     a labeled table with units. (Also acceptable: no controlled variables
     named — same car, same release point, same surface must be stated.)
- **A2.** a. **4.5 cm**. b. **150 cm**. c. **0.75 L**. d. **225 cm**.
- **A3.** Table sketch must show: column headers **"String length (cm)"**,
  **"Trial"**, **"Time for 10 swings (s)"** (wording may vary; quantity + unit
  required), and 9 data rows (3 lengths × 3 trials). Sentence: the
  changed-variable column shows what was set for each trial, so a reader can
  verify that only one thing changed — it is the proof of the fair test.
- **A4.** a. **Line graph** — ramp height is a continuous scale (in-between
  heights make sense) and the question is about the trend. b. Sketch: x-axis
  "Ramp height (cm)", y-axis "Mean roll distance (cm)", points (10, 63),
  (20, 90), (30, 119) plotted in the right neighborhoods, title naming the
  test (e.g., "Roll distance vs. ramp height"). c. Sample: "In this sample,
  the mean roll distance rose from 63 cm to 119 cm as ramp height went from
  10 cm to 30 cm."
- **A5.** Requires: two includes (any two of: board, book-stack height, car,
  release point at the top, roll-direction arrow, ruler/landing zone), two
  leaves-out (any two of: friction, air resistance, exact release, table
  bumps, stopwatch reaction time), and the usefulness sentence (the model
  shows the plan and variables clearly even though it cannot predict exact
  results).
- **A6.** a. **Yes, testable** — she measured the water's temperature in °C
  every 10 minutes for 30 minutes; the measurement answers the question.
  b. The drops shrink each interval (6, then 5, then 4 °C) — **the cooling
  slows down over time**. Any one cited number pair accepted.
  c. **Overclaim.** One cup, one kitchen, one day cannot prove anything about
  all water everywhere — Maya's honest sentence was "in this sample," and her
  brother dropped the frame.
  d. Accept any steadiness move with a reason: repeat the 30-minute run on
  other days (repeated trials — checks whether the pattern holds or was a
  fluke); test additional cups; have a second person time/read. The reason
  must invoke checking consistency, not just "more data."

## Project Scoring

Score each criterion 0–4 against the observable descriptors in
[project.md](project.md#rubric-20-points--observable-criteria). Guidance on
acceptable variation:

- **Plan (4):** Full credit needs all four lines present and correct
  (testable question; changed/measured/≥3 controlled variables; one variable
  changed; ≥3 trials with a reason). Award 3 if the variables are right but
  the trial reason is missing or vague; 2 if a controlled variable is
  actually the measured one; 0–1 if two variables change in the plan.
- **Data table (4):** Full credit needs units in every header, ≥3 settings ×
  ≥3 trials, and the changed-variable column. Deduct 1 per missing unit
  header (max −2); deduct 1 if a suspicious trial was silently dropped
  instead of marked — the honesty habit outranks neat numbers. A learner who
  marks "Trial 2 — car hit the wall, re-ran" earns full honesty credit even
  with the extra row.
- **Graph (4):** Full credit: line graph, changed variable on x-axis with
  units, measured on y-axis with units, points from the table's means,
  title. Accept a bar graph at 3 points *only if* the learner's changed
  variable was genuinely categorical (it should not be for these two
  options — ramp height and string length are continuous). Deduct 1 for a
  missing axis unit; deduct 1 for plotting raw trials instead of means only
  if the learner cannot explain the choice.
- **Model (4):** Full credit: ≥4 correct labels + includes (≥2) + leaves-out
  (≥2) + usefulness sentence. Award 3 if the drawing is strong but the
  statement names only one leaves-out; 2 if the statement claims the model
  "shows exactly what happened."
- **Claim and honesty (4):** Full credit: "in this sample" frame, claim
  matches the graphed pattern, one real limitation, one concrete
  improvement, no "proves/always." Deduct 1 for each missing element; a
  single "proves" costs 1 point but triggers a reteach conversation, not just
  a deduction.
- **Simulation route:** score identically; the model-vs.-reality statement
  must name the simulation as the apparatus (counts as one of the
  leaves-out/honesty elements).
