# Lesson 04 — Data Tables and Line Graphs

**Unit:** 01 — Measurement, Graphing, Models, and Investigation Design · **Grade 6**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 9 of 16

## Goal

You will turn a data table into a **line graph** with labeled **axes** and
units, and describe a **trend** in the data honestly — "the pattern in this
sample," never more than the data shows.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil; graph paper or a printed grid
- The unit's [`sample-line-graph.svg`](../assets/sample-line-graph.svg) open
  or printed — the modeled example for this lesson
- The learner's own Week-4 ramp/pendulum trial table (started in Session 8)

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult shows this tiny table (*fictional practice data*) and asks:

| Time (minutes) | Water temperature (°C) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 80 |
| 10 | 57 |

1. "What was the temperature at 0 minutes?" → *80 °C*
2. "Did the temperature go up or down over the 10 minutes?" → *down*
3. "What is missing if I write the column headers as just 'Time' and
   'Temperature'?" → *the units — (minutes) and (°C)*

Today we learn the display that makes two-column change-over-time data
*visible*: the line graph.

## Teaching

### Part A — When a line graph is the right choice

Use a **line graph** when one quantity changes *over time* (or over another
continuous scale, like ramp height) and you want to see the *shape* of that
change. The **changed variable** (what you set — time, ramp height) goes on
the horizontal **axis** (the x-axis); the **measured variable** (what you
measured — temperature, roll distance) goes on the vertical **axis** (the
y-axis). Every axis gets a **label with its unit**.

Study the modeled example — a deterministic chart drawn from fictional
practice data (a cup of warm water cooling on a counter). The data are printed
on the chart itself, so you can check every point:

### Part B — Reading the graph honestly

**Worked example 1 — describe the trend.**
*Thinking:* Read the points left to right. At 0 minutes the water is 80 °C;
at 10 minutes it is 57 °C — a drop of 23 °C. The line falls the whole way, so
the **trend** is *decreasing*. Honest sentence: "In this sample, the water
cooled from 80 °C to 57 °C over 10 minutes." What I must NOT say: "Water
always cools exactly like this" — one cup, one room, one day proves nothing
about all water.

**Worked example 2 — notice the shape of the change.**
*Thinking:* The first 2 minutes drop 6 °C (80 → 74); the last 2 minutes drop
only 3 °C (60 → 57). The cooling *slows down* — the line is steeper early and
flatter later. Honest sentence: "In this sample, the temperature fell faster
at the start (6 °C in the first 2 minutes) than at the end (3 °C in the last
2 minutes)." This is a *pattern in the data*, and noticing the shape is the
whole point of graphing.

**Worked example 3 — spot the broken graph.**
A learner graphs the same data but forgets the y-axis label: the axis just
says numbers 50–85. *Thinking:* Without "(°C)" a reader cannot tell whether
those numbers are degrees, minutes, or centimeters. And a second learner
plots temperature on the horizontal axis and time on the vertical axis.
*Thinking:* That breaks the convention — time (what was set/observed
sequentially) belongs on the horizontal axis, so the trend reads left to
right like a story. Both graphs are fixable: label every axis with its unit,
and put the changed variable on the x-axis.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. From the modeled graph: what was the temperature at 6 minutes? How do you
   know?
   - *Hint: find 6 on the time axis, go straight up to the line, then
     straight left to the temperature axis.*
2. From the modeled graph: between which two readings did the temperature
   drop the *least*? Show the subtraction.
   - *Hint: compute each 2-minute drop: 80→74, 74→69, 69→64, 64→60, 60→57.*
3. A friend says, "The graph proves all liquids cool at exactly this rate."
   Write the one-sentence correction you would give.
   - *Hint: "in this sample" + what the sample was (one cup, one room).*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Why does a line graph suit "temperature over time" better than a list of
   numbers?
2. In a graph of "roll distance vs. ramp height," which variable goes on the
   horizontal axis, and why?
3. A graph's vertical axis is labeled only "Distance." Name what is missing
   and write the corrected label for a ramp investigation.
4. From the modeled graph: what is the total temperature drop from 0 to 10
   minutes? Show the subtraction.
5. From the modeled graph: write one honest sentence describing the trend,
   using the phrase "in this sample."
6. From the modeled graph: is the cooling faster at the start or at the end?
   Cite two numbers as evidence.
7. Error analysis: A learner connects the points (0 min, 80 °C) and
   (10 min, 57 °C) with a straight ruler line and ignores the points in
   between. Explain why the real plotted points tell a truer story.
8. A learner says, "The line keeps going down, so if we waited 30 minutes the
   water would reach 0 °C." Explain why extending the trend beyond the data
   is risky.

## Application — graph your own data

Take your Week-4 ramp or pendulum trial table (3+ trials at each of at least
two heights/lengths — finish any missing trials first). Compute the *mean*
(average) of the trials for each setting, then draw a line graph: changed
variable on the horizontal axis with units, mean measured value on the
vertical axis with units, every point plotted, title naming what was tested.
Bring it to Session 11 — it becomes your project's graph draft.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. What belongs on the horizontal axis of a line graph, and what belongs on
   the vertical axis?
2. Complete this sentence honestly: "In this sample, the water temperature
   ______."

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** "axis" anchored physically — the adult points
  to the horizontal and vertical lines on the printed SVG while naming them;
  trend vocabulary (rising, falling, leveling off) on the word wall with
  arrows.
- **Alternate response mode:** describe trends orally while tracing the line
  with a finger; plot points with stickers on large grid paper; the adult
  scribes sentences.
- **Extension:** variability — the lesson's table shows single readings, but
  real investigations have repeated trials. Given fictional trial data for
  cooling (three thermometers reading 79, 81, 80 °C at 0 minutes), explain in
  three sentences what the *spread* tells you and how you would show it on a
  graph (plot the mean point and note the range).

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners read "the line goes down" as "the
temperature is low" rather than "the temperature is *falling*" — practice the
difference between a *value* and a *change*. (2) Overclaiming ("this proves
…") — enforce the "in this sample" sentence frame until it is automatic.
(3) Extrapolating beyond the data (task 8) — praise the curiosity, then show
why the model of the trend is not a promise. **Answer key:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-04--data-tables-and-line-graphs).
