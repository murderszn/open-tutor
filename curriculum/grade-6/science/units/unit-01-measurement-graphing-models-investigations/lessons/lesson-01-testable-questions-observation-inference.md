# Lesson 01 — Testable Questions, Observations, and Inferences

**Unit:** 01 — Measurement, Graphing, Models, and Investigation Design · **Grade 6**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 1 of 16

## Goal

You will sort questions into **testable** and **not testable**, name the
measurement that would answer each testable one, and tell an **observation**
apart from an **inference**.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil; a fresh vocabulary page or word-wall cards
- Session-2 station cards (the adult sets these up later): a mystery object in
  an opaque bag (touch only), a sealed jar of water, a plant or leaf, an ice
  cube in a cup

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult reads three questions aloud. You answer "testable" or "not testable
— yet," out loud.

1. "How many windows are on the front of our home?" → *testable* (you can look
   and count)
2. "Is rainy weather the gloomiest weather?" → *not testable* ("gloomiest" is
   an opinion word)
3. "Does bread get moldy faster in a warm room or a cool room?" → *testable*
   (you could observe two pieces of bread over days and record)

No writing yet — just talk. If #3 felt hard, that is exactly what this lesson
teaches.

## Teaching

### Part A — Testable questions

Science answers questions you can investigate by **observing, measuring, or
testing**. A question is a **testable question** when you can name *what you
would observe or measure* to answer it — and someone else could repeat your
test and get a comparable answer.

> **Testable:** "Does a ramp's height change how far a toy car rolls?"
> You could raise a ramp to three different heights, roll the same car from
> the top each time, and **measure** how far it rolls in centimeters.

> **Not testable:** "Are tall ramps more exciting than short ramps?"
> "Exciting" is a feeling word — no tool measures excitement. But the
> curiosity is real, so *reword* it: "Does a taller ramp make the car roll
> farther?" is the testable version of the same wonder.

Three clues a question is testable:

1. It asks about something you can **see, count, measure, or time**.
2. You can **name the measurement or observation** that would answer it.
3. Someone else could repeat the test and check your answer.

**Worked example 1 — sort and reword.**
Question: "Which brand of paper towel absorbs the most water?"
*Thinking:* "Absorbs the most water" can be measured — pour a fixed amount of
water (say 50 mL) on each towel and measure how much is left, or weigh the
towel before and after. Testable. The measurement: milliliters absorbed.
Question: "Which brand of paper towel is the coolest?"
*Thinking:* "Coolest" is an opinion word — no tool measures coolness. Not
testable. A testable reword: "Which brand holds the most water before it
drips?"

**Worked example 2 — name the measurement.**
Question: "Does a longer pendulum string make a slower swing?"
*Thinking:* Testable. What would I measure? The **time** for one complete
swing (a *period*), in seconds, for a short string and a long string. Then I
compare the two times. Naming the measurement is the most important step —
if you cannot name it, the question is not ready.

**Worked example 3 — rewording practice.**
Question: "Do dogs dream happy dreams?"
*Thinking:* "Happy" is a feeling word, and we cannot ask a dog. Not testable
as written. Testable reword: "Do sleeping dogs move their legs more during
one part of the night than another?" The measurement: minutes of leg
movement per hour of sleep, counted from video. Notice the reword kept the
curiosity (dog sleep) but found something countable.

### Part B — Observation vs. inference

An **observation** is something you notice with your senses or a tool — it
describes what is there. An **inference** is an idea about what the
observation *means* — it goes one step beyond what you directly saw.
Scientists need both, but they must never confuse them: an inference is a
starting idea, and you test it by making *more* observations.

> You see wet footprints leading away from the classroom door. "There are wet
> footprints by the door" is an **observation**. "Someone came in from the
> rain" is an **inference** — it might be true, but you did not see the person.
> A test: check whether the floor near the door is wet all the way outside.

**Worked example 4 — the cold cup.**
A learner holds a cold cup of water and notices drops on the outside.
- Observation: "There are small drops of water on the outside of the cup."
- Observation: "The cup feels cold."
- Inference: "Water leaked through the cup from the inside." (An inference!
  Nobody saw a leak. Another possible inference: water from the *air* gathered
  on the cold cup. A test could decide between them — wipe the cup dry, leave
  it empty, and see if drops return.)

**Worked example 5 — the empty feeder.**
- Observation: "The bird feeder is empty at 3:00 PM."
- Inference: "Birds ate all the seeds this morning." (Maybe — but wind could
  have spilled them, or the feeder could have a hole. An inference needs
  testing, not just believing.)

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Sort each question. Say "testable" or "not testable," and for testable
   ones, name the measurement.
   - *Hint: ask yourself "what would I count, measure, or time?"*
   a. "How long is our kitchen table in centimeters?"
   b. "Is pizza the greatest food ever invented?"
   c. "Do plants near a window grow taller than plants in a dark corner?"
   d. "What do stars think about at night?"
2. The adult shows you the mystery bag (touch only, no peeking). Say two
   observations ("It feels rough," "It is smaller than my hand"). Then say one
   inference ("I think it is a pinecone") and name one more observation you
   could make to *test* that inference ("I could smell it" / "I could weigh
   it").
   - *Hint: observations use your senses; the inference is your guess about
     what it is.*
3. Read: "The hallway floor is wet near the water fountain." Give one
   observation (already stated) and two different inferences (someone spilled
   water; the fountain is leaking). Which inference could a test rule out, and
   what would you observe?
   - *Hint: a good test changes what you would expect to see.*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Testable or not? "How many milliliters of water does my water bottle
   hold?" If testable, name the measurement.
2. Testable or not? "Are rainy days better than sunny days?" If not, reword
   it into a testable question.
3. Testable or not? "Does a ball bounce higher on tile or on carpet?" Name
   the measurement.
4. Testable or not? "Why is the ocean so mysterious?" Reword it into a
   testable question.
5. Observation or inference? "The leaf's edge feels rough."
6. Observation or inference? "The plant needs more sunlight."
7. Observation or inference? "The thermometer reads 22 °C."
8. Observation or inference? "The sidewalk is hot because the sun shone on it
   all morning." Explain which part is observation and which is inference.

## Application — the year-long question board

Start the unit's **question board** (a notebook page or wall space you will
keep all year). Write **three testable questions you genuinely wonder about**,
and for each one write what you would measure or observe to answer it. Circle
the one you most want to test this year. The adult dates the page. You will
revisit this board in every later unit — good investigators never run out of
questions.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. In your own words, what makes a question testable?
2. Give one observation and one inference about the weather outside right now.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** vocabulary is introduced with examples first,
  word second; the adult reads questions aloud on request; home-language
  labels welcome on the question board.
- **Alternate response mode:** all practice may be answered orally, by
  drawing, or by sorting printed question cards into two piles; the adult
  scribes dictated answers.
- **Extension:** error analysis — "This ramp made the car roll 80 cm, so tall
  ramps make cars roll far." What is missing? (A comparison: how far did the
  car roll from a *short* ramp? One measurement proves nothing without
  something to compare it to — the seed of Lesson 02's fair tests.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners call any science-sounding question
testable — insist on *naming the measurement*; a question without a named
measurement is not ready. (2) "The plant needs sunlight" feels like an
observation to many learners — press for what was *directly* noticed (pale,
bendy stem) versus the idea about it. (3) Some learners treat inferences as
wrong answers — affirm that inferences are useful *starting* ideas; the error
is only *stopping* at the inference. **Answer key:**
