# Lesson 02 — Variables, Fair Tests, and Repeated Trials

**Unit:** 01 — Measurement, Graphing, Models, and Investigation Design · **Grade 6**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 3 of 16

## Goal

You will name the **changed variable**, the **measured variable**, and the
**controlled variables** in an investigation, explain why a fair test changes
only one thing, and explain why scientists repeat trials.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- The unit's testable question from the question board (Lesson 01)
- Tabletop ramp kit for the demonstration: a smooth board (about 60 cm long),
  a stack of books, a toy car, a ruler or tape measure, a stopwatch, a
  notebook — the adult sets this up on a table

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult reads three quick sorts aloud. You answer: observation or inference?

1. "The car rolled 64 centimeters." → *observation* (a measurement)
2. "The tall ramp made the car go faster." → *inference* (an idea about what
   the measurement means — and it needs a fair test before we believe it)
3. "The stopwatch reads 3.2 seconds." → *observation*

If #2 felt like an observation, revisit Lesson 01: *why* the car behaved that
way is a step beyond what was seen.

## Teaching

### Part A — The three kinds of variables

Every investigation has three jobs for its **variables** (the things that can
change):

- The **changed variable** is the one thing the investigator *deliberately
  changes*. (Scientists also call this the *independent variable* — it is
  independent because the investigator sets it.)
- The **measured variable** is what you measure to see the *effect*. (Also
  called the *dependent variable* — it depends on what you changed.)
- The **controlled variables** are everything else, kept the *same on purpose*
  so the test stays fair. "Controlled" here does not mean "under control" in
  the everyday sense — it means deliberately held constant.

Now name the variables for this question: *"Does a taller ramp make the car
roll farther?"*

- Changed variable: **ramp height** (low, medium, high — set with 1, 2, or 3
  books).
- Measured variable: **how far the car rolls past the ramp** (in centimeters,
  measured with the ruler).
- Controlled variables: **the same car**, **the same board**, **the car
  released from the very top of the ramp every time** (not pushed), **the same
  table surface**.

If you also changed the car between heights, you would not know whether the
height or the car caused the difference. **A fair test changes exactly one
thing.** Everything else stays the same on purpose.

### Part B — Why repeat trials?

A **trial** is one run of a test. **Repeated trials** mean running the same
test several times. One trial can lie to you: the car might hit a crumb, your
finger might slip, you might misread the ruler. With three trials, a weird
result stands out, and the *pattern across trials* is what you trust.

**Worked example 1 — name all three variable types.**
Question: "Does a longer pendulum string make a slower swing?"
*Thinking:* The investigator changes the string length on purpose — that is
the **changed variable**. They measure the time for one complete swing in
seconds — that is the **measured variable**. They keep the same bob (mass),
the same release angle, and no friction — those are the **controlled
variables**. If they changed the mass *and* the length together, a slower
swing could be caused by either one — an unfair test.

**Worked example 2 — spot the unfair test.**
A learner tests whether ramp height changes roll distance. She uses 1 book,
then 3 books — but for the 3-book run she also switches to a heavier car.
*Thinking:* Two things changed (height AND car). If the car rolls farther, we
cannot tell which change caused it. Fix: use the *same car* for both heights —
keep the car a controlled variable.

**Worked example 3 — repeated trials catch mistakes.**
Fictional practice data: a learner times 10 swings of a 50-cm pendulum three
times and gets 14.3 s, 14.1 s, and 21.6 s. The third trial is wildly
different — probably a stopwatch mistake (started late, or counted an extra
swing). *Thinking:* With only one trial, she would report 21.6 s as the
answer. With three trials, the odd one out is visible. The honest move:
re-run the third trial, then use the two or three consistent measurements.
Repeated trials do not just give you more numbers — they give you a way to
*check* your numbers.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. For "Does the amount of sunlight change how tall a bean plant grows in two
   weeks?" name the changed variable, the measured variable, and two
   controlled variables.
   - *Hint: what does the investigator set? What gets measured at the end?
     What must stay equal for both plants?*
2. A learner asks, "Does warmer water dissolve sugar faster?" He stirs the
   warm cup but not the cold cup. What is unfair, and how would you fix it?
   - *Hint: look for something that changed besides the temperature.*
3. Fictional practice data — three trials measuring the same desk with a
   ruler: 118 cm, 119 cm, 132 cm. Which trial looks suspicious, and what
   should the learner do?
   - *Hint: two numbers agree closely; one does not. What is the honest
     response to an outlier?*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. For "Does ramp height change how far a toy car rolls?" name the changed
   variable and the measured variable.
2. For the same ramp question, name **three** controlled variables.
3. For "Does the mass of the pendulum bob change the swing time?" name the
   changed variable and the measured variable.
4. For the same pendulum question, name **two** controlled variables.
5. A learner tests paper-towel absorbency: Brand A gets 50 mL of water, Brand
   B gets 100 mL of water. Name what is unfair and how to fix it.
6. A learner rolls a car down a ramp once and reports the distance. Explain
   in two sentences why one trial is not enough.
7. Fictional practice data — three temperature readings of the same cup of
   water: 61 °C, 62 °C, 61 °C. Are these trials consistent? What does the
   consistency tell you?
8. Error analysis: "I changed the ramp height AND the car, and the car went
   farther, so taller ramps make cars go farther." Explain the flaw and
   rewrite the conclusion honestly.

## Application — plan your project test

On your question board page, pick the question you circled in Lesson 01 (or
the ramp/pendulum question for this unit's project). Write a **test plan**
with four labeled lines: *Changed variable*, *Measured variable*,
*Controlled variables (at least three)*, *Trials (how many, and why that
many)*. The adult initials the plan — it becomes your project proposal in
Session 12.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. Why must a fair test change only one variable?
2. What do repeated trials protect you against?

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** variable types taught with the ramp objects in
  hand first, terms second; the adult reads scenarios aloud; learners may
  label the three variable types with home-language words alongside the
  English terms.
- **Alternate response mode:** variable-sorting cards (each scenario on a
  card; learner points to or moves "changed / measured / controlled" labels);
  oral answers accepted; the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** design a *two-question* investigation — "Does ramp height
  change roll distance, AND does car mass change roll distance?" Explain why
  these must be run as *two separate* one-variable tests rather than one test
  that changes both, and sketch the data table each test would need.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners label the measured variable as the
changed one ("the distance changed, so distance is the changed variable") —
press on *who set it*: the investigator sets the changed variable; nature
answers with the measured one. (2) "Controlled variable" gets confused with
"controlling the experiment" — anchor it as "kept the *same* on purpose."
(3) Learners think more trials means changing something each time — trials
repeat the *same* conditions. **Answer key:**
