# Lesson 05 — Bar Graphs, Patterns, and Models vs. Reality

**Unit:** 01 — Measurement, Graphing, Models, and Investigation Design · **Grade 6**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 10 of 16

## Goal

You will choose **bar graphs** for category data (and know when a line graph
fits instead), describe **patterns** and **variability** honestly, and write a
**model-vs.-reality** statement naming what a model includes and what it
leaves out.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil; graph paper
- The learner's graph draft from Lesson 04's application
- A simple drawn model of the ramp investigation (board, books, car, arrows
  showing the roll) — the learner sketches it in this lesson

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult names a dataset; you answer "line graph" or "bar graph":

1. "Temperature of cooling water every 2 minutes" → *line graph* (change over
   time)
2. "Number of learners who chose ramp, pendulum, cooling water, or shadows
   for their project" → *bar graph* (categories — no time order, no
   in-between values)
3. "Roll distance at ramp heights of 10, 20, and 30 cm" → *line graph* (a
   continuous scale — height 15 cm would make sense between 10 and 20)

If #3 was tricky: the test is whether *in-between values make sense*. Between
"ramp" and "pendulum" nothing exists — that is a category, so bars.

## Teaching

### Part A — Bar graphs for categories

A **bar graph** compares amounts across **categories** — groups with no
natural order and no meaningful in-between values. Bars stay *separate* (never
touching), because nothing exists between the categories. Axes are still
labeled with units: categories on the horizontal axis, the measured amount on
the vertical axis.

*Fictional practice data:* a class votes on project topics.

| Project topic | Learners choosing it |
|---|---|
| Ramp | 9 |
| Pendulum | 7 |
| Cooling water | 5 |
| Shadows | 4 |

The bar graph: four separated bars, horizontal axis labeled "Project topic,"
vertical axis labeled "Number of learners." The tallest bar is Ramp (9); the
shortest is Shadows (4). The *pattern*: ramp and pendulum together drew 16 of
25 votes — motion investigations were the most popular *in this sample*.

### Part B — Choosing honestly: line or bar?

| Use a line graph when… | Use a bar graph when… |
|---|---|
| The horizontal axis is time or a continuous scale (temperature over minutes; distance over ramp heights) | The horizontal axis is categories (topics, materials, surfaces) |
| You want to show a trend or the shape of change | You want to compare amounts across groups |
| Points between measurements would make sense | Nothing exists between the bars |

**Worked example 1 — choose and defend.**
Dataset: "Bounce height of the same ball on tile, wood, carpet, and grass."
*Thinking:* Tile, wood, carpet, grass are categories — there is no "in
between" surface, and no time order. Bar graph. The horizontal axis: "Surface";
the vertical axis: "Bounce height (cm)." If someone drew a line graph, the
sloping line between "tile" and "wood" would *imply* a surface that does not
exist — a dishonest picture.

**Worked example 2 — spot the wrong choice.**
Dataset: "Plant height measured each week for 6 weeks," drawn as a bar graph.
*Thinking:* Weeks are a time order, and the plant grew *between* measurements
— a line graph shows the growth trend honestly. The bar graph is not wrong
arithmetically, but it hides the trend. Better choice: line graph, weeks on
the horizontal axis, height (cm) on the vertical.

**Worked example 3 — describe variability, not just the pattern.**
Fictional practice data: three trials of roll distance at one ramp height:
62 cm, 65 cm, 63 cm. *Thinking:* The pattern: the distances cluster near
63–64 cm (mean = (62+65+63)/3 = 190/3 ≈ 63.3 cm). The **variability**: the
trials spread over 3 cm (65 − 62). Honest sentence: "In this sample, the car
rolled about 63 cm on average, with trials varying by 3 cm." Variability is
not failure — it is information about how steady the measurement is. A second
fictional set — 40 cm, 65 cm, 90 cm — has the same mean (65 cm) but huge
variability, and should be trusted *less*.

### Part C — Models vs. reality

A **model** is a drawing, object, or description that represents a real system
and helps explain it. Every model **includes** some things and **leaves out**
others — and a good scientist says both out loud. This is the track's
year-long discipline, starting today.

Look at a simple model of the ramp investigation: a side-view drawing with a
labeled board, a stack of books setting the height, a toy car at the top, an
arrow showing the roll direction, and a ruler marking the landing zone.

- **What the model includes:** the ramp height (the changed variable), the
  car, the roll distance being measured, the release point at the top.
- **What the model leaves out:** friction between the car and the board, air
  resistance, the exact way the car was released, the table's tiny bumps, the
  stopwatch timing, the person doing the measuring.

The model is *useful* (it shows the plan clearly) and *incomplete* (the real
roll depends on friction too) — both true at once. In Session 12 you will draw
this model for your project and write its includes/leaves-out statement.

**Worked example 4 — write the statement.**
Model: a diagram of the pendulum investigation (string, bob, protractor
showing release angle, arrow showing swing).
*Thinking:* Includes: string length (changed variable), the bob, the release
angle, the swing being timed. Leaves out: air resistance slowing the bob,
friction at the pivot, the person's reaction time on the stopwatch, tiny
wobbles in the release. Honest statement: "This model shows the string length,
bob, release angle, and swing being timed. It leaves out air resistance,
pivot friction, and stopwatch reaction time — so a real swing time will
differ slightly from anything the model predicts."

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Dataset: "Mass (g) of five different rocks." Line graph or bar graph? Why?
   - *Hint: are rocks categories, or points on a continuous scale?*
2. Dataset: "Number of rainy days each month, January to June." Line graph or
   bar graph? Why?
   - *Hint: months have a time order — what does the line between bars add?*
3. For your ramp or pendulum project model sketch: name two things it
   includes and two things it leaves out.
   - *Hint: includes = what the drawing shows; leaves out = real-world
     details the drawing cannot show.*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. A dataset lists "preferred investigation topic" for 25 learners. Which
   graph type, and why?
2. A dataset lists "water temperature every 2 minutes for 10 minutes." Which
   graph type, and why?
3. Sketch the bar graph for the project-topic table in Part A (axes labeled
   with units where they apply). Which topic won *in this sample*?
4. Fictional practice data — pendulum swing-time trials: 1.4 s, 1.5 s,
   1.4 s. Compute the mean and the range (max − min). Write one honest
   sentence using both.
5. A learner's bar graph has bars touching each other like a histogram.
   Explain why separated bars are more honest for category data.
6. A graph's axes have no units. Explain in two sentences why the graph
   cannot be trusted as drawn.
7. Draw a simple side-view model of the ramp investigation with at least four
   labels. List two things your model includes and two things it leaves out.
8. Error analysis: "My model shows exactly what happens in the real
   investigation." Explain the flaw in this claim using your ramp model.

## Application — model draft for the project

Finish the labeled model sketch from task 7 and write its
**model-vs.-reality statement** (two to four sentences: what the model
includes, what it leaves out, and why the model is still useful). Bring both
to Session 12 — they become the model section of your project report.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. How do you decide between a line graph and a bar graph?
2. Name one thing your ramp model includes and one thing it leaves out.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** "category" anchored with familiar groups
  (pets, fruits, colors) before data categories; the line-vs-bar decision
  taught as a two-question flowchart on the word wall ("Is there a time
  order? Would in-between values make sense?").
- **Alternate response mode:** sort printed dataset cards onto "line graph"
  / "bar graph" mats; dictate the model-vs.-reality statement while pointing
  at the sketch; the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** the *ethics* of pictures — find or imagine a graph that
  misleads (a y-axis that starts at 80 instead of 0, making a small change
  look huge). Explain in three sentences how the picture misleads and how to
  fix it. (This foreshadows Unit 07's evidence-evaluation work.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners pick bar graphs for everything
because bars feel easier — drill the two-question flowchart until the choice
is justified, not guessed. (2) "The model is wrong because it leaves things
out" — reframe: *all* models leave things out; the question is whether what
was left out matters for the question being asked. (3) Variability read as
failure — celebrate the range as data about steadiness. **Answer key:**
