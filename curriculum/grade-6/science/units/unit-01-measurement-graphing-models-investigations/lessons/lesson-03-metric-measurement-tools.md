# Lesson 03 — Measuring with Standard Tools and the Metric System

**Unit:** 01 — Measurement, Graphing, Models, and Investigation Design · **Grade 6**
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Session:** 5 of 16

## Goal

You will **measure** length (nearest millimeter), mass (nearest gram), volume
(nearest 10 milliliters), and temperature (nearest degree Celsius) with
standard tools, **convert** within the metric system by place value, and record
everything in **labeled data tables with units**.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil
- Meter stick or tape measure, ruler (the unit's printable
  [`ruler-scale.svg`](../assets/ruler-scale.svg) works for the estimating
  game), kitchen or spring scale, graduated cylinder or metric measuring cup,
  alcohol thermometer (never mercury — the adult handles all thermometers)
- 5 small objects of different lengths, a cup of room-temperature water

## Readiness check (4 minutes)

The adult holds up each tool. You name what it measures:

1. Meter stick → *length*
2. Kitchen scale → *mass* (how much matter — scientists say "mass," not
   "weight," for what a scale measures here)
3. Graduated cylinder → *volume* (how much space a liquid fills)
4. Thermometer → *temperature* (how hot or cold)

Then: "Why do scientists everywhere use the *metric* system instead of each
country inventing its own units?" (Because shared units let anyone repeat and
check anyone else's measurements — the same reason science needs testable
questions.)

## Teaching

### Part A — Four tools, four quantities

- **Length** — measured with a ruler, meter stick, or tape measure. Metric
  units: **millimeter (mm)**, **centimeter (cm)**, **meter (m)**,
  **kilometer (km)**. The unit's printable ruler shows 15 cm with every
  millimeter marked: the numbered marks are centimeters; the ten small spaces
  between them are millimeters.
- **Mass** — measured with a balance or scale. Metric units: **gram (g)**,
  **kilogram (kg)**.
- **Volume** — measured with a graduated cylinder or metric measuring cup.
  Metric units: **milliliter (mL)**, **liter (L)**. Read the *bottom of the
  curve* (the meniscus) at eye level.
- **Temperature** — measured with a thermometer. Metric unit: **degree Celsius
  (°C)**. In science we write °C, and we always include the unit — "22" is
  not a temperature; "22 °C" is.

**The measuring ritual** (use it at every station, all year): **estimate
first** (write your guess), **measure** (tool at eye level, zero at the edge),
**record with the unit** (every number gets its unit, every column gets its
label).

### Part B — Converting by place value

Metric units are powers of ten, so converting is moving the decimal point —
no new arithmetic to memorize, just place value.

| Relationship | Shorthand |
|---|---|
| 10 mm = 1 cm | mm → cm: 10 small steps make 1 cm |
| 100 cm = 1 m | cm → m: 100 cm make 1 m |
| 1,000 m = 1 km | m → km: 1,000 m make 1 km |
| 1,000 g = 1 kg | g → kg |
| 1,000 mL = 1 L | mL → L |

Bigger unit → the number gets *smaller* (fewer big units fit). Smaller unit
→ the number gets *bigger* (more small units fit). Check: 5 cm = 0.05 m —
meters are bigger than centimeters, so 5 cm is only five-hundredths of a
meter. ✓

**Worked example 1 — cm to m.**
Convert 250 cm to meters. *Thinking:* 100 cm = 1 m, and meters are the bigger
unit, so the number shrinks: move the decimal two places left.
250 cm = 2.50 m. Check: 2.50 m is about two and a half meter sticks — and 250
cm is two and a half meter sticks. ✓

**Worked example 2 — L to mL.**
Convert 2.5 L to milliliters. *Thinking:* 1,000 mL = 1 L, and milliliters are
the smaller unit, so the number grows: move the decimal three places right.
2.5 L = 2,500 mL. Check: a 2-liter bottle plus a half-liter bottle is 2,500
one-milliliter sips — reasonable. ✓

**Worked example 3 — g to kg.**
Convert 3,400 g to kilograms. *Thinking:* 1,000 g = 1 kg, kilograms are
bigger, so the number shrinks: move the decimal three places left.
3,400 g = 3.4 kg. Check: 3.4 kg is about the mass of a small bag of flour —
and 3,400 g is the same bag. ✓

### Part C — Data tables with units

A **data table** holds measurements in rows and columns. The rule: **every
column header names the quantity AND its unit** — "Roll distance (cm)", not
"distance." A reader should understand the table without asking you anything.

| Trial | Ramp height (cm) | Roll distance (cm) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 62 |
| 2 | 10 | 65 |
| 3 | 10 | 63 |

*Fictional practice data — not measurements anyone made.* Notice: the units
sit in the headers, so the cells hold clean numbers; the changed variable
(ramp height) has its own column even though it stayed the same across these
trials — that is how a reader sees what was controlled.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Convert: 45 mm → ? cm; 1.2 km → ? m.
   - *Hint: mm→cm is one small step up (÷10); km→m is three big steps down
     (×1,000).*
2. Convert: 750 mL → ? L; 0.5 kg → ? g.
   - *Hint: which unit is bigger? The number moves the opposite way.*
3. The adult measures an object as "14." Ask: "14 *what*?" Then re-record it
   correctly with a unit, and explain why the unit matters.
   - *Hint: 14 mm, 14 cm, and 14 m describe very different objects.*

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Convert 7.5 cm to millimeters.
2. Convert 620 mm to centimeters.
3. Convert 1.5 m to centimeters.
4. Convert 2,000 mL to liters.
5. Convert 0.75 L to milliliters.
6. Convert 500 g to kilograms.
7. A table column is headed "Temperature." What is wrong with this header,
   and how would you fix it?
8. Error analysis: A learner writes "The board is 61 long." Name two things
   wrong with this record and rewrite it correctly.

## Application — the measuring ritual at home

Estimate, then measure, then record: the length of your bed (cm), the mass of
a full water bottle (g), the volume of your drinking glass (mL), and the
temperature of cold tap water (°C). Put all four in one labeled data table
with units in every column header. Bring it to Session 6 — your estimates-vs.-
measurements become the station warm-up.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. Why does converting 250 cm to meters make the *number* smaller even
   though nothing physical changed?
2. What two things must every data-table column header include?

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** prefixes taught as "kilo = thousand (like
  kilobyte)," "centi = hundredth (like century/100)," "milli = thousandth
  (like millennium/1,000)" — the words carry the math; visual prefix staircase
  on the word wall.
- **Alternate response mode:** place-value sliders or a decimal-point "hop"
  mat on the floor — physically hop the decimal left/right for each
  conversion; oral answers accepted.
- **Extension:** the *why* behind the ritual — measure the same desk edge
  five times and record all five readings. How much do they vary? Write two
  sentences connecting this variability to why Lesson 02 demanded repeated
  trials.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners move the decimal the wrong way —
anchor with "bigger unit, smaller number" and always *check against reality*
(2.5 m of string vs. 250 cm of string: same string). (2) "Mass" vs. "weight"
— at this level, accept "how heavy" language but model "mass" for what the
scale measures; do not introduce the mass/weight distinction formally (above
band). (3) Reading a graduated cylinder from above instead of eye level —
demonstrate the meniscus once, then require eye-level reads. **Answer key:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-03--measuring-with-standard-tools-and-the-metric-system).
