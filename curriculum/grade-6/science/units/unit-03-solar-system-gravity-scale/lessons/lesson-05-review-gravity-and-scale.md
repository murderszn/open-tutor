# Lesson 05 — Review: What the Models Show and What They Hide

**Student goal:** I can audit every model in this unit, reject the unit's
three big misconceptions with evidence, and explain gravity and scale in my
own words.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S13 (plus S14–S16 showcase, assessment, debrief)
**Materials:** the size strip (Lesson 03), the distance line sketch
(Lesson 04), the orbit illustration (Lesson 01), the dataset table.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Without looking at your notes, write one
sentence each: (a) what gravity does in the solar system; (b) why one model
cannot show true sizes and true distances together. You will compare these
sentences with your Lesson 01–04 work at the end of this lesson.

## Teaching

Every model in this unit told a partial truth. That is not a flaw — it is
what models are for — but a scientist must be able to say *which* part is
true and which part is bent. Audit each model with the same three questions:

1. **What does it include?** (What relationships does it show correctly?)
2. **What does it leave out or distort?** (Sizes? Distances? Time? Shape?)
3. **What claim would be wrong if you trusted it too far?**

Apply them:

- **The orbit illustration (Lesson 01)** includes a central Sun with
  planets on orbits, and one reliably identifiable planet (Saturn, by its
  rings). It leaves out true sizes, true distances, AND true order — planet
  positions in the picture are decorative. Trusting it too far would give
  you "Neptune is one of the biggest planets" (it is 4th of 8 by diameter,
  not 2nd), "the planets are evenly spaced" (they are not — see the AU
  gaps), or any order read off the picture (the picture does not encode
  order at all).
- **The size strip (Lesson 03)** includes true size ratios (Earth = 1 cm).
  It leaves out distances entirely and leaves out the Sun (109 cm does not
  fit). Trusting it too far would give you "the planets sit next to each
  other" — they do not; the strip is a lineup, not a map.
- **The distance line (Lesson 04)** includes true distance ratios
  (1 AU = 10 m). It leaves out sizes — on that scale Earth would be a speck
  under a millimetre wide. Trusting it too far would give you "the planets
  are dots" — they are worlds, just very far apart.
- **The ball-and-string demo (Lesson 02)** includes the two ingredients of
  an orbit (inward pull + sideways motion). It leaves out the mechanism:
  there is no string in space, and gravity acts without contact. Trusting
  it too far would give you "something holds the planets up" — nothing
  holds them; they are falling, and missing, forever.

### The three misconceptions — clinic

Say each one out loud, then say why it fails, with evidence:

1. **"Gravity is Earth's thing."** Fails because gravity is a pull between
   any two masses. Evidence: the Sun's pull holds all eight planets in
   orbit; Jupiter's surface gravity (24.79 m/s²) is stronger than Earth's
   (9.81 m/s²) — if gravity were Earth's alone, Jupiter's number would be
   meaningless.
2. **"Orbits are tracks planets ride on."** Fails because there is nothing
   to ride on. Evidence: the ball-and-string model works only while the
   string pulls; in space, the "string" is the Sun's pull itself, and the
   orbit is the path of a continuous fall combined with sideways motion.
3. **"Textbook solar-system pictures are to scale."** Fails on both scales.
   Evidence: at true size scale (Earth = 1 cm), Neptune sits 3.5 km away;
   at true distance scale (1 AU = 10 m), Earth is a sub-millimetre speck.
   No single classroom picture can be true to both.

### Putting gravity and scale together

The unit's two ideas meet in one sentence: **gravity's pull weakens with
distance, and distance is what makes the solar system vast.** The Sun holds
Mercury tightly on a short, fast orbit (88 days) and holds Neptune loosely
on a long, slow one (about 165 years) — same force, different distances,
different years. And the scale models show why that vastness is hard to
picture: the distances are so large compared with the planets that any
honest picture must distort one dimension and label the distortion. A
learner who can say what a model distorts is a learner who cannot be fooled
by it.

## Guided practice

1. Audit the orbit illustration with the three questions above. Write one
   sentence for each question.
2. A friend looks at your size strip and says, "Wow, Jupiter and Saturn are
   right next to each other." What does the strip actually show, and what
   should you tell your friend about the real distance between them?
   (Use the AU table: Jupiter 5.20 AU, Saturn 9.58 AU — a gap of 4.38 AU.)
3. Which model in this unit would you use to answer "how big is Jupiter
   compared with Earth?" Which would you use for "how far is Neptune?"
   Why can't one model answer both?
4. Revisit your readiness-check sentences. Correct or improve each one in a
   different color, using one number from the dataset in each correction.

## Independent practice

1. Write a paragraph (5–7 sentences) that explains gravity and scale to a
   younger learner: what holds the planets up (and why "holds up" is the
   wrong phrase), and why pictures of the solar system lie about size or
   distance. Include the numbers 11 (Jupiter's Earth-widths), 30 (Neptune's
   AU), and 3.5 km (Neptune's true-scale distance at Earth = 1 cm).
2. **Error analysis:** A poster shows the Sun and all eight planets in a
   row, evenly spaced, with the Sun only twice as wide as Jupiter, and the
   caption "Drawn to scale." List three things wrong with that caption,
   with a number for each.
3. Predict: if a planet were found at 40 AU, would its year be longer or
   shorter than Neptune's? Would it more likely be rocky or giant, by the
   patterns in this unit? Give one reason for each prediction.

## Applied task

**Exhibit defense (rehearsal for the project showcase).** Stand by your
size strip and distance line and defend them to your adult as if to a
museum visitor: (a) one thing each model shows truly; (b) one thing each
model distorts; (c) the answer to "so which model is right?" (Both — each
for its own question.) The adult notes any claim you cannot support with a
number; those claims go on your reteach list before the assessment.

## Exit check

1. Name one thing the distance line distorts.
2. In one sentence, why is "orbit as a track" wrong?
3. Neptune's year is longer than Mercury's because Neptune is ______.
   (One word, then one number as evidence.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the misconception clinic aloud; the
  learner supplies the "why it fails" half from memory before checking the
  text.
- **Alternate response mode:** the audit may be given orally, with the
  learner pointing to each model as it is discussed.
- **Extension:** write a limits card for a real published solar-system
  picture (adult-approved source): what does it include, what does it
  distort, and what claim would be wrong if you trusted it too far?

## Teacher note

This lesson is the unit's hinge: learners who can audit models here will
carry the skill into Unit 04's rock-cycle models and Unit 08's capstone.
Watch for learners who can recite "3.5 km" without knowing what scale it
belongs to — always ask "at what scale?" Answer-key reference: Practice
Set E and the assessment key.
