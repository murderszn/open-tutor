# Lesson 02 — Gravity Holds It Together

**Student goal:** I can explain how gravity holds the solar system together,
why an orbit is a fall that never lands, and what changes when mass or
distance changes.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S5 (plus S6–S8 lab and practice)
**Materials:** a soft ball on a string (adult-supervised, clear space); a
smooth floor or table for rolling demos; the dataset table in the
[unit README](../README.md).

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Drop a soft ball and watch it fall. Write
one sentence: what pulled it down? Then guess: does the Sun pull on Earth
the same way? Keep your sentence — this lesson tests it.

## Teaching

**Gravity is a pull between any two masses.** That sentence is the whole
lesson, so read it twice. It does not say "gravity is something Earth has."
Your mass pulls on Earth, and Earth pulls on you — that is why the ball
falls. The Moon's mass pulls on Earth, and Earth's mass pulls on the Moon.
And the Sun's mass — enormous, more than 99% of the system's mass — pulls
on every planet. Gravity needs no air, no rope, and no contact: it acts
across empty space.

So why don't the planets fall *into* the Sun? Because they are also moving
*sideways*, fast. An **orbit** is what you get when you combine those two
things: the Sun's pull constantly bends a planet's path toward the Sun,
while the planet's forward motion constantly carries it past the Sun. The
result is a closed loop — a continuous fall *around* the Sun instead of a
fall *into* it. Think of it this way: a planet is always falling toward the
Sun, and always missing, because it is moving sideways fast enough to keep
missing forever.

The ball-and-string demo makes this visible. When you swing a ball on a
string around your hand, your hand (like the Sun) pulls inward through the
string, and the ball's sideways motion keeps it circling instead of flying
off or collapsing inward. Two things you can test with it, and both match
the real solar system qualitatively (this unit stays qualitative — no
gravity equations, per the MS-ESS1-2 assessment boundary):

- **More pull, tighter hold:** shorten the string (like moving a planet
  closer to the Sun) and the ball needs to move faster to stay in its circle;
  the inward pull is stronger. Mercury, closest to the Sun, has the shortest
  year (88 days) — it races around its small orbit.
- **Less pull, looser hold:** lengthen the string and the ball circles more
  slowly and widely. Neptune, farthest out, takes about 165 Earth years to
  complete one orbit.

The same force works bigger than the solar system, too: gravity holds stars
together in galaxies, including our Milky Way. MS-ESS1-2 asks for a model
of gravity's role "in the motions within galaxies and the solar system" —
one force, two scales. You do not need galaxy details for this unit; you
need the idea that the Sun is not special in *having* gravity, only in
having *the most mass nearby*.

**Mass vs. weight — do not mix these up.** Your **mass** is how much matter
you contain, and it stays the same wherever you go. Your **weight** is the
pull of gravity on that mass, and it changes from planet to planet, because
each planet's surface gravity is different. The dataset table's last column
gives surface gravity in meters per second squared (m/s²). Earth's is 9.81.
Jupiter's is 24.79 — about 2.5 times Earth's — so the same mass weighs about
2.5 times as much on Jupiter as on Earth. Mars's is 3.71 — about 0.38 times
Earth's — so the same mass weighs a little over a third as much on Mars.
(You would *feel* heavy on Jupiter and light on Mars, but your body itself
would not gain or lose matter.)

### Worked example 1 — Why doesn't Earth crash into the Sun?

Question: *The Sun's gravity pulls on Earth all the time. Why doesn't Earth
spiral into the Sun?*

Step-by-step:

1. Name the two motions: Earth is pulled toward the Sun **and** moving
   sideways along its orbit at the same time.
2. The pull bends Earth's path into a curve — without the pull, Earth
   would fly off in a straight line; without the sideways motion, Earth
   would fall straight in.
3. The balance of the two is an orbit: a fall that keeps missing.
4. Answer: **Earth is falling toward the Sun continuously, but its sideways
   motion carries it around the Sun instead of into it.** That is what an
   orbit is.

### Worked example 2 — Weight on another planet

Question: *A spring scale reads 50 newtons (N) for a rock on Earth. What
would it read on Mars and on Jupiter? Show your working.*

Step-by-step:

1. The scale reading is weight, and weight scales with surface gravity:
   new weight = old weight × (new planet's gravity ÷ Earth's gravity).
2. Mars: 50 × (3.71 ÷ 9.81) = 50 × 0.378 ≈ **19 N**.
3. Jupiter: 50 × (24.79 ÷ 9.81) = 50 × 2.527 ≈ **126 N**.
4. Check the sense: Mars's reading is smaller than Earth's (weaker pull),
   Jupiter's is larger (stronger pull). Both match the gravity column.
5. The rock's mass did not change — only the pull on it did.

## Guided practice

Use the dataset table. Show your working for every calculation.

1. In your own words, explain why the Moon does not fall to Earth and also
   does not fly away into space. Use the words "pull" and "sideways."
2. A ball on a string circles your hand. You shorten the string and keep
   the ball circling. Does the ball need to move faster or slower? What
   does this model suggest about Mercury compared with Neptune?
3. Which planet's gravity would make you feel heaviest: Jupiter, Saturn, or
   Uranus? Use the surface-gravity column as evidence.
4. A rock weighs 80 N on Earth. Calculate its weight on Mercury (gravity
   3.7 m/s²) and on Neptune (11.15 m/s²). Round to the nearest whole newton.
5. True or false, with reasoning: "Gravity only works on Earth, because
   Earth is the only planet with an atmosphere." (Hint: what does gravity
   need, according to this lesson — air, or mass?)
6. **Error analysis:** A classmate says, "Planets stay in orbit because they
   are stuck on invisible tracks around the Sun." What is wrong with the
   "tracks" idea, and what replaces it?

## Independent practice

1. Draw and label a two-arrow diagram for a planet in orbit: one arrow
   pointing toward the Sun labeled "gravity pulls inward," one arrow
   tangent to the orbit labeled "planet moves sideways." Write one sentence
   under it explaining why the two arrows together make an orbit.
2. If the Sun suddenly vanished (a thought experiment — it will not),
   what would happen to Earth's motion, and why? Answer in two sentences.
3. A rock weighs 100 N on Earth. Calculate its weight on Venus (8.87 m/s²)
   and on Saturn (10.44 m/s²). Which is closer to its Earth weight, and why
   does that make sense from the gravity column?
4. **Explain your reasoning:** Two planets orbit the same star. Planet A is
   close to the star; Planet B is far away. Which one most likely has the
   shorter year, and what two pieces of evidence from this unit support
   your answer? (Use the ball-and-string model and the dataset pattern.)
5. Why is "the Sun holds the solar system together because it has the most
   mass" a better explanation than "the Sun holds the solar system together
   because it is the brightest"? Give one reason.

## Applied task

**Ball-and-string fair test (adult-supervised, soft ball only, clear space).**
Question: *How does string length change how fast the ball must move to
stay in a steady circle?* Change only the string length; keep the ball, the
swinger, and the effort description the same; repeat each length three
times and record whether the circle stayed steady. Write a three-sentence
conclusion that connects your result to Mercury vs. Neptune. (Full
fair-test table in the project file's lab notes; this task uses the Unit 01
fair-test format: changed variable, controlled variables, repeated trials.)

## Exit check

1. What two things combine to make an orbit?
2. Does your mass change if you travel to Mars? Does your weight? One
   sentence each.
3. Which holds the solar system together: the Sun's light, or the Sun's
   gravity? Why?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the worked examples aloud first; the
  learner follows the numbers with a finger on the table.
- **Alternate response mode:** the orbit diagram may be built with a ball
  and a curved arrow of string on the floor instead of drawn.
- **Extension:** research question (adult-approved source only): how do
  scientists know the Sun contains more than 99% of the system's mass?
  Write a two-sentence answer that mentions observing orbits (planets move
  as if pulled by one dominant mass).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: gravity as Earth-only; orbits as tracks or rails;
"falling" meaning "getting closer" (in an orbit, the fall is continuous but
the distance does not shrink); weight and mass as synonyms. The
ball-and-string model is an analogy, not a mechanism: there is no string in
space, and the pull acts without contact. Answer-key reference: Practice
Set B and the lab notes in the answer key.
