# Lesson 03 — The Rock Cycle: One Story, Many Paths

**Student goal:** I can trace matter through the rock cycle, naming
the process at every step, and show that the cycle has no single
starting point.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S9 (plus S11 practice)
**Materials:** the rock-cycle diagram below; Lesson 02's six sort
samples; paper for path tracing.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Write the three rock families in a
triangle. Draw one arrow between two of them and label it with the
process that changes the first into the second. Keep your triangle —
this lesson completes it.

## Teaching

The **rock cycle** is a model, not a machine. It says: Earth materials
change form through a set of processes, and those processes can run in
many orders. There is no starting line and no finish line. A rock can
loop, skip steps, or sit unchanged for millions of years and then move
suddenly. The diagram below is the unit's shared model — every path you
trace in this unit must be traceable on it.

![Rock cycle diagram: five boxes arranged in a loop — Magma, Igneous rock, Sediment, Sedimentary rock, Metamorphic rock. Labeled arrows show the processes between them: cooling and crystallization (magma to igneous rock); weathering, erosion, and deposition (igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rock to sediment); compaction and cementation (sediment to sedimentary rock); heat and pressure without melting (sedimentary and igneous rock to metamorphic rock); melting (metamorphic and igneous rock to magma); and uplift (rocks at depth raised to the surface where weathering can act). Arrows driven mainly by the Sun's energy are tagged S; arrows driven mainly by Earth's interior heat are tagged E.](../assets/rock-cycle-diagram.svg)

*Caption: The unit's rock-cycle model (deterministic diagram, drawn to
the process list in the teaching below — not a generated image). Read
the arrows, not just the boxes: the arrows are the processes, and the
processes are what the assessment tests. The diagram leaves out
timescales, locations, and rates; Lesson 04 names those limits.*

The processes on the diagram, in plain language:

- **Melting** — rock heated until it becomes magma. (Energy: Earth's
  interior.)
- **Cooling and crystallization** — magma cools; minerals grow as
  crystals; the result is igneous rock. Slow underground = large
  crystals; fast at the surface = small crystals or glass. (Energy:
  Earth's interior heat is what made the magma; cooling itself is heat
  escaping.)
- **Weathering, erosion, deposition** — weathering breaks rock down in
  place; erosion moves the pieces; deposition lays them down as
  sediment. Any rock family can enter this path once it is exposed at
  the surface. (Energy: the Sun, through wind, rain, ice, and moving
  water.)
- **Compaction and cementation** — buried sediment is squeezed
  (compaction) and glued by crystallizing minerals (cementation) into
  sedimentary rock.
- **Heat and pressure (metamorphism)** — buried rock is heated and
  squeezed until its minerals change, without melting, into metamorphic
  rock. (Energy: Earth's interior.)
- **Uplift** — rock at depth is raised toward the surface (for example
  by mountain building), where weathering can attack it again.
  (Energy: Earth's interior.)

Two reading rules. **First, arrows can skip.** Igneous rock can weather
straight to sediment; it does not have to become metamorphic first.
Metamorphic rock can melt straight to magma. Sedimentary rock can be
uplifted and weathered again without ever becoming metamorphic.
**Second, the same box can be reached by different paths.** Sediment
comes from any exposed rock; magma comes from any rock that melts.
A path is valid if every arrow on it is a real process in the right
direction — not if it visits every box.

### Worked example 1 — A full loop, step by step

Start: granite (igneous), deep underground.

1. **Uplift, then weathering, erosion, deposition** raise the granite
   to the surface and break it into sand laid in a riverbed. (Energy:
   interior for uplift, then Sun for weathering and transport — the
   diagram combines them on one arrow.)
2. **Compaction and cementation** turn the buried sand into sandstone
   (sedimentary). (Energy: burial — neither engine alone; see Lesson 04.)
3. Deeper burial adds **heat and pressure**; the sandstone becomes a
   banded metamorphic rock, without melting. (Energy: interior.)
4. Still deeper, it **melts** into magma. (Energy: interior.)
5. The magma **cools and crystallizes** into new igneous rock.
   (Energy: interior heat escaping.)

Five arrows, six processes, three rock families, one batch of matter,
zero matter created or destroyed. Notice step 2's energy tag is
honestly awkward — compaction and cementation are driven by burial
weight and groundwater chemistry, not directly by either engine.
Lesson 04 teaches you to say so instead of forcing a tag.

### Worked example 2 — A shortcut that is still a valid path

Start: basalt (igneous) erupted at the surface.

1. **Weathering, erosion, deposition** break the basalt into sediment
   on a beach. (Energy: Sun.)
2. **Compaction and cementation** turn the sediment into sedimentary
   rock.

Two arrows. The path never visits metamorphic rock or magma, and it is
still a complete, correct rock-cycle path — because the cycle is a
network, not a single road you must walk end to end. A classmate who
insists every path must visit all five boxes has mistaken the diagram
for a board game.

## Guided practice

Work in pairs, out loud, before writing:

1. Trace this path and name the missing processes: limestone
   (sedimentary) → ??? → marble (metamorphic) → ??? → magma. (Two
   processes; the first one has a condition: what must *not* happen
   when limestone becomes marble?)
2. A classmate traces: sediment → melting → igneous rock. Find the
   error. What step is missing, and why can sediment not melt directly
   into an igneous rock in this model?
3. Start from any box. Find a path back to the same box that uses
   exactly three arrows. Is your path the only one? Why or why not?
4. Which arrow on the diagram could run fastest in real life, and
   which slowest? Give a reason for each — and name what the diagram
   does not tell you about speed.

## Application

Pick one Lesson 02 sort sample (R1–R6). Write its "next chapter": one
plausible next process, the rock it would become, and the evidence a
geologist millions of years later could use to prove the chapter
happened. Then write one "impossible chapter" for the same sample —
a step the diagram forbids — and explain why it is forbidden.

## Exit check

Draw the five-box cycle from memory (boxes + arrows + process labels).
Circle the one arrow you are least sure about, and write one question
about it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** trace paths with a finger on the
  diagram while saying each process aloud before writing anything.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the cycle with five labeled cards
  and arrow cards on a table, and walk a token along a path while
  narrating, instead of drawing.
- **Extension:** add a "living things" side-note to the diagram: where
  do shells, plants, and soil fit into the sedimentary path, and why
  does the diagram leave them out? (Answer in the teacher guide.)

## Teacher note

Misconceptions to listen for: the cycle must visit every box in order;
melting = metamorphism (metamorphism explicitly excludes melting);
sediment can melt directly (it must first become rock, or melt as part
of a rock mass — the model's arrows run rock → magma, never loose
sediment → magma); uplift is weathering (uplift only *exposes* rock to
weathering). Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 03.
