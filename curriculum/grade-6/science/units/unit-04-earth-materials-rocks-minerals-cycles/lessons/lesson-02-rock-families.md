# Lesson 02 — Three Rock Families from How They Formed

**Student goal:** I can classify a rock as igneous, sedimentary, or
metamorphic from the evidence of how it formed, and defend the call.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S5 (plus S6 rock-sort lab)
**Materials:** the six sort samples below; hand lens; eye protection;
real samples only with an adult present.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** A rock is a mixture of minerals. So
why would two rocks made of similar minerals look completely
different? Write your best guess in one sentence — Lesson 02's answer
is that the *history* is different, and history leaves marks.

## Teaching

Geologists sort rocks into three families **by how they formed**, not
by what color they are. Formation leaves evidence you can read: crystal
size, layers, grains, banding, fossils. Learn the three stories, and
the sort almost does itself.

### Igneous: cooled from melt

**Igneous rock** forms when molten material cools and crystallizes.
Below the surface it is called **magma**; at the surface, **lava**. The
cooling speed decides the texture, and the texture is the evidence:

- **Slow cooling, deep underground** → crystals have time to grow large
  and interlocking. Granite is the classroom example: speckled, with
  crystals you can see without a lens, because it cooled over thousands
  of years or more inside the crust.
- **Fast cooling at the surface** → crystals stay tiny or never form.
  Basalt is dark and fine-grained (its crystals are too small to see
  easily) because lava at the surface cools quickly. Obsidian cools so
  fast it forms glass, with no crystals at all. Pumice is full of holes
  where gas bubbles were trapped as lava hardened — light enough that
  some pieces float.

**Rule of thumb:** visible interlocking crystals + no layers = cooled
slowly underground. Fine or glassy + no layers = cooled fast at the
surface. Either way, the rock was once molten.

### Sedimentary: built from sediment

**Sedimentary rock** forms in four steps, in order:

1. **Weathering** breaks existing rock down in place (cracking,
   freezing water, chemical change).
2. **Erosion** moves the broken pieces; **deposition** lays them down
   somewhere new — a riverbed, a lake bottom, a sea floor, a desert.
3. **Burial** piles more sediment on top. The weight **compacts** the
   lower layers, squeezing water and air out of the spaces between
   grains.
4. **Cementation** glues the grains together as minerals crystallize
   in the remaining spaces.

**Rule of thumb:** layers, visible grains or rounded pebbles cemented
together, or fossils = sedimentary. Sandstone (sand grains cemented),
shale (fine mud layers that split into sheets), limestone (often from
sea-shell material), and conglomerate (rounded pebbles in a cemented
mix) are the examples this unit uses. Fossils turn up here, not in the
other two families, because only sedimentary rock forms gently enough,
at low temperature, for remains to survive burial.

### Metamorphic: changed without melting

**Metamorphic rock** forms when an existing rock is heated, squeezed,
or soaked in reactive fluids — but **not melted**. If it melted, it
would become magma and the story would restart as igneous. Under heat
and pressure, old minerals recrystallize into new ones, and flat or
platy minerals line up into **bands** (foliation). Slate forms when
shale is squeezed (it splits into flat sheets); marble forms when
limestone is heated and pressed (its shell fragments recrystallize into
interlocking crystals); gneiss shows bold light-and-dark banding from
intense squeezing deep in the crust.

**Rule of thumb:** banding, or a rock whose story says "this used to be
something else and got cooked and squeezed" = metamorphic. The key
question is always: *did it melt?* Melted → igneous. Changed solid →
metamorphic.

### Worked example 1 — Crystals you can see, no layers

A sample is speckled pink, white, and black, with crystals about the
size of rice grains locked together, and no layers anywhere. **Step 1:**
no layers rules out the sedimentary story (sediment settles in layers).
**Step 2:** interlocking crystals mean minerals grew from a melt —
crystals that size need time, so cooling was slow. **Step 3:** slow
cooling happens underground, not at the surface. **Conclusion:**
igneous, intrusive (cooled underground). The evidence chain matters
more than the conclusion: a classmate who says "igneous because it is
pink" has the right label and no argument.

### Worked example 2 — Layers with a fossil

A sample splits into thin sheets. Between two sheets sits the clear
imprint of a shell. **Step 1:** sheets = layers = deposited sediment.
**Step 2:** a fossil survived, so the rock never got hot enough to melt
or recrystallize — that rules out igneous and metamorphic. **Step 3:**
layers + fossil = sediment buried, compacted, and cemented.
**Conclusion:** sedimentary (shale-like). Note what the fossil proves
and does not prove: it proves gentle, low-temperature burial; it does
not prove *which* sedimentary rock, and it says nothing about the
sample's age in years.

## The six-sample sort (Session S6)

Classify each sample and give the formation reason. Use the evidence
words: crystals, layers, grains, pebbles, banding, glassy, fossil.

| Sample | Description |
|---|---|
| R1 | Dark, fine-grained; no crystals visible even with a hand lens; no layers. Found near an old lava flow. |
| R2 | Light tan; you can see sand-sized grains; breaks along flat layers; a small shell fossil sits on one surface. |
| R3 | Bold alternating light and dark bands; minerals lined up in stripes; no fossils; hard and dense. |
| R4 | Rounded pebbles of many colors stuck together in a cemented mix; no crystals, no banding. |
| R5 | Black and glassy with a smooth, curved break; no crystals; sharp edges. |
| R6 | White, with interlocking crystals; the adult's note says it formed where limestone was heated and pressed deep underground. |

## Guided practice

Work in pairs, out loud, before writing:

1. R1 and R5 are both dark and both igneous. What is the *same* about
   their stories, and what is *different*? (Hint: cooling speed.)
2. Why does R6's adult note matter more than its color? What would you
   conclude from "white with crystals" alone, and why is that unsafe?
3. A classmate says R4 is metamorphic "because it looks squished
   together." Rewrite the argument correctly, or refute it.
4. Which family can never contain a fossil, and why? (Two families —
   give a reason for each.)

## Application

Write a one-paragraph "birth certificate" for a rock of your choice
(real or imagined): family, the process that formed it, the evidence a
geologist would see, and one thing that would prove you wrong. Trade
with a partner and check each other's evidence chains.

## Exit check

Name the three families and, for each, one piece of evidence that
points to it and one process that formed it. No color words allowed.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the three stories are told in order
  above; retell each story in your own words before sorting.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort by placing sample cards (or real
  samples) into three labeled trays and explaining each placement
  orally, instead of writing.
- **Extension:** pick one rock (granite, sandstone, or marble) and
  trace it one step backward and one step forward in the rock cycle
  (Lesson 03): what was it before, and what could it become next?

## Teacher note

Misconceptions to listen for: color or hardness as a family test;
"metamorphic means melted" (it means changed *without* melting);
sedimentary = "soft" (some sedimentary rocks are very hard);
fossils prove age in years (they prove environment and relative
order, not a number). Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 02.
