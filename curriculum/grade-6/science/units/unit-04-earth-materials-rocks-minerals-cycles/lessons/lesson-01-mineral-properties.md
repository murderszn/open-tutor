# Lesson 01 — Minerals Are Identified by Properties, Not by Looks

**Student goal:** I can describe a mineral sample with testable
properties and explain why color alone is not enough to identify one.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S1 (plus S2 test-station work)
**Materials:** the test-station illustration below; the four practice
sample cards in the [unit README](../README.md); a notebook; eye
protection and real samples only with an adult present.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Pick up (or picture) any rock you know.
Write one sentence describing it. Now cross out every word that is only
about how it looks. What is left? Usually nothing — and that is the
problem this lesson fixes.

## Teaching

A **mineral** is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite
chemical composition and an orderly crystal structure. Five tests on that
definition matter for this lesson:

- **Naturally occurring** — it formed in nature, not in a factory.
- **Inorganic** — it was never alive (so coal, which comes from plants,
  is not a mineral, and neither is a seashell).
- **Solid** — not a liquid or a gas.
- **Definite chemical composition** — the same mineral always has the same
  recipe of elements (quartz is always silicon dioxide, SiO₂).
- **Orderly crystal structure** — its atoms line up in a repeating
  pattern. That pattern is why some minerals grow flat faces and break
  along flat planes.

A **rock** is different: a solid made of one or more minerals (and
sometimes other materials, such as volcanic glass). Granite is a rock
because you can see several different minerals speckled through it.
One mineral is an ingredient; a rock is the dish.

Geologists do not identify minerals by admiring them. They test
**properties** — features you can observe or measure the same way every
time. The five this unit uses:

1. **Color** — the color you see. Useful, but the weakest test in the
   set, for a reason you will see below.
2. **Streak** — the color of the mineral's powder. Rub the sample on an
   unglazed porcelain **streak plate** (the white tile in the picture
   below) and look at the powder line. Streak often disagrees with color,
   and streak is usually the more reliable of the two. The test only
   works for minerals softer than the plate — do not force a sample that
   will not leave a mark.
3. **Hardness** — how resistant the mineral is to being scratched.
   Hardness is **not** how hard the mineral is to break: a diamond is the
   hardest mineral on Earth and it can still shatter if you hit it. The
   **Mohs scale** ranks scratch hardness from 1 (talc, the softest) to
   10 (diamond, the hardest): talc 1, gypsum 2, calcite 3, fluorite 4,
   apatite 5, feldspar 6, quartz 7, topaz 8, corundum 9, diamond 10.
   Classroom references: fingernail ≈ 2.5, copper penny ≈ 3, glass
   plate ≈ 5.5, steel nail ≈ 5.5. A scratch test tells you only which of
   two materials is harder — it never gives an exact hardness number.
4. **Luster** — how the surface reflects light: metallic (shines like
   metal), glassy (shines like glass), dull or earthy (hardly shines at
   all), pearly, silky, and others. Judge luster on a fresh surface, not
   a weathered one.
5. **Cleavage or fracture** — how the mineral breaks. **Cleavage** means
   it splits along flat planes (mica peels into sheets; that is cleavage
   in one direction). **Fracture** means it breaks along irregular
   surfaces (quartz breaks in curved, shell-like pieces; that is
   fracture, not cleavage).

### Worked example 1 — One mineral, many colors

Quartz comes in clear, pink, purple, smoky gray, and milky white forms.
If color identified minerals, those would be five different minerals.
They are not: every one of them is quartz — same composition (SiO₂),
same hardness (7), same glassy luster, same curved fracture. The color
changes because tiny impurities sneak into the crystal. **Reasoning:**
when one property varies inside a single mineral, that property cannot
identify it. Color varies; therefore color alone cannot identify a
mineral. Any claim that starts "it is pink, so it must be…" is already
in trouble.

### Worked example 2 — Streak beats color

Sample B on the practice cards is dark gray — but its streak is
red-brown. A learner who trusted color would file it with the other
dark samples. A learner who ran the streak test would notice the
red-brown powder, which no dark-gray look-alike in the set shares.
**Reasoning:** the streak test turns the mineral into powder, removing
the surface color and weathering that mislead the eye. When color and
streak disagree, trust the streak — and then confirm with a second
property (Sample B is also metallic and fairly hard, 5.5–6.5) before
claiming anything. Two properties agreeing beat one property shouting.

**Activity — Match the tool to the test (10 minutes).** For each tool
in the picture, write which property it tests and one sentence about
how the test works:

1. The hand lens — which property does looking closely help with, and
   what would you look for?
2. The white tile — which test is it for, and what exactly do you
   observe?
3. The penny and the nail — which test are they for, and what does a
   scratch (or no scratch) tell you?
4. The notebook — why is writing down the result part of the test
   itself, not just homework?

**Text-only alternative:** the picture shows, on a light table: five
rough samples (speckled gray; pink and layered; clear crystals; dark
gray-black; mottled gray); one blank white rectangular streak plate; one
round hand lens with a gold rim; one copper penny; one iron nail; one
blank notebook and one pencil. Every question above can be answered
from this list without seeing the picture.

## Guided practice

Work in pairs, out loud, before writing:

1. A sample is pink and glassy. A second sample is also pink and
   glassy, but one is scratched by a penny and the other is not. What
   have you learned, and what do you still not know?
2. Sample C and Sample D are both white with a white streak. Name two
   properties from the cards that separate them, and quote the values.
3. Why is "it is hard to break, so it must be hard" a faulty argument?
   Use the diamond fact from the teaching above.
4. A sample leaves no mark on the streak plate no matter how firmly you
   rub. What does that tell you, and what should you **not** do next?

## Application

Your adult hands you a real sample (or a photo of one) and asks: "What
can you say about it, and how sure are you?" Answer with properties
only — no guessing a name — and rank your evidence from strongest to
weakest, with a reason for the ranking.

## Exit check

Write two sentences: (1) the definition of a mineral in your own words;
(2) why a geologist runs at least two tests instead of trusting color.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** property words are defined in context
  above and in the unit vocabulary list; say each test's name while
  pointing at its tool in the picture.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer the activity orally, or by
  pointing at tools in the picture and naming the test, instead of
  writing.
- **Extension:** research (adult-selected source) one property this
  unit does not test — for example, magnetism or density — and write
  three sentences on what new information it would add and what it
  still could not prove alone.

## Teacher note

Misconceptions to listen for: color = identity (the unit's central
target); hardness = unbreakability; streak plate works on every mineral
(it does not — only softer ones streak); a mineral and a rock are the
same thing. Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md), Lesson 01.
