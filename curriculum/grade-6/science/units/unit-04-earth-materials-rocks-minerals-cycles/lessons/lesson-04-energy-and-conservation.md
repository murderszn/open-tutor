# Lesson 04 — Two Engines: The Sun and Earth's Interior

**Student goal:** I can tag every rock-cycle arrow with the energy
that drives it, explain conservation of matter through the cycle, and
name what the cycle model leaves out.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S10 (plus S11 practice)
**Materials:** the Lesson 03 diagram; Lesson 01's sample cards; paper
for energy tagging.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** A river carries sand to the sea. A
volcano erupts lava. Which one is powered by the Sun, and which by
Earth's interior — and how could you tell without being told? Write
your rule before reading on.

## Teaching

Every arrow on the rock-cycle diagram needs energy. Something has to
break the rock, move the pieces, squeeze them, heat them, or melt them.
That energy comes from exactly two engines, and telling them apart is
the main thinking skill of this lesson.

### Engine S: the Sun, at the surface

The Sun's energy drives the surface processes: **weathering, erosion,
transport, and deposition**. Sunlight evaporates water, powers winds,
drives rain and ice, and keeps water moving downhill. A river does not
carry sand because the river "wants" to — it carries sand because
Sun-driven water is moving. Tag these arrows **S**: weathering,
erosion, deposition. (In Lesson 03's worked example, step 2 — granite
to beach sand — is S.)

### Engine E: Earth's interior heat, below the surface

Earth's hot interior drives the deep processes: **melting,
crystallization, deformation (metamorphism), and uplift**. Magma
exists because the interior is hot; mountains rise because the
interior moves the crust; buried rock is cooked and squeezed because
it sits closer to that heat. Tag these arrows **E**: melting, heat and
pressure, uplift, and (with the note below) cooling and crystallization.

### The honest exceptions

Two arrows resist a clean tag, and a good scientist says so instead of
faking certainty:

- **Cooling and crystallization:** the *magma* exists because of
  interior heat (E), but cooling is heat *leaving* — the rock gives
  energy away to its surroundings. Tag it E-with-a-note: "interior heat
  made the magma; cooling is heat escaping." An answer that tags it E
  without the note is acceptable; an answer that tags it S is wrong
  (sunlight does not cool magma).
- **Compaction and cementation:** driven by the weight of overlying
  sediment (gravity + burial) and by minerals crystallizing from
  groundwater — not directly by either engine. Tag it "neither, directly:
  burial weight and groundwater chemistry." Forcing an S or E tag here
  is the most common error in this lesson, and the answer key accepts
  any response that names burial/gravity/groundwater instead of
  guessing an engine.

### Worked example 1 — Tag the loop

Take Lesson 03's full loop: granite → uplift + weathering/erosion/
deposition → compaction/cementation → heat and pressure → melting →
cooling/crystallization → new igneous rock.

- Uplift + weathering, erosion, deposition: **E, then S** (interior
  moves the crust up; Sun-driven water, wind, and ice do the breaking
  and carrying).
- Compaction and cementation: **neither directly** (burial weight,
  groundwater minerals).
- Heat and pressure: **E** (interior heat + burial pressure).
- Melting: **E** (interior heat).
- Cooling and crystallization: **E-with-a-note** (heat escaping from
  interior-made magma).

Count: one mixed arrow (E then S), one neither, three E arrows (one
with a note). That is a normal loop — the engines do not take turns
evenly, and no law says they must.

### Worked example 2 — Conservation: the sand is still there

A student says: "The granite is gone — it weathered away. So matter
was destroyed." **Step 1:** name what happened: granite broke into
mineral grains (sand). **Step 2:** check the mass: the sand's mass
equals the mass lost by the granite (minus grains carried elsewhere —
and those grains still exist somewhere downstream; moving is not
destroying). **Step 3:** follow the story: sand → sandstone →
metamorphic rock → magma → new igneous rock. At every step the matter
changed form, and at no step did matter appear from nothing or vanish.
**Conclusion:** the rock cycle conserves matter. "Gone" usually means
"moved," "broken smaller," or "changed form" — and in science, those
three are claims you can check, while "destroyed" is a claim that
needs extraordinary evidence.

### What the model leaves out

A model audit names limits, not just features. The rock-cycle diagram
leaves out:

1. **Timescales.** Cooling lava can harden in days; burial into
   sedimentary rock takes thousands to millions of years. The diagram's
   arrows are all the same length, but the real processes are not the
   same speed — not even close.
2. **Locations.** The diagram does not say *where* each process
   happens (a beach, a mountain belt, deep crust, a volcano).
3. **Rates and amounts.** It does not say how much material moves, or
   how often each path runs.
4. **Living things and chemistry details.** Shells building limestone,
   plants breaking rock with roots, the exact minerals in the cement —
   all real, all omitted for clarity.
5. **The starting point problem.** The diagram is a loop; a loop has
   no start. Any "first rock" is a storytelling choice, not a fact.

None of these limits makes the model wrong. A map that left out
elevation would still get you to the next town; you just would not use
it to plan a climb. Use the cycle model for processes, paths, and
energy — never for dates, places, or amounts.

## Guided practice

Work in pairs, out loud, before writing:

1. Tag every arrow in this path: metamorphic rock → melting → magma →
   cooling → igneous rock → weathering → sediment. Which engine
   appears most, and does that surprise you? Why or why not?
2. A classmate tags compaction and cementation as S "because the Sun
   made the river that carried the sand." What is right about that
   reasoning, and where does it overreach?
3. A beach loses sand every winter and gains it every summer. Has
   matter been created or destroyed? Use the conservation rule from
   Worked example 2.
4. Name one question the cycle diagram *cannot* answer, and one
   question it answers well. Explain the difference in one sentence
   each.

## Application

Choose a real landscape you know (a riverbank, a cliff, a gravel
driveway, a mountain photo). Write five sentences: which rock-cycle
process is visible or implied there, which engine drives it, what the
matter was before, what it could become next, and one thing the
diagram leaves out about this place.

## Exit check

Tag three arrows (weathering, melting, compaction/cementation) with
S, E, or "neither directly," and write one sentence defending each
tag. Then write one sentence stating what conservation of matter
means for a rock that "disappeared."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** say "S = Sun at the surface, E =
  Earth's heat below" as a chant while tagging; color S arrows yellow
  and E arrows red if you print the diagram.
- **Alternate response mode:** tag arrows by placing S, E, and
  "neither" tokens on the diagram and explaining each placement
  orally.
- **Extension:** the water cycle (Unit 06 preview) is also Sun-driven
  at the surface. Write three sentences comparing the Sun's role in
  the rock cycle and in the water cycle — and one sentence on how
  they interact (water is the main agent of erosion).

## Teacher note

Misconceptions to listen for: every arrow must alternate engines;
compaction = Sun-driven (see the honest exceptions); "gone" =
destroyed; the diagram shows real times (all arrows look equal);
cooling is Sun-driven. Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 04.
