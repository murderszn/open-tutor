# Lesson 01 — Day and Night: Earth's Rotation

**Student goal:** I can explain day and night using Earth's rotation, and I can
read a shadow to tell where the Sun is.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S1 (plus S2–S4 practice/observation)
**Materials:** a straight stick or pencil; a sunny outdoor spot (or a
flashlight, a ball, and a darkened room as the backup); ruler; data table;
the generated image `assets/shadow-stick-three-times.png`.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** The Sun looks like it moves across the sky
every day — it rises in the east and sets in the west. Is the Sun actually
moving around Earth, or could something else explain what we see? Write one
sentence, then keep it — you will check it at the end of this lesson.

## Teaching

The Sun looks like it travels across the sky, but that motion is an
illusion. What really happens is that **Earth spins** — this spinning is
called **rotation**. Earth rotates from west to east, turning all the way
around once about every **24 hours** (one day). As your part of Earth turns
*toward* the Sun, the Sun appears to rise and your day begins; as your part
turns *away*, the Sun appears to set and night begins. Day and night are not
the Sun switching on and off — they are your side of a spinning Earth facing
toward or away from the Sun.

Two words that are easy to mix up:

- **Rotation** = spinning in place, like a basketball spinning on a finger.
  Earth rotates once per day → day and night.
- **Revolution** = traveling around something else, like a runner circling a
  track. Earth revolves around the Sun once per year → the year (and, with
  tilt, the seasons — Lesson 04).

Shadows are the evidence you can collect yourself. A shadow always points
**away from the light source**, and it is **longest when the light is lowest**.
At sunrise the Sun is low in the east, so shadows are long and point west. Near
midday the Sun is high, so shadows are short. At sunset the Sun is low in the
west, so shadows are long and point east. If you can read a shadow, you can
read the Sun's position — and the Sun's position tells you the time of day.

### Worked example 1 — The shadow detective

Look at the three-panel image below. The same stick is drawn at three times of
day. Your job: match each panel to **morning**, **near midday**, or
**evening**, and explain *why*.

![Three panels showing the same vertical wooden stick in grass. Panel A: the Sun sits low near the letter E at the left horizon, and a long dark shadow stretches to the right. Panel B: the Sun is high overhead and the shadow is short, hugging the stick's base. Panel C: the Sun sits low near the letter W at the right horizon, and a long dark shadow stretches to the left.](../assets/shadow-stick-three-times.png)

*Caption: Three views of the same shadow stick. This is an AI-generated
illustration made for this activity — a model to reason about, not a
photograph of a real observation.*

*Text-only alternative: Panel A — Sun low in the east (E), long shadow
pointing west. Panel B — Sun high overhead, very short shadow. Panel C — Sun
low in the west (W), long shadow pointing east.*

Reasoning it through, step by step:

1. **Panel A:** The Sun is low and labeled E (east). Light comes from the
   east, so the shadow must point the opposite way — west (right). The Sun
   is near the horizon, so the shadow is long. Low Sun in the east with long
   shadows = **morning**.
2. **Panel B:** The Sun is nearly overhead, so the shadow is very short and
   hugs the stick. The highest, shortest-shadow moment of the day = **near
   midday**.
3. **Panel C:** The Sun is low and labeled W (west). Shadow points east (left)
   and is long. Low Sun in the west with long shadows = **evening**.

Check: the panels go long → short → long, and the Sun goes low-east → high →
low-west. That is exactly the pattern Earth's rotation makes. Notice the
reasoning never says "the Sun moved" — it says where the Sun *appears* and
what the shadow proves.

### Worked example 2 — The flashlight Earth

An adult holds a flashlight (the Sun) steady while you slowly turn a ball (Earth)
west to east, with a small sticker "X" marking your town. Watch what happens
at X:

1. Start with X facing the flashlight: X is in daylight.
2. Turn the ball slowly. X rotates toward the dark side — at X, the light
   fades: **sunset at X**.
3. Keep turning. X is fully dark: **night at X**.
4. Keep turning. X rotates back toward the light: **sunrise at X**.

Now the key question: did the flashlight move? No — your hand never moved it.
The *appearances* of sunrise and sunset came entirely from the ball's
**rotation**. This is the same reason the real Sun appears to move: Earth does
the turning, not the Sun.

A common error: "The Sun goes around Earth each day." The flashlight test
disproves it — day and night happen with the light standing still. (The real
Sun *does* move through space, but that has nothing to do with day and
night.)

## Guided practice

1. With an adult, set up the flashlight-and-ball model. Point to the spot
   where it is sunrise, noon, sunset, and midnight on the ball, and say each
   one aloud as you turn it. Repeat until you can do it without stopping.
2. Draw the ball at four rotation positions and label each drawing with the
   time of day at X and one sentence explaining *why*.
3. Predict: if Earth rotated twice as fast (a 12-hour day), what would happen
   to the length of daytime? Explain using the model, not a guess.
4. Error analysis: a friend says, "Shadows point toward the Sun." Use Panel A
   of the image to show why that is wrong — what would the shadow look like
   if your friend were right?

## Independent practice

1. Define **rotation** and **revolution** in your own words, and give the
   real example for each (what rotates, what revolves, how long each takes).
2. A stick's shadow points east and is very long. Where is the Sun (direction
   and height), and what time of day is it most likely? Explain both parts.
3. A stick's shadow is the shortest it will be all day. What does that tell
   you about the Sun's position? About the time?
4. The Sun appears to rise in the east. Explain why we say "appears to" —
   what is actually moving?
5. Two towns are on opposite sides of Earth. It is noon in one town. What is
   it in the other town, and why?
6. Explain-your-reasoning: could there be a place on Earth where the Sun is
   directly overhead every single day at noon? Use the rotation model to argue
   yes or no. (Hint: think about what the ball's tilt — coming in Lesson 04 —
   does to the Sun's height. For now, reason from the model you have.)

## Applied task (S2 observation)

With an adult, push the stick upright into the ground in a sunny spot in the
morning. Every hour (or as often as your adult allows), measure the shadow's
length in centimeters and note its direction (N, S, E, W, or between). Record
in a labeled table with units — columns: **Time**, **Shadow length (cm)**,
**Shadow direction**. Take at least 4 readings. Then make a **line graph**
(shadow length vs. time) with labeled axes and units, reusing your Unit 01
graphing skill. Write two sentences: what pattern does the graph show, and
what does that pattern say about the Sun's apparent motion? If the day is
overcast, use the three-panel image instead: measure each drawn shadow with a
ruler in centimeters, label the numbers *sample data from the illustration*
(not your measurement), and answer the same two questions.

## Exit check

In two sentences, explain why the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in
the west. Use the word **rotation** correctly.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the two worked examples can be read aloud by the adult;
  the learner answers orally and the adult scribes key phrases.
- **Alternate response mode:** for the exit check, the learner may point to
  Panels A/B/C and narrate the Sun's apparent path instead of writing.
- **Extension:** research what a **sundial** is and explain in three sentences
  why a sundial works — connect every claim to this lesson's shadow rules.

## Misconception note (for the adult)

Watch for "the Sun moves around Earth." The flashlight model directly tests
this: the light never moves, yet sunrise and sunset still happen. Also watch
for "shadows point toward the Sun" — Panels A and C correct it visually.
Keep the language "the Sun *appears* to rise" all unit; it pays off in
Lessons 02–04.
