# Lesson 05 — Review: What the Models Show and What They Hide

**Student goal:** I can audit every model in this unit — what it includes,
what it leaves out — and fix a claim that overreaches.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S13 (with the formative quiz)
**Materials:** the three models (shadow stick / flashlight-ball, lamp-and-two-
balls, tilted globe); a "model audit" table.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Write the Unit 01 rule about models in one
sentence from memory: a model ______, and you must always name ______.

## Teaching

This unit ran on models: a stick's shadow stood in for Earth's rotation, a
lamp and balls stood in for the Sun–Earth–Moon system, a tilted globe stood
in for the seasons. Every one of them taught something true — and every one
lies by omission. A scientist's job is to know exactly where the truth ends.
That is the **model audit**: for each model, name what it **includes** (what
it shows faithfully) and what it **leaves out** (what it distorts or omits),
then check whether your claims stayed inside the faithful part.

Audit the unit's three models:

**1. Shadow stick / flashlight-and-ball (Lesson 01).**
Includes: the direction light comes from; that shadows point away from light;
that a spinning body makes day/night without the light moving. Leaves out:
the real sizes and distances; the fact that Earth also revolves while it
rotates; clouds, atmosphere, and everything else that affects real daylight.

**2. Lamp-and-two-balls (Lessons 02–03).**
Includes: the half-lit geometry that makes phases; the alignments that make
eclipses; the tilt that usually prevents them. Leaves out: true scale — the
real Moon is about 30 Earth-diameters away and the Sun is enormously farther
(your lamp is far too close, which is why classroom "eclipses" happen too
easily); the Moon's real orbital tilt in 3-D; the 29.5-day timing.

**3. Tilted globe and lamp (Lesson 04).**
Includes: the fixed-direction 23.5° tilt; which hemisphere leans sunward;
direct vs. spread light. Leaves out: the true Earth–Sun distance (if the
globe were to scale, the lamp would be in another room — about 11,700
globe-diameters away); the slight orbital ellipse; day-to-day weather.

The pattern: **desk models get the geometry right and the scale wrong.**
That is fine — as long as you never use them to claim something about size or
distance. "The tilt causes seasons" is inside the model's faithful zone;
"the Sun is about ten times wider than Earth" is not — no model here shows
that.

### Worked example 1 — Auditing a claim

Claim: "My lamp-and-ball model proves the Moon is much smaller than the Sun."

Audit:

1. What does the model include? Relative positions and lighting geometry.
2. What does it leave out? True sizes and distances — the balls and lamp were
   chosen for visibility, not scale.
3. Is the claim about geometry or about size? **Size** — the unfaithful zone.
4. Verdict: the model cannot prove this. (The real size ratio comes from
   measurement, not from this model — and it arrives in Unit 03.)

### Worked example 2 — Fixing an overreach

Claim: "Because my tilted globe showed summer in June, summers are always
hot everywhere in June."

1. The model includes the tilt geometry for the **northern** hemisphere's
   June.
2. It leaves out the southern hemisphere's experience in the same setup — but
   the model *can* show it: look at the other half of the globe.
3. Fixed claim: "The model shows June is summer in the hemisphere leaning
   toward the Sun — and winter in the other one." The fix came from using
   *more* of the model, not less.

## Guided practice

1. Fill a model-audit table with three rows (one per model) and two columns:
   **Includes** / **Leaves out**. Your adult checks that "not to scale"
   appears for all three, with the specific distortion named.
2. Each of these claims: mark **safe** (inside the model) or **overreach**,
   and fix the overreaches:
   a. "Phases repeat about every 29.5 days." (from the lamp-and-ball model)
   b. "The Moon is about one-quarter the width of Earth." (from the
      lamp-and-ball model)
   c. "Shadows are shortest when the Sun is highest." (from the shadow stick)
   d. "The Sun is 150 million km away." (from the tilted globe)
3. Revisit your Lesson 01 readiness-check sentence. Was your first guess
   right? Write what changed your mind, citing the model that did it.

## Independent practice

1. State the model-audit rule in your own words.
2. Pick the eclipse model: name two things it includes and two things it
   leaves out. Be specific (not just "scale" — *what* about scale?).
3. A classmate says, "The seasons diagram proves Earth is closest to the Sun
   in July." Audit the diagram: does it include distance information at all?
   What is the correct response, with the real numbers?
4. Explain-your-reasoning: why do scientists bother with models that are
   "wrong about scale"? What do we gain that a to-scale model could not give
   us? (Hint: Lesson 04, practice 6.)

## Exit check

"Desk models get the ______ right and the ______ wrong. Before I believe a
claim from a model, I check ______." Complete it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the audits aloud while the learner
  points to the part of each physical model being discussed.
- **Alternate response mode:** the audit table may be dictated; the learner
  sorts pre-written claim cards into "safe" / "overreach" piles.
- **Extension:** choose one model and *improve* it: change one thing to make
  it more faithful (e.g., move the lamp much farther away; add the 5° tilt
  with a wedge). Write what your change fixed and what it still leaves out.
