# Lesson 02 — Lunar Phases: A Pattern, Not a Changing Moon

**Student goal:** I can use a model to predict the Moon's phase from its
position, and I can explain why Earth's shadow is not the cause.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S5 (plus S6–S8 lab and practice)
**Materials:** a lamp with a bare-ish bulb (adult handles it — bulbs get
hot; an LED lamp is safest), a white ball (the Moon) on a stick, a larger ball
or globe (Earth); the orbit diagram `assets/moon-phase-orbit-diagram.svg`.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Draw the Moon as it looked the last time you
noticed it. Was the bright part a full circle, half, or a sliver? Write one
sentence guessing *why* it looked that way.

## Teaching

The Moon does not change shape. That is the whole secret of this lesson. The
Moon is a ball of rock, and the Sun always lights **exactly half of it** — the
half facing the Sun. What changes is **how much of that lit half we can see
from Earth** as the Moon orbits us. The Moon takes about **29.5 days** to go
around Earth once (new moon to new moon), and during that trip we see a
repeating pattern of eight **phases**.

The rule is simple geometry: **sunlight comes from one direction, the Moon is
always half-lit, and your viewpoint moves around the orbit.** When the Moon is
roughly between Earth and the Sun, the lit half faces *away* from us — we see
almost nothing: **new moon**. When Earth is roughly between the Sun and the
Moon, the lit half faces *toward* us — we see the whole disk: **full moon**.
In between, we see fractions: **crescents** (less than half), **quarters**
(half), and **gibbous** (more than half). **Waxing** means the lit part is
growing toward full; **waning** means it is shrinking toward new.

The most stubborn wrong idea in astronomy is that **Earth's shadow** causes
the phases — that Earth blocks the sunlight. The model disproves it in one
move: in the lamp-and-ball setup there is no Earth-shadow involved at all
(the ball orbits *your head*, which stands in for Earth, and your head casts
no shadow on it), yet every phase still appears. Phases come from **viewing
geometry**, not shadows. Shadows only enter the story in Lesson 03 (eclipses).

Also note: the word **quarter** does not mean "one-quarter lit." At first and
last quarter, **half** the visible disk is lit. "Quarter" means the Moon is
one-quarter (or three-quarters) of the way through its monthly cycle.

### Worked example 1 — Reading the orbit diagram

Use `assets/moon-phase-orbit-diagram.svg` (sunlight from the left, top-down
view, the Moon moving counterclockwise as seen from the north). Position 3:
the Moon is at the bottom of its orbit, one-quarter of the way through the
cycle.

Step-by-step:

1. Sunlight comes from the left, so the Moon's **sunward half** (the half
   facing the Sun) is lit — always exactly half the ball.
2. From Earth (center), we look at the Moon's disk. The half of the disk
   that appears lit is the sunward half.
3. Position 3 is one-quarter of the way through the 29.5-day cycle, so the
   name is **first quarter**.
4. Is the lit part growing or shrinking? The Moon is moving toward full
   (position 5), so it is **waxing**.

Position 7 (top of the orbit): the sunward half is still the lit half; the
Moon is three-quarters through the cycle → **last quarter** (waning, heading
back toward new).

Sky check (Northern Hemisphere): at first quarter the lit side appears on the
**right**; at last quarter it appears on the **left**. If your model predicts
this, your geometry agrees with the real sky.

### Worked example 2 — "Quarter" means position, not 25%

Claim: "First quarter means one-quarter of the Moon is lit."
Test it with the model:

1. At first quarter (position 3), the Sun lights the Moon's left half —
   exactly **half** the ball.
2. From Earth we see the full disk, and exactly **half of that disk** is the
   lit half facing us.
3. So "first quarter" shows **50%** lit, not 25%. The name counts the Moon's
   **position in the cycle** (one quarter of 29.5 days ≈ 7.4 days in), not the
   fraction lit.

The same logic fixes "last quarter." And it explains why the names
*waxing/waning gibbous* exist: they describe the in-between states where more
than half is lit but the Moon is not yet full (waxing) or past full (waning).

## Guided practice

1. Operate the lamp-and-ball model: stand the "Earth" ball on a table, hold
   the "Moon" ball at arm's length, and walk it slowly around (adult watches
   the lamp cord). Stop at 8 evenly spaced positions. Before looking at the
   lit side, **predict** the phase name, then check. Record hits and misses in
   a table: columns **Position**, **Prediction**, **What I saw**.
2. Using the orbit diagram: for positions 2, 4, 6, and 8, state (a) the phase
   name, (b) whether it is waxing or waning, and (c) roughly what fraction of
   the disk looks lit.
3. A friend says, "The Moon is a crescent tonight because Earth's shadow is
   covering most of it." Give two reasons from this lesson why that is wrong.
   (Hint 1: think about when you saw a crescent in the model — was anything
   blocking the light? Hint 2: Earth's shadow *does* sometimes cover the Moon
   — what is that event called, and how often does it happen?)

## Independent practice

1. In your own words: what is a lunar phase? What changes, and what stays the
   same?
2. New moon tonight. About how many days until the next full moon? Show the
   arithmetic (use the 29.5-day cycle).
3. Draw the Sun–Earth–Moon arrangement for a **full moon** (top-down, label
   all three). Explain in one sentence why we see the whole lit half.
4. Draw the arrangement for a **new moon**. Explain why we see almost nothing.
5. Explain-your-reasoning: why can't Earth's shadow be the cause of the
   regular monthly phases? Give the strongest single piece of evidence from
   the lamp-and-ball model.
6. **Waning gibbous** — break the name apart: what does each word tell you
   about (a) how much of the disk is lit and (b) where the Moon is in its
   cycle?

## Applied task

Keep a **three-night Moon journal** (adult helps with timing; cloudy nights
are fine — record "clouded out" and what you *predicted*). Each night, sketch
the Moon's shape, note the time, and write which phase you think it is and
whether it is waxing or waning. After three nights, answer: did the lit part
grow or shrink? Does that match your waxing/waning call? Label the sketches
*your observations*.

## Exit check

"The Moon looks different each night because ______. The one thing that does
NOT change is ______." Fill in both blanks with a complete, correct idea.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the worked examples aloud while the
  learner tracks positions on the diagram with a finger.
- **Alternate response mode:** predictions in guided practice may be shown by
  shading a blank circle instead of naming the phase.
- **Extension:** the Moon always shows us nearly the same face (it is
  *tidally locked* — it rotates once per orbit). Use the model to show why:
  mark a dot on the "Moon" ball, walk it around once while keeping the dot
  facing Earth, and count how many times the ball spun.

## Misconception note (for the adult)

The shadow misconception is the big one — research shows it survives years of
schooling. Do not just state the right answer; make the learner *use* the
model to produce a crescent with no shadow involved, then ask what that
proves. Keep "phases = geometry, eclipses = shadows" as a chant all unit.
