# Lesson 03 — Eclipses: When Shadows Line Up

**Student goal:** I can model solar and lunar eclipses, and I can explain why
they don't happen every month.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S9 (plus S11–S12 practice/project)
**Materials:** the lamp-and-ball model from Lesson 02; a pinhole-viewer build
(two sheets of card, a pin, tape — adult supervises the pin); never look
directly at the Sun.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Lesson 02 said phases are *not* caused by
shadows. But sometimes the Moon really does go dark in Earth's shadow. What
must be different about the lineup on those nights? Sketch and guess.

## Teaching

**Safety first, and it is not negotiable:** never look directly at the Sun —
not during an eclipse, not through sunglasses, not through a telescope. The
safe way to watch a solar eclipse is indirectly: a **pinhole viewer** projects
the Sun's image onto paper so your eyes never face the light. Your adult
builds the viewer with you and checks it before use.

Now the geometry. An **eclipse** is the one case where shadows really do run
the show:

- A **solar eclipse** happens when the **Moon passes between the Sun and
  Earth** and the Moon's shadow falls on part of Earth. People inside that
  shadow see the Sun covered. (It happens at **new moon** — but not every new
  moon, as you'll see.)
- A **lunar eclipse** happens when **Earth passes between the Sun and the
  Moon** and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. The Moon darkens and often
  turns a dim red. (It happens at **full moon** — but not every full moon.)

Why not every month? Because the Moon's orbit is **tilted about 5°** relative
to Earth's orbit around the Sun. Most months, at new moon the Moon passes a
little *above or below* the Sun–Earth line, so its shadow misses Earth; at
full moon the Moon passes a little above or below Earth's shadow, so nothing
happens. Eclipses only happen when the lineup is nearly perfect — the Moon
near one of the two points where its tilted orbit crosses Earth's orbital
plane. That is also why a **total solar eclipse is rare at any one place**:
the Moon's dark inner shadow (the **umbra**) is narrow, so totality touches
only a thin path on Earth's surface.

Two shadow words worth knowing: the **umbra** is the darkest, central part of
a shadow (total eclipse inside it); the **penumbra** is the lighter outer part
(partial eclipse inside it).

### Worked example 1 — Which eclipse is this?

Top-down diagram, sunlight from the left. The Moon sits exactly between the
Sun and Earth, all three in a straight line.

Step-by-step:

1. Which body is in the middle? The **Moon**.
2. Whose shadow falls on whom? The Moon blocks sunlight; its shadow falls on
   **Earth**.
3. So people under that shadow see the Sun covered → **solar eclipse**.
4. What phase is the Moon in this lineup? The Moon is between Sun and Earth —
   the same lineup as **new moon**. Check: solar eclipses only happen at new
   moon. Consistent.

Now flip it: Earth in the middle, Moon on the far side. Earth's shadow falls
on the Moon → **lunar eclipse**, and the lineup matches **full moon**. If your
answer ever says "solar eclipse at full moon," the model is telling you to
recheck — that lineup is geometrically impossible.

### Worked example 2 — Why no eclipse this month?

It is a new moon tonight, but no solar eclipse is happening. Explain why,
using the tilt.

1. For a solar eclipse, the Moon must be *exactly* on the Sun–Earth line so
   its shadow hits Earth.
2. The Moon's orbit is tilted ~5°. "New moon" only means the Moon is on the
   Sun's side of Earth — it can be up to 5° **above or below** the line.
3. Tonight it is above (or below) the line, so the shadow passes over (or
   under) Earth. No eclipse.
4. The model test: tilt the "Moon" ball's orbit slightly in the lamp setup and
   watch the shadow miss Earth most laps, hitting only when the crossing
   lines up. That is exactly what happens in space.

Common error: "Eclipses happen every month because the phases repeat monthly."
The phases repeat because *viewing geometry* repeats; eclipses need *shadow
alignment*, which the tilt usually spoils.

## Guided practice

1. With the lamp-and-ball model, demonstrate a solar eclipse, then a lunar
   eclipse. For each, state: which body is in the middle, whose shadow falls
   on whom, and which phase the Moon is in. Have your adult check all three.
2. Tilt the Moon's orbit ~5° (a book under one side of the orbit path works).
   Run six "months" and count how many produce an eclipse. Record: *sample
   model result, not a real prediction*.
3. A friend says, "A lunar eclipse is just a really dark new moon." Explain
   the two errors in that sentence (wrong phase, wrong mechanism).

## Independent practice

1. Define **solar eclipse** and **lunar eclipse** using the words *shadow*,
   *between*, and the three body names.
2. At which phase does each eclipse type occur? Explain *why* the phase is
   forced by the geometry (not a coincidence to memorize).
3. Explain why there is no solar eclipse at every new moon. Use the number
   **5°** in your answer.
4. Why is a *total* solar eclipse rare for any one town, even though solar
   eclipses happen a few times a year somewhere on Earth? (Hint: think about
   the umbra's size.)
5. During a lunar eclipse the Moon often looks dim red instead of black.
   Propose a reason: what could still reach the Moon when direct sunlight is
   blocked? (Acceptable: sunlight bent through Earth's atmosphere — the same
   air that makes sunsets red. This is enrichment; the key point is that *some*
   light still arrives.)
6. Explain-your-reasoning: could a solar eclipse ever happen at full moon?
   Build the lineup and show why or why not.

## Applied task — Build a pinhole viewer (adult-supervised)

With your adult: poke a small clean hole with a pin through the center of one
card. On a sunny day, stand with your **back to the Sun**, hold the holed card
above your shoulder, and let sunlight pass through the hole onto the second
card held below it. You will see a small projected disk — the Sun's image,
viewed safely. Sketch what you see and label it *your observation*. Write one
sentence explaining why this is safe (your eyes never face the Sun) and one
sentence naming what the viewer **leaves out** (it shows shape only — not the
eclipse geometry of Lesson 02's models).

## Exit check

"A solar eclipse needs the Moon ______ the Sun and Earth, which only happens
at ______ moon — but not every one, because ______." Complete the sentence
correctly.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads worked examples aloud; the learner
  manipulates the model balls to match each step.
- **Alternate response mode:** the exit check may be answered by arranging the
  three balls and narrating, instead of writing.
- **Extension:** find out when the next solar or lunar eclipse visible from
  your region occurs (adult searches with you; record the source and date
  checked). Classify it: total, partial, or annular — and say what the Moon's
  shadow is doing in each case.

## Misconception note (for the adult)

Learners often merge Lessons 02 and 03: "phases are mini-eclipses." Keep the
units' chant visible: **phases = geometry, eclipses = shadows**. The tilt
demonstration (guided practice 2) is the single most convincing piece — let
the learner *see* the shadow miss.
