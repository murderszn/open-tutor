# Lesson 04 — Seasons by Tilt, Not Distance

**Student goal:** I can explain seasons with axial tilt, and I can use
evidence to reject the "closer to the Sun" explanation.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Sessions:** S10 (plus S11 evidence task)
**Materials:** a globe or ball on a stick (axis), a lamp (adult handles it);
the diagram `assets/seasons-tilt-diagram.svg`.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** When it is summer where you live, what season
is it on the opposite side of the planet? If seasons came from Earth's
distance to the Sun, could both hemispheres have opposite seasons? Write your
thinking — this lesson tests it.

## Teaching

Here is the fact that breaks the most common wrong idea in this unit: **Earth
is closest to the Sun in early January** — during Northern Hemisphere winter.
The closest point, **perihelion**, is about **147.1 million km**; the farthest
point, **aphelion**, is about **152.1 million km** in early July — during
Northern Hemisphere summer. (Corroborated 2026-10-19: NOAA/NESDIS lists 91.4
million miles / 94.5 million miles for the same two points.) If distance caused
seasons, January would be the hottest month and the whole planet would share
one season at a time. Neither is true — so distance is out. Seasons come from
**tilt**.

Earth's **axis** is tilted **23.5°** from straight-up relative to its orbit,
and — this is the crucial part — the axis **keeps pointing the same
direction in space** all year long, like a spinning top that leans without
wobbling around. As Earth **revolves** around the Sun, first one hemisphere
leans *toward* the Sun, then half a year later the other one does. Look at
`assets/seasons-tilt-diagram.svg`: the two axes are parallel — the tilt never
changes direction.

Why does leaning toward the Sun make summer? Two reasons, both about
sunlight:

1. **More direct light.** When your hemisphere leans toward the Sun, sunlight
   hits it more straight-on, concentrating the Sun's energy on a smaller
   area. Lean away, and the same sunlight spreads over a larger area —
   weaker, like a flashlight held at a slant.
2. **Longer days.** The tilted-toward hemisphere keeps the Sun above the
   horizon longer each day — more hours of heating. (This is the same
   Sun-path idea from Lesson 01's shadow work, stretched across the year.)

The year's markers: the **June solstice** (~June 21) is the Northern
Hemisphere's longest day; the **December solstice** (~December 21) is its
shortest; the two **equinoxes** (~March 20, ~September 22) split the
difference, with day and night about equal everywhere. And because the tilt
is what matters, the hemispheres always take turns: **when it is summer in
the northern hemisphere, it is winter in the southern hemisphere**, and vice
versa.

### Worked example 1 — Reading the tilt diagram

Use `assets/seasons-tilt-diagram.svg`. Question: in June, why is it summer in
the northern hemisphere and winter in the southern?

1. Find the June Earth (right side). Its axis top leans **left, toward the
   Sun**.
2. The northern hemisphere is on the Sun's side of the tilt → sunlight hits
   it more directly, and that hemisphere gets longer days → **summer**.
3. The southern hemisphere leans **away** → spread-out light, shorter days
   → **winter**.
4. Check the December Earth: the axis points the *same way in space* (the
   two axis lines are parallel), but Earth is on the other side of the Sun —
   so now the northern hemisphere leans away → **winter** there, **summer**
   in the south. The diagram's own labels confirm it.

### Worked example 2 — Testing the distance claim

Claim: "Summer happens because Earth is closer to the Sun."

Test it like a scientist — derive the prediction, then check reality:

1. **Prediction from the claim:** the hottest months should fall near
   **perihelion** (closest approach), and both hemispheres should share the
   same season.
2. **Reality:** perihelion happens in **early January** — the Northern
   Hemisphere's coldest month. And the hemispheres have **opposite** seasons.
3. **Verdict:** the claim predicts the exact opposite of what happens. It is
   rejected. (The ~5 million km difference exists, but it warms the whole
   planet slightly at once — it cannot make hemispheres take turns.)

This is the Unit 01 habit applied to an idea: state what a claim predicts,
compare with evidence, keep or reject.

## Guided practice

1. With the tilted globe and lamp: set "June" (northern hemisphere leaning
   toward the lamp). Point to where light hits most directly. Now move the
   globe halfway around *without changing the axis direction* — where does
   the light hit most directly now? Narrate both.
2. A town in the southern hemisphere: which solstice gives it the longest
   day — June or December? Justify with the diagram.
3. On the equinoxes, neither hemisphere leans strongly toward the Sun. What
   does that predict about day length at the equator vs. the poles? (Accept:
   about 12 hours of daylight everywhere — the tilt advantage is switched
   off.)
4. Error analysis: "Earth's orbit is an oval, so sometimes we're much closer
   to the Sun — that's summer." Name the two facts that defeat this
   (perihelion timing; opposite hemispheres).

## Independent practice

1. In your own words: what causes the seasons? Use **tilt**, **revolution**,
   and **hemisphere** correctly.
2. Explain the two reasons a hemisphere leaning toward the Sun gets summer
   (direct light + longer days) — one sentence each.
3. It is July. What season is it in Australia, and why? (Australia is in the
   southern hemisphere.)
4. The perihelion/aphelion evidence: state the two numbers (with units) and
   explain in two sentences why they kill the distance explanation.
5. Suppose Earth's axis had **no tilt** (0°). Describe what would happen to
   the seasons, and say what would *still* vary through the year (the small
   perihelion/aphelion difference — the whole planet slightly warmer in
   January, slightly cooler in July).
6. Explain-your-reasoning: the diagram says "NOT TO SCALE." Why would a
   to-scale drawing of the Sun–Earth system be useless in a textbook? (Hint:
   the Sun is about 109 times wider than Earth, and 11,700 Earth-diameters
   away. What would Earth look like as a dot on this page?)

## Applied task — The seasons evidence card (S11)

Make a two-sided evidence card. **Side 1:** the tilt explanation in your own
words with a labeled sketch (axis, Sun, one hemisphere leaning in). **Side 2:**
the distance explanation's prediction vs. reality in a two-column table
(Predicted by "closer = summer" | What actually happens), ending with a
one-sentence verdict. Label the perihelion/aphelion numbers as *reference
values*. Your adult checks: does Side 2 actually contradict Side 1's rival,
or just restate it?

## Exit check

"Earth is closest to the Sun in ______, which is winter in the ______
hemisphere. Therefore seasons are caused by ______, not distance." Fill in
the blanks.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the worked examples while the learner
  traces the diagram's axis lines with a finger to verify they stay parallel.
- **Alternate response mode:** the exit check may be answered by posing the
  globe in "January" and "July" positions and narrating.
- **Extension:** look up your town's sunrise/sunset times for the June and
  December solstices (adult helps; record source and date). Compute the two
  day lengths and connect the difference to this lesson's "longer days"
  mechanism.

## Misconception note (for the adult)

The distance idea is intuitive and usually taught by well-meaning diagrams
that exaggerate the orbit's oval shape. Keep returning to the two killer
facts: **January perihelion** and **opposite hemispheres**. If the learner
says "but the orbit is elliptical," agree — then ask what an ellipse predicts
about the two hemispheres sharing a season.
