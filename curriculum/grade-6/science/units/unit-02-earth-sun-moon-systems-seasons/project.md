# Project — Build It and Defend It: An Earth–Sun–Moon Explainer Model

**Sessions:** launched S7, built S12, showcased S14 · **Adult role:** safety
supervisor, audience, rubric scorer.

## The challenge

Build **one working physical model** of the Sun–Earth–Moon system and use it
to **defend three claims** — one about phases, one about eclipses, one about
seasons — in front of your adult. Then write a **model-limits card** naming
what your model includes and leaves out. A model that cannot be questioned is
a decoration; yours must survive questions.

## Materials (safe, household)

- A lamp with a shade removed or a bright flashlight (**adult handles the
  lamp**; prefer LED — bulbs get hot; never look into the light).
- Two balls of different sizes (e.g., tennis ball = Earth, golf ball/table-
  tennis ball = Moon) on sticks/skewers (**adult handles sharp ends** or use
  clay bases instead).
- A protractor or a printed 23.5° angle guide; string or a drawn orbit circle
  on large paper; tape; labels and a marker.
- Optional: the unit's SVG diagrams printed for reference.

## Steps

1. **Plan (S7, 20 min).** Choose your three claims — one per phenomenon.
   Examples: "The lit part grows after new moon because of viewing geometry,
   not shadow" (phases); "A solar eclipse needs the Moon exactly on the
   Sun–Earth line, which is why it doesn't happen monthly" (eclipses);
   "June is summer here because our hemisphere leans toward the Sun"
   (seasons). Write each as one sentence. Your adult approves the set.
2. **Build (S12, 45 min).** Assemble the model: the lamp is the Sun (fixed),
   the big ball is Earth (spins + orbits), the small ball is the Moon
   (orbits Earth). Add the 23.5° tilt to Earth's axis with a wedge or marked
   stick, and mark the ~5° tilt of the Moon's orbit (a slightly tilted orbit
   ring). Label everything.
3. **Test (S12, 20 min).** Run each claim through the model. For each: pose
   the model, state the claim, point to the evidence in the model, and name
   one thing the model *cannot* show about that claim. Revise weak claims now.
4. **Model-limits card (S12/S14).** A 3×5-style card (paper is fine) with two
   columns — **Includes** / **Leaves out** — at least three honest entries
   per column. "Not to scale" must appear with the *specific* distortion
   (e.g., "the lamp is far too close; real eclipses are rarer").
5. **Showcase (S14).** Demonstrate all three claims live. Your adult asks at
   least two **challenge questions** (examples: "Show me why there's no
   eclipse this month." "What would break if the tilt were 0°?"). Answer with
   the model, not from memory.

## Deliverables

1. The three approved claim sentences (written before building).
2. The physical model (photographed or sketched with labels — no photos of
   the learner in the repo; the sketch stays in the learner's notebook).
3. The model-limits card.
4. A one-paragraph reflection: which claim was hardest to defend, and what
   did the model teach you that the diagrams alone did not?

## Rubric

| Criterion | Strong (3) | Developing (2) | Starting (1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Claims** | All three claims are correct, specific, and each matches its phenomenon (phase / eclipse / season). | Two claims correct, or claims are vague ("the Moon changes"). | Claims incorrect or missing a phenomenon. |
| **Model use** | Each claim is *demonstrated* with the model posed correctly; evidence is pointed to, not just described. | Model posed for most claims; some evidence only described. | Model present but claims recited from memory. |
| **Limits card** | ≥3 honest entries per column; scale distortion named specifically; no overclaims. | Card complete but generic ("not to scale" with no detail). | Card missing or claims the model proves what it cannot. |
| **Challenge questions** | Both answered with the model; reasoning names geometry (positions, tilt, alignment). | One answered well; the other answered from memory. | Unable to use the model to answer. |
| **Reflection** | Names the hardest claim *and* a specific insight the physical model gave beyond diagrams. | Names the hardest claim only. | Missing or generic ("it was fun"). |

**Scoring:** 13–15 = strong; 9–12 = developing (revise one weak criterion and
re-defend); below 9 = rebuild with the adult using the teacher guide's
reteaching moves. The rubric assesses the *defense*, not craft quality — a
messy model that explains wins over a pretty one that can't.
