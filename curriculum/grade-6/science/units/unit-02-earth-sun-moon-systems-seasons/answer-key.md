# Answer Key — Unit 02 (Earth–Sun–Moon systems and seasons)

Every item was solved independently and reconciled with the student wording.
Keep this file separate from learners.

## Lesson 01 — Day and night

**Worked example 1 (expected):** A = morning (Sun low in east, long shadow
pointing west); B = near midday (Sun high, shortest shadow); C = evening (Sun
low in west, long shadow pointing east).
**Worked example 2 (expected):** The flashlight never moves; sunrise/sunset at
X come from the ball's rotation. This disproves "the Sun orbits Earth daily."

**Guided practice:**
1. Accept if the learner points correctly to all four positions twice running
   (sunrise = X rotating into light; noon = X facing light; sunset = X rotating
   out; midnight = X facing away).
2. Four drawings labeled; each sentence must credit rotation, not Sun motion.
3. A 12-hour day halves daytime (and nighttime): each side of Earth spends
   half the spin in light. Accept any correct proportional reasoning.
4. If shadows pointed toward the Sun, Panel A's shadow would stretch *left*
   toward the E-labeled Sun. It stretches right — away. So the friend is wrong.

**Independent practice:**
1. Rotation = spinning on an axis (Earth, ~24 h → day/night). Revolution =
   orbiting another body (Earth around Sun, ~1 year → the year).
2. Sun is low in the west; evening. (Shadow points away from the Sun; long =
   low Sun.)
3. The Sun is near its highest point; it is near midday.
4. "Appears to" because Earth is what moves — it rotates west to east, so the
   Sun only *seems* to travel east to west.
5. Midnight (night): the towns are on opposite sides, so one faces the Sun
   while the other faces away.
6. Accept either argued position if reasoned from the model: the strong answer
   is **no for most places** — with the ball's axis straight up, the Sun's
   height at noon depends on latitude; only near the equator is it near
   overhead, and tilt (Lesson 04) moves that band. Credit honest
   model-based reasoning over the "right" answer.

**Applied task (sample):** Table with ≥4 rows, units in every column; line
graph with labeled axes ("Time of day" / "Shadow length (cm)"); pattern
sentence ("shadow shortened toward midday, then lengthened"); interpretation
("the Sun appeared to rise in the east, climb, and set in the west"). If the
image substitute was used, numbers must be labeled *sample data from the
illustration*.

**Exit check (sample):** "The Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the
west because Earth rotates from west to east, turning us toward the Sun in
the morning and away at night."

## Lesson 02 — Moon phases

**Guided practice:**
1. Table with 8 rows; predictions scored, not graded — the point is
   prediction-before-checking. Adult verifies the *final* names: 1 new, 2
   waxing crescent, 3 first quarter, 4 waxing gibbous, 5 full, 6 waning
   gibbous, 7 last quarter, 8 waning crescent.
2. Position 2: waxing crescent, waxing, small fraction (<50%). Position 4:
   waxing gibbous, waxing, >50%. Position 6: waning gibbous, waning, >50%.
   Position 8: waning crescent, waning, <50%.
3. (a) In the model, crescents appear with nothing blocking the light — the
   lit half simply turns partly away. (b) When Earth's shadow *does* cover
   the Moon it is a lunar eclipse, which happens only occasionally at full
   moon — not monthly.

**Independent practice:**
1. A phase = how much of the Moon's lit half faces Earth. What changes: the
   visible lit fraction. What stays the same: the Moon's shape; exactly half
   of it is always sunlit.
2. Full → new is half a cycle: 29.5 ÷ 2 = 14.75 ≈ 14–15 days.
3. Drawing: Sun left, Earth middle, Moon right (top-down), all labeled.
   Sentence: the Moon's fully lit half faces Earth.
4. Drawing: Sun left, Moon middle, Earth right. Sentence: the lit half faces
   away from Earth, so we see the dark side.
5. Strongest evidence: the lamp-and-ball model produces every phase with no
   Earth-shadow involved at all — if shadows caused phases, removing the
   shadow would remove the phases, and it doesn't.
6. Waning = lit part shrinking toward new. Gibbous = more than half lit.
   So: (a) more than half of the disk looks lit; (b) the Moon is past full,
   heading back toward new (positions 5→1 side of the cycle).

**Applied task:** ≥2 usable nights (clouded-out entries accepted with
predictions); sketches + phase names + waxing/waning call; final comparison
sentence agrees with the call. Sketches labeled *your observations*.

**Exit check (sample):** "The Moon looks different each night because we see
different amounts of its always-lit half as it orbits Earth. The one thing
that does NOT change is that exactly half the Moon is lit by the Sun."

## Lesson 03 — Eclipses

**Guided practice:**
1. Solar: Moon middle; Moon's shadow on Earth; new moon. Lunar: Earth middle;
   Earth's shadow on the Moon; full moon. All three stated per demo.
2. Sample model result, e.g., "1 eclipse in 6 months" — accept any count with
   the tilt named as the cause of the misses; must be labeled *sample model
   result*.
3. Errors: (a) a lunar eclipse happens at *full* moon, not new; (b) the
   mechanism is Earth's shadow on the Moon — a special alignment, not the
   everyday phase geometry.

**Independent practice:**
1. Solar eclipse: the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and the Moon's
   shadow falls on (part of) Earth. Lunar eclipse: Earth passes between the
   Sun and the Moon and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon.
2. Solar → new moon (Moon must be on the Sun's side); lunar → full moon (Moon
   must be opposite the Sun). The phase is forced because the eclipse lineup
   *is* the phase lineup.
3. "New moon" only fixes the Moon's side, not its height: the orbit's ~5°
   tilt usually puts the Moon above/below the Sun–Earth line, so the shadow
   misses Earth.
4. The umbra is narrow — totality only touches a thin path; most places see a
   partial eclipse or none. (A few solar eclipses per year *somewhere* ≠ one
   overhead.)
5. Accept: sunlight bent/filtered through Earth's atmosphere still reaches the
   Moon (the sunset-reddened light). Credit any answer naming refracted
   atmospheric light; do not penalize "I don't know, but direct sunlight is
   blocked" if the blocked-direct-light part is right.
6. No: full moon puts Earth between Sun and Moon, so the Moon cannot be
   between Sun and Earth. The lineup contradicts the requirement.

**Applied task:** Viewer built and checked by the adult; sketch of the
projected disk labeled *your observation*; safety sentence (eyes never face
the Sun); limits sentence (shape only — not the eclipse geometry).

**Exit check (sample):** "A solar eclipse needs the Moon *between* the Sun
and Earth, which only happens at *new* moon — but not every one, because the
Moon's tilted orbit usually carries it above or below the line."

## Lesson 04 — Seasons

**Guided practice:**
1. June pose: most-direct light on the northern hemisphere. Half-orbit later
   (axis direction unchanged): most-direct light on the southern hemisphere.
   Narration must mention the axis *not* changing direction.
2. December solstice (southern hemisphere leans sunward then).
3. About 12 hours of daylight everywhere — with no tilt advantage, day/night
   split evenly.
4. Defeated by: (a) perihelion is in early January (winter, N hemisphere);
   (b) the hemispheres have opposite seasons, which distance cannot produce.

**Independent practice:**
1. Sample: "Earth's axis is tilted 23.5° and keeps pointing the same way as
   Earth revolves around the Sun, so each hemisphere takes turns leaning
   toward the Sun — that leaning sets the seasons."
2. Direct light: sunlight hits more straight-on, concentrating energy on a
   smaller area. Longer days: the Sun stays up more hours, heating longer.
3. Winter — Australia is in the southern hemisphere, which leans away from the
   Sun in July.
4. Perihelion ≈ 147.1 million km (early January); aphelion ≈ 152.1 million km
   (early July). Rebuttal: January is the N hemisphere's coldest month despite
   being closest — and distance would give both hemispheres the same season,
   but they are opposite.
5. Nearly no seasons: the tilt-driven cycle disappears. What still varies: the
   small whole-planet perihelion/aphelion difference (slightly more solar
   energy in January for everyone).
6. To scale, Earth would be an invisible dot: the Sun is ~109× wider and
   ~11,700 Earth-diameters away. A readable diagram must compress scale to
   show the *geometry* (tilt direction), which is what the lesson is about.

**Applied task:** Side 1: tilt explanation + labeled sketch (axis, Sun,
leaning hemisphere). Side 2: two-column prediction-vs-reality table ending in
a verdict sentence; numbers labeled *reference values*. Adult checks the
contradiction is real.

**Exit check:** "Earth is closest to the Sun in *early January*, which is
winter in the *northern* hemisphere. Therefore seasons are caused by *axial
tilt* (and revolution), not distance."

## Lesson 05 — Review

**Guided practice:**
1. Table must list "not to scale" for all three with the specific distortion:
   shadow stick (no revolution/real distances), lamp-and-balls (lamp far too
   close; no 29.5-day timing), tilted globe (lamp should be ~11,700
   globe-diameters away; no orbital ellipse).
2. a. Overreach if justified by the *model* (the model doesn't include
   timing) — but note: the 29.5-day value itself is a correct reference
   value; the error is *sourcing* it to the model. b. Overreach (size not in
   the model). c. Safe. d. Overreach (distance not in the model).
3. Accept any honest before/after citing the model that changed the mind
   (usually the flashlight ball).

**Independent practice:**
1. Sample: "Name what a model includes and leaves out before trusting a claim
   from it; desk models get geometry right and scale wrong."
2. Includes (any two): half-lit geometry of phases; eclipse alignments; the
   tilt that prevents monthly eclipses. Leaves out (two specific): true
   sizes/distances (lamp far too close); the 29.5-day cycle timing.
3. The diagram includes no distance information (labeled NOT TO SCALE).
   Correct response: perihelion is early January (~147.1M km) — Earth is
   *closest* in January, so the classmate has it backwards; and distance
   can't explain opposite hemispheres anyway.
4. Because the geometry (angles, alignments, lean direction) is what explains
   the phenomena, and a to-scale model would make the bodies invisible dots
   — you couldn't *see* the geometry you're trying to learn.

**Exit check:** "Desk models get the *geometry* right and the *scale* wrong.
Before I believe a claim from a model, I check *whether the claim is about
what the model actually includes*."

## Practice bank

**P1.** (a) The Sun is low in the west. (b) Evening — long shadow means low
Sun; shadow points away from the Sun, so the Sun is opposite the shadow.
**P2.** Midnight / night: opposite side of a rotating Earth faces away from
the Sun.
**P3.** Rotation diagram: curved arrows on the ball → day/night. Revolution
diagram: arrow around the Sun → the year. Labels required.
**P4.** Correction: shadows are *shortest* when the Sun is highest (midday),
longest when low. The friend reversed it; summer's shorter noon shadows come
from the Sun's greater height, not the season itself.
**P5.** A sundial's hour lines assume a specific Sun-path angle, which depends
on latitude (tilt geometry). Near the poles the Sun's path is radically
different, so the same markings misread the time. Credit any answer tying
markings to latitude-dependent Sun angles.
**P6.** (a) Sun–Earth–Moon in a line, Earth middle, all labeled. (b) Half a
29.5-day cycle ≈ 14–15 days.
**P7.** Waxing gibbous; waxing; more than half (>50%) of the disk looks lit.
**P8.** Waning crescent; waning; less than half (<50%) looks lit.
**P9.** Earth's phases are opposite ours: when we see a full moon
(Earth-between-Sun-and-Moon lineup), the astronaut sees Earth's night side —
a "new Earth." When we see a new moon, they see a "full Earth." Credit the
lineup reasoning.
**P10.** Sample: "The dark part isn't a shadow at all — it's just the side of
the Moon facing away from the Sun. In our lamp model the crescent showed up
with nothing blocking the light."
**P11.** Sketch: Sun–Earth–Moon line, Earth middle; arrow/label "Earth's
shadow falls on the Moon"; phase labeled full moon.
**P12.** Full moon puts Earth *between* the Sun and Moon; a solar eclipse
needs the Moon between Sun and Earth — the lineups are mutually exclusive.
**P13.** More eclipses — nearly every new/full moon would align closely
enough for one. (This is the counterfactual that proves the tilt's role.)
**P14.** Correction: the Sun didn't turn off; the Moon moved in front of it
and blocked its light from reaching us — a shadow, not a switch.
**P15.** A partial solar eclipse is in progress; the viewer is safe because
you watch the *projection* with your back to the Sun — your eyes never face
the light.
**P16.** (a) The southern hemisphere. (b) Summer in the south, winter in the
north. (c) The southern hemisphere.
**P17.** Almost no seasons — no hemisphere ever leans sunward. What still
changes: the small perihelion/aphelion whole-planet difference (and
day-to-day weather, which is not the seasons).
**P18.** Extreme seasons: each pole would point nearly straight at the Sun for
half the year (months of daylight, intense summer) and directly away for the
other half (months of darkness, deep winter). Credit mechanism-based
reasoning.
**P19.** (a) Aphelion is larger by ~5.0 million km (152.1 − 147.1).
(b) Rebuttal: January is the coldest month in the N hemisphere *despite*
being closest, and distance would warm/cool the whole planet together — but
the hemispheres have opposite seasons.
**P20.** Parallel axes = the axis keeps one fixed direction in space all
year — that is what makes hemispheres *take turns* leaning sunward. If the
axis always leaned toward the Sun, one hemisphere would have eternal summer
and the other eternal winter.
**P21.** Rotation: day/night, sunrise/sunset. Revolution (+ tilt): the year,
the seasons' cycle. Orbit of the Moon: the Moon's phases.
**P22.** a. Supported (viewing geometry repeats ~monthly; 29.5-day reference).
b. Contradicted (seasons come from tilt; shadows belong to eclipses).
c. Untestable by our models (none includes true sizes/distances — the audit
rule; the fact is true but not provable *here*).
**P23.** Honest answer: "No — the lamp is far too close; in reality the Sun
is enormously farther, which is why real eclipses are rarer than in our
model. The model is still good for *positions and alignments* — the
geometry."
**P24.** Four sentences, one vocab word each used correctly (rotation,
phase, eclipse, tilt, hemisphere, etc.). Score usage, not elegance.

## Unit assessment

**Part A:** A1 b · A2 c · A3 c · A4 b · A5 b · A6 b · A7 b · A8 b.
**B1.** A = morning, B = near midday, C = evening (1 pt). Reasoning for one
panel (2 pts): e.g., Panel A — the Sun is low at E, so light comes from the
east; the shadow points west (away from light) and is long because the Sun is
low → morning.
**B2.** (a) Waning gibbous. (b) Waning. (c) Sunlight comes from the left, so
the Moon's sunward half is lit; at position 6 more than half of the visible
disk is the sunward side, and the Moon is past full heading toward new.
**B3.** (1) The lamp-and-ball model produces every phase with no shadow
involved — if shadows caused phases, the phases would vanish without one.
(2) Earth's shadow *does* sometimes fall on the Moon — that's a lunar
eclipse, a rare full-moon event, not a monthly phase.
**B4.** Drawing: Sun–Moon–Earth straight line, labeled (1 pt). New moon
because the Moon is on the Sun's side (1 pt). Not every new moon because the
~5° orbital tilt usually carries the Moon above/below the line so its shadow
misses Earth (1 pt).
**B5.** The axis keeps its 23.5° tilt pointing one way while Earth revolves;
in June the northern hemisphere leans sunward → more direct light on a
smaller area + longer days = summer; the southern hemisphere leans away →
spread light + shorter days = winter. (1 pt per mechanism + hemisphere
contrast.)
**B6.** (a) Perihelion ≈ 147.1 million km (early January); aphelion ≈ 152.1
million km (early July) (1 pt). (b) January is the N hemisphere's winter
despite closest approach — the opposite of the claim's prediction — and
distance would give the whole planet one season at a time, but the hemispheres
are opposite (2 pts).
**C1.** (a) The orbit takes about **29.5 days** (synodic month), not exactly
30 (2 pts). (b) The model includes lighting geometry/alignments only — it was
built with convenient sizes, distances, and timing, so no number read off it
counts as a measurement of the real system; the audit rule limits claims to
what the model faithfully includes (2 pts).

## Project scoring guidance

Use the rubric in [project.md](project.md). Common judgments:
- **Claims:** "summer is warmer" is vague (Developing at best); "June is
  summer in the northern hemisphere because it leans toward the Sun" is
  specific (Strong).
- **Model use:** posing the balls for each claim = Strong; describing while
  the model sits untouched = Starting.
- **Limits card:** must name the *specific* scale distortion; "not to scale"
  alone = Developing.
- **Reflection:** look for a named insight (e.g., "I finally saw why the
  tilt has to stay fixed") — generic praise = Starting.
