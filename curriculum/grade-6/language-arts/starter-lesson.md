# Starter Lesson — Evaluate a claim and support an argument

**Status:** draft supplement. **Time:** three 35–40 minute sessions. **Goal:** Identify an author's claim, test whether evidence supports it, and draft an argument that qualifies its conclusion.

## Adult preparation and readiness

Read the original passage. Readiness: learner can identify a claim and a supporting detail with prompts. Provide dictionary access only if useful; all essential evidence is on this page.

## Original passage: “Should a neighborhood add a refill station?”

> A community center is considering adding a bottle-refill station. During one fictional week, staff counted 36 people using the center on Monday, 41 on Wednesday, and 38 on Friday. The center currently has one drinking fountain in the lobby. A refill station could make it easier to fill reusable bottles. Counts from three days do not show how many visitors carry bottles, and staff have not checked installation costs or maintenance needs. Before making a permanent change, the center could ask visitors what they use and compare possible locations.

## Teach and model

Explain: a **claim** is a position; **evidence** is information that can support or weaken it; reasoning explains the link. Evidence can be relevant yet insufficient.

**Worked example 1:** Claim: “The center should explore a refill station.” Evidence: 36–41 people used the center on the three counted days. Reasoning: repeated use gives a reason to ask whether visitors need the service, but attendance alone does not show bottle demand.

**Worked example 2:** Counterclaim: “The center should not buy one yet.” Evidence: costs and maintenance have not been checked. Reasoning: a permanent purchase should consider ongoing resources. This does not prove the station is a bad idea; it supports gathering more information first.

## Pacing and tasks

Session 1: adult models claim/evidence; guided tasks 1–2. Session 2: learner independently completes tasks 3–4 and adult discusses uncertainty. Session 3: write/revise tasks 5–6; exit check.

1. **Guided:** Underline the proposed action.
2. **Guided:** List two facts and one unknown from the passage.
3. **Independent:** Decide whether visitor counts prove that visitors want a refill station. Explain.
4. **Independent:** Name one additional piece of evidence and a fair way to collect it.
5. **Adult-guided error check:** “There were 41 people Wednesday; therefore every visitor wants the station.” Identify the leap.
6. **Application:** Write a paragraph recommending buy now, gather more evidence, or reject; include two facts and one limitation.

**Answer checks:** the proposal is adding a station; facts include one fountain and counts 36/41/38; unknowns include demand/cost/maintenance. Counts alone do not prove demand. An anonymous voluntary tally/question or cost estimate may help. Counts from three days do not represent every visitor or establish their preference. Any recommendation can earn full credit with accurate evidence and qualified reasoning.

## Exit check and product

Learner labels claim/evidence/reasoning in their paragraph. Product: revised argument paragraph with at least two cited passage facts and a limitation.

## Review rubric

| Criterion | 2 — shows skill | 1 — developing | 0 — not yet observed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim | Clear and responds to the choice | Position implied | Use a claim frame |
| Evidence | Uses two accurate facts | Uses one fact | Annotate passage first |
| Reasoning | Explains connection and limitation | Gives evidence without link | Use “This matters because…” |

Offer chunked read-aloud, vocabulary definitions, speech-to-text, outline frames, or extra planning time. The passage is fictional and does not describe a real center. Do not collect visitor names or health information. Keep student writing private. The lesson provides a small argument sample, not a complete ELA scope.
