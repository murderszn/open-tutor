# Answer Key — Unit 02: Literature: Theme, Plot, and Narrator

**Adult only.** Worked solutions for every lesson item,
practice set, quiz question, assessment task, and project
criterion. Open responses accept any defensible alternative
that cites textual evidence; samples below show the expected
level.

## Lesson 01 — Theme from details

**Guided 1 — topic or theme:**
- (a) "friendship" → **Topic** (one-word label).
- (b) "True friendship survives arguments" → **Theme**,
  specific (makes a claim about friendship).
- (c) "Being brave is good" → **Theme, too vague** — it fits
  almost any story about bravery; fix: "Bravery means acting
  even when your hands shake" (a claim this story would have
  to earn).
- (d) "Standing up for someone can cost you something, and be
  worth it anyway" → **Theme**, specific.

**Guided 2 — one more detail:** Acceptable: "Mr. Alder stood
in the rain with his newspaper over his head and waited"
(paragraph 2) — he endures discomfort without complaint, and
Maya's umbrella answers it; or "he always arrived before the
first bus" / "always chose the same bench" (paragraph 1) —
his quiet routine is what Maya notices and repays. Any
paraphrase with paragraph number counts.

**Guided 3 — objective summary of paragraph 2:** Sample:
"The third bench was gone, removed for repair. Mr. Alder
stood in the rain with his newspaper over his head and did
not sit anywhere else." Opinion words to catch: *poor,
sad, stubborn, lonely* — e.g., "The poor man stood
miserably" → "poor" and "miserably" are opinions.

**Independent 4 — defend or reject "Public benches are
important":** Reject (stronger): "The story is not about
benches being important — Mr. Alder refuses to sit anywhere
else even when the bench is gone (paragraph 2), so the bench
itself is not the point; the point is the kindness between
him and Maya." Defend is acceptable only with evidence that
the bench *enables* the meeting — adult judges the citation,
not the side.

**Independent 5 — four-sentence summary:** Sample: "Each
morning Mr. Alder sat on the third bench at the bus stop.
When the bench was removed for repair, he stood in the rain
rather than sit elsewhere. Maya held her umbrella over both
of them without speaking. When the bench returned, Mr. Alder
gave Maya a new bus schedule." Least-sure sentence is
usually sentence 3 ("without speaking" is explicit —
"without saying a word," paragraph 3 — so it is safe).

**Applied — theme vs. moral:** Sample: "A moral is a rule the
story hands you, like 'always share your umbrella.' A theme
is an idea the story explores through what happens, like
'small kindnesses can be repaid in unexpected ways.' Morals
tell you what to do; themes give you something to think
about — and two readers can find different themes in the
same story."

**Exit check:** 1. A topic names what happens (a label); a
theme is a full sentence making a claim about life that the
details support. 2. Sample: "Small kindnesses can be repaid
in ways the giver never expects" — detail: Maya's umbrella
(paragraph 3) answered by the new schedule (paragraph 4).
3. (b) — it reports only what the text shows; (a) adds the
opinion "lonely."

## Lesson 02 — Plot episodes and character change

**Guided 1 — climax:** Paragraph 4: "Dev picked up the stick
and walked onto the stage." After he steps onstage he cannot
go back to never having performed — the direction of the
story turns there.

**Guided 2 — falling action:** The second learner is closer.
"Dev plays the piece" is falling action (consequences of the
turning point unfolding); the applause is the *resolution*
(the new settled state). Falling action = results unfolding;
resolution = where things land.

**Guided 3 — chart:** Early: "'read it four times and walked
away without signing up' (paragraph 1) — shows fear deciding
for him." Late: "'He walked onto the stage, counted himself
in — *one, two, three* — and played' (paragraph 4) — shows
him acting while still afraid." Any equivalent quotes count.

**Independent 4 — "Bench" episode map:** Exposition: Mr.
Alder's 6:40 routine on the third bench (paragraph 1).
Rising action: the bench is removed; he stands in the rain;
Maya brings the umbrella (paragraphs 2–3). Climax: the bench
returns and Mr. Alder presses the new schedule into Maya's
hand (paragraph 4) — the kindness is repaid; the relationship
turns. Falling action/resolution: the gesture lands ("In
case yours tears") — the new settled state between them.
(Short story: climax and resolution share paragraph 4 —
acceptable to note.)

**Independent 5 — error analysis:** The error: a performance
is an *event*, not a *turning point*. Dev had already played
for Ms. Okafor (paragraph 3) — the performance itself
changes nothing. The turning point is the *decision* before
it: picking up the dropped stick and walking onstage. After
that step, "never performed" is gone forever.

**Applied:** Accept any real example with a named climax and
a before/after response contrast (e.g., a sports film's final
play + the character's changed response to pressure).

**Exit check:** 1. Exposition (setup: who/where/when);
rising action (problems grow); climax (turning point);
falling action (results unfold); resolution (how it ends).
2. Compare how the character *responds* to a key event early
vs. late — quote both responses. 3. Paragraph 4 — Dev picks
up the stick and walks onto the stage.

## Lesson 03 — Word choice and craft

**Guided 1 — two more figures:** Acceptable picks:
- "the sea stacked itself into gray hills that *marched*
  toward the shore" (paragraph 1) — personification; sea
  compared to marching soldiers/hills that move; adds menace
  and deliberate force.
- "The rain *stitched* silver lines across the pane"
  (paragraph 2) — metaphor (or personification of rain as
  sewing); rain compared to stitching thread; adds delicate,
  careful patterning — Lena experiences the storm as craft,
  not chaos.
- "the whole house seemed to *hold its breath*"
  (paragraph 2) — personification; house compared to a
  person holding breath; adds suspense/tension.
(Any two, correctly typed and explained.)

**Guided 2 — connotation swap:** Sample: "The sky over
Marrow Bay turned the color of old coins" → "The sky over
Marrow Bay turned the color of *warm honey*." *Old coins*
is cold, gray, hard; *warm honey* is golden and inviting —
the tone shifts from threatening to welcoming while the
fact (the sky's color at storm-light) stays.

**Guided 3 — why paragraph 3 is last:** Sample: "Paragraph 3
delivers the theme — 'the same storm, two opposite answers.'
Tomas's 'glorious' only means something *after* Lena's
tense, breath-held paragraphs built the storm as
threatening. If it came first, there would be no contrast to
resolve; the story's point *is* the contrast, so it must
land last."

**Independent 4 — "The Climb" stanza 1:** Setting (and the
start of theme): "The path begins where the brave ones
pause, / A thread of dust between the pines" — places us at
the foot of the climb; the journey about to start. (Theme
also acceptable with justification — "My legs are
questions" sets up the self-doubt the climb will answer.)

**Independent 5 — connotation sort:** Warmer → colder:
*determined* (warm) / *stubborn* (cold); *thrifty* (warm) /
*stingy* (cold); *curious* (warm) / *nosy* (cold). Cold-word
sentence sample: "The *nosy* neighbor pressed her ear to the
wall" — the coldness is the point (intrusive, unlike
*curious*).

**Applied — welcoming rewrite:** Sample: "The sky over
Marrow Bay turned the color of warm honey. The wind *danced*
with the shutters, and the sea *rolled* its gray hills
gently toward the shore." Changes: *argued → danced*
(hostile → playful personification); *marched → rolled
gently* (military → soft motion); *old coins → warm honey*
(cold → warm connotation). Facts kept: sky color, wind,
sea.

**Exit check:** 1. What two things are compared? What feeling
or picture does the comparison add that plain words would
not? 2. Any correct pair, e.g., *cozy/cramped*, *cheap/
inexpensive*, *glorious/terrible*. 3. Stanza 3 releases what
stanzas 1–2 built ("What I carried up, I set gently down")
— first, there would be no climb and nothing to set down;
the arrival needs the journey.

## Lesson 04 — Narrator and point of view

**Guided 1 — POV identification:**
- (a) **Second person** — "You walk," "Your hand."
- (b) **Third person limited** — "she/her," one mind
  (Amara's wondering).
- (c) **Third-person omniscient** — "she/her" plus a second
  mind (the grandmother "smiling" while dialing — a thought/
  feeling Amara cannot see).

**Guided 2 — what the narrator cannot know:** Sample: "Why
Mom laughed — 'she laughed' (paragraph 3) shows the action
but never her reason; the narrator was never inside Mom's
head." Also acceptable: whether Marcus knocked the paint
over on purpose ("holding an empty can" shows the scene,
not his intent).

**Guided 3 — third-person-limited rewrite:** Sample: "She
heard the crash from the garage. By the time she got there,
blue paint was spreading across the floor like a small lake,
and her brother Marcus was standing in the middle of it
holding an empty can." Changes: pronouns (I → she); the
defensive first sentence ("I was *not* the one") must go or
become shown thought — limited third person *can* report
her thoughts ("She told herself she hadn't touched it").
Stays the same: no access to Marcus's or Mom's minds.

**Independent 4 — omniscient rewrite:** Sample adds:
"Marcus stared at the spreading blue, wondering if the
color would come out of his shoes. [Marcus's thought]" and
"Mom, turning into the driveway, was already deciding the
blue looked better than the oil stains. [Mom's thought]"
Both labeled; neither was available to the first-person
narrator.

**Independent 5 — "The Tryout" POV:** The classmate is
**wrong**. "The Tryout" uses "he/his," never "I" — it is
third person (limited, following Dev). Knowing a
character's feelings does not make it first person; the
pronouns decide, and here an outside narrator reports Dev's
feelings.

**Applied — dog narrator:** Sample: "The two-legs in yellow
crouched down and held out something that smelled like
bread. I sat very still so it would not take the smell
away. From the wall, the cat watched, useless as always."
POV sentence: "The dog's version is all smell and stillness
— it cannot know *why* the child brought bread (the child's
kindness), only that food appeared; the child's version
would explain the reason the dog can only guess at."

**Exit check:** 1. First (I/we — narrator is a character);
second (you — narrator addresses the reader); third limited
(he/she/they, one mind); third omniscient (he/she/they, all
minds). 2. Limited follows one character's thoughts;
omniscient reports several characters' thoughts. 3. Sample:
we never learn why Mom laughed — the first-person narrator
can only report "she laughed," never her reason.

## Lesson 05 — Reading vs. listening

**Guided 1 — rushed voice:** Sample: "Read fast and nervous,
Priya's 'Sign your name' sounds like a dare she is scared to
lose — urgent, almost annoyed — instead of the warm nudge it
reads as on the page. The performance adds pressure the
print does not require."

**Guided 2 — rereading:** Print — you control the pace.
Sample: "Rereading '*one, two, three*' in italics, I
realized it was Dev's calming ritual, not just counting —
the first read I slid right past it."

**Independent 3 — marking "The Storm at Marrow Bay":**
Accept any two sentences with marked pauses/stresses and a
tone explanation, e.g.: "The wind *argued* // with the
shutters (stress *argued*, pause after) — the stress makes
the storm sound angry and deliberate, which matches the
tense tone."

**Independent 4 — error analysis:** Sample: "It gets the
comparison backwards. Silent reading lets *you* control the
pace — slow down, stop, reread a hard sentence — and lets
you notice print-only clues like italics and paragraph
breaks. Listening hands control to the reader's voice.
'Less work' is not the same as 'more understanding.'"

**Applied — 60-second plan:** Check for: underlined stress
words, // pause marks, one feeling-goal sentence (e.g.,
"I want the listener to feel Dev's fear turning into
courage"). The plan, not a performance, is the deliverable.

**Exit check:** 1. Pace, emphasis/stress, pauses, mood/
feeling (any two). 2. Italics, dashes, paragraph breaks,
spelling; the reader's own pace and rereading (any two).
3. Reading revealed the *structure* (italics on "*one, two,
three*," the dash); listening revealed the *fear* (the
pause before "dropped").

## Lesson 06 — Story and poem, one theme

**Guided 1 — story detail:** Acceptable: "the neighbors
appeared: Mrs. Okafor with her candles... the twins with
glow sticks" (paragraph 2) — the light spreads person to
person; or "June kept the lantern lit for another minute
anyway" (paragraph 3) — she holds the light even when it is
no longer needed.

**Guided 2 — poem detail:** Acceptable: "'Hold it steady,
hold it higher, / Let the neighbors find the glow'"
(stanza 2) — the imperative spreads the light outward; or
"'The dark is only a question / The light is the answer
sent'" (stanza 1).

**Guided 3 — why vs. how:** Sample: "The *story* tells me
why June lit the lantern — the blackout left the block dark
(paragraph 1), so her act answers a real need. The *poem*
tells me how the light *feels* — 'The dark is only a
question / The light is the answer sent' makes light feel
like certainty. Events vs. image."

**Independent 4 — comparison paragraph:** Check for: shared
theme named in one sentence; one story detail + one poem
detail, each quoted; a closing sentence on what each form
does best (story: shows the theme working on people over
time; poem: makes the theme memorable through image and
repetition).

**Independent 5 — error analysis:** Sample: "Same theme is
not the same text. The *form* changes how the theme reaches
you: the story builds it through events in order — you
watch kindness spread — while the poem builds it through a
repeated image — you feel it all at once. Sharing a theme
is sharing the water, not the container."

**Applied:** (a) Comic: 3 panels in story order with the
theme visible; (b) Stanza 3: 4 lines keeping the lantern
image and theme, e.g., "And when the morning takes the
night, / The lanterns dim, the sun takes flight — / But
what we shared in candle-glow / Is something streets will
always know." One sentence on form fit required.

**Exit check:** 1. Sample: "When people share what little
light they have, darkness loses its power over them."
2. Events in order / plot / characters acting (story tools).
3. Concentrated images, repetition, rhythm/sound, line
breaks (poem tools).

## Practice

**A1:** 1. Topic. 2. Theme, specific. 3. Theme, too vague
(fix: "Refusing to quit a hard task can turn fear into
skill" — must be earned by details). 4. Theme, specific.
5. Topic. 6. Theme, specific.

**A2:** Sample: "Every Saturday Rosa unlocked the
community garden shed. One Saturday the lock was already
open, the tools oiled, and the broken rake mended. Rosa
never learned who did it, and she hung the spare key back
on its nail." Adult circles opinion words (*kind,
mysterious, sweet*).

**A3 — "The Spare Key" theme:** Sample: "Unseen kindness
keeps a community going." Details: "the tools were oiled
and the broken rake handle was mended" (someone's secret
work); "she hung the spare key on its nail, tied the red
ribbon a little tighter, and smiled" (Rosa answers
anonymity with continued care).

**B1 — "The Spare Key" stages:** Exposition: Rosa's Saturday
shed routine (sentence 1–2). Rising action: the lock
already open; tools oiled; rake mended (sentences 4–5).
Climax: Rosa finds the work done and never learns who did
it — the mystery turns her routine into a question.
Falling action: she hangs the key back, ties the ribbon
tighter. Resolution: she "smiled at the empty shed" — the
new settled state.

**B2 — Dev chart:** See Lesson 02 Guided 3 key.

**B3 — "The Spare Key" climax:** (b). Sentences: "The
already-open lock is the turning point — before it, Rosa's
Saturdays were routine; after it, she knows someone shares
her work in secret. (a) is exposition and (c) is the
resolution."

**C1:** Warmer → colder: *house* (warm) / *shack* (cold);
*confident* (warm) / *cocky* (cold); *slim* (warm) /
*scrawny* (cold); *curious* (warm) / *nosy* (cold).
Cold-point sentence sample: "The *shack* leaned against the
fence, its door hanging by one hinge" — *shack* (not
*house*) makes the neglect the point.

**C2:** 1. Personification — truck compared to a person
coughing awake; adds age, effort, reluctance. 2. Simile —
excuse compared to tissue paper; adds flimsiness,
transparency. 3. Metaphor — classroom compared to a
beehive; adds buzzing energy and chaos. 4. Personification
(moon *poured*) — moon compared to someone pouring;
adds deliberate, generous light.

**C3:** Sample: "The *sunlit* hallway *carried* the sound of
her footsteps." *Gloomy → sunlit* (dark → warm
connotation); *swallowed → carried* (threatening →
gentle). Tone shifts from menacing to welcoming.

**C4:** Stanza 2 — "Halfway up, the town shrinks to a
thumbnail" — the physical halfway point places the
speaker; the shrinking town shows altitude and distance.

**D1:** 1. First person — "I," "my." 2. Second person —
"You," "Your." 3. Third limited — "He/his," one mind
(Jonah's). 4. Third omniscient — "he" plus Ms. Rivera's
unseen knowledge ("had seen everything and was deciding").

**D2 — "The Spilled Paint" limits:** "Why Mom laughed"
('she laughed,' paragraph 3 — reason hidden); "Whether
Marcus knocked the can over on purpose" ('holding an empty
can' shows the scene, not his intent).

**D3 — Jonah rewrite:** Sample: "I hid the note behind the
loose brick. My hands were shaking. I told myself it was
just for one day." What it hides: the outside view — the
old version let the reader *see* Jonah ("His hands were
shaking") from outside; the new "I" version can only
*report* the shaking, and it can no longer show anything
Jonah himself does not notice.

**M1:** (a) A full-sentence idea a story is really about,
built from details. (b) The turning point — after it the
story cannot go back. (c) The feeling a word carries beyond
its dictionary meaning. (d) An outside narrator who knows
every character's thoughts.

**M2:** Disagree — "A theme is never stated directly in a
good story; in 'The Bench at the Bus Stop' no one says the
theme — Maya's umbrella (paragraph 3) and the returned
schedule (paragraph 4) build it."

**M3:** Diagram labels: exposition / rising action / climax
/ falling action / resolution. "The Tryout" episodes: see
Lesson 02 Modeled example 1.

## Quiz key (week-10_quiz.md)

1. **B** — a full-sentence claim about the passage.
2. **B** — two details showing nervousness (hugged
   backpack) and comfort (re-read letter).
3. Sample: "Tomas rode the dawn ferry to the island alone.
   He carried his grandmother's letter, which he had read
   eleven times. The captain pointed out the island, and
   Tomas decided her words were true of both the island and
   his grandmother." No opinions.
4. **C** — "Both were true" is the turn: loneliness becomes
   belonging.
5. Sample: "He 'decided it did not matter. Both were true'
   — he stops puzzling over the captain's meaning and
   accepts comfort. In paragraph 1 he 'hugged' his backpack,
   closed and guarded; by paragraph 3 he is open to the
   island waiting for him."
6. **B**.
7. **Warm** — "Come for the summer" (invitation); "still
   too many for my knees" is self-deprecating and fond, not
   bitter.
8. Sample: "Paragraph 3 is the resolution — the captain's
   'She's been waiting' and Tomas's 'Both were true' land
   the story's comfort after two paragraphs of lonely
   travel. It resolves the tension instead of just
   describing the trip."
9. **Third person limited** — "He/his/him," one mind
   (Tomas's: "He decided," "He knew"). Cannot tell us: what
   the captain meant ("She's been waiting" — island or
   grandmother — is never resolved from her mind), or what
   the grandmother feels writing the letter.
10. Sample: "'The Quiet Harbor' uses light as something
    *left behind* — 'harbor lights blur and shrink' marks
    distance and loneliness. 'Two Small Lanterns' uses
    light as something *shared* — 'One flame borrowed, one
    flame lent' marks generosity. Same image family,
    opposite emotional work."

**Quiz scoring:** 10 items; Q3, Q5, Q8, Q10 are 2 points
each (content + evidence), others 1 point. 12–14 = secure;
9–11 = review missed objectives; below 9 = reteach per the
teacher guide before the assessment.

## Assessment key (unit-assessment.md)

**A1 — theme:** Sample: "Assuming the worst about someone
can hide the help they actually need." Details: "the old
gate *creaked* open... Mr. Abernathy stepped through with
an empty basket" (paragraph 3) — Maya assumes theft; "it
was lined with seed packets... 'Yours grow,' he said
quietly. 'Mine won't. Teach me?'" (paragraph 4) — the
basket held a request, not stolen tomatoes. (Also
acceptable: "Sharing what you know can open a gate that
has been shut for years" — with the paragraph 5 details.)

**A2 — summary:** Sample: "A fence with a long-shut gate
stood between Maya's garden and Mr. Abernathy's bare yard.
When Maya's tomatoes vanished, she caught Mr. Abernathy
coming through the gate with a basket. The basket held
seed packets, not stolen tomatoes — he wanted her to teach
him to garden. By August the gate stayed open and both
yards grew tomatoes."

**A3 — stages:** Exposition: the fence, the two yards,
Maya's garden vs. bare dirt (paragraphs 1–2). Rising
action: tomatoes vanish; Maya sets the alarm and watches
(paragraph 3). Climax: the gate creaks open and Maya
confronts Mr. Abernathy — "Those are *mine*!" (paragraphs
3–4) — accusation turns the story. Falling action: the
basket's seed packets; "Teach me?" (paragraph 4).
Resolution: the gate stays open; both yards grow; the
tomato "tasted like being ten years old" (paragraph 5).

**A4 — Abernathy's change:** Paragraph 3: "stepped through
with an empty basket" — he *takes* (or seems to), silent
and furtive at dawn. Paragraph 5: "ate a tomato warm from
the sun" — he *receives* openly, in daylight, among people.
Change: from a solitary taker hiding behind a shut gate to
a neighbor learning and sharing in the open.

**A5 — figurative language:** Simile — vines compared to
green ladders; adds upward energy and order: the garden is
climbing, thriving, reaching.

**A6 — connotation:** *Uneasy* — a creak is a worn,
complaining sound; it makes the gate's first opening feel
tense and suspicious, matching Maya's alarm. Welcoming
alternative: the gate "*swung*" open, or "*eased*" open.

**A7 — repetition's work:** Sample: "The repeated image is
the story's before-and-after proof. In paragraph 2 the
ladders grow on Maya's side only — her garden, her skill.
In paragraph 5 they grow 'on both sides' — the image now
measures what changed: knowledge shared, the gate open.
The repetition *is* the resolution, shown instead of
told."

**A8 — POV:** Third person limited — "she/her," "he/his";
one mind followed (Maya's: "She set her alarm," "Maya
threw the window open"). Cannot tell us: why Mr.
Abernathy stopped gardening forty years ago, or what he
felt carrying the basket at dawn — his mind is never
entered.

**B1 — poem theme:** Sample: "Rain reconnects what dryness
separated" — "What the dry weeks held apart, / Water joins
them, heart to heart."

**B2 — figurative language:** Personification — garden and
gutters given human actions (drinking, singing); makes the
rain feel joyful and alive, the whole world welcoming it.

**B3 — stanza 2's fit:** Sample: "Stanza 2 develops the
theme: stanza 1 *describes* the rain (silver needles,
singing gutters); stanza 2 *explains what the rain does*
— it 'joins' what dryness 'held apart.' Without it the
poem would be pretty description with no point; stanza 2
lands the meaning."

**C1 — comparison:** Sample: "Both texts use water and
growing as images of repair. The *story* builds the idea
through *events in order* — theft suspected, truth
revealed, gate opened, two gardens growing — so repair
happens between *people* over time. The *poem* builds it
through *compressed image* — rain joining 'heart to heart'
in eight lines — so repair feels *immediate and musical*.
The story shows reconciliation working; the poem makes
reconciliation sing."

**C2 — reading vs. listening:** Sample: "Listening would
emphasize the *sounds* — the gate's *creak*, Maya's shouted
'Those are *mine*!' — a voice would make the confrontation
feel louder and tenser. Silent reading lets me *stop and
reread* — I could go back to paragraph 2's ladder image
when it repeats in paragraph 5, which a single listen
would blur past."

**D1 — short writing:** Check: 4–5 sentences; ≥3 unit
words used correctly (e.g., "The *theme* of a story is
built from details. The *climax* is the turning point of
the plot's episodes. The *narrator*'s point of view
decides what the reader can know. An *objective* summary
leaves opinions out.").

**Assessment scoring:** A1–A8: 2 pts each (16); B1–B3: 2
pts each (6); C1–C2: 3 pts each (6); D1: 2 pts (30 total).
26–30 = secure; 21–25 = review missed strands; below 21 =
reteach per teacher guide, then reassess with the quiz
passage.

## Project — sample responses (rubric reference)

**Theme sentence (sample):** "When people share what little
light they have, darkness loses its power over them."
(supported by both "The Lantern Path" and "Two Small
Lanterns").

**Story evidence (sample):** "June lit the camping lantern
from the hall closet" (paragraph 2) — one girl's light
starts the gathering; "the neighbors appeared: Mrs. Okafor
with her candles... the twins with glow sticks" (paragraph
2) — the light multiplies person to person; "June kept the
lantern lit for another minute anyway" (paragraph 3) — she
holds the circle even after need passes.

**Poem evidence (sample):** "'One flame borrowed, one flame
lent' (stanza 1)" — light as shared currency; "'Let the
neighbors find the glow' (stanza 2)" — the imperative
spreads it outward.

**Narrator lens (sample):** "I crouched lower and held the
bread very still. The dog's nose twitched. [child] The
child-narrator shows *why* — she brought the bread on
purpose — but hides what the dog *thinks* the bread means;
she cannot know if the dog trusts her yet. A camera would
show everything; my narrator only knows her own hope."

**Essay (sample, meets all 2-point bands):** "Both 'The
Lantern Path' and 'Two Small Lanterns' share one theme:
when people share what little light they have, darkness
loses its power over them. The story builds this theme
through events in order — the blackout, June's lantern,
the neighbors arriving with candles and glow sticks, and
the circle of faces that stays even after the power
returns. The poem builds the same theme through image and
repetition instead of events: 'One flame borrowed, one
flame lent' turns light into something passed hand to
hand. The story does best what only a story can — it shows
the theme *working on people* over an evening, episode by
episode. The poem does best what only a poem can — it
makes the theme *memorable*: 'The dark is only a question
/ The light is the answer sent' is a line you carry home.
Different containers, same water."
