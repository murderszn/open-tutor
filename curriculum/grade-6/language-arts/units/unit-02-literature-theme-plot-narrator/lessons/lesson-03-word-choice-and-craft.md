# Lesson 03 — Word Choice and Craft: What the Words Are Doing

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find figurative language and
connotative words, explain how word choices change tone, and
show how one scene or stanza fits into the whole story or poem."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and
paper.
**Standards:** RL.6.4 (determine figurative and connotative
meanings; analyze word choice's impact on meaning and tone),
RL.6.5 (analyze how a sentence, chapter, scene, or stanza fits
into the overall structure and develops theme, setting, or
plot).

## Launch (5 min) — same fact, different words

"The room was small." Now: "The room was *cozy*." Now: "The room
was *cramped*." Same fact — a small room. Three different
**feelings**. *Cozy* feels warm and safe; *cramped* feels
squeezed and unhappy. That feeling a word carries *beyond* its
dictionary meaning is its **connotation**; the dictionary meaning
is its **denotation**. Authors choose words for their
connotations the way painters choose colors.

## Readiness check (3 min)

"Which word has the warmer connotation: *cheap* or
*inexpensive*? Which would you use to compliment a friend's
jacket?" (*Inexpensive* is neutral-to-warm; *cheap* suggests
poor quality. Same denotation — low price — different
connotation.)

## Explicit teaching

**Figurative language** says something non-literally to make you
feel it more strongly:

- **Simile:** a comparison using *like* or *as* — "The applause
  sounded like rain on a tin roof."
- **Metaphor:** a direct comparison without *like* or *as* —
  "The stage was a deep breath before a dive."
- **Personification:** giving human qualities to non-human
  things — "The wind argued with the shutters."

Ask two questions of every figure of speech: *What two things
are being compared?* and *What feeling or picture does the
comparison add that plain words would not?*

**Tone** is the author's attitude toward the subject — playful,
serious, tense, tender — and word choice builds it. Change the
words, change the tone.

**Fitting into the whole** (RL.6.5): no scene or stanza exists
alone. Ask: *Why is this scene here and not somewhere else? What
does it develop — theme, setting, or plot?* A scene in the
rising action usually raises tension or deepens a character; a
final stanza usually lands the theme.

### The passage — "The Storm at Marrow Bay" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** The sky over Marrow Bay turned the color of
> old coins. The wind *argued* with the shutters, and the sea
> stacked itself into gray hills that *marched* toward the
> shore.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** Lena stood at the window with her forehead
> against the glass. The rain *stitched* silver lines across the
> pane, and the whole house seemed to *hold its breath*. "Like
> drums," she whispered, listening to the drops, "like a
> thousand tiny drums."
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Her brother Tomas burst through the door,
> soaked and grinning, his jacket heavy with water. "It's
> *glorious* out there!" he shouted. Lena stared. The same
> storm, two opposite answers.

### The poem — "The Climb" (original)

> **Stanza 1.**
> The path begins where the brave ones pause,
> A thread of dust between the pines.
> My legs are questions; the hill gives no answers,
> Only the steady pull of the climb.
>
> **Stanza 2.**
> Halfway up, the town shrinks to a thumbnail,
> A small bright coin I used to hold.
> The wind rewrites the map of my face;
> I am taller than the stories I was told.
>
> **Stanza 3.**
> At the top, the sky pours itself out —
> An ocean with no opposite shore.
> What I carried up, I set gently down.
> I do not need to carry it anymore.

### Modeled example 1 — figurative language, twice

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Paragraph 1: 'the wind *argued* with the
shutters' — personification. Two things compared: the wind and a
person arguing. What it adds: the storm feels angry and
deliberate, not just breezy — plain 'the wind was strong' would
not do that. Paragraph 2: 'Like drums,' she whispered, 'like a
thousand tiny drums' — simile. Compared: raindrops and drums.
What it adds: sound and rhythm; Lena is *listening* closely, and
the comparison tells me she finds the storm musical, almost
comforting — even though paragraph 1 made it sound hostile."

### Modeled example 2 — connotation changes tone

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Take paragraph 3: Tomas calls the storm
'*glorious*.' Now swap in '*terrible*': 'It's *terrible* out
there!' Same denotation — the storm is intense — opposite
connotation. *Glorious* makes Tomas sound thrilled and brave;
*terrible* would make him sound frightened. The author's word
choice *is* the tone: with 'glorious,' the paragraph's tone is
exuberant. Notice the author also chose 'burst' and 'grinning'
for Tomas — all warm, energetic connotations — against Lena's
'hold its breath' tension. The word choices stage the
paragraph's whole point: the same storm, two opposite answers."

### Modeled example 3 — how a stanza fits the whole

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Stanza 2 of 'The Climb' — 'Halfway up,
the town shrinks to a thumbnail, / A small bright coin I used
to hold.' Why is this stanza *here*, in the middle? It develops
**theme** and **setting** at once. Setting: we are literally
halfway up — the town looks small below. Theme: the speaker's
old life ('the stories I was told') is shrinking the way the
town shrinks; the climb is changing how big her past feels. If
you moved this stanza to the end, the poem would have no
arrival — stanza 3's release ('I do not need to carry it
anymore') only lands because stanza 2 showed the past getting
smaller."

## Guided practice

1. Find **two more** examples of figurative language in "The
   Storm at Marrow Bay" (not the ones modeled). For each: name
   the type, name the two things compared, and say what feeling
   it adds.
2. Replace one word in paragraph 1 with a word of opposite
   connotation (for example, swap a harsh word for a gentle
   one). Write the new sentence and describe how the tone
   changes.
3. Why does paragraph 3 come *last*? Write 2–3 sentences about
   how it develops the story's theme ("the same storm, two
   opposite answers") — and why the story would be weaker if
   paragraph 3 came first.

## Independent practice

4. In "The Climb," what does stanza 1 develop — setting, plot,
   or theme? Quote one line and explain your choice.
5. Connotation sort: for each pair, label which word is warmer
   and which is colder — *stubborn / determined*, *stingy /
   thrifty*, *curious / nosy*. Then write one sentence using the
   colder word where the coldness is the point.

## Applied task

Rewrite the first two sentences of paragraph 1 of "The Storm at
Marrow Bay" so the storm feels **welcoming** instead of
threatening. Keep the facts (sky, wind, sea) but change the
figurative language and connotations. Then write one sentence
explaining what you changed and why it works.

## Exit check

1. What two questions do you ask of every figure of speech?
2. Give one example of two words with the same denotation but
   different connotations, from this lesson.
3. Why is stanza 3 of "The Climb" a good *ending* stanza — what
   would break if it came first?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult pre-highlights the figurative
  phrases; the learner's job is only the *explain what it
  adds* step.
- **Language access:** a connotation word bank (warm: *glorious,
  cozy, determined, thrifty, curious*; cold: *terrible, cramped,
  stubborn, stingy, nosy*); sentence frame — "The word ___
  makes the tone feel ___ because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may act out the two
  tones of paragraph 3 (Lena's vs. Tomas's) instead of writing
  the rewrite.
- **Extension:** find the personification in stanza 2 of "The
  Climb" ("The wind rewrites the map of my face"). What two
  things are compared, and what does it suggest about how the
  climb changes the speaker? 3–4 sentences.

## Teacher note — misconceptions and key reference

Learners often label every *like/as* as a simile without asking
what is compared ("like drums" — drums compared to *what*? —
raindrops; the missing half is the point). Others treat tone and
mood as the same word; keep it simple: **tone** is the author's
attitude (built by word choice), **mood** is how the reader
feels. For RL.6.5, the common error is describing *what* the
stanza says instead of *what work it does* — redirect with "why
here and not somewhere else?" Answer key:
