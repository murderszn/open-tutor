# Lesson 04 — Narrator and Point of View: Who Is Telling This?

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can identify a story's narrator and
point of view, explain how the author develops it, and show how
the narrator's position shapes what the reader can and cannot
know."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson;
[three-narrators-one-scene.png](../assets/three-narrators-one-scene.png);
pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RL.6.6 (explain how an author develops the
narrator's or speaker's point of view); L.6.6 (acquire and use
grade-appropriate academic vocabulary).

## Launch (7 min) — three narrators, one scene

Look closely at this picture:

![A rainy night under a glowing streetlamp. A child in a yellow raincoat crouches and offers a piece of bread toward a small scruffy dog. A cat sits perched on a brick wall at the right, watching. Puddles on the wet sidewalk reflect the warm lamp glow against dark blue rainy tones.](../assets/three-narrators-one-scene.png)

*Three narrators, one scene (AI-generated illustration): the same
rainy moment under the streetlamp — but the child, the dog, and
the cat would each tell it differently.*

**Text-only alternative (for learners who cannot see the image):**
"Picture a rainy night under a glowing streetlamp. A child in a
yellow raincoat and yellow boots crouches on the wet sidewalk,
holding out a piece of bread toward a small scruffy dog with a
pink collar. The dog sits facing the child, tail slightly
raised. On the right, a striped cat sits perched on top of a
brick wall, watching the scene. Puddles on the sidewalk reflect
the warm yellow lamp glow against the dark blue rainy night."

1. Write **two sentences as the child** telling what is
   happening ("I crouched down because...").
2. Write **two sentences as the cat** telling what is happening
   ("From my wall, I watched...").
3. Now the hard question: what does the **cat** know that the
   **child** cannot know — and what does the **child** know that
   the **cat** cannot know? (Example: the cat knows what the
   child's face looks like from above; the child knows why she
   brought the bread.)

(The adult's point: the *scene* is the same, but each narrator
knows different things and cares about different things. The
narrator is not a camera — a camera records everything and feels
nothing. A narrator *chooses*.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

"In the story 'The Tryout,' who tells the story — Dev, or
someone watching Dev? How do you know?" (Someone watching Dev:
the story says "he," "his," never "I." The narrator is outside
Dev, but follows him closely.)

## Explicit teaching

The **narrator** is the voice telling the story. The **point of
view** is the narrator's position — where they stand, which
decides what they can see:

- **First person:** the narrator is a character in the story —
  "I." They know their own thoughts, but not anyone else's.
- **Second person:** the narrator talks to *you* — "You open
  the door." Rare; used for instructions and some experiments.
- **Third person limited:** the narrator is outside the story —
  "he/she/they" — and follows **one** character's thoughts and
  feelings.
- **Third-person omniscient:** the narrator is outside the
  story and knows **everyone's** thoughts — all-knowing.

How to spot it: check the **pronouns** first (I/we = first;
you = second; he/she/they = third). Then check the **mind
limit**: does the narrator report only one character's thoughts
(limited), or jump between minds (omniscient)?

The key RL.6.6 move: a narrator's position **shapes the
events**. A first-person narrator cannot tell you what another
character was thinking — only what they *said* or *did*. So the
author develops the point of view through **what is shown and
what is hidden**: the same events, told by a different narrator,
become a different story.

### The passage — "The Spilled Paint" (original, first person)

> **Paragraph 1.** I was *not* the one who knocked over the
> paint. I was sitting on the stairs, minding my own business,
> when I heard the crash from the garage.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** By the time I got there, blue paint was
> spreading across the floor like a small lake, and my brother
> Marcus was standing in the middle of it holding an empty can.
> He looked at me. I looked at him. Neither of us said a word.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Mom came home ten minutes later. She looked
> at the lake, at Marcus, at me — and she laughed. "Well,"
> she said, "I always wanted a blue garage floor."

### Modeled example 1 — identifying POV with evidence

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Pronouns: 'I,' 'my,' 'me' — first
person. The narrator is the younger sibling, a character in the
scene. Mind limit: we get the narrator's thoughts ('I was
sitting on the stairs, minding my own business') but never
Marcus's thoughts and never Mom's — we only see what they *do*
('He looked at me,' 'she laughed'). So: first person, and the
author develops it by *hiding* every other mind. The narrator
even defends herself in the first sentence — 'I was *not* the
one' — which tells me this narrator has a stake in the story:
she wants us on her side."

### Modeled example 2 — how POV shapes the events

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Question: *Why did Mom laugh?* From this
narrator, we cannot know — we were never inside Mom's head. The
first-person position *hides* Mom's reason, and that hiding is a
choice: it leaves a mystery the reader must solve from clues
('I always wanted a blue garage floor'). Now imagine the same
scene in third-person omniscient: the narrator could dip into
Mom's mind — 'Mom laughed because the blue reminded her of the
kitchen she grew up in' — and the mystery would vanish. Same
events, different story. The POV *is* part of the plot."

## Guided practice

1. Identify the point of view of each (first / third limited /
   third omniscient), with one piece of evidence:
   - (a) "You walk into the dark kitchen. Your hand finds the
     light switch."
   - (b) "Amara stared at the letter. She wondered if her
     grandmother had known all along."
   - (c) "Amara stared at the letter. Across town, her
     grandmother was already dialing the phone, smiling."
2. In "The Spilled Paint," what is **one thing the narrator
   cannot know**? Quote the sentence that shows the limit.
3. Rewrite paragraph 2 of "The Spilled Paint" in **third-person
   limited** (follow the younger sibling; use he/she). What
   changes, and what stays the same?

## Independent practice

4. Rewrite the *same* paragraph 2 in **third-person
   omniscient**: add one thought for Marcus and one thought for
   Mom that the first-person narrator could never report.
   Label each added thought.
5. A classmate says, "'The Tryout' is first person because we
   know Dev's feelings." Is the classmate right? Write 2–3
   sentences correcting or confirming, with pronoun evidence.

## Applied task

Return to the streetlamp picture. Write **four sentences as the
dog** telling the scene — but obey the rule: the dog-narrator
can only know what the dog could know (no reading the child's
mind; the dog does not know the word "streetlamp"). Then write
one sentence explaining how the dog's version differs from the
child's version from the launch.

## Exit check

1. Name the four points of view and the pronoun clue for each.
2. How do you tell third-person limited from third-person
   omniscient?
3. Why does it matter *who* tells a story — give one example
   from "The Spilled Paint" of something the narrator's
   position hides?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** a POV pronoun chart on the desk
  (I/we → first; you → second; he/she/they → third); the adult
  reads the passage while the learner tracks pronouns.
- **Language access:** sentence frames — "The narrator is ___
  because the pronouns are ___. The narrator cannot know ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may tell the two
  retellings aloud as a dramatic monologue instead of writing.
- **Extension:** write the streetlamp scene from the
  **streetlamp's** point of view — an object narrator that sees
  everything but understands nothing. What does that POV hide
  and reveal? 4–5 sentences.

## Teacher note — misconceptions and key reference

The classic error: "first person = the main character." Not
always — a first-person narrator can be a side character (as
here, the sibling watching Marcus). Teach *position*, not
*importance*. Second error: confusing third-person limited with
omniscient the moment the narrator reports *any* thought —
limited reports one mind; omniscient reports several. Third:
learners rewrite POV by swapping pronouns only ("I" → "she")
without changing the *mind limit* — the rewrite must change
what can be known. Answer key:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04 section.
