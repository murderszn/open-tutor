# Lesson 02 — Plot Episodes and Character Change

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can map a story's episodes onto the
five plot stages and explain how a character responds to events
and changes by the end."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson;
[story-mountain.svg](../assets/story-mountain.svg); pencil and
paper.
**Standards:** RL.6.3 (describe how a story's plot unfolds in a
series of episodes; describe how characters respond or change as
the plot moves toward a resolution).

## Launch (5 min) — the mountain

Look at the story mountain:

![The story mountain: a mountain-shaped plot line with five labeled stages — 1. Exposition (who, where, when), 2. Rising action (problems grow), 3. Climax (the turning point), 4. Falling action (results unfold), 5. Resolution (how it ends). An arrow shows the reading direction from left to right.](../assets/story-mountain.svg)

*The story mountain (deterministic diagram): every story climbs
from setup to a turning point and comes back down to an ending.
Plotting the episodes on the mountain shows the story's shape.*

**Text-only alternative:** "Imagine a simple mountain drawn on
paper. At the far left, low, is point 1, **Exposition** — the
setup: who, where, when. The line slopes upward to point 2,
**Rising action** — problems grow. At the peak sits point 3, the
**Climax** — the turning point, the moment everything changes.
The line slopes down to point 4, **Falling action** — the results
of the turning point unfold. At the far right, low, is point 5,
**Resolution** — how the story ends. You read the story from left
to right, the way you climb and descend the mountain."

Name the five stages from memory, pointing to each one on the
diagram.

## Readiness check (3 min)

"Is this an episode or the whole plot: 'Maya brings the umbrella
and holds it over Mr. Alder'? What makes an *episode* different
from a *summary*?" (It is one episode — a single event in the
chain. A plot is the whole chain of episodes in order.)

## Explicit teaching

A plot is not just "what happens." It is what happens **in the
order the author gives it**, and each episode pushes the story up
or down the mountain:

- **Exposition:** the setup — characters, setting, starting
  situation.
- **Rising action:** a chain of episodes where problems or
  tension grow. Each episode makes the next one matter more.
- **Climax:** the turning point — the single moment after which
  nothing can go back to the way it was.
- **Falling action:** the episodes that follow from the turning
  point — consequences working themselves out.
- **Resolution:** the ending — the new settled state.

Characters change because of episodes, not beside them. To show a
change, find **how the character responds** to a key event early
in the story and **how they respond** to a similar event late in
the story. The contrast *is* the change — quote both responses.

### The passage — "The Tryout" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** Dev had played the drums in his bedroom for
> two years, but he had never played in front of anyone except
> his little sister. When the flyer for the school talent show
> appeared on the hallway wall, he read it four times and walked
> away without signing up.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** His sister Priya found him that evening,
> tapping rhythms on the kitchen table. "You read that flyer
> like it was a treasure map," she said. "Sign your name." Dev
> laughed, but the laugh came out thin. "Maybe next year," he
> said.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** The next day Priya taped the sign-up sheet to
> the refrigerator with his name already written on it. Dev
> stared at it through breakfast. After school he went to the
> music room, sat behind the drum kit, and played the piece he
> had practiced a hundred times — for Ms. Okafor, who listened
> with her eyes closed and nodded twice.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** On the night of the show, Dev's hands shook
> so hard he dropped a stick during the sound check. Backstage,
> Priya squeezed his shoulder. "You already played it for Ms.
> Okafor," she whispered. "The room is just bigger." Dev picked
> up the stick. He walked onto the stage, counted himself in —
> *one, two, three* — and played straight through to the last
> beat. The applause sounded, he told Priya later, like rain on
> a tin roof.

### Modeled example 1 — mapping the episodes

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Exposition (paragraph 1): Dev plays drums
alone in his bedroom; he reads the talent-show flyer and walks
away — I know who, where, and the starting situation. Rising
action: Priya pushes him (paragraph 2), then signs him up anyway
(paragraph 3), then he plays for Ms. Okafor (paragraph 3) —
tension grows with each push. Climax: paragraph 4 — Dev drops a
stick, picks it up, and walks onto the stage. *That* is the
turning point: after he steps onstage, he cannot go back to
never having performed. Falling action: he plays straight
through to the last beat. Resolution: the applause, and his
rain-on-a-tin-roof line — the new settled state. Notice the
climax is not the applause; the applause is the *result*. The
turning point is the step onto the stage."

### Modeled example 2 — character change through response

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Early response — paragraph 1: Dev 'read
it four times and walked away without signing up.' Late
response — paragraph 4: 'He walked onto the stage, counted
himself in — *one, two, three* — and played.' Same boy, same
fear, different response: walking *away* became walking
*onto the stage*. The change: Dev learned to act while afraid.
Evidence for the fear is still there in both — 'the laugh came
out thin' early, 'his hands shook' late — so the change is not
'he stopped being scared.' It is 'he stopped letting scared
decide.'"

## Guided practice

1. Which episode belongs on the mountain's peak — the climax —
   of "The Tryout"? Write it in one sentence and explain why
   nothing could go back to normal after it.
2. One learner says the falling action is "Dev plays the piece."
   Another says the falling action is "Dev plays *and* the
   applause happens." Who is closer to right? Explain using the
   diagram.
3. Fill a two-column chart: **Dev early (paragraphs 1–2)** vs.
   **Dev late (paragraph 4)**. Each column needs one quoted
   response and one sentence saying what it shows.

## Independent practice

4. Write a 5-stage episode map (one sentence per stage) for "The
   Bench at the Bus Stop" from Lesson 01.
5. Error analysis: "The climax of 'The Tryout' is when Dev plays
   the drums perfectly." Explain the error — what is wrong with
   calling a *performance* the turning point? (Hint: what did he
   do *before* the performance that changed everything?)

## Applied task

Think of a movie or book you know well. Name its climax in one
sentence, and name one way a main character responds differently
after the climax than before it. If you cannot think of one, use
"The Tryout."

## Exit check

1. List the five plot stages in order, with one phrase saying
   what each one does.
2. How do you prove a character changed — what two things do you
   compare?
3. In "The Tryout," which paragraph holds the climax, and what is
   the single turning-point action?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** episode strips — the adult pre-cuts the
  story into 5 episode cards; the learner orders them on the
  mountain.
- **Language access:** sentence frames — "The climax is when
  ___, because after that ___." "Early, Dev ___. Late, Dev ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** draw the mountain and place
  episode drawings on it instead of writing sentences.
- **Extension:** find the **sub-climax** in paragraph 3 (Dev
  plays for Ms. Okafor). Why is it a turning point on the way
  up, but not *the* climax? Write 3 sentences.

## Teacher note — misconceptions and key reference

Two errors dominate: (1) calling the *most exciting* moment the
climax instead of the *turning* moment — excitement is a feeling,
turning is a fact about the story's direction; (2) describing
character change as "he was sad, then happy," an emotion swap
with no response contrast. Insist on quoted responses, before
and after. Answer key: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson
02 section.
