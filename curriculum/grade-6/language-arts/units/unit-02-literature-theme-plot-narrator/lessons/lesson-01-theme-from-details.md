# Lesson 01 — Theme from Details: What a Story Is Really About

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell the difference between a
story's topic and its theme, find the details that build the theme,
and write an objective summary that leaves my opinions out."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and
paper.
**Standards:** RL.6.2 (determine a theme and how it is conveyed
through particular details; provide an objective summary distinct
from personal opinions).

## Launch (5 min) — one sentence or one whole story?

Read these two sentences about the same imaginary story:

- (a) "This story is about a lost dog."
- (b) "This story is about how loyalty can survive even when
  everything familiar disappears."

Sentence (a) names the **topic** — what happens, in a few words.
Sentence (b) names the **theme** — the idea the story is really
about, the thing the author wants you to carry out of the story
and into your life. A topic is a label; a theme is a message.
You can usually say the topic after the first page. The theme
takes the whole story to earn.

## Readiness check (3 min)

"Which of these is a theme and which is a topic: 'bravery,'
'courage means acting even when you're afraid'?" ("Bravery" is a
topic — one word. "Courage means acting even when you're afraid"
is a theme — a full sentence that makes a claim about life.)

## Explicit teaching

A theme is **never stated directly** in a good story. The author
shows you details — what a character does, what happens to them,
what repeats — and the theme rises out of the details like a
pattern. Your job: collect the details, then write the theme as
one complete sentence that the story *supports*.

The test of a theme sentence: could a reader argue with it? A
theme makes a claim about life — "small kindnesses can change
someone's whole day" — and the story's details are its evidence.
"Kindness is nice" is too vague to be a theme; it could be true
of any story.

**Objective summary** means retelling what happened without your
opinions, feelings, or guesses. "The man was lonely" is an
opinion — maybe true, but it is *your* reading. "The man sat alone
at the bus stop each morning" is objective — the text supports
it. Summaries stay inside what the text shows.

### The passage — "The Bench at the Bus Stop" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** Every morning at six-forty, Mr. Alder sat on
> the third bench at the bus stop on Clover Street. He always
> chose the same bench, even when the others were empty, and he
> always arrived before the first bus.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** One Tuesday the third bench was gone. In its
> place stood a sign: *BENCH REMOVED FOR REPAIR.* Mr. Alder
> stood in the rain with his newspaper over his head and waited
> for the bus. He did not sit anywhere else.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Maya, who waited for the school bus beside
> him, watched him get soaked. The next morning she brought an
> extra umbrella from her hallway closet — the blue one with the
> broken spoke. She held it over both of them without saying a
> word.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** By Friday the bench was back, its slats
> sanded smooth and smelling of fresh paint. Mr. Alder sat down
> on the third bench as if the week had never happened. But
> before the bus came, he stood, walked to Maya, and pressed a
> folded bus schedule into her hand. "In case yours tears," he
> said. It was the newest schedule, printed that morning.

### Modeled example 1 — theme from two details

*Adult thinks aloud.* "First, the topic: this is a story about a
man, a girl, and a bench at a bus stop. Now the theme — what idea
do the details build? Detail 1: Maya brings the broken blue
umbrella and holds it over Mr. Alder 'without saying a word'
(paragraph 3) — a quiet kindness to a stranger. Detail 2: Mr.
Alder gives Maya the newest bus schedule 'printed that morning'
(paragraph 4) — he repays her kindness with thoughtfulness of his
own. The pattern: kindness answered with kindness. My theme
sentence: **'Small kindnesses can be repaid in ways the giver
never expects.'** Could someone argue with it? Yes — and I have
two details to defend it. 'Kindness is good' would be too vague;
my sentence is supported by *these* details."

### Modeled example 2 — objective summary vs. opinion

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Bad summary first: 'A sad old man who had
nothing better to do sat at a bus stop, and a nice girl felt
sorry for him.' 'Sad,' 'nothing better to do,' 'felt sorry' —
all opinions I added. Objective version: 'Each morning Mr. Alder
sat on the third bench at the bus stop. When the bench was
removed for repair, Maya held her umbrella over him in the rain.
When the bench returned, Mr. Alder gave Maya a new bus
schedule.' Every sentence sits on a detail from the text. Three
sentences, no opinions — that is an objective summary."

## Guided practice

1. Topic or theme? Sort each into **Topic** or **Theme**, and fix
   any theme sentence that is too vague:
   - (a) "friendship"
   - (b) "True friendship survives arguments"
   - (c) "Being brave is good"
   - (d) "Standing up for someone can cost you something, and be
     worth it anyway"
2. In "The Bench at the Bus Stop," find **one more detail** (not
   the umbrella, not the schedule) that supports the theme "Small
   kindnesses can be repaid in ways the giver never expects."
   Quote or paraphrase it and name its paragraph.
3. Write a **two-sentence objective summary** of paragraph 2
   only. Then underline any word you added that is an opinion,
   not a detail.

## Independent practice

4. Defend or reject: a classmate says the theme of "The Bench at
   the Bus Stop" is "Public benches are important." Write
   2–3 sentences agreeing or disagreeing, citing **one detail**.
5. Write a **four-sentence objective summary** of the whole story.
   Then circle the one sentence you are least sure is
   opinion-free, and rewrite it.

## Applied task

A younger reader asks: "How is a theme different from a moral?"
Write 3–4 sentences answering them. (Hint: a moral is a rule —
"always share"; a theme is an idea a story *explores* — "sharing
can change both people." Morals tell you what to do; themes give
you something to think about.)

## Exit check

1. In one sentence: what is the difference between a topic and a
   theme?
2. Name the theme of "The Bench at the Bus Stop" in your own
   words, and give one detail that supports it.
3. Which of these is an objective summary sentence: (a) "Mr.
   Alder was a lonely old man," or (b) "Mr. Alder sat on the
   third bench every morning at six-forty"? Why?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the passage aloud while the
  learner follows; paragraph numbers are pre-marked.
- **Language access:** sentence frame for the theme — "This story
  shows that ___." Frame for evidence — "In paragraph ___, ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the theme
  sentence and summary to the adult.
- **Extension:** find a **second** defensible theme for the story
  (for example, about routine, or about noticing strangers) and
  support it with two different details.

## Teacher note — misconceptions and key reference

Learners often write the topic ("kindness") and stop, or write a
theme so vague it fits every story ("be nice"). Push for a
sentence the *details* earn. Another common slip: smuggling
opinions into summaries ("the poor man," "the kind girl").
Adults should mark exactly which word is opinion and ask for the
detail beneath it. Answer key: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 01 section.
