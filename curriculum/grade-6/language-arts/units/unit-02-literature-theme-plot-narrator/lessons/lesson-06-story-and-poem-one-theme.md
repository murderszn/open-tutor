# Lesson 06 — One Theme, Two Forms: Story and Poem Side by Side

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find the same theme in a story
and a poem, and explain how each form conveys it differently."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and
paper.
**Standards:** RL.6.9 (compare and contrast texts in different
forms or genres on similar themes or topics); RL.6.10 (read
grade-band literature with scaffolding as needed).

## Launch (5 min) — two containers, one water

Pour (real or imagined) the same water into a tall glass and a
wide bowl. Same water, different shape. A theme is the water; a
story and a poem are the containers. Today you will taste the
same theme in two different containers and describe how each
container changes the taste.

## Readiness check (3 min)

"How is a poem's shape different from a story's shape — just
looking at the page?" (A poem is broken into lines and stanzas
with white space; a story runs in paragraphs. The shape tells
you how to read it before you read a word.)

## Explicit teaching

Stories and poems convey theme with **different tools**:

- A **story** builds theme through *events*: characters act,
  things happen, consequences follow. You watch the theme
  *happen*.
- A **poem** builds theme through *images and sound*: a few
  sharp pictures, repeated words, rhythm. You *feel* the theme
  all at once.

To compare: first find the **shared theme** (one sentence both
texts support). Then name **how each form builds it** — one
story detail and one poem detail. Then say **what each form
does best**: what does the story let you see that the poem
cannot, and what does the poem let you feel that the story
cannot?

### The story — "The Lantern Path" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** The power went out on Alder Street at
> eight o'clock, and the whole block went dark at once — every
> window, every porch light, every glowing television.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** Nine-year-old June lit the camping lantern
> from the hall closet and carried it to the front steps. One
> by one, the neighbors appeared: Mrs. Okafor with her
> candles, old Mr. Abernathy with his flashlight, the twins
> from number twelve with glow sticks around their necks.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** They sat on the curb together and told
> stories until the lights buzzed back on at ten. June kept
> the lantern lit for another minute anyway, just to watch the
> small circle of faces it held.

### The poem — "Two Small Lanterns" (original)

> **Stanza 1.**
> Two small lanterns in a hallway,
> One flame borrowed, one flame lent —
> The dark is only a question
> The light is the answer sent.
>
> **Stanza 2.**
> Hold it steady, hold it higher,
> Let the neighbors find the glow.
> What is carried into darkness
> Is the brightest thing we know.

### Modeled example 1 — the shared theme

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Story details: June lights the lantern
(paragraph 2); neighbors gather with their own lights; they
stay even after the power returns (paragraph 3). Poem details:
'One flame borrowed, one flame lent' (stanza 1); 'Let the
neighbors find the glow' (stanza 2). Both texts show people
bringing light — real and figurative — to others in the dark.
Shared theme sentence: **'When people share what little light
they have, darkness loses its power over them.'** Both texts
support it; neither states it."

### Modeled example 2 — how the forms differ

*Adult thinks aloud.* "The *story* builds the theme through
*events in order*: blackout → June's lantern → neighbors
arrive → they stay. I watch kindness *spread*, episode by
episode — the plot does the work. The *poem* builds the theme
through *image and repetition*: 'borrowed'/'lent,' 'Hold it
steady, hold it higher' — no plot at all, just the lantern
image turned over and over until it means generosity. What the
story does best: it shows the theme *working on people* — the
circle of faces. What the poem does best: it makes the theme
*memorable* — 'The dark is only a question / The light is the
answer sent' is a line you carry in your head."

## Guided practice

1. Find **one more detail** in "The Lantern Path" (not the
   lantern lighting) that supports the shared theme. Quote or
   paraphrase it.
2. Find **one more detail** in "Two Small Lanterns" (not the
   borrowed/lent lines) that supports the shared theme. Quote
   it.
3. Which form — story or poem — tells you *why* June lit the
   lantern? Which form tells you *how the light feels*? Answer
   in 2 sentences, quoting one line or detail for each.

## Independent practice

4. Write a **comparison paragraph** (5–7 sentences): name the
   shared theme, give one story detail and one poem detail that
   support it, and finish with one sentence on what each form
   does best.
5. Error analysis: "The poem and the story have the same theme,
   so they are basically the same text." Explain the error in
   2–3 sentences — what does *form* change even when theme is
   shared?

## Applied task

Choose one: (a) draw the story's theme as a 3-panel comic
(June's lantern → neighbors → the circle of faces), or (b)
write a 4-line stanza that could be stanza 3 of "Two Small
Lanterns," keeping the lantern image and the theme. Label
which form you chose and write one sentence on why that form
fits your idea.

## Exit check

1. In one sentence: what is the shared theme of "The Lantern
   Path" and "Two Small Lanterns"?
2. Name one tool a *story* uses to build theme that a poem
   usually does not.
3. Name one tool a *poem* uses to build theme that a story
   usually does not.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads both texts aloud; the
  learner follows with the theme sentence stem visible.
- **Language access:** comparison frames — "Both texts show
  ___. The story shows it through ___. The poem shows it
  through ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build the
  comparison as a labeled two-column poster instead of a
  paragraph.
- **Extension:** write 3–4 sentences on whether "The Bench at
  the Bus Stop" (Lesson 01) shares this unit's lantern theme.
  Defend with one detail from each text.

## Teacher note — misconceptions and key reference

Learners often stop at "they're both about light" — a shared
*topic*, not a shared *theme*. Push for the sentence both
texts *support*. Others list differences in *length* ("the
poem is shorter") instead of differences in *how theme is
built* — redirect to tools: events vs. images. Answer key:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 06 section.
