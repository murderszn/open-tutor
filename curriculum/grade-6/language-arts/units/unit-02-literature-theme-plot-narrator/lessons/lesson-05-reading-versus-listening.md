# Lesson 05 — Reading vs. Listening: The Same Story, Two Experiences

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare reading a story myself
with listening to it read aloud, and explain what each version
reveals or hides."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; "The Tryout"
(Lesson 02); pencil and paper; an adult reader.
**Standards:** RL.6.7 (compare and contrast the experience of
reading a story, drama, or poem with listening to or viewing an
audio, video, or live version of the text).

## Launch (5 min) — hear the difference

The adult reads this sentence aloud **three different ways** —
flat, excited, and sad: "He walked onto the stage, counted
himself in — *one, two, three* — and played." The learner
listens with eyes closed. Discussion: the *words* never changed,
but the *meaning* did. A voice adds pace, emphasis, and feeling
that print cannot show — and print shows spelling, paragraph
breaks, and italics that a voice can blur past.

## Readiness check (3 min)

"When you read silently, who decides how fast to go — you or
the text? When you listen, who decides?" (Reading: you control
the pace — you can reread a hard sentence. Listening: the reader
controls the pace — you hear it once, in their rhythm.)

## Explicit teaching

Reading and listening to the same text are **different
experiences of the same words**. Compare them with three
lenses:

1. **Pace and control:** reading lets you slow down, stop, and
   reread; listening carries you forward at the reader's speed.
2. **Emphasis and sound:** a reader's voice stresses some words,
   pauses in some places, and sets a mood; on the page, *you*
   supply the voice in your head — italics, dashes, and
   paragraph breaks are the author's only stage directions.
3. **Attention:** listening highlights what the voice
   emphasizes; reading highlights what *you* notice — you might
   catch a repeated word the ear slides past, or miss a joke
   the voice makes obvious.

Neither version is "better." The question is always: *what does
each version make you notice that the other does not?*

### Modeled example — the same paragraph, two experiences

*Adult thinks aloud, after reading paragraph 4 of "The Tryout"
aloud.* "Reading it myself, I noticed the *italics* — '*one,
two, three*' — and the dash before 'and played.' The print told
me those words were special: a ritual, a breath. Listening, I
barely noticed the italics — but I *felt* the shake in 'his
hands shook so hard he dropped a stick,' because the adult
paused before 'dropped.' Reading gave me the *structure*;
listening gave me the *fear*. Same paragraph, two different
takeaways — and together they are fuller than either alone."

## The comparison activity (15 min)

1. **Read** paragraph 4 of "The Tryout" silently (2 min).
   Jot what *you* notice: one emphasized word or mark, one
   feeling.
2. **Listen** while the adult reads the same paragraph aloud
   with expression (2 min). Jot what the *voice* adds: pace,
   pauses, emphasis, mood.
3. Build a **T-chart**:

   | Reading it myself | Listening to it |
   |---|---|
   | | |
   | | |
   | | |

   Each side needs at least **two** specific observations (quote
   the words involved). Then write **one conclusion sentence**:
   "Listening changed my understanding of ___ because ___" or
   "Reading showed me ___ that listening hid because ___."

## Guided practice

1. The adult reads paragraph 2 of "The Tryout" aloud in a
   **rushed, nervous** voice. How does that performance change
   Priya's line, "Sign your name"? Write 2 sentences.
2. Which is easier to *reread* — print or audio? Give one
   example from "The Tryout" where rereading a sentence
   changed (or could change) your understanding.

## Independent practice

3. Choose any **two sentences** from "The Storm at Marrow Bay"
   (Lesson 03). Mark where you would pause and which word you
   would stress if reading aloud. Then write 2–3 sentences on
   what your performance choices reveal about the tone.
4. Error analysis: "The audio version is always better because
   you don't have to do the work of reading." Explain what this
   gets wrong about RL.6.7 — what does silent reading let a
   reader do that listening does not?

## Applied task

Plan a 60-second read-aloud of the last paragraph of "The
Tryout" for a younger listener. Mark your script: underline
words to stress, add // where you will pause, and write one
sentence at the top saying what feeling you want the listener
to get. (You do not have to perform it — the plan is the task.)

## Exit check

1. Name two things a *voice* adds that print cannot show.
2. Name two things *print* shows that a voice can blur past.
3. In the modeled example, what did reading reveal and what did
   listening reveal about paragraph 4?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads each version; the learner
  tracks with a finger and marks the T-chart with drawings or
  single words.
- **Language access:** T-chart sentence starters — "When I
  read, I noticed ___." "When I listened, the voice made ___
  feel ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner records their own
  read-aloud (any device) and compares it with the adult's.
- **Extension:** compare the *poem* "The Climb" read silently
  vs. heard aloud. Which stanza changes most in the listening,
  and why? 3–4 sentences.

## Teacher note — misconceptions and key reference

Learners often conclude "listening is easier, so it is better"
— redirect to *evidence*: the task is not to pick a winner but
to name what each version reveals. Others write vague
comparisons ("the audio was more exciting") without quoting the
words the voice changed. Insist on quoted words on both sides
of the chart. Note: the unit's "audio version" is the adult's
live read-aloud of the unit's original passage — no external
recording is required, and nothing copyrighted is reproduced.
