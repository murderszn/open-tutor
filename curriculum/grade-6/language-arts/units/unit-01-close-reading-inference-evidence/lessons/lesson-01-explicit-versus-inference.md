# Lesson 01 — Explicit vs. Inference: The Detective's Two Tools

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell what a text says outright from
what I have to figure out, and I can point to the exact words that
prove each one."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson;
[evidence-magnifier.png](../assets/evidence-magnifier.png); pencil
and paper.
**Standards:** RL.6.1 (cite textual evidence for explicit meaning and
inferences).

## Launch (7 min) — the magnifier picture

![An open book on a wooden desk with a magnifying glass hovering over the right-hand page. On the pages is a simple drawn scene: muddy dog paw prints trail from an open door on the left across the page toward an empty dog food bowl on the right. A small potted plant sits at the top of the desk.](../assets/evidence-magnifier.png)

*The evidence magnifier (AI-generated illustration): the picture shows
clues. Some things you can see directly; what happened you have to
figure out.*

**Text-only alternative (for learners who cannot see the image):**
"Picture an open book on a wooden desk. A big magnifying glass
hovers over the right-hand page. Drawn on the pages is a simple
scene: on the left page, a door stands open; a trail of muddy dog
paw prints runs from the door, across both pages, to the right
page, where an empty dog food bowl sits. Nothing is written on the
pages — only the drawing."

1. "Name **three things you can actually see** in this picture.
   (Things the drawing shows directly — no guessing.)"
2. "Now write **one inference**: what do you think happened here?
   After it, write the **evidence** — the things you saw that led
   you there."

(The adult accepts any defensible inference, e.g., "A dog came in
through the open door and ate from its bowl": evidence = open door,
paw-print trail leading to the bowl, empty bowl. The point: the
*evidence* is visible; the *story* is inferred.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

"Which of these does the picture *show*, and which do you have to
figure out: (a) the paw prints are muddy, (b) the dog was hungry?"
((a) is visible — brown smudges; (b) is an inference — the empty
bowl *suggests* hunger, but the picture never says so. Hunger is
never shown, only hinted.)

## Explicit teaching

**Explicit** means the text says it right out loud — you can point
to the words. **An inference** is a smart conclusion you draw from
the words plus what you know — the text never says it directly.
Readers make both, but they must never be confused: an inference is
only as strong as the evidence holding it up.

The citing routine you will use all unit:

1. **State** the explicit fact or the inference.
2. **Quote** the exact words (or give a close paraphrase).
3. **Point** to where they are: "paragraph 2," "the third sentence."

A **paraphrase** restates the text in your own words without
changing the meaning; a **quotation** copies the words exactly.

### The passage — "The Lantern in the Window" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** The porch light had been out for a week, and
> June did not trust the dark steps. She held the railing with both
> hands and counted — *one, two, three* — before she let her foot
> find the next tread.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** From the kitchen window, a square of yellow light
> fell across the yard. Her grandmother stood inside with her arms
> folded, watching. She did not wave or call out. She only lifted
> one finger and pointed at the steps, the way a conductor points
> with a baton.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** June laughed — a short, surprised sound — and
> climbed the rest of the way without counting. At the door her
> grandmother squeezed her shoulder once, hard, and said, "Took
> you long enough."

### Modeled example 1 — an explicit fact with citation

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Question: *What is broken at June's house?*
The text says it outright: 'The porch light had been out for a
week.' That's **explicit** — I can point to paragraph 1, first
sentence. My citation: 'The porch light had been out for a week'
(paragraph 1). No guessing needed."

### Modeled example 2 — an inference with two evidence pieces

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Question: *How does the grandmother feel
about June?* The text never says 'she loves her' — so I must
infer. Evidence 1: she 'stood inside with her arms folded,
watching' (paragraph 2) — she was waiting up, watching for June.
Evidence 2: she 'squeezed her shoulder once, hard' (paragraph 3) —
a firm, fond touch. Inference: she cares about June and was
worried. Two pieces of evidence, both quoted. If someone asks 'how
do you know?', I don't say 'I just feel it' — I show the words."

### Modeled example 3 — a weak inference, fixed

"Question: *Is the grandmother angry?* A weak answer: 'Yes, because
her arms were folded.' Watch out — folded arms *can* mean anger,
but paragraph 3 gives counter-evidence: the shoulder squeeze and
the teasing 'Took you long enough.' A stronger inference: she was
worried, not angry — and I can cite the squeeze and the joke as
evidence. **Strong readers change the inference when the evidence
points the other way.**"

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 7 tasks)

1. "Is this explicit or an inference: 'June counted the steps'?"
   (Explicit — "counted — *one, two, three*" — paragraph 1.)
   **Hint:** "Can you point to the words?"
2. "Is this explicit or an inference: 'It is nighttime'?"
   (Inference — the text says "porch light," "dark steps," "square
   of yellow light," but never the word "night." **Hint:** "Find the
   word 'night.' Can you?")
3. "Cite evidence that June was nervous on the steps."
   ("held the railing with both hands," "counted" — paragraph 1.)
   **Hint:** "What do her hands do?"
4. "What does the grandmother pointing 'the way a conductor points
   with a baton' suggest about her?" (She is giving clear, calm
   direction — like a conductor guiding musicians. **Hint:** "What
   does a conductor do?")
5. "Is 'June is afraid of the dark' explicit or inferred? Cite."
   (Inferred: "did not trust the dark steps," paragraph 1 —
   "trust" is about the steps, not the dark itself. **Hint:**
   "Does the text say the word 'afraid'?")
6. "Paraphrase this sentence without changing its meaning: 'She
   did not wave or call out.'" (Example: "She stayed silent and
   still." **Hint:** "Keep 'not waving' and 'not calling' — just
   say it your way.")
7. Error-analysis: "A reader says, 'The grandmother ignored June
   because she didn't wave.' What is wrong with that inference?"
   (It ignores counter-evidence: she was watching, pointing, then
   squeezed June's shoulder — paragraph 2–3. One detail is not
   enough when the rest of the text disagrees.)

## Independent practice (5 tasks)

1. List two more **explicit** facts from the passage, each with
   its paragraph number.
2. "June laughed — a short, surprised sound — and climbed the rest
   of the way without counting." What can you infer about how she
   feels now? Cite two pieces of evidence.
3. Is the grandmother inside or outside the house? Cite the exact
   words.
4. What inference can you draw from "Took you long enough"? Give
   one evidence phrase and explain the connection in one sentence.
5. Write one inference a careless reader might make from
   paragraph 2 alone, and explain what paragraph 3 evidence
   corrects it.

## Application

Look at the magnifier picture from the launch again. Write three
sentences: (1) one explicit observation, (2) one inference, and
(3) one sentence explaining how the evidence supports the
inference. Use the word *suggest* or *indicate* in sentence 3.

## Exit check

"Explain the difference between explicit and inference to an
alien who has never read a book. Use one example from 'The
Lantern in the Window.'"

## Supports, alternate response mode, extension

- **Support:** the adult pre-teaches *tread* (a step's flat top)
  and *baton*; the learner may underline evidence in the passage
  instead of copying quotes.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may answer guided
  practice orally and dictate the application sentences for the
  adult to scribe.
- **Extension:** find one sentence in the passage that supports
  *two different* defensible inferences, and write both with
  their evidence.

## Teacher note

Common misconceptions: (1) treating any reasonable guess as an
inference — insist on cited evidence; (2) quoting the whole
paragraph instead of the exact phrase — teach the "shortest words
that prove it" rule; (3) confusing *imply* (the text hints) with
*infer* (the reader concludes) — the text implies, the reader
infers. Answer key: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
