# Lesson 05 — Two Passages, One Method

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can use the same cite routine on a
story and an informational passage without hints, and solve new
words with context clues."
**Time:** 40 minutes × 2 sessions (Part A literary, Part B
informational) · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RL.6.1, RI.6.1; L.6.4.a; RF.6.4.

This lesson is deliberately light on teaching and heavy on doing:
the adult gives no hints unless the learner is stuck for more
than two minutes. Everything from Lessons 01–04 is now the
learner's own toolkit.

## Part A — Literary: "Rain Check" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** The sky over the field was the color of a
> bruise, and Coach Ray called the game at the top of the fifth.
> "Everybody in!" he barked. "Bats in the bag, bases stacked by
> the fence. Move!"
>
> **Paragraph 2.** Sam stood at the edge of the dugout, his glove
> still on his hand, water running off the brim of his cap. He
> had been two outs away from finishing his first complete game
> on the mound. He did not move when the others ran past him.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Coach Ray stopped beside him. For a long
> moment the only sound was rain hammering the dugout roof.
> Then the coach took Sam's cap, wrung it out over the bench,
> and set it back on his head — backwards. "Thursday," he said.
> "You get Thursday."

### Worked example — one full model, then you take over

*Adult models once:* "Inference: Sam is disappointed. Evidence 1:
'He had been two outs away from finishing his first complete
game' (paragraph 2) — he was *this close*. Evidence 2: 'He did
not move when the others ran past him' (paragraph 2) — frozen,
not hurrying. Both quoted, both pointed. Now it's your turn —
no more models."

### Independent tasks — Part A (6 tasks, no hints)

1. What can you infer about the weather? Cite two explicit
   details.
2. How does Sam feel at the end of paragraph 2? Give two
   evidence quotes and one sentence of reasoning.
3. What does Coach Ray wringing out Sam's cap and putting it
   back "backwards" suggest about him? Cite and explain.
4. What does "You get Thursday" imply? (One inference, one
   quote, one sentence.)
5. Find one sentence that could support two different
   inferences about Coach Ray. Write both inferences with the
   sentence quoted.
6. Paraphrase "The sky over the field was the color of a
   bruise" without changing its meaning.

## Part B — Informational: "Why Your Ears Pop on Airplanes" (original; facts verified 2026-10-12)

> **Paragraph 1.** As a plane climbs, the air outside gets
> thinner — there is less air pressing down from above. Your
> eardrum is a thin membrane with air on both sides: cabin air
> outside it, and trapped air in the middle ear behind it. When
> the outside pressure drops faster than the trapped air can
> adjust, the eardrum bulges outward like a tiny drumhead pulled
> tight.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** A narrow passage called the Eustachian tube
> connects the middle ear to the back of your throat. Most of
> the time it stays closed, but swallowing, yawning, or chewing
> pulls it open for a moment. When it opens, air rushes in or
> out, the pressure on both sides of the eardrum equalizes — and
> you hear the *pop*.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** That is why flight attendants hand out candy
> during descent, and why babies — whose Eustachian tubes are
> narrower — often cry on landing. The pop is not the problem
> being fixed; it is the sound of the fix happening.

### Context-clue warm-up (L.6.4.a) — do this before the tasks

Four underlined words from the passage; for each, name the clue
type (definition, example, restatement, contrast) and solve:

1. "*membrane*" (paragraph 1) — "a thin membrane" defines it:
   a thin layer of tissue. **Clue type:** definition.
2. "*equalizes*" (paragraph 2) — "the pressure on both sides
   ... equalizes" + "air rushes in or out" shows it means
   *becomes equal*. **Clue type:** example/restatement.
3. "*descent*" (paragraph 3) — contrast with "climbs" in
   paragraph 1; the plane coming down. **Clue type:** contrast.
4. "*narrower*" (paragraph 3) — "narrow passage" in paragraph 2
   restates the idea; narrower = more narrow. **Clue type:**
   restatement.

### Independent tasks — Part B (6 tasks, no hints)

1. Why do ears pop more on the way *down* than at cruising
   altitude? Cite two facts.
2. What can you infer about why chewing gum helps? Give the
   evidence chain in two sentences.
3. The author calls the pop "the sound of the fix happening."
   What does that imply about the pop itself? (One inference,
   one quote.)
4. A reader infers: "Babies cry on planes because they are
   bored." Evaluate with evidence — support, weaken, or say
   nothing?
5. What does "bulges outward like a tiny drumhead pulled tight"
   help you understand? (One sentence on the comparison's job.)
6. Paraphrase the last sentence of paragraph 2 without changing
   its meaning.

## Self-correction spotlight (RF.6.4)

While reading Part B aloud, the learner marks every
self-correction with a small check in the margin. Afterwards:
"Tell me one place you fixed yourself. What tripped you — a
word part, a skipped word, or a wrong guess? What did you do?"
The adult praises the *catch*, never penalizes the stumble.

## Application

Write a six-sentence "two-passage report": which passage was
harder to infer from — the story or the airplane article — and
why? Cite one example from each passage. Use *infer* and
*imply* correctly (one each).

## Exit check

"Without looking back: what are the three moves of the cite
routine, and which academic verb describes what *you* do with
clues?" (State → quote → point; *infer*.)

## Supports, alternate response mode, extension

- **Support:** the adult allows the evidence bank from Lesson 02
  to be reused; the learner may answer three tasks per part
  instead of six, choosing which.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may record answers
  orally; the adult scribes the application paragraph.
- **Extension:** write a seventh task for Part B — a
  trick question a careless reader would miss — and its answer.

## Teacher note

Watch for: (1) learners treating the *comparison* ("like a tiny
drumhead") as a literal fact — ask "is the eardrum *actually* a
drumhead?"; (2) over-inferring from the candy detail ("candy
fixes ears" — no, swallowing does); (3) freezing on *Eustachian*
— apply the root method (*eu-* "good" is a bonus; just sound it
out and move on). If the learner needed hints on more than two
tasks, revisit Lesson 01–02 routines before the project.
