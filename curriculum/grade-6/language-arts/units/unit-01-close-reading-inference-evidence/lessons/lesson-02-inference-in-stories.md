# Lesson 02 — Inference in Stories: Reading What Characters Don't Say

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can figure out how a character feels
and why they act the way they do, using at least two pieces of
evidence from the story."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RL.6.1.

## Launch (5 min)

"Yesterday's rule: the text implies, the reader infers. Today we
infer about people — the hardest kind, because stories rarely say
'he was lonely.' They show it. Read this and tell me one thing the
boy does, and one thing he might be feeling: *'Marcus ate his
sandwich standing up, three lockers away from the lunch tables.
He kept his eyes on his shoes.'*" (Does: stands apart, watches
his shoes. Might feel: lonely, shy, unwelcome — the adult notes
that *feeling* words are always inferences.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

"From 'The Lantern in the Window': give one inference about June
and the two evidence quotes that support it." (Reviews Lesson 01;
the adult checks that both quotes are short and exact.)

## Explicit teaching

Characters reveal themselves through **actions, words, and what
others notice** — almost never through a label. The method:

1. **Collect** two or three small details about what the
   character does or says.
2. **Ask** what feeling or motive could explain *all* of them.
3. **Test** the inference against the rest of the story — if one
   detail contradicts it, revise.

A **motive** is the reason behind an action. Readers infer motives
the way detectives infer them: nobody announces theirs.

### The passage — "The Quiet Seat" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** On Maya's first day, the lunchroom roared like
> a gym before a game. She carried her tray past table after table
> — every bench packed, every conversation already started without
> her. At the far wall, one stool stood empty beside a boy with a
> trumpet case at his feet.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** "Is this taken?" she asked. The boy looked up,
> startled, as if the question had woken him. "It's — no. I mean,
> it's free." He slid the trumpet case under the stool and pushed
> his milk carton toward the middle of the table, making room.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** They ate without talking. Twice Maya caught him
> watching her, and twice he looked quickly away, suddenly very
> interested in his apple. When the bell rang, he stood so fast his
> stool scraped the floor. "See you tomorrow?" he said, not quite
> looking at her. "Same seat?"

### Modeled example 1 — inferring a feeling (the boy)

*Adult thinks aloud.* "How does the boy feel when Maya arrives?
The text never says. Detail 1: he looks up 'startled, as if the
question had woken him' (paragraph 2) — surprise. Detail 2: he
slides the trumpet case away and pushes his milk carton 'toward
the middle of the table, making room' (paragraph 2) — welcoming.
Detail 3: he watches her, looks away, gets 'suddenly very
interested in his apple' (paragraph 3) — shy, maybe
embarrassed. Put together: he is surprised, shy, but glad she
came. Three details, one inference, all cited."

### Modeled example 2 — inferring a motive (Maya)

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Why does Maya choose *that* stool? Detail 1:
'every bench packed, every conversation already started without
her' (paragraph 1) — she has nowhere else. Detail 2: the stool
is 'at the far wall,' beside a boy who also sits alone (paragraph
1). Motive: not friendship yet — necessity. She sits where there
is space, and maybe where another solo sitter feels safer. The
text implies loneliness for both of them; I infer it from the
empty stool and the packed benches."

### Modeled example 3 — revising with new evidence

"First impression from paragraph 1: the boy might be unfriendly —
he sits alone at the far wall. But paragraph 2 counter-evidence:
he makes room, shares table space. The inference *revises*:
alone ≠ unfriendly. **New evidence outranks first impressions.**
Good readers hold inferences loosely until the story finishes."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 8 tasks)

1. "What does the lunchroom sound like? Cite." ("roared like a
   gym before a game" — paragraph 1.) **Hint:** "Find the
   comparison word *like*."
2. "Is 'Maya is new' explicit or inferred? Cite." (Explicit —
   "On Maya's first day" — paragraph 1.) **Hint:** "Check the
   very first words."
3. "What does the boy do with the trumpet case, and what does it
   suggest?" (Slides it under the stool — suggests he wants her
   to stay, is making space. **Hint:** "Would he move it if he
   wanted her to leave?")
4. "Infer how Maya feels in paragraph 1. Give two evidence
   details." (Overwhelmed/left out: "every bench packed," "every
   conversation already started without her." **Hint:** "How
   would *you* feel walking past full tables?")
5. "'See you tomorrow? ... Same seat?' What does this suggest
   about the boy's motive?" (He wants her to come back — the
   question is an invitation. **Hint:** "Why ask about tomorrow?")
6. "The boy 'not quite' looks at her when he speaks. What feeling
   does that detail support?" (Shyness — consistent with the
   apple-watching. **Hint:** "Match it with another shy detail.")
7. "Could the boy be *annoyed* she sat down? Use evidence to
   argue yes or no." (No: he makes room, pushes the milk carton,
   invites her back — three details against it. **Hint:** "List
   everything he does after she sits.")
8. Error-analysis: "A reader says, 'The boy plays the trumpet in
   the lunchroom.' What is wrong?" (The case is "at his feet"
   — paragraph 1; nobody plays it. The reader invented a fact.
   **Hint:** "Find the sentence about the trumpet case.")

## Independent practice (6 tasks)

1. Infer two feelings Maya has by the end of the story. Cite two
   evidence details for each.
2. Why does the boy stand "so fast his stool scraped the floor"
   when the bell rings? Give an inference with one evidence
   detail and one sentence of reasoning.
3. Find one sentence that supports two different defensible
   inferences about the boy. Write both, each with the sentence
   quoted.
4. Paraphrase "every conversation already started without her"
   without changing the meaning.
5. What can you infer about the boy's usual lunch routine? Cite
   two details.
6. Write a two-sentence prediction: what happens at lunch
   tomorrow? Mark which parts are inference and which rest on
   explicit details.

## Application

Choose one character. Write a paragraph (4–6 sentences) answering:
"What does this character want most in this scene?" Cite at
least two pieces of evidence and use one academic verb (*suggest*,
*indicate*, *imply*) correctly.

## Exit check

"Your friend says, 'The boy is rude.' Using the cite routine
(state → quote → point), prove your friend wrong in two
sentences."

## Supports, alternate response mode, extension

- **Support:** the adult provides an evidence bank (six short
  quotes from the passage); the learner matches each to a feeling
  word before writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may act out the scene
  with the adult and answer orally; the adult scribes the
  application paragraph.
- **Extension:** rewrite paragraph 3 from the boy's point of view
  in first person, keeping every explicit fact the same.

## Teacher note

Misconceptions: (1) "he sits alone, so he's mean" — teach that
setting details need confirming actions; (2) single-detail
inferences — require two citations before accepting; (3) *imply*
vs. *infer* confusion — drill: the *text* implies, the *reader*
infers. Answer key: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
