# Lesson 03 — Inference in Informational Text: What the Facts Point To

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can make inferences from facts,
numbers, and word choices in an informational passage, and explain
how each piece of evidence supports or weakens my inference."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RI.6.1.

## Launch (5 min)

"Stories hide feelings; informational texts hide *conclusions*.
The author gives you facts and lets you connect them. Quick drill:
'A bakery sells 400 loaves before 8 a.m. and 40 loaves after
6 p.m.' What can you infer about when people buy bread? What
evidence?" (Morning rush; evidence = the two numbers. The numbers
never say 'morning rush' — you do.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

"State the cite routine in your own words." (State → quote →
point. The adult checks the learner names all three moves.)

## Explicit teaching

In informational text, evidence comes in three flavors:

- **Stated facts** — "The dance happens on the vertical comb."
- **Numbers and measurements** — "a 3-second waggle run" means
  farther than "a 1-second run."
- **Word choices** — "deciphered the code" suggests the dance was
  mysterious before it was understood.

The inference test is stricter here than in stories: **an
inference must be checkable against the facts.** If a new fact
contradicts it, the inference loses — no matter how clever it
sounded.

### The passage — "The Dance That Points to Flowers" (original; facts verified 2026-10-12)

> **Paragraph 1.** When a honeybee finds a rich patch of flowers,
> she flies home and performs a strange dance on the vertical
> honeycomb inside the dark hive. She runs straight ahead while
> waggling her abdomen side to side, then loops back in a
> figure-eight and does it again. Other bees crowd around,
> touching her with their antennae.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** The dance is a message. The angle of the
> straight waggle run, measured against straight-up on the comb,
> matches the angle between the sun's direction and the flowers
> outside the hive. A run pointing straight up means "fly toward
> the sun"; a run tilted 60 degrees left of up means "fly 60
> degrees left of the sun." The length of the waggle run tells the
> distance: the longer the bee waggles, the farther the flowers.
> The bee measures that distance on her flight by watching the
> ground stream past beneath her.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** In the 1940s, the Austrian scientist Karl von
> Frisch deciphered this code and later won the Nobel Prize for
> his work on animal behavior. No other animal is known to
> communicate directions this way — with a symbolic dance
> performed in total darkness, read by touch.

### Modeled example 1 — inferring from numbers

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Question: *Two bees dance. Bee A's waggle
run lasts 1 second; Bee B's lasts 3 seconds. Which food is
farther?* The passage says 'the longer the bee waggles, the
farther the flowers' (paragraph 2). Inference: Bee B's flowers
are farther. Evidence: the rule (quoted) plus the two durations.
Notice the numbers do the work — I didn't need to know anything
about bees beyond the passage."

### Modeled example 2 — inferring from word choice

*Adult thinks aloud.* "Question: *What does 'deciphered this
code' (paragraph 3) suggest about the dance before von Frisch?*
'Decipher' is what you do with a secret message. The word choice
implies the dance looked meaningless — 'strange' is even the
word paragraph 1 uses — until someone cracked it. Evidence: the
verb 'deciphered' plus 'strange dance' in paragraph 1. Word
choices are evidence too."

### Modeled example 3 — an inference the facts weaken

"Question: *Do the watching bees see the dance?* A careless
inference: 'Yes — they watch it.' But paragraph 1 says the dance
happens 'inside the dark hive,' and paragraph 3 says it is
'performed in total darkness, read by touch.' The facts weaken —
actually defeat — the inference. Revised inference: they *feel*
it with their antennae ('touching her with their antennae,'
paragraph 1). **In informational text, one contradicting fact
beats three supporting guesses.**"

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 8 tasks)

1. "A waggle run points straight down the comb. Using paragraph
   2, which direction should the other bees fly?" (Directly away
   from the sun — straight up means "toward the sun," so straight
   down means the opposite. **Hint:** "If up = toward the sun,
   what is down?")
2. "Is 'bees dance in the dark' explicit or inferred? Cite."
   (Explicit — "inside the dark hive," "total darkness" —
   paragraphs 1, 3.) **Hint:** "Find the word 'dark.'"
3. "What can you infer about why the dance happens on a
   *vertical* comb? Cite." (The angle is measured "against
   straight-up" — gravity gives the reference line; on a flat
   surface there would be no "up." **Hint:** "What does the bee
   measure the angle against?")
4. "The passage says von Frisch won the Nobel Prize. What does
   that suggest about his discovery?" (It was a major scientific
   achievement. **Hint:** "What kind of work wins a Nobel Prize?")
5. "How do the follower bees learn what flower to look for?
   Infer from paragraph 1." (They touch the dancer with antennae
   and probably pick up the flower's scent from her body —
   "touching her with their antennae" is explicit; scent is a
   reasonable inference from touching. **Hint:** "What do
   antennae do?")
6. "A reader infers: 'Bees invented the dance to entertain the
   hive.' Which facts weaken this?" ("The dance is a message"
   about food direction/distance; followers then fly to the
   flowers — paragraphs 1–2. **Hint:** "What happens *after*
   the dance?")
7. "What does 'No other animal is known to communicate directions
   this way' (paragraph 3) imply about the dance?" (It is
   unique/remarkable in the animal kingdom. **Hint:** "What does
   'no other' tell you?")
8. Error-analysis: "A reader says, 'The longer the dance, the
   closer the food.' What is wrong?" (The passage states the
   opposite: "the longer the bee waggles, the farther the
   flowers" — paragraph 2. The reader reversed the relationship.
   **Hint:** "Re-read the distance sentence slowly.")

## Independent practice (6 tasks)

1. Write three inferences from paragraph 2, each supported by a
   different quoted phrase.
2. "The bee measures that distance on her flight by watching the
   ground stream past beneath her." What can you infer about how
   the bee experiences a long flight? (One paragraph, 3–4
   sentences, two citations.)
3. Is "Karl von Frisch was Austrian" explicit or inferred? Cite.
4. What does the phrase "crowd around" (paragraph 1) suggest
   about the follower bees' interest? One inference, one quote.
5. A classmate infers: "The dance works even in daylight."
   Evaluate: does the passage support, weaken, or say nothing
   about this? Explain with evidence.
6. Paraphrase the second sentence of paragraph 2 without
   changing its meaning.

## Application

You are a bee scientist. A forager's waggle run tilts 45 degrees
*right* of straight-up and lasts 2 seconds; another's tilts 45
degrees *left* and lasts 4 seconds. Write a field note (5–7
sentences): where should each group of followers fly, which food
is farther, and what evidence from the passage supports each
claim? Use *indicate* and *conclude* correctly.

## Exit check

"Explain why 'the text implies, the reader infers' matters MORE
in informational text than in stories. One example from the bee
passage."

## Supports, alternate response mode, extension

- **Support:** the adult provides a fact bank (six numbered facts
  from the passage); the learner matches each inference to its
  fact numbers before writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may draw the
  figure-eight dance, label the angle and the waggle run, and
  explain the message orally.
- **Extension:** research check — the adult and learner open one
  resource-pack link about the waggle dance and confirm or
  correct one passage fact, recording the check date.

## Teacher note

Misconceptions: (1) treating the passage's *analogies* ("like a
secret code") as literal facts; (2) reversing the
longer-run = farther relationship — have the learner chant the
rule; (3) inferring beyond the passage ("bees are smarter than
dogs") — teach the "stay inside the paragraph" boundary.
