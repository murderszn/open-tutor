# Answer Key — Unit 01: Close Reading, Inference, and Textual Evidence

**Adult only.** Worked solutions for every lesson task, practice
item, quiz question, assessment task, and project criterion.
Open responses: accept any wording with valid cited evidence and
sound reasoning; the samples below show acceptable reasoning, not
the only correct wording.

## Lesson 01

**Launch:** Visible: the open door, the paw-print trail, the empty
bowl (also: the magnifying glass, the book, the plant). Sample
inference: "A dog came in through the open door and ate from its
bowl" — evidence: open door, prints leading to the bowl, empty
bowl. Accept any defensible inference with cited visible details.

**Readiness:** (a) visible — brown smudges are drawn; (b)
inference — hunger is never shown, only suggested by the empty
bowl.

**Guided practice:**
1. Explicit — "counted — *one, two, three*" (¶1).
2. Inference — the words "porch light," "dark steps," "square of
   yellow light" imply night; the word "night" never appears.
3. "held the railing with both hands"; "counted — *one, two,
   three*" (¶1).
4. She gives clear, calm direction — a conductor guides
   musicians with precise gestures; she is guiding June
   the same way.
5. Inferred — "did not trust the dark steps" (¶1); the text
   never says "afraid."
6. Sample: "She stayed silent and still." (Keeps "not waving"
   and "not calling," drops nothing, adds nothing.)
7. It ignores counter-evidence: she was "watching" (¶2),
   pointed guidance (¶2), "squeezed her shoulder once, hard"
   (¶3), and the teasing "Took you long enough" (¶3). One
   detail cannot stand against four.

**Independent practice:**
1. Any two, e.g.: "The porch light had been out for a week"
   (¶1); "Her grandmother stood inside with her arms folded"
   (¶2); "'Took you long enough'" (¶3).
2. Inference: relief/comfort — she is no longer afraid. Evidence:
   "laughed — a short, surprised sound"; "climbed the rest of
   the way without counting" (counting was her fear routine).
3. Inside — "From the kitchen window, a square of yellow light
   fell across the yard" (¶2); she watches from within.
4. Inference: teasing affection — she was waiting and is glad
   June made it. Evidence: "squeezed her shoulder once, hard"
   (¶3); the sentence is a joke, not a scolding.
5. Careless: "she is angry/cold" (folded arms, no wave — ¶2).
   Correction: ¶3's squeeze and teasing line show worry and
   fondness, not anger.

**Application (sample):** (1) "I can see muddy paw prints
crossing the page." (2) "I infer a dog walked from the open
door to its bowl." (3) "The prints *indicate* this because they
form a trail starting at the door and ending at the empty bowl."

**Exit (sample):** "Explicit is what the book says out loud —
'The porch light had been out for a week' (paragraph 1). An
inference is what I figure out — the grandmother was worried,
because she 'squeezed her shoulder once, hard' (paragraph 3)."

## Lesson 02

**Launch:** Does: stands three lockers away, eyes on shoes.
Feeling (inference): lonely/shy/unwelcome.

**Readiness:** Sample: "June was nervous on the steps" —
evidence: "held the railing with both hands," "counted — *one,
two, three*" (¶1).

**Guided practice:**
1. "roared like a gym before a game" (¶1).
2. Explicit — "On Maya's first day" (¶1).
3. He slides it under the stool — he wants her to stay; he is
   making space, which is welcoming.
4. Overwhelmed/left out: "every bench packed"; "every
   conversation already started without her" (¶1).
5. He wants her to come back tomorrow — the question is an
   invitation.
6. Shyness — consistent with watching her then getting
   "suddenly very interested in his apple" (¶3).
7. No: he makes room, pushes the milk carton to share space,
   and invites her back — three welcoming actions.
8. Nobody plays it — the case sits "at his feet" (¶1); the
   reader invented the playing.

**Independent practice:**
1. Sample: welcomed/hopeful — "See you tomorrow?"; "Same seat?"
   (¶3). Less alone — someone chose to sit with *him*.
2. Inference: eager, nervous energy — he jumps up the instant
   he can. Evidence: "stood so fast his stool scraped the
   floor" (¶3); reasoning: the speed shows he was waiting for
   the moment, not dreading it.
3. Sample sentence: "twice he looked quickly away, suddenly
   very interested in his apple" — (a) he is shy; (b) he is
   being polite, trying not to stare.
4. Sample: "All the talking was already going on, and none of
   it included her."
5. He usually sits alone: "one stool stood empty beside a boy"
   (¶1) — the far-wall solo seat is his spot; "as if the
   question had woken him" (¶2) suggests he is not used to
   company.
6. Sample: "Inference: they become lunch friends — 'Same seat?'
   (¶3). Explicit basis: he invited her; she has no other seat
   ('every bench packed,' ¶1)."

**Application (sample):** "The boy wants connection most. He
'slid the trumpet case under the stool and pushed his milk
carton toward the middle of the table, making room' (paragraph
2), which *suggests* he is glad she came, and he asks 'Same
seat?' (paragraph 3), which *indicates* he wants her back."

**Exit (sample):** "He 'pushed his milk carton toward the
middle of the table, making room' (paragraph 2) — rude people
do not share table space. Then he asks 'See you tomorrow?'
(paragraph 3) — rude people do not invite you back."

## Lesson 03

**Launch:** Morning rush — evidence: "400 loaves before 8 a.m."
vs. "40 loaves after 6 p.m."

**Guided practice:**
1. Directly away from the sun — straight up means "toward the
   sun," so straight down means the opposite (¶2).
2. Explicit — "inside the dark hive" (¶1); "total darkness"
   (¶3).
3. The angle is measured "against straight-up" (¶2) — gravity
   gives the reference line; a flat surface has no "up."
4. It was a major scientific achievement (Nobel-level).
5. They touch the dancer with their antennae (explicit, ¶1);
   they likely pick up the flower's scent from her body
   (reasonable inference from touching).
6. Weakened/defeated: "The dance is a message" about food
   direction and distance, and followers then fly to the
   flowers (¶¶1–2) — it has a job, not an audience.
7. It is unique in the animal kingdom — remarkable.
8. Reversed: the passage states "the longer the bee waggles,
   the farther the flowers" (¶2).

**Independent practice:**
1. Any three, e.g.: 60°-left run → food 60° left of the sun
   ("a run tilted 60 degrees left of up means 'fly 60 degrees
   left of the sun'"); 3-second run → farther than 1-second
   ("the longer the bee waggles, the farther the flowers");
   followers can't see the dance ("performed in total
   darkness, read by touch").
2. Sample: "She experiences the flight as ground rushing past —
   'watching the ground stream past beneath her' (paragraph 2).
   A longer flight means more streaming ground, which her
   brain converts into a longer waggle run. I *infer* distance
   feels like duration to a bee."
3. Explicit — "the Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch" (¶3).
4. Eager interest — bees "crowd around" (¶1); crowding shows
   they want the message.
5. The passage says nothing about daylight — it describes only
   the dark-hive dance; no evidence either way.
6. Sample: "The tilt of the straight part of the dance, compared
   to straight-up on the comb, equals the angle between where
   the sun is and where the flowers are."

**Application (sample field note):** "Bee 1's run tilts 45°
right of up for 2 seconds; Bee 2's tilts 45° left for 4
seconds. I *conclude* group 1 should fly 45° right of the sun
and group 2 should fly 45° left of the sun — the run's angle
'against straight-up on the comb, matches the angle between
the sun's direction and the flowers' (paragraph 2). Bee 2's
food is farther: 'the longer the bee waggles, the farther the
flowers' (paragraph 2). The angle *indicates* direction; the
duration *indicates* distance."

**Exit (sample):** "In a story, a wrong inference just
misunderstands a character. In informational text, a wrong
inference misstates how the world works — and one fact can
kill it. I first inferred the follower bees *watch* the dance,
but 'performed in total darkness, read by touch' (paragraph 3)
defeated that."

## Lesson 04

**Launch:** *port-* = "carry" — transport, portable, import,
export all involve carrying things somewhere.

**Readiness:** *pre-* ("before") + *view* ("look at") = "look
at before."

**Guided practice:**
1. *trans-* ("across") + *port* ("carry") = "carry across."
2. *re-* ("back") + *ject* ("throw") + *-ed* = "thrown back."
3. *auto-* ("self") + *graph* ("write") = "self-writing" — a
   signature.
4. *tract* ("pull") — "the act of pulling toward."
5. Sample script: "Stop — 'in-SPECKT' isn't a word I know.
   Reread slowly... re-split: *in-* + *spect* ('look') — oh,
   in-SPECT, 'look into.' That fits 'the engine.' Continue."
6. *indicate* — the prints do the pointing.
7. *conclude* — decided after weighing evidence.
8. Wrong split: *port-* is the root ("carry"); *por* + *table*
   fails the meaning test — "a table for pouring" is nonsense.

**Independent practice:**
1. *prediction*: pre- ("before") + dict ("say") + -ion ("act
   of") = "the act of saying before." *spectator*: spect
   ("look, see") + -ator ("one who") = "one who looks."
   *instructor*: in- ("into") + struct ("build") + -or ("one
   who") = "one who builds (knowledge) into." *export*: ex-
   ("out") + port ("carry") = "carry out."
2. Sample: "The detective will *inspect* the footprints
   closely." / "The caterpillar will *transform* into a
   butterfly."
3. Sample: "The empty bowl *implies* the dog was hungry."
   / "I *infer* the boy is shy from the apple-watching."
4. Sample script: "Stop — that didn't make sense. Reread:
   'un-pre-dict-able.' Re-split: *un-* ('not') + *pre-*
   ('before') + *dict* ('say') + *-able* ('can be') = 'cannot
   be said before.' Fix: un-pre-DICT-able. Reread the
   sentence to confirm."
5. *suggests* — points toward; hunger isn't proven. (Accept
   *implies* with reasoning: the text hints at it.)
6. Any one: *instruct* (in- + struct = "build into," teach);
   *destruction*; *construct*.

**Application (sample):** "*manu-* ('hand') + *script*
('write') = 'written by hand.' Check: 'The museum displays the
author's original manuscript.' A handwritten original — fits."

**Exit (sample):** "1. Find the root. 2. Name its meaning. 3.
Assemble. 4. Check in a sentence. *Television*: *tele-*
('far') + *vision* ('see') = 'see far.' The check step catches
me — if 'see far' doesn't fit the sentence, I re-split."

## Lesson 05

**Part A:**
1. Storm/rain coming or arriving: "The sky over the field was
   the color of a bruise" (¶1); "rain hammering the dugout
   roof" (¶3).
2. Disappointed: "He had been two outs away from finishing his
   first complete game" (¶2); "He did not move when the others
   ran past him" (¶2). Reasoning: being *this close* to a
   first, then frozen while others move, reads as crushed.
3. Gruff kindness: wringing out the cap is caretaking; putting
   it back "backwards" is playful. The actions *suggest* he
   understands and is comforting Sam without a speech.
4. "You get Thursday" *implies* Sam will pitch Thursday — a
   second chance at the complete game.
5. Sample: "'Everybody in!' he barked" — (a) he is angry; (b)
   he is urgent, managing safety in a storm. Both defensible;
   (b) fits the bruise-colored sky better.
6. Sample: "The sky above the field was dark purple-gray,
   like a bruise."

**Part B:**
1. Descent changes pressure fastest: candy is handed out
   "during descent" (¶3); the tube must open to let "air rush
   in or out" and "equalize" (¶2) — at cruise the pressure is
   steady, so nothing needs fixing.
2. Chewing makes you swallow; "swallowing, yawning, or chewing
   pulls it [the Eustachian tube] open for a moment" (¶2), and
   the open tube equalizes the pressure.
3. The pop is not damage or a problem — it is the audible sign
   that equalization succeeded.
4. Weakened: ¶3 attributes crying to "narrower" Eustachian
   tubes that can't equalize pressure — discomfort, not
   boredom.
5. The comparison's job: it makes the invisible visible — you
   can picture the eardrum stretching tight like a drumhead.
6. Sample: "When the tube opens, air moves either way until
   the pressure matches on both sides of the eardrum — and
   that's the pop you hear."

**Application (sample):** "The story was harder: feelings are
never stated — I *inferred* Sam was crushed from 'He did not
move when the others ran past him' (paragraph 2). The airplane
article was easier because facts do the work — I *inferred*
candy helps from 'swallowing, yawning, or chewing pulls it
open' (paragraph 2). The text *implies*; I *infer*."

**Exit:** State → quote → point (paragraph number). The reader
*infers*.

## Project

**Intended claim:** Theo took the sunflower seeds — he
confesses it directly in paragraph 4, so this is an evidence-
assembly case, not a whodunit.

**Strong evidence (any 3+):** the zigzag-tread match —
"a muddy footprint — small, with a zigzag tread" (¶2) and "a
muddy sneaker with a zigzag tread" (¶4); "three empty
sunflower-seed packets" in Theo's bag (¶4); "nobody had signed
for them" (¶1) with the rule "Take only what you signed for"
(¶1); the shed door "unlocked" though "she always locked it on
Friday nights" (¶2); Theo's own words: "I took the sunflower
seeds" (¶4).

**Strong ruled-out alternatives:** the marigold spill — messy
but the marigolds are still there ("Sunflower seeds were gone;
marigold seeds were merely messy," ¶3), so it is a red herring;
a second unknown visitor — no evidence supports one (single
footprint trail, ¶2). A report that treats the confession as
the *only* evidence scores 1 on Evidence — the rubric demands
assembled textual proof, not just the quote "I took them."

**Academic verbs:** e.g., "The footprint *indicates* Theo was
in the shed"; "I *conclude* he acted on impulse, not malice";
"The marigold spill *suggests* nothing about the theft." Text
implies / reader infers must not be swapped.

**Oral summary:** claim + 2 quotes + alternative, in formal
complete sentences.
