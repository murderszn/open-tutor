# Answer Key — Unit 03 (Adult Only)

**Do not show learners this file.** Every lesson item, practice
item, quiz question, assessment task, and project criterion is
solved or guided below. Where an answer admits defensible
variation, the key says so — accept any answer that meets the
stated test, not only the wording here.

## Lesson 01 — Central idea and objective summary

**Guided practice**

1. Central idea (acceptable wording): "Over time, Riverbend's
   river changed from a neglected waterway into a shared community
   project because residents and the town kept working on it."
2. Paragraph 2 detail: the Saturday crew grew from a dozen people
   to more than eighty volunteers. Paragraph 4 detail: children
   walk the bank path to school / anglers fish from the
   footbridge at dawn / the crew still meets to plant, prune, and
   count herons. (Any one paragraph-4 detail with its paragraph
   earns the point.)
3. (a) is objective: it reports a number the text gives.
   (b) adds an opinion: "most wonderful" is a judgment the text
   never makes.
4. Objective rewrite (acceptable): "Riverbend's river was long
   treated as a back lot. Neighbors began Saturday cleanups, and
   the town council added bins, plantings, and a footbridge.
   Today the river is part of daily life." ("Beautiful" and
   "smart" are cut; nothing new is added.)

**Independent practice**

1. Topic: Riverbend's river. Central idea vs. topic: the topic
   names what the text is about; the central idea states what the
   text says about the topic (the change, and its cause).
2. That sentence rules out a one-person explanation and points to
   the text's real cause: steady shared work. It sharpens the
   central idea from "the river changed" to "the river changed
   because many people kept working."
3. Objective summary (acceptable, 3 sentences): "Riverbend's river
   was long treated as a back lot. Neighbors began Saturday
   cleanups, the town council added bins, plantings, and a
   footbridge, and the river became part of daily life. The text
   credits steady work over time, by residents and the town, for
   the change." (Any summary with the same three moves, no
   opinions, and no new facts earns full credit.)
4. No — incomplete. The footbridge is one council action in
   paragraph 3; the central idea also needs the neighbors'
   cleanups, the passage of time, and the cause (steady work).
5. (c) is off-topic: rivers in other countries are not discussed
   in this passage and do not support its central idea. (a) is a
   context detail; (b) is daily-life evidence from paragraph 4.
6. Council details: paid for trash bins along the banks; planted
   reeds and willows where banks had worn away; built a wooden
   footbridge. (Any two.) They support the central idea by
   showing the town's share of the steady work, not just the
   neighbors'.

**Applied task (model):** "Over time, Riverbend's river changed
from a neglected waterway into a shared community project because
residents and the town kept working on it. In paragraph 2, the
Saturday crew grew from a dozen people to more than eighty
volunteers. In paragraph 3, the council added bins, plantings,
and a footbridge. The text credits steady work over time, by
residents and the town, for the change." (Accept any briefing
with the same four-sentence structure and no opinions.)

**Exit check:** a central idea is what the text itself says as
its main point; an opinion is what the reader thinks about the
text or topic, whether or not the text says it.

## Lesson 02 — Key individual and structure

**Guided practice**

1. Introducing phrase (acceptable): "Mara Ellison moved to
   Riverbend as a young teacher" — it gives her name, newcomer
   status, and occupation.
2. Paragraph 2 illustrates her, with the anecdote of one spring
   Saturday: three hours pulling debris, hauling a rusted bicycle
   frame with two teenagers, mud on her shoes.
3. Elaborating details (any two): became the crew's record keeper;
   counted volunteers each Saturday; noted planted bank sections;
   wrote a yearly note for the council. Result: her notes helped
   the council decide where to place bins and which banks to
   plant next.
4. Without paragraph 4, the passage would lose its reflection:
   Ellison's habit of listing others first and calling herself
   "one of the crew," and the closing idea that the project grew
   like a river from many small streams. The facts would remain;
   the meaning would not.

**Independent practice**

1. Table: Introduce — paragraph 1 — "moved to Riverbend as a
   young teacher," barely noticed the river. Illustrate —
   paragraph 2 — Saturday debris cleanup, bicycle frame, muddy
   shoes. Elaborate — paragraph 3 — record keeper, counts, yearly
   notes, council decisions informed. (Paragraph 4 may be listed
   as reflection; accept it as a fourth row.)
2. It is a detail, not decoration: mud on her shoes shows she
   actually worked, marking her change from passer-by to
   participant in one concrete image.
3. Her yearly notes gave the council the counts and planting
   records it used to place bins and choose banks for planting
   (paragraph 3). Without her notes, the council's decisions
   would lack that record.
4. Paragraph 1 presents a newcomer who barely notices the river;
   paragraph 4 presents a crew member who credits others first
   and sees herself as one small stream among many. The change
   is from outsider to participant who refuses sole credit.
5. Disagree (expected): paragraph 4 adds no new job, but it adds
   the passage's reflection and its connection to the central
   idea (shared work, not one person). Accept a reasoned "agree"
   only if the learner distinguishes "no new facts" from "no new
   meaning" — the lesson's point is that meaning counts.
6. Acceptable: "The passage moves from who Ellison was (a
   newcomer teacher), through one morning that changed her, to
   years of record keeping that shaped council decisions, ending
   with her refusal of sole credit. That sequence shows the
   central idea: the river changed through many people's steady
   work, not one hero."

**Applied task:** accept any four-sentence sequence with the
labels in the right order (introduce → illustrate → elaborate)
and a concrete example in the middle. The person may be real,
fictional, or the Saturday crew as a group.

**Exit check:** introducing names a person and a starting role;
elaborating adds detail, explanation, or results that show why
the person matters to the text's idea.

## Lesson 03 — Author purpose and word choice

**Guided practice**

1. Text A: inform — signals: dates, times, meeting place,
   practical details ("Gloves and bags are provided"). Text B:
   persuade — signals: "cherished," direct address ("Join the
   Saturday crew"), urgency ("Do not let another season slip
   away").
2. Positive connotation: "cherished." Negative connotation:
   "chokes" (also accept "sour" if the learner cites Text B's
   surrounding language; the key word in Text B is "chokes").
3. Watershed (own words): the area of land whose water drains
   into a particular river.
4. Informing rewrite: the original gives no date, time, or place,
   so an honest informing version must say so, e.g.:
   "A river cleanup work day will be held. (Date, time, and
   meeting place are not stated in the original sentence and
   must not be invented.)" Deduct if the learner invents a date
   or time — that is the trap in this item.

**Independent practice**

1. "All ages welcome with an adult" is practical information, not
   a persuasion signal: it tells readers who may come and under
   what condition, without praise, blame, or urgency.
2. The omission fits persuasion: a persuading letter aims to move
   the reader to act and feel, so it spends words on feeling and
   urgency instead of logistics; an informing notice would lead
   with date, time, and place. (Accept: the letter relies on the
   reader already knowing, or seeking out, the practical details.)
3. (a) cherished — connotative (warm, valued feeling).
   (b) runoff — technical (water flowing over land into a river).
   (c) chokes — connotative (blocked, unhealthy feeling).
   (d) riparian — technical (of riverbank plants/land).
   (e) beloved — connotative (loved, valued feeling).
   (f) debris — technical (scattered waste, cleanup vocabulary).
4. Informing: it states a measurable fact with no command,
   praise, blame, or urgency.
5. Acceptable: an author can persuade through word choice alone
   by loading descriptions with feeling — e.g., calling a plan
   "a precious chance" versus "a proposal" — so the reader feels
   the author's judgment without being told what to do.
6. Scolding/urging (accept either word; not neutral, not purely
   trusting): the mayor implies residents have broken promises
   ("every summer we promise to do better") and commands,
   "Do not let another season slip away."

**Applied task (model):** Notice 1 (inform): "The school garden
day will be held Saturday, 9 a.m. to noon, at the school garden.
Tools are provided. Wear sturdy shoes." Notice 2 (persuade):
"Our beloved school garden needs you this Saturday — join us
and help it thrive before the season ends! (Same date, time,
and place as Notice 1.)" Check: Notice 2 has a positive
connotation word ("beloved"), direct address ("needs you"),
and urgency ("before the season ends"), with no new facts.

**Exit check:** any one valid signal: a command, a praise/blame
word, direct address ("you"), urgency language, or one-sided
selection of facts.

## Lesson 04 — Trace and evaluate an argument

**Guided practice**

1. Overall claim: Riverbend should build a riverwalk along the
   east bank this year.
2. "Pay for itself" trace: evidence = a state grant letter quoted
   in the town office stating the state will pay half the
   construction cost, up to a fixed maximum. Left out: the total
   cost, the maximum amount, and yearly maintenance cost.
   (Without those, "pay for itself" cannot be judged.)
3. "Double in value": unsupported — no study, sale record, or
   expert is cited; it is a prediction stated as fact.
4. There is no clearly irrelevant evidence item in the article:
   each paragraph's evidence bears on its own claim. The
   article's weaknesses are scope (three streets ≠ every family),
   sufficiency (one day; missing cost figures), and absence of
   evidence (value prediction) — not irrelevance. (Award this
   item for the "none, because..." answer; do not award an
   invented irrelevance.)

**Independent practice**

1. Claim table:
   - Popularity — 300 walkers on one Saturday in May on the
     footbridge — relevant: yes — sufficient: no (one day, one
     season, a different structure) — **partly supported**.
   - Every family wants it — neighbors on three streets near the
     river said they would use it — relevant: yes, but narrow —
     sufficient: no (three streets ≠ every family; "would use"
     ≠ "want built") — **unsupported**.
   - Pay for itself — grant letter: state pays half, up to a
     maximum — relevant: yes — sufficient: no (total cost,
     maximum, maintenance unknown) — **partly supported**.
   - Homes will double in value — no study, sale record, or
     expert cited — relevant: n/a — sufficient: no —
     **unsupported**.
2. Strongest: the grant claim — it cites a document (the grant
   letter) rather than a single observation or a small sample.
   It is still only partly supported (see table). (Accept the
   popularity claim as strongest only if the learner argues
   from the counted observation and acknowledges the one-day
   limit; do not accept "every family" or "double in value.")
3. Weakest: "double in value" (no evidence at all). One piece
   that would most strengthen it: a study or sale records
   comparing home values near similar completed riverwalks with
   values elsewhere (accept any cited study, appraisal, or
   expert evidence on values).
4. Agreement is a vote, not an evaluation: evaluation judges the
   evidence's relevance and sufficiency for each claim, which
   can come out against a claim the reader likes (or for a
   claim the reader dislikes).
5. Supported rewrite (acceptable): "Neighbors on three streets
   near the river who were asked said they would use a
   riverwalk." (The claim is shrunk to fit the evidence; do not
   accept a rewrite that keeps "every family.")
6. Accept any genuine counterclaim the article omits, e.g.:
   construction cost and disruption, yearly maintenance, or
   effects on wildlife — with the reason that answering it would
   show the author weighed costs, not only benefits.

**Applied task (model):** "The article claims Riverbend should
build a riverwalk this year. Its popularity claim is partly
supported: one Saturday's 300 footbridge walkers suggest interest
but prove nothing about year-round use. Its every-family claim is
unsupported: three streets are not every family. Its pay-for-itself
claim is partly supported: a grant letter promises half the cost,
but the total, the maximum, and maintenance are missing. Overall,
the argument is mixed: it shows real interest and partial funding,
but it overstates what its evidence proves."

**Exit check:** partly supported = some relevant evidence, but
thin, narrow, or incomplete; unsupported = no evidence, or
evidence that does not bear on the claim, or a prediction stated
as fact with nothing behind it.

## Lesson 05 — Compare accounts; integrate media

**Guided practice**

1. Text A includes a feeling/moment Text B leaves out (e.g., the
   smell after the storm, the laughter over the tire, deciding to
   come back). Text B includes a role/result Text A leaves out
   (e.g., record keeper, yearly notes, council decisions).
2. Feelings: Text A — e.g., "I thought about going home" / "I
   stayed." Sequence: Text B — e.g., "joined ... and later served
   as its record keeper."
3. No contradiction: the texts select different slices of the
   same effort; a single morning (Text A) fits inside a sequence
   of years (Text B), and neither denies the other's facts.
4. Image adds (any one): volunteers still working now; a heron
   in the reeds; a child planting; the footbridge in use.
   Texts state what the image cannot show (any one): names
   (Ellison), dates/sequence, reasons, or numbers.

**Independent practice**

1. Acceptable: "Text A is written by a participant, so it
   remembers smells, mud, and a decision; Text B is written by
   an observer, so it records roles and results instead of
   feelings. Position, not honesty, explains the difference."
2. No: "more personal" means closer to feeling, not closer to
   fact. A memoir can misremember; a biography can check
   records. Reliability depends on evidence and checking, not
   on warmth.
3. Accept any two-sentence paragraph that draws on all three
   sources without adding unsupported facts, e.g.: "Ellison
   joined the Saturday crew and later kept its records, which
   helped the council place bins and plantings (Text B), and
   she remembers one storm morning's mud, laughter, and decision
   to return (Text A). Today the riverbank still shows that
   work continuing, with volunteers, a heron in the reeds, and
   a child planting (image)."
4. A careful reader compares sources, weighs which is closer to
   the event or better documented, and holds the date as
   uncertain until another source confirms it — rather than
   picking the version they prefer.
5. What the crew did: Text B (sequence, roles, decisions, with
   records behind them). What it felt like: Text A (first-person
   sensations and decision). Accept a swapped answer only with
   a reasoned defense (e.g., Text A for a specific morning's
   actions) — the default split above is the lesson's point.
6. Blank papers are a strength because any words on the board
   would invent a date, name, notice, or claim the lesson never
   teaches; blank paper shows "a place for notices" without
   fabricating content.

**Applied task (model):** "Both accounts agree Ellison joined
the Saturday crew and that her work mattered to the river
project. Text A emphasizes a single morning's feelings ('I
stayed'), while Text B emphasizes a sequence of roles ('later
served as its record keeper'). The image adds the present-day
scene — volunteers, a heron, a seedling — but it cannot prove
who organized any cleanup, because it is an illustration that
names no one."

**Exit check:** because authors select, emphasize, and voice
events differently according to their position, purpose, and
audience — not necessarily because one of them is lying.

## Practice

**A. Central-idea sorting:** 1 C (the full central idea);
2 S (context detail); 3 S (crew growth supports the change);
4 O (world rivers are not this passage's topic); 5 S (council
action supports the change); 6 S (herons show the result);
7 C (acceptable central-idea wording: change + cause);
8 S (daily-life evidence); 9 O (a judgment about visiting, not
in the passage); 10 S (it sharpens the central idea's cause;
accept C only if the learner argues it states the cause — the
distinction is discussed, not penalized, in reteach).

**B. Word meanings:** 1 cherished — connotative (warm, valued);
2 runoff — technical (water flowing over land into a river);
3 chokes — connotative (blocked, unhealthy); 4 riparian —
technical (riverbank plants/land); 5 pristine — connotative
(untouched, pure feeling); 6 debris — technical (cleanup
vocabulary: scattered waste); 7 watershed — technical (land
draining into a river); 8 beloved — connotative (loved, valued);
9 culvert — technical (drainage structure); 10 neglected —
connotative (uncared-for judgment).

**C. Relevance check (for "The riverwalk will be popular"):**
1 relevant — walker counts directly bear on popularity.
2 irrelevant — river length says nothing about walker numbers.
3 relevant — neighbors saying they would use it bears on
popularity (small sample affects sufficiency, not relevance).
4 irrelevant — funding says nothing about popularity.
5 irrelevant — a bench design choice is not evidence of use.
6 relevant (weak) — a large volunteer crew suggests community
interest, though volunteers ≠ walkers.
7 irrelevant — home values say nothing about walker numbers.
8 relevant — the bridge's narrowness and lack of benches
contextualize the count (interest despite poor facilities).

**D. Two-account drill:** 1 Text A first person ("I"); Text B
third person ("she"). 2 Any feeling from Text A: the smell, the
thought of going home, the laughter, deciding to return.
3 Any role from Text B: record keeper; counting volunteers;
writing yearly notes. 4 No — the texts select differently but
do not contradict each other on any shared fact. 5 Accept any
sentence both texts support, e.g.: "Ellison joined the Saturday
crew and her work mattered to the river project."

**E. Paragraph-function drill:** 1 Introduce — paragraph 1;
illustrate — paragraph 2; elaborate — paragraph 3; reflect —
paragraph 4. 2 Without paragraph 3, a reader would no longer
know that her notes helped the council place bins and choose
planting banks. 3 Acceptable: "Paragraph 4 adds Ellison's
character and the passage's meaning — she credits others first
and calls herself 'one of the crew' — which paragraphs 1–3 do
not state."

## Formative quiz (20 points)

1. Central idea (acceptable): "The bank path story shows the
   river project's pattern: a good start, a setback, a recorded
   observation, and a fix that lasts because someone wrote it
   down." (2 points: pattern + recording/cause.)
2. Objective summary (acceptable, 2 sentences): "Volunteers
   marked a bank path with wooden posts to protect newly planted
   reeds. After rain washed out two posts and walkers cut across
   the reeds, Ellison's note recommended stone markers, the
   council followed it, and the path has held since." (Deduct
   1 point per opinion or new fact.)
3. Ellison's role: she recorded the washed-out posts in her
   yearly note and recommended stone markers (paragraph 3);
   result: the council followed her recommendation the next
   spring and the path has held since.
4. Paragraph 4 adds the general pattern and meaning: good start,
   setback, recorded observation, lasting fix — the lesson the
   story illustrates. Paragraphs 1–3 give the events; paragraph
   4 states what they show.
5. Mainly explain (accept inform): the passage lays out a
   sequence and its pattern rather than commanding action or
   praising/blaming. Signal: the closing pattern statement
   ("shows the same pattern as ..."), or the neutral, sequential
   narration. (Do not accept "persuade": there is no command,
   urgency, or one-sided selection.)
6. "Tall" is plain descriptive here: it reports an observable
   height, not a feeling-loaded judgment and not a subject's
   technical term.
7. Supported: wooden posts washed out in rain, while the stone
   markers' path "has held since" — a before/after comparison
   inside the passage supports stone over wood for this path.
8. No: after the posts washed out, walkers "began cutting across
   the reeds again," so not every walker followed the path.
9. Expected difference: the memoir would report personal
   feelings (loving the markers, feeling permanence) while the
   passage reports sequence and decisions — because the memoirist
   is a participant and this passage's author is an observer.
10. (a) is an objective summary sentence: it reports what the
    passage states ("has held since"). (b) is an opinion:
    "smartest thing the town ever did" is a superlative judgment
    the passage never makes.

## Unit assessment (25 points)

1. Central idea (acceptable): "Riverbend is considering turning
   an unused mill pond into a learning pond, a proposal that
   promises learning value but leaves cost, maintenance, and
   safety questions unanswered."
2. Objective summary (acceptable, 3 sentences): "An old mill
   pond in Riverbend sits unused with a rotted dock. Residents
   led by retired science teacher Ana Ruiz propose a learning
   pond where school groups test water, count plants, and record
   findings, citing walking distance, a builders' offer, and
   volunteer training — but no total cost, maintenance plan, or
   safety plan. The council is considering the proposal and has
   not yet decided." (Deduct 1 point per opinion or new fact.)
3. Introduce (paragraph 2): retired science teacher named Ana
   Ruiz, author of the proposal. Illustrate (paragraph 2): her
   idea that students learn more from a pond they can visit
   than from one they only read about. Elaborate (paragraph 3):
   three reasons (walking distance, builders' materials-only
   dock offer, her volunteer-training offer) plus the gaps (no
   total cost, maintenance, or safety plan).
4. Paragraph 5 adds reflection and framing: whatever the decision,
   the pond has given the town a question worth arguing about
   carefully (children vs. budget). Paragraphs 1–4 give facts
   and status; paragraph 5 states what the decision means.
5. "Visit" is plain descriptive: an ordinary verb for going to
   see a place, not a technical term and not feeling-loaded.
   (Contrast with Lesson 03's "cherished" — connotative.)
6. Inform: paragraph 4 reports the council's consideration
   neutrally, giving both worries (safety, cost) and support
   (learning value), and states "No decision has been made yet."
   No command, praise, or urgency appears.
7. "Cheap to build": **partly supported** (3 points: verdict 1,
   reason 2). The builders' materials-only offer and Ruiz's
   volunteer training point toward lower cost, but the proposal
   gives no total cost, no maintenance cost, and no safety cost
   — so cheapness as a whole is not established.
8. Expected difference: the memoir would give Ruiz's personal
   motive and feelings ("I missed teaching") while this passage
   gives the proposal's reasons and the council's status —
   because the memoirist is a participant remembering, and this
   passage's author is an observer reporting.
9. Image adds (any one): a present-day Riverbend riverbank scene
   — volunteers working, a heron, a child planting — showing the
   town's river work continuing. Image cannot show (any one):
   the mill pond, the council's decision (or lack of one), Ruiz,
   costs, or safety plans — it depicts a different place and
   names/dates nothing.
10. Evaluation paragraph (6 points: 1 for stating the proposal,
    1 for each reason evaluated with a verdict and a reason (3),
    1 for an overall readiness judgment tied to the presented
    evidence, 1 for keeping the evaluation separate from a
    personal for/against position).
    Model: "Ruiz proposes turning the unused mill pond into a
    learning pond for school visits. Walking distance is partly
    supported: the proposal asserts the pond is within walking
    distance of two schools but gives no distance or walking
    time. The builders' offer is partly supported: a
    materials-only dock offer is stated, but no total cost
    follows from it. Volunteer training is partly supported:
    Ruiz's offer is stated, but no volunteers, schedule, or
    safety plan is given. Overall, the proposal as presented is
    not ready for a decision: it shows learning value and
    goodwill, but it lacks the cost, maintenance, and safety
    facts a decision needs." (Award full credit to any paragraph
    that evaluates all three reasons with verdicts and reasons
    and judges readiness on the presented evidence — not on
    whether the learner personally favors the pond.)

## Project — scoring guidance

Score the four written parts against the rubric in
[project.md](project.md) (2 points each, 8 total). A
portfolio-ready report (7–8 + complete oral summary) typically:
states both central ideas correctly (Text A: one storm morning
that turned a passer-by into a returning volunteer; Text B: a
sequence of roles — joiner, record keeper — whose notes shaped
council decisions); explains two position-based differences with
quotes; states one genuine image addition (present-day scene,
heron, planting) and one correct limit (illustration, names no
one, shows the present, not the past); and evaluates one Lesson
04 claim with the correct verdict from the Lesson 04 table above.
Oral summary: complete = all four parts in order + two academic
verbs used correctly (e.g., "The memoir *suggests* ..." /
"The evidence *indicates* ..."). Below 5: re-plan with the adult
using the four-part plan in project.md before revising.
