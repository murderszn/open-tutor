# Lesson 05 — Comparing Two Accounts and Reading an Image with a Text

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare how two authors present
the same events, and I can combine what a picture shows with what
words say — without letting the picture prove things it cannot prove."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson;
[riverbend-cleanup.png](../assets/riverbend-cleanup.png); pencil
and paper.
**Standards:** RI.6.9 (compare authors' presentations); RI.6.7
(integrate information across media and formats).

## Launch (5 min) — the image

![A sunny riverbank scene: on the left, three volunteers in gloves carry a bag of collected litter toward a large blue recycling bin; in the middle, a wooden footbridge crosses a clean blue river; on the right bank, a heron stands in shallow water near tall reeds, and a child plants a small green seedling; a wooden bulletin board with blank papers stands near the bridge.](../assets/riverbend-cleanup.png)

*The Riverbend cleanup (AI-generated illustration for this lesson):
volunteers, a footbridge, a heron, and a seedling on one riverbank.*

**Text-only alternative (for learners who cannot see the image):**
"Picture a sunny riverbank. On the left, three volunteers in
gloves carry a bag of collected litter toward a large blue
recycling bin. In the middle, a wooden footbridge crosses a clean
blue river. On the right bank, a grey heron stands in shallow water
near tall reeds, and a child plants a small green seedling in fresh
soil. A wooden bulletin board holding blank white papers stands
near the bridge. Houses and trees sit in the distance."

1. "Name three things the image shows that suggest people are
   caring for the river."
2. "Does the image prove that Mara Ellison organized a cleanup?
   Why or why not?" (It does not: the image shows volunteers in
   general, names no one, and is an illustration made for this
   lesson — not a photograph of a real event. Images can *support*
   a topic's present-day picture; they cannot name an organizer.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

A memoir is written by the person who lived the events; a
biography is written by someone else about that person. Which
one is more likely to include the writer's own feelings, and
which one is more likely to include dates and a life sequence?
(Memoir → feelings and personal moments; biography → dates,
sequence, and outside view. Neither is automatically more truthful
— they select differently.)

## Explicit teaching

When comparing two authors' presentations of the same events,
compare **selection** (what each includes and leaves out),
**emphasis** (what each spends the most words on), and **voice**
(first-person feelings vs. third-person overview). Two accounts
can both be honest and still differ: the memoirist remembers the
smell of the mud; the biographer records the year the crew began.
Ask, for each difference: "Does this difference come from the
author's *position* (participant vs. observer), their *purpose*,
or their *audience*?"

**RI.6.7 move:** combine media deliberately. Ask: "What does the
image add that the words do not?" (a present-day scene, the
heron's return, the scale of the bank) and "What do the words add
that the image cannot?" (names, dates, reasons, numbers). A
coherent understanding uses both — and knows each medium's limit.

## Text A — "The Morning After the Storm" (memoir-style excerpt, original)

> I remember the smell after the storm — mud, wet leaves, and
> something sour from the culvert. My shoes sank at the bank, and
> for a moment I thought about going home. Then I saw two
> teenagers already hauling a tire out of the shallows, laughing
> at how heavy it was, and I stayed. I do not remember the date.
> I remember the tire, the laughter, and deciding to come back
> the next Saturday.

## Text B — "Mara Ellison: A Town Profile" (biography-style excerpt, original)

> Ellison joined the Riverbend Saturday crew in its early years
> and later served as its record keeper, counting volunteers and
> writing yearly notes for the town council. Her notes helped the
> council place bins and choose banks for planting. The profile
> does not record what she felt on any single morning; it records
> the sequence of roles she held and the decisions her notes
> informed.

*Both texts are fictional and written for this unit. They describe
the same fictional person and cleanup effort as Lesson 02.*

## Worked example 1 — selection and emphasis

**Model:** "Text A selects a single morning: smell, mud, a tire,
laughter. It spends its words on feelings and a decision ('I
stayed,' 'deciding to come back'). Text B selects a sequence:
joined, became record keeper, notes helped council decisions. It
spends its words on roles and results, and it explicitly says it
does not record feelings. The difference comes from position:
Text A is a participant remembering; Text B is an observer
summarizing a life of work. Neither contradicts the other — the
tire morning could be one of the Saturdays inside Text B's
sequence — but a reader who only read Text A would not know about
the record keeping, and a reader who only read Text B would not
know what a morning on the crew felt like."

## Worked example 2 — integrating the image

**Model:** "The image adds present-day, visual information the
texts do not give: volunteers still working, a heron standing in
the reeds, a child planting. That supports the Lesson 01 central
idea that the river became part of daily life. But the image does
not add names, dates, or reasons: it cannot confirm that Ellison
was record keeper, or that the council placed bins because of her
notes. Coherent understanding = image (what the riverbank looks
like now, and that wildlife has returned) + Text B (roles and
decisions) + Text A (what participation felt like). Each source
does one job."

## Guided practice

1. Name one detail Text A includes that Text B leaves out, and
   one detail Text B includes that Text A leaves out.
2. Which text emphasizes feelings, and which emphasizes sequence?
   Quote one phrase for each.
3. Does Text A contradict Text B? Answer yes or no, with one
   reason.
4. Name one thing the image adds that neither text states, and
   one thing the texts state that the image cannot show.

## Independent practice

1. In two sentences, explain how the authors' positions
   (participant vs. observer) explain one difference between the
   texts.
2. A reader says, "Text A is better because it is more personal."
   Is "more personal" the same as "more reliable"? Answer in two
   sentences.
3. Write a two-sentence paragraph that integrates all three
   sources (Text A, Text B, image) into one coherent statement
   about the Riverbend cleanup. Each sentence must draw on at
   least one source, and your paragraph must not claim anything
   no source supports.
4. Suppose a third account — a town council minute — said the
   crew began in a different year than Text B implies. What
   should a careful reader do? Answer in two sentences.
5. Which source would you trust most for *what the crew did*,
   and which for *what it felt like to be there*? Give one reason
   for each choice.
6. The image's bulletin board papers are blank. Why is that a
   strength, not a flaw, for this lesson? Answer in one sentence.
   (Hint: what false information would words on the board risk?)

## Applied task

Write a four-sentence comparison for a new reader: sentence 1
states what both accounts agree on; sentences 2–3 give one
difference each, with a quoted phrase; sentence 4 states what the
image adds and what it cannot prove. Do not declare one account
"true" and the other "false."

## Exit check

In one sentence: why can two honest accounts of the same events
still look different?

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** pre-teach *memoir, biography,
  selection, emphasis*. A learner may answer the comparison in a
  two-column chart (Text A | Text B) instead of sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** the image question may be answered
  entirely from the text-only alternative above; no learner needs
  to see the image to complete the lesson.
- **Extension:** write a third, two-sentence account — a town
  council minute — about the same cleanup morning, in a neutral,
  record-keeping voice, using only facts that Texts A and B agree
  on.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners treat the memoir as "true because
she was there" and the biography as "true because it is formal,"
or they treat the illustration as a photograph proving the story.
Reteach with the position question ("What can this source see
from where it stands, and what can it not see?") and the image
limit (illustration ≠ photograph, and it names no one). Answer
key: see [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
