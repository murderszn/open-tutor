# Lesson 02 — How a Key Individual Is Built, Paragraph by Paragraph

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can trace how a text introduces a key
person, illustrates them with an example, and elaborates on them —
and I can explain what each paragraph adds to the whole."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RI.6.3 (key individual introduced, illustrated,
elaborated); RI.6.5 (how a part fits the whole).

## Launch (5 min)

"When a text introduces a person, it rarely tells you everything at
once. First it names them and their role. Then it shows them doing
something. Then it explains what that doing led to. If you only read
the first paragraph, you know *who*; if you read all three stages,
you know *why they matter to the text's idea*."

## Readiness check (3 min)

A text says: "Mara Ellison lives in Riverbend." What do you know
about her from that sentence alone? What do you still not know?
(You know a name and a town. You do not know what she did, why the
text mentions her, or what changed because of her — the text must
*illustrate* and *elaborate* before you can say that.)

## Explicit teaching

Three stages to trace in any key-individual passage:

1. **Introduce** — the text names the person and gives a role or
   starting fact ("Mara Ellison, a Riverbend neighbor, ...").
2. **Illustrate** — the text shows the person through an example
   or anecdote, a small story that makes the role concrete.
3. **Elaborate** — the text adds detail, explanation, or results:
   what happened next, what it led to, how it connects to the
   bigger idea.

**RI.6.5 move:** after tracing the stages, ask of each paragraph,
"What would the text lose if this paragraph were removed?" A
paragraph that only repeats the introduction adds little; a
paragraph that supplies the example or the result carries the
development of the idea.

## The passage — "Mara Ellison and the River" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** Mara Ellison moved to Riverbend as a young
> teacher and, like most newcomers, barely noticed the river. She
> crossed the road bridge on her way to school and thought of the
> water mainly as something to get across.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** One Saturday in spring, Ellison joined the
> neighbors who were pulling debris from the shallows near the old
> mill. She stayed for three hours, hauled out a rusted bicycle
> frame with two teenagers, and went home with mud on her shoes and
> a list of the crew's next meeting dates in her pocket.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Over the following years, Ellison became the
> crew's record keeper. She counted volunteers each Saturday,
> noted which bank sections had been planted, and wrote a short
> yearly note for the town council describing what had been done
> and what still needed doing. Her notes helped the council decide
> where to place new bins and which banks to plant next.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** Ellison never claimed the river's change as her
> own. In her yearly notes she always listed other volunteers first
> and described herself last, as "one of the crew." The town's
> river project, the notes suggest, grew the way the river itself
> did — not from a single source, but from many small streams
> joining.

*All events and details are invented practice material. Mara
Ellison is a fictional person.*

## Worked example 1 — tracing the three stages

**Model:** "Paragraph 1 *introduces* Ellison: a teacher, a
newcomer, someone who barely noticed the river. Paragraph 2
*illustrates* her with an anecdote: one Saturday, three hours, a
rusted bicycle frame, mud on her shoes — you can picture the
change from passer-by to participant. Paragraph 3 *elaborates*:
record keeper, volunteer counts, yearly notes, and a result — her
notes helped the council place bins and plantings. If paragraph 3
were removed, you would know she joined once, but not why she
mattered to the project."

## Worked example 2 — what a paragraph adds (RI.6.5)

**Model:** "Paragraph 4 does not add a new job for Ellison. It
adds *character and connection*: she lists others first, calls
herself 'one of the crew,' and the final sentence links her habit
to the text's bigger idea — many small streams joining. Without
paragraph 4, the passage would be a résumé. With it, the passage
says something about how shared projects work. That is how a
closing paragraph can develop an idea rather than just repeat it."

## Guided practice

1. Quote one phrase from paragraph 1 that introduces Ellison, and
   explain in one sentence what role it gives her.
2. Which paragraph illustrates Ellison with an anecdote? Name the
   example it uses.
3. Name two details from paragraph 3 that elaborate her role, and
   state one result of her record keeping.
4. What would the passage lose if paragraph 4 were removed? Answer
   in one or two sentences.

## Independent practice

1. Trace all three stages in a small table: Stage | Paragraph |
   Evidence (quote or close paraphrase). Fill in all three rows.
2. Paragraph 2 says Ellison "went home with mud on her shoes."
   Is that a detail or a decoration? Explain in one sentence how
   it helps illustrate her change.
3. How does paragraph 3 connect Ellison to the town council's
   decisions? Answer in two sentences, citing one detail.
4. Compare paragraph 1 and paragraph 4: how has the text's
   presentation of Ellison changed? Answer in two sentences.
5. A classmate says paragraph 4 is unnecessary because it adds no
   new facts about Ellison's jobs. Do you agree? Give one reason
   for your answer.
6. Write two sentences explaining how this passage's structure —
   introduction, anecdote, elaboration, reflection — serves the
   central idea that shared work, not one person, changed the river.

## Applied task

Choose a real or fictional person you know well from another text
you have read (or from this unit's Lesson 01 passage: the Saturday
crew as a group). Write a four-sentence paragraph sequence in the
same three stages: sentence 1 introduces, sentences 2–3 illustrate
with one concrete example, sentence 4 elaborates with a result.
Label each sentence I (introduce), L (illustrate), or E
(elaborate) in the margin.

## Exit check

In your own words: what is the difference between *introducing*
a person and *elaborating* on them? (One sentence.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** pre-teach *debris* (scattered
  waste), *anecdote* (a short, true-seeming story used as an
  example), and *record keeper* (the person who writes down what
  happened). A learner may complete the stage table orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** the trace may be drawn as three
  stepping stones labeled Introduce / Illustrate / Elaborate, each
  with one quoted phrase.
- **Extension:** rewrite paragraph 4 from the point of view of a
  council member reading Ellison's notes, in two sentences, keeping
  every fact the same and changing only the voice.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners treat every paragraph as equal
weight ("she is introduced in paragraph 1, so paragraph 1 is the
most important"). Reteach with the removal test: "What does the
text lose without this paragraph?" Answer key: see
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
