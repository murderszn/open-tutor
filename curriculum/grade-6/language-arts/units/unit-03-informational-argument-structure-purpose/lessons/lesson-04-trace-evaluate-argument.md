# Lesson 04 — Trace and Evaluate an Argument

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can trace an argument's claims, check
each claim against its reasons and evidence, and judge whether the
claim is supported, partly supported, or unsupported — without
just agreeing or disagreeing."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RI.6.8 (trace and evaluate argument and claims);
RI.6.1 (cite textual evidence).

## Launch (5 min)

"Someone says, 'We should build a riverwalk, because everyone
wants one.' That sounds convincing — until you ask two questions:
(1) How does the speaker know *everyone* wants one? (2) Even if
many people want one, does wanting prove building is affordable,
safe, or the best use of the money? An argument is a chain:
**claim** (what the author wants you to accept) → **reasons**
(why) → **evidence** (facts, numbers, examples, or expert words
that back the reasons). Your job is to test every link."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Claim: "The riverwalk will be popular." Evidence offered: "On one
Saturday in May, 300 walkers used the footbridge." Does that
evidence fully support the claim? (No — partly. One Saturday is
one day, in one season, on a different structure. It suggests
interest, but it does not prove year-round popularity. "Partly
supported" is a real verdict, not a dodge.)

## Explicit teaching

Judge each claim in three steps:

1. **Trace:** underline the claim, then find the reason and the
   evidence the author gives *for that claim* (not for another
   claim).
2. **Test relevance:** does the evidence actually bear on the
   claim? A true fact can still be irrelevant: "The river is
   twelve miles long" does not prove "the riverwalk will be
   cheap."
3. **Test sufficiency:** is there *enough* evidence, from a
   trustworthy-enough source, to accept the claim? One day's count,
   one person's opinion, or a prediction with no numbers is
   usually not sufficient.

Verdicts: **Supported** (relevant, sufficient evidence);
**Partly supported** (some relevant evidence, but thin, narrow,
or one-sided); **Unsupported** (no evidence, irrelevant evidence,
or a prediction stated as fact). Never judge by agreement:
a claim you like can still be unsupported, and a claim you dislike
can still be supported.

## The passage — "Build the Riverwalk Now" (opinion article, original)

> **Paragraph 1.** Riverbend should build a riverwalk along the
> east bank this year. A riverwalk would give walkers, runners, and
> families a safe path away from traffic, and it would let more
> people enjoy the river the Saturday crew has worked so hard to
> restore.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** The riverwalk will be popular. On one Saturday
> in May, 300 walkers crossed the wooden footbridge — a bridge
> that is narrow, has no benches, and does not run the length of
> the bank. If that many people use a narrow bridge on one
> spring day, a full riverwalk will surely draw even more.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Every family in Riverbend wants the riverwalk.
> The writer spoke with neighbors on three streets near the
> river, and every one of them said they would use it.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** The riverwalk will also pay for itself. A state
> grant letter quoted in the town office states that the state
> will pay half the construction cost, up to a fixed maximum.
> The article does not say what the total cost is, what the
> maximum is, or what maintenance will cost each year.
>
> **Paragraph 5.** Finally, homes near the river will double in
> value once the riverwalk opens. No study, sale record, or
> expert is cited for this prediction.

*This article is fictional and written for this unit. All numbers
are invented practice data. The article's claims are presented so
learners can evaluate them; the unit takes no position on whether
a real riverwalk should be built.*

## Worked example 1 — tracing one claim

**Model (Claim: "The riverwalk will be popular," paragraph 2):**
"Reason: people already use the footbridge in large numbers.
Evidence: 300 walkers crossed the footbridge on one Saturday in
May. Relevance: yes — footbridge walkers are the closest available
evidence about walker interest. Sufficiency: weak — one day, one
season, a different (shorter) structure, and no count of how many
of those walkers would use a longer path regularly. Verdict:
**partly supported**. Note the careful wording: the evidence
does not fail, but it does not carry the whole claim either."

## Worked example 2 — an unsupported claim

**Model (Claim: "Every family in Riverbend wants the riverwalk,"
paragraph 3):** "Evidence offered: the writer spoke with
neighbors on three streets near the river, and all of them said
they would use it. Two failures: (1) *scope* — three streets
near the river are not 'every family' in town; (2) *wording* —
'said they would use it' is not the same as 'want the town to
build it.' The evidence, even if true, cannot support a claim
about every family. Verdict: **unsupported**. A smaller claim —
'neighbors near the river who were asked said they would use it'
— would be supported. Precision about scope is part of evaluation."

## Guided practice

1. State the article's overall claim (paragraph 1) in one
   sentence.
2. Trace the "pay for itself" claim (paragraph 4): what evidence
   does the article give, and what key cost facts does it leave
   out?
3. Judge paragraph 5's "double in value" claim: supported, partly
   supported, or unsupported? Give one reason.
4. Find one piece of evidence in the article that is true-sounding
   but irrelevant to the claim it sits beside — or explain why
   there is none. (Hint: check whether each paragraph's evidence
   matches its own claim.)

## Independent practice

1. Complete a claim trace table for all four specific claims
   (popularity, every family, pay for itself, double in value):
   Claim | Evidence given | Relevant? | Sufficient? | Verdict.
2. Which claim is the strongest in the article, and why? Answer
   in two sentences.
3. Which claim is the weakest, and what one piece of evidence
   would most strengthen it? Answer in two sentences.
4. A classmate says, "I agree with the article, so its argument
   is strong." Explain in two sentences why agreement is not an
   evaluation.
5. Rewrite the paragraph 3 claim so that it is fully supported by
   the evidence the article actually gives. (Change the claim, not
   the evidence.)
6. The article never mentions a counterclaim (a reason someone
   might oppose the riverwalk). In one sentence, name one
   counterclaim the article should have addressed, and explain
   why addressing it would have strengthened the argument.

## Applied task

Write a five-sentence evaluation paragraph about the article:
sentence 1 states the overall claim; sentences 2–4 give one
verdict each for three different claims, each with a reason;
sentence 5 gives an overall judgment ("The argument is strong /
mixed / weak because ..."). Do not argue for or against building
the riverwalk itself — evaluate the *argument*.

## Exit check

In your own words: what is the difference between "partly
supported" and "unsupported"? (One or two sentences.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** pre-teach *claim, reason,
  evidence, relevant, sufficient*. A learner may complete the
  trace table orally, with the adult scribing verdicts.
- **Alternate response mode:** verdicts may be shown with a
  three-color code (supported / partly / unsupported) on a copy
  of the article, with one reason written per claim.
- **Extension:** add a paragraph 6 to the article that
  acknowledges one counterclaim (cost, maintenance, or disruption
  during construction) and answers it with evidence. Keep the new
  evidence clearly labeled as invented practice data.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners judge claims by whether they agree
with them, or treat any number as proof ("it has a number, so it
must be supported"). Reteach with the relevance and sufficiency
tests, and with the readiness-check example (one Saturday ≠
year-round popularity). Answer key: see
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
