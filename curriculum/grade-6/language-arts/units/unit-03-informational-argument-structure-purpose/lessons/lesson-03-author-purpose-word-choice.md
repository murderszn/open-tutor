# Lesson 03 — Author Purpose, Point of View, and Word Choice

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell what an author is trying to
do — inform, explain, or persuade — and show how their words give
it away, including words with special or feeling-loaded meanings."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RI.6.6 (point of view or purpose); RI.6.4
(figurative, connotative, technical meanings).

## Launch (5 min)

"Read these two sentences about the same work day. (a) 'Work days
will be held on the first Saturday of each month, 9 a.m. to noon.'
(b) 'Our cherished river needs you — do not let another Saturday
slip away!' Both are about Saturday work. Only one is just giving
information. Words are fingerprints: they show what the author is
trying to do."

## Readiness check (3 min)

An author writes, "You must act now before it is too late!" Is the
author informing or persuading? What word gives it away?
(Persuading: "must" is a command, and "before it is too late"
creates urgency. An informing text would give a date, a time, and
a place instead.)

## Explicit teaching

**Author's purpose** is the author's main reason for writing.
Common purposes: **to inform** (give facts), **to explain** (show
how or why), **to persuade** (convince the reader to think or act
a certain way). Find the purpose in signals, not in the topic:
commands ("join us," "vote"), praise or blame words, selected
facts (only the good news, or only the bad news), and direct
address ("you") all point to purpose. **Point of view** is the
author's position or attitude toward the topic, shown by those
same choices.

**Word meanings (RI.6.4):** a word's **denotation** is its
dictionary meaning; its **connotation** is the feeling it carries
("cherished" feels warm and valued; "choked" feels blocked and
unhealthy). A **technical meaning** is the special meaning a word
has in a subject: in river work, a **watershed** is the area of
land whose water drains into a river; **runoff** is water that
flows over land into the river, often carrying soil or litter;
**riparian** describes the plants and land along a riverbank.
A text's purpose often hides in which kind of meaning the author
leans on.

## Text A — "Riverbank Work Days" (town notice, original)

> Work days will be held on the first Saturday of each month,
> 9 a.m. to noon, meeting at the footbridge. Gloves and bags are
> provided. Volunteers should wear sturdy shoes. For questions,
> contact the town office. All ages welcome with an adult.

## Text B — "A Letter from the Mayor" (excerpt, original)

> Our cherished river has waited long enough. Every spring, runoff
> chokes the shallows with debris, and every summer we promise to
> do better. Join the Saturday crew, and help us prove that this
> town keeps its promises. Do not let another season slip away.

*Both texts are fictional and written for this unit.*

## Worked example 1 — naming purpose from signals

**Model:** "Text A *informs*: it gives dates, times, a meeting
place, and practical details (gloves provided, sturdy shoes), with
no commands beyond practical instructions and no praise or blame
words. Text B *persuades*: it opens with the feeling-loaded word
'cherished,' uses 'chokes' for the river's problem, addresses the
reader directly ('Join the Saturday crew'), and ends with urgency
('Do not let another season slip away'). Same topic, different
purpose — and the words prove it."

## Worked example 2 — connotative vs. technical meaning

**Model:** "In Text B, 'cherished' is connotative: its dictionary
meaning is 'valued and cared for,' but its feeling is warm pride
— the mayor is building affection for the river, not just naming
it. 'Runoff' in the same sentence is technical: it names a
specific process, water flowing over land into the river. A
persuading author often pairs the two: a technical fact ('runoff')
plus a feeling word ('cherished,' 'chokes') so the fact feels
urgent. A purely informing text would more likely use 'runoff'
with a neutral verb like 'carries.'"

## Guided practice

1. State the purpose of Text A and of Text B, each in one word
   (inform / explain / persuade), and give one signal for each.
2. In Text B, find one word with a strong positive connotation and
   one with a strong negative connotation. Quote each word.
3. Explain in one sentence what "watershed" would mean if it
   appeared in a river text, using the technical definition taught
   above. (Use your own words.)
4. Rewrite this persuading sentence as a purely informing one,
   keeping the facts: "Our beautiful river desperately needs heroes
   this Saturday!" (Keep date, time, and place facts only if the
   original gives them — it does not, so your rewrite must say
   what is missing.)

## Independent practice

1. Text A says "All ages welcome with an adult." What purpose
   signal is that, if any? Answer in one sentence.
2. Text B never gives a date, time, or meeting place. How does
   that omission support your purpose judgment? Answer in two
   sentences.
3. Classify each word as connotative or technical *as used in
   river writing*, with a one-phrase reason: (a) cherished,
   (b) runoff, (c) chokes, (d) riparian, (e) beloved, (f) debris.
4. An author writes, "The watershed covers twelve square miles."
   Is that sentence informing or persuading? Give one reason.
5. Explain in two sentences how an author can persuade *without*
   ever giving a command, using word choice alone. Give one
   invented example phrase.
6. Reread Text B's last sentence. What point of view does it show
   toward Riverbend's residents — trusting, scolding, or neutral?
   Defend your answer with one quoted phrase.

## Applied task

Write two three-sentence notices about the same invented school
event (a book fair, a garden day, a recycling drive — your choice).
Notice 1 must inform only: dates, times, place, practical details,
no praise, blame, or commands beyond practical instructions.
Notice 2 must persuade: use at least one positive connotation word,
one direct address to the reader, and one urgency phrase — but
keep every fact in it true to Notice 1. Label each notice with its
purpose.

## Exit check

In one sentence: what is one signal that tells you an author is
persuading rather than informing?

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** pre-teach *connotation*,
  *denotation*, and *watershed* with the examples above before
  the independent practice. A learner may answer guided practice
  orally or by highlighting signal words in the texts.
- **Alternate response mode:** purpose may be shown with a
  two-column chart (Text A signals | Text B signals) instead of
  sentences.
- **Extension:** take one sentence from Text B and rewrite it
  three ways — informing, explaining, and persuading — changing
  only word choice and sentence shape, not the facts.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners decide purpose from the topic
("it's about volunteering, so it must persuade") instead of from
signals. Reteach by covering the topic and asking, "What do the
words *do*?" Answer key: see [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md)
(adult only).
