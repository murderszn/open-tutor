# Lesson 01 — Central Idea and Objective Summary

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find the central idea of an
informational text, show the details that carry it, and write a
summary that reports what the text says — without adding my opinion."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RI.6.2 (central idea; objective summary).

## Launch (5 min)

"Think of a news article about a school event. If a friend asked,
'What was it about?' you would not read them the whole article.
You would give the one big idea in a sentence, then maybe two
important details. That one big idea is the **central idea**. The
details that prove it is really the text's idea — not just your
guess — are its **supporting details**."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which of these is a central idea, and which is just a detail?
(a) "The river runs through the middle of Riverbend."
(b) "Over time, Riverbend's river changed from a neglected waterway
into a project the whole town shares."
((b) is a central-idea candidate: it covers the whole text's arc.
(a) is a single detail — true, but too small to be the whole point.)

## Explicit teaching

**Central idea** = the most important point a text makes about its
topic. The topic is *what* the text is about ("Riverbend's river");
the central idea is *what the text says about* that topic. Find it
by asking, after each paragraph: "What point does this paragraph
keep coming back to?" Details that repeat across paragraphs, appear
in the first or last paragraph, or explain a change are usually
carrying the central idea.

**Objective summary** = a short retelling of the text's main points
in your own words, with no opinions, judgments, or new facts added.
Test every sentence of your summary: "Does the text actually say
this?" If a sentence says "the best," "terrible," or "should," and
the text never said that, it is your opinion — cut it.

## The passage — "Riverbend's River" (original)

> **Paragraph 1.** The river that runs through Riverbend is twelve
> miles long inside the town limits, and for most of the town's
> history it was treated as a back lot. People crossed it on two
> bridges and rarely looked down.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** That changed slowly, not all at once. A group of
> neighbors began meeting on Saturday mornings to pull tires, cans,
> and branches out of the shallow water near the old mill. At first
> only a dozen people came. Within three years, the Saturday crew
> numbered more than eighty volunteers.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** The town council then joined the effort. It paid
> for trash bins along the banks, planted reeds and willows where
> the banks had worn away, and built a wooden footbridge so walkers
> could cross without using the busy road bridge.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** Today the river is part of daily life in
> Riverbend. Children walk the bank path to school, anglers fish
> from the footbridge at dawn, and the Saturday crew still meets —
> now mostly to plant, prune, and count the herons that have
> returned to the reeds.
>
> **Paragraph 5.** The river did not change because one person
> ordered it changed. It changed because neighbors kept showing up,
> the town kept investing, and the work kept going long after the
> first cleanup.

*All numbers and events in this passage are invented practice data
for this unit. Riverbend is a fictional town.*

## Worked example 1 — finding the central idea

**Model:** "Paragraph 1 says the river was ignored. Paragraphs 2
and 3 show neighbors, then the council, working on it. Paragraph 4
shows it in daily life, and paragraph 5 names the cause: steady
work over time. So the central idea is: *Over time, Riverbend's
river changed from a neglected waterway into a shared community
project because residents and the town kept working on it.* Note
what the central idea does: it names the topic (the river), the
change (neglected → shared project), and the cause (steady work).
A sentence that only says 'the river is nice now' would miss the
change and the cause."

## Worked example 2 — objective vs. opinion summary

**Bad summary (opinion smuggled in):** "Riverbend has the best river
in the world, and the Saturday crew are heroes who saved it."
Why it fails: "best in the world" and "heroes" are judgments the
text never makes; "saved it" overstates what the text claims.

**Good objective summary:** "Riverbend's river was long treated as
a back lot. Neighbors began Saturday cleanups, the town council
added bins, plantings, and a footbridge, and the river became part
of daily life. The text credits steady work over time, by residents
and the town, for the change." Every sentence can be traced to a
paragraph; no new praise or blame is added.

## Guided practice

1. In one sentence, state the central idea of "Riverbend's River"
   in your own words.
2. Name one detail from paragraph 2 and one from paragraph 4 that
   support your central idea. Give the paragraph for each.
3. Which sentence is objective, and which adds an opinion?
   (a) "The Saturday crew grew from a dozen people to more than
   eighty volunteers." (b) "The Saturday crew was the most wonderful
   group in town." Explain your choice in one sentence.
4. Rewrite this opinion summary as an objective one: "Riverbend's
   river is beautiful now because the town finally did something
   smart." (Keep only what the text says.)

## Independent practice

1. What is the topic of the passage, and how is the central idea
   different from the topic? Answer in two sentences.
2. Paragraph 5 says the river "did not change because one person
   ordered it changed." What does that sentence add to the central
   idea? Answer in one or two sentences.
3. Write a three-sentence objective summary of the whole passage.
   Do not add opinions or new facts.
4. A classmate writes: "The river changed because the council built
   a footbridge." Is that a complete central idea? Why or why not?
5. Which detail is *off-topic* for a summary of this passage, and
   why? (a) "The river is twelve miles long inside the town
   limits." (b) "Anglers fish from the footbridge at dawn."
   (c) "Rivers in other countries are also long." Explain in one
   sentence.
6. Reread paragraph 3. Name two details that show the town
   council's role, and explain in one sentence how they support
   the central idea.

## Applied task

Imagine a new student who has not read the passage. Write a
four-sentence briefing for that student: sentence 1 states the
central idea; sentences 2–3 give one supporting detail each (with
paragraph references); sentence 4 is an objective closing sentence
about what the text credits for the change. Do not add your own
opinion about the river.

## Exit check

In one sentence, what is the difference between a central idea and
an opinion about a text? (Answer in your own words.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** read the passage aloud together
  first; pre-teach *back lot* (an ignored, unused area) and
  *worn away* (eroded). A learner may answer guided practice orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** the central idea may be drawn as a
  three-box chain (Before → Work → Now) with a caption sentence.
- **Extension:** find the sentence in paragraph 5 that states the
  central idea most directly, and explain why the author placed it
  last instead of first.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners equate the central idea with the
topic ("the river") or with the most interesting detail (the
herons). Reteach by asking, "Does this sentence cover paragraphs
1 through 5, or just one paragraph?" Answer key: see
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
