# Unit 03 — Informational Reading: Argument, Structure, and Author Purpose

**Grade:** 6 · **Subject:** Language Arts · **Track weeks:** 11–14
**Backlog:** [issue #32](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/32), section U03
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Informational texts do not just hand you facts. They choose an order,
choose words, choose a purpose — and sometimes they argue for something
while sounding like they are only reporting. This unit teaches the
reader to see those choices: find the central idea and the details
that carry it, follow how a key person, event, or idea is built up
paragraph by paragraph, name what the author is trying to do and how
you can tell, and — the unit's sharpest tool — trace an argument's
claims and judge, claim by claim, whether the reasons and evidence
actually support them. The unit ends by putting two accounts of the
same person side by side, so the learner can see how two honest
writers can still present the same events differently.

All unit passages are original and set in the fictional town of
Riverbend. Riverbend, its river, its residents (including Mara
Ellison), and every number in the passages are invented practice
material for this unit — not real town statistics, not real people,
and not evidence about any real place. That fiction is deliberate:
it lets the learner judge *how* an argument is built without needing
outside facts, and it keeps the evaluation honest — a claim is judged
by the evidence inside the text, not by what the reader already
believes about the topic.

## Essential question

How can I tell what an informational text is really doing — informing,
explaining, persuading — and whether its argument deserves my agreement?

## Prerequisites

From Unit 01, Unit 02, and the diagnostic weeks:

- Cite textual evidence (exact quotation or close paraphrase with a
  paragraph reference) for explicit meaning and for inferences
  (RL.6.1, RI.6.1).
- Determine a theme or central idea from details and summarize
  distinct from personal opinion (RL.6.2; the same move is now
  applied to informational text, RI.6.2).
- Explain how word choice affects meaning and tone (RL.6.4) and how a
  narrator's point of view is developed (RL.6.6).
- Use context clues, Greek and Latin affixes and roots, and reference
  materials to clarify unknown words (L.6.4); use academic verbs —
  *infer, conclude, suggest, imply, indicate, demonstrate* — precisely.

Lesson 01 re-teaches the central-idea move in informational text
explicitly rather than assuming the Unit 02 literary version
transferred on its own.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. Determine the **central idea** of an informational text and how it
   is conveyed through particular details, and provide an **objective
   summary** distinct from personal opinions or judgments (RI.6.2).
2. Analyze in detail how a **key individual, event, or idea** is
   introduced, illustrated, and elaborated in a text, and how a
   sentence, paragraph, chapter, or section **fits the whole** and
   develops the ideas (RI.6.3, RI.6.5).
3. Determine the meaning of **figurative, connotative, and technical**
   words as used in a text, and determine an author's **point of view
   or purpose** and explain how it is conveyed (RI.6.4, RI.6.6).
4. **Trace and evaluate** the argument and specific claims in a text,
   distinguishing claims **supported by reasons and evidence** from
   claims that are not (RI.6.8).
5. **Compare and contrast** one author's presentation of events with
   another's, and **integrate information** presented in different
   media or formats, as well as in words, to develop a coherent
   understanding of a topic (RI.6.9, RI.6.7).

## Unit passages (original; written for this unit)

- **"Riverbend's River"** (~230 words) — Lesson 01. A short history of
  a fictional river's change from a neglected waterway to a shared
  community project.
- **"Mara Ellison and the River"** (~260 words) — Lesson 02. How one
  fictional resident is introduced, illustrated with an example, and
  elaborated across paragraphs.
- **"Riverbank Work Days" (town notice) and "A Letter from the
  Mayor" (excerpt)** (~120 words each) — Lesson 03. Two short texts
  with different purposes about the same work days.
- **"Build the Riverwalk Now" (opinion article, fictional)** (~280
  words) — Lesson 04. An argument with a mix of supported,
  partly supported, and unsupported claims, built for evaluation.
- **"The Morning After the Storm" (memoir-style excerpt) and "Mara
  Ellison: A Town Profile" (biography-style excerpt)** (~150 words
  each) — Lesson 05. Two accounts of the same fictional person and
  the same cleanup effort, presented differently.
- **"The Mill Pond Decision"** (~230 words) — unit assessment.
  A new informational article with a claim to evaluate; not
  previewed in any lesson.

## Session pacing (16 sessions, ~40 minutes each)

Session model per the track audit: modeled informational lesson →
structure/purpose practice → argument-evaluation practice → review
and discussion.

| # | Session | Activity | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Week 11 | Lesson 01 — central idea and objective summary ("Riverbend's River") | Central idea stated in one sentence; 2 supporting details cited |
| 2 | Week 11 | Central-idea practice — detail sorting: central, supporting, off-topic | 8 of 10 details sorted correctly with a reason each |
| 3 | Week 11 | Lesson 02 — how Mara Ellison is introduced, illustrated, elaborated | 3-stage trace completed with a quote per stage |
| 4 | Week 11 | Review/discussion — "Which paragraph does the most work?" | Each learner defends a paragraph choice with evidence |
| 5 | Week 12 | Lesson 03 — author purpose and point of view; word choice | Purpose of both short texts named with 2 signals each |
| 6 | Week 12 | Word-meaning practice — connotative vs. technical meanings | 8 of 10 words classified with a context reason |
| 7 | Week 12 | Lesson 04 — trace and evaluate an argument ("Build the Riverwalk Now") | 4 claims traced; each judged supported, partly, or unsupported |
| 8 | Week 12 | Argument practice — relevance check: true but irrelevant evidence | 6 of 8 evidence items judged relevant or irrelevant, with reasons |
| 9 | Week 13 | Lesson 05 — memoir vs. biography; image integration (generated image) | 3 presentation differences found; image question answered from image + text |
| 10 | Week 13 | Project — "Two Accounts, One River": read, annotate, plan report | Plan has a central-idea sentence per account + 3 differences |
| 11 | Week 13 | Project — draft the comparison and argument-evaluation report | Draft covers all 4 report parts (see project.md) |
| 12 | Week 13 | Project — revise with rubric; prepare 2-minute oral summary | Report meets every 2-point rubric band; summary uses 2 academic verbs |
| 13 | Week 14 | Formative quiz ([week-04_quiz.md](assessments/week-04_quiz.md)) | Adult scores same day; reteach list made |
| 14 | Week 14 | Reteach and review — targets from the quiz; claim-sorting relay | Missed items re-answered correctly with evidence |
| 15 | Week 14 | Unit assessment ([unit-assessment.md](assessments/unit-assessment.md)) | Completed under adult supervision |
| 16 | Week 14 | Unit wrap — reread "Build the Riverwalk Now"; reflect on the essential question | 3-sentence reflection citing one evaluation move learned |

## Standards and source notes

Standards codes and descriptions follow the track's verified
crosswalk (scope-and-sequence §12, verified 2026-10-05 against the
published Common Core State Standards for English Language Arts,
thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/), re-checked for this unit's codes
on 2026-10-25: RI.6.1 (cite textual evidence for explicit meaning
and inferences); RI.6.2 (determine a central idea and how it is
conveyed through particular details; provide a summary distinct from
personal opinions or judgments); RI.6.3 (analyze in detail how a key
individual, event, or idea is introduced, illustrated, and elaborated,
e.g., through examples or anecdotes); RI.6.4 (determine the meaning
of words and phrases as used in a text, including figurative,
connotative, and technical meanings); RI.6.5 (analyze how a
particular sentence, paragraph, chapter, or section fits into the
overall structure of a text and contributes to the development of
the ideas); RI.6.6 (determine an author's point of view or purpose
and explain how it is conveyed); RI.6.7 (integrate information
presented in different media or formats, e.g., visually and
quantitatively, as well as in words, to develop a coherent
understanding of a topic or issue); RI.6.8 (trace and evaluate the
argument and specific claims, distinguishing claims supported by
reasons and evidence from claims that are not); RI.6.9 (compare and
contrast one author's presentation of events with another's, e.g.,
a memoir written by and a biography on the same person); RI.6.10
(read and comprehend literary nonfiction in the grades 6–8 text
complexity band proficiently, with scaffolding as needed at the high
end). The framework is a subject reference only; nothing here claims
state adoption, accreditation, or complete alignment.

No passage in this unit reproduces a copyrighted text, worksheet, or
article. The Riverbend passages are original fiction written for
teaching; any resemblance to a real town or person is coincidental.
Numbers inside the passages are invented practice data, labeled as
such wherever a learner is asked to judge evidence.
