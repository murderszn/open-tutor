# Answer Key — Unit 05 (Adult Only)

**How to use:** worked solutions and acceptable variation for
every lesson item, practice item, quiz question, assessment task,
and project criterion. Open responses accept any answer that
meets the stated reasoning — the samples below are models, not
the only right answers. Score against the reasoning, not the
exact wording. Do not show learners this file.

## Lesson 01 — Event sequences that unfold naturally

**Guided practice**

1. Context: the talent show is tonight at seven; Maya is in her
   bedroom in the morning. Narrator: Maya, first person ("I").
   Main character: Maya (and her guitar, the second character of
   the story).
2. Episodes in order: (1) Maya opens the case and finds it empty.
   (2) She remembers practicing in the garden and carrying the
   case home. (3) She searches the house. (4) She calls Ben.
3. Episode 2 happens because the show is tonight and she needs to
   tune up, so she opens the case. Episode 3 happens because the
   case is empty. Episode 4 happens because the guitar is not in
   the house.
4. Moving the house search before opening the case breaks the
   chain: Maya would be searching before she knows anything is
   wrong. The search only happens *because* the case is empty.

**Independent practice**

1. "The talent show was tonight at seven" — it tells the reader
   when the story happens (tonight, seven o'clock) and that the
   morning's events happen before it.
2. Third person: "The talent show was tonight at seven, and Maya
   was going to play 'River Light,' the song she wrote about the
   river." Lost: being inside Maya's feelings ("My stomach felt
   like a stone in a sock" becomes something observed from
   outside). Gained: the narrator can show what other characters
   are doing when Maya is not there.
3. Sample orientation: "On the last day of summer camp, at the
   lake dock just after breakfast, I — June — realized my
   notebook was gone, and the talent show was that night."
   (Context: camp dock, morning, show tonight. Narrator: "I."
   Character: June.) Accept any orientation with all three parts.
4. Order: 1 (b) practices in the garden after school; 2 (a)
   carries the empty case home at dusk; 3 (c) opens the case in
   the morning; 4 (d) calls Ben. Because: she opens the case in
   the morning because she carried it home last night, and she
   calls Ben because the case is empty.
5. Missing episode: checking where she keeps the trophy — the
   step between knowing she won it and searching the auditorium.
   The story jumps from background ("won it last spring") to a
   far-away search without the first search where the trophy
   lives (her room, her shelf).
6. "Light as a lunchbox" matters because it plants that the case
   was already empty when Maya carried it home — she did not
   notice at the time. It is the clue that the guitar was never
   in the case that evening, which the resolution (Ana borrowed
   it) depends on.

**Applied task (sample)**

Five-episode plan (a character loses a bus pass on the day of a
field trip): (1) Sam packs his bag — because the field trip is
today. (2) Sam reaches into his pocket at the bus and finds no
pass — because he packed in a hurry. (3) Sam searches his bag —
because the pass is not in his pocket. (4) Sam remembers lending
the pass to his sister — because he retraces yesterday. (5) Sam
calls his sister and gets the pass back — because he knows where
it is. Orientation: "The field trip was today, and the bus left
at nine. I — Sam — was standing at the gate with my bag, my
lunch, and no bus pass." Accept any plan with five linked
episodes and a three-part orientation.

**Exit check**

Sample: "An orientation tells the reader where and when the story
happens, who is telling it, and who it is about, before the
action starts — so the reader never has to guess."

## Lesson 02 — Narrator and characters: response and change

**Guided practice**

1. Narrator: Maya, first person. Evidence: "I followed him" and
   "My hands were shaking" — the narrator is inside the story
   using "I" and "my."
2. Ben is impatient (or hasty, eager). Evidence: he opens the
   same cupboard twice, and he shouts "Found it?" every minute.
3. (a) Old response: she searches the way Ben does — "fast,
   loud, and everywhere at once." (b) Middle step: she stops on
   the stage and asks, "Where did I last have it?" (c) New
   response: she leads Ben to the footbridge — "I was the one
   with the plan."
4. Paragraph 3 is the most important because it is the middle
   step: the stop and the question. Without it, Maya would jump
   from frantic to planful with no cause, and the reader would
   not believe the change.

**Independent practice**

1. Third person: "Ben arrived with a flashlight, a spare key to
   the auditorium, and a plan that was mostly shouting." Gained:
   the narrator can show Ben's feelings and thoughts too. Lost:
   being inside Maya's body — the shaking hands, the worry.
2. Ben does not need to change: he is a supporting character
   whose job is to contrast with Maya and push the search
   forward. Not every character changes in every scene; the
   story's change belongs to the main character, Maya.
3. "Maya was scared. Then she was brave." does not show a change
   because it only names two labels with nothing between them.
   The missing middle step is the pause, the question, or the
   small thing noticed that causes the change.
4. Sample: "Ana set the watering can down gently, checked the
   gate latch twice, and wrote the time on the garden log."
   (Careful, shown through action; no "careful" used.)
5. "For the first time that day, I was the one with the plan."
   Maya speaks in a full sentence with a decision; Ben's
   paragraph-1 words are exclamations and a shout ("We'll check
   everywhere!").
6. Sample first sentence: "Maya called me in a panic, and I
   grabbed my flashlight before she even finished the sentence."
   Gain: Ben's energy and loyalty from inside. Lose: Maya's
   inner change — the shaking hands and the question that turns
   the search around.

**Applied task (sample)**

Next two paragraphs for the Lesson 01 story: "I tore through my
bag like it owed me money, dumping books on the bus-stop bench.
(Old response — frantic, shown through action.) Then I stopped,
sat down, and asked myself when I had last seen the pass. I
pictured yesterday: my sister at the kitchen table, holding out
her hand. (Middle step — the pause and the memory.)" Accept any
two paragraphs that show an old response and a middle step.

**Exit check**

Sample: "A change needs a middle step because the reader must
see why and how the character changed — without the pause or the
question, the new behavior appears out of nowhere and is not
believed."

## Lesson 03 — Dialogue that does work

**Image question**

The pick tells Maya she was on the footbridge yesterday when she
packed up: the empty case alone only says the guitar is gone,
but the pick says *where* she last had it — and that she dropped
something while packing, so she was careless, not robbed.
Sample dialogue — Maya: "It's my pick — the red one with the
chip. I dropped it here yesterday." Ben: "So the guitar was here
yesterday. Then where did it go?" Accept any two lines that say
what the pick means without narrating it.

**Guided practice**

1. Reveals character: Ben's "A pick? That's it? We've been
   searching all day for a pick?" (impatient). Moves the plot:
   Maya's "It's proof I was here." (turns a tiny object into the
   clue that changes the search). Raises tension: Ben's "So
   nobody took it. Then where is it?" (a new question).
2. "Slowly" tells the reader Ben is finally thinking instead of
   rushing — he is catching up to Maya's realization.
3. Beat in paragraph 6: "I turned the pick over in my fingers."
   It shows Maya thinking carefully, handling the clue, calm and
   methodical — the words state the conclusion; the beat shows
   the new Maya.
4. Sample rewrite: "'I think I left it on the footbridge,' Maya
   said. Ben stopped walking. 'The footbridge? Then let's go
   look.'" Every line does a job: the first moves the plot (new
   plan), the beat shows Ben's surprise, the second moves the
   plot (agreement). Accept any 2–3 lines with no small talk.

**Independent practice**

1. Correctly punctuated: "Did you find it?" Ben asked. / "Not
   yet," Maya said, "but I found this pick." (New paragraph per
   speaker; comma inside the closing quotation mark before the
   tag; question mark inside the quotes.)
2. Two problems: (1) "said sadly" plus "sadly" — the adverb
   repeats the tag and tells instead of showing; (2) both lines
   say the same thing — neither line does a new job. Sample fix:
   "'I am very sad,' Maya said, staring at the empty case. Ben
   did not answer. He picked up the case and shook it, as if the
   guitar might fall out." (Beat replaces the repeated tag.)
3. Sample: Ana: "I saw the pick from the path. I knew it was
   yours before I even bent down." (Reveals character: Ana is
   observant and knows Maya's things.) Ana: "Maya — about your
   guitar. I need to tell you something." (Moves the plot: the
   confession is coming.)
4. (a) "said Maya" is stronger. Plain tags disappear and let the
   words carry the meaning; (b) "exclaimed loudly and angrily"
   over-directs the reader (telling how to feel) and the adverb
   repeats the verb.
5. Sample: "'Great. Just great,' Maya said, staring at the empty
   case." She really means the opposite: this is terrible, and
   she is upset (sarcasm).
6. Sample: Maya: "Whose kite is that? It's been up there since
   yesterday." Ben: "If it's been there since yesterday, someone
   lost it the same day you lost your guitar." The second line
   raises the question: are the two losses connected?

**Applied task (sample)**

Six-line scene (from the Lesson 01 story — Sam and the bus pass):
"Sam," his sister said, "did you check your coat?" / Sam shook
his head. "I checked everywhere." / "Did you check the kitchen
table?" / "Why would it be on the kitchen table?" / "Because
that's where you left it yesterday. I watched you." / Sam stared
at her. "Then I've been searching the wrong places all morning."
The underlined line (the last one) does the most work: it moves
the plot (new plan) and shows Sam's change. Accept any 6–8 lines
with two beats, varied tags, and every line doing a job.

**Exit check**

Sample: "Dialogue that does a job reveals character, moves the
plot, or raises tension; small talk does none of those and can be
cut without the story losing anything."

## Lesson 04 — Pacing: slow the moment, summarize the rest

**Guided practice**

1. Small action: "I knelt" or "My fingers closed around it."
   Sense detail: "The planks were cold through my jeans" or "red
   against the grey wood." Short sentence: "It was warm from the
   sun." or "The heron did not move at all." Each slows the
   minute down by making the reader feel the time pass.
2. Time words: "That morning" and "By noon." List: "the
   auditorium, the garden shed, and the library" (places) and
   "dust, a lost mitten, and a pencil with no eraser" (findings).
   They speed the morning up by covering hours in four
   sentences.
3. Part A is a scene: the reader is there for the turning point,
   the clue that changes the search. Part B is a summary: the
   reader only needs to know the morning failed, not live through
   every cupboard.
4. Sample: "Maya stepped onto the footbridge. The boards creaked
   under her shoes. The river slid past below, brown and slow,
   and the case bumped against her leg with every step."

**Independent practice**

1. The pacing is backwards: tying a shoe is a small moment
   slowed too much, and the talent show is the climax summarized
   too fast. Slow what matters; summarize what does not.
2. Sample: "Ana's hand hovered over the door handle. She took a
   breath, pushed the door open a crack, and listened. The room
   smelled of wood polish and old rosin." (Actions one at a
   time; one sense.)
3. Sample: "That morning Maya opened the case, searched the
   house, and called Ben. By the time he arrived, she was
   waiting at the gate." (Time words: "That morning," "By the
   time"; list: "opened…, searched…, called…")
4. Scene: the minute the curtain opens — it is the climax the
   reader waited for, so the reader should be there for it.
   Summary: the week of rehearsals — repetition the reader only
   needs to know happened.
5. Sample slowed version: "Maya lifted the guitar out of its
   case. The neck was cool under her hand. She pressed the third
   string, and it buzzed against the fret like a trapped bee."
   Sample sped-up version: "By the end of rehearsal, Maya knew
   the guitar was in the music room, the strings were new, and
   the show was tomorrow."
6. By episode six the reader will be bored or exhausted: every
   episode at the same slow speed means no contrast and no
   momentum, so the story feels much longer than it is.
   Pacing works by contrast — slow against fast.

**Applied task (sample)**

Scene (the most important minute): "Sam's fingers closed around
the pass. It was warm from his sister's pocket. He stared at it
for a whole second before he believed it." Summary of what came
before: "That morning Sam had searched his bag, his coat, and
the kitchen drawer. By the bus stop, he had given up." Accept any
4–6 sentence scene and 2-sentence summary, correctly labeled.

**Exit check**

Sample: "You decide by the reader's question: if the reader
would rather be there for the moment, write a scene; if they
only need to know it happened, write a summary."

## Lesson 05 — Precise words and sensory detail

**Guided practice**

1. Precise verb: "pushed" (with one finger — careful, almost
   afraid). Sensory detail: "wood polish and old rosin" (smell)
   or "new guitar strings" (smell). Each shows more than a
   general word would.
2. "Gleamed" tells the reader the guitar was cared for — wiped,
   tended, loved. "Was there" only says it exists; "gleamed"
   says someone looked after it, which is the clue that it was
   borrowed by a friend, not taken by a thief.
3. Three body details: "I did not run to it"; "I stood in the
   doorway and let my breathing slow down"; "My hands had
   stopped shaking."
4. Sample: "Ben bounced on his toes at the auditorium door and
   checked his watch three times before the first chord." (Body
   and action show excitement; "excited" is never used.)

**Independent practice**

1. (a) "walked" → "crept" — careful and quiet, as if afraid to
   disturb what she might find. (b) "sounded good" → "rang
   clear" — a precise sound, not a vague judgment. (c) "nice" →
   "steady" — Ana is reliable: she tuned the piano and laid out
   two chairs. Accept any precise replacement with a reason tied
   to meaning.
2. Sample: sound — "Chairs scraped as the audience settled."
   Smell — "The auditorium smelled of dust and floor wax."
   Touch — "The velvet seat was cool under Maya's hands." No
   "nice" in any of them.
3. "The room was very, very beautiful and amazing" shows almost
   nothing: the intensifiers add no information, and "beautiful"
   and "amazing" are judgments, not evidence. Sample rewrite:
   "The late light fell through the high windows and turned the
   dust in the air to gold."
4. (b) "a kind surprise" has the right connotation: the note and
   the new strings show care. (a) "a sneaky trick" implies
   deceit, which contradicts the evidence — Ana left a note,
   restrung the guitar, and invited a duet.
5. Sample: "Ana kept glancing at the music-room door and turning
   the new string packet over in her pocket, smiling every time
   someone said Maya's name." (No "proud"; the body shows it.)
6. Most important clue: the new strings (and the wiped, shining
   wood). A thief does not restring and polish a stolen guitar;
   someone who cared for it did — so it was borrowed, not stolen.

**Applied task (sample)**

Sensory (place, two sentences): "The footbridge smelled of rain
and river mud. The planks were damp under Maya's knees." Showing
(rewriting "she was nervous"): "Maya's fingers kept missing the
pick, and she had to wipe her palms on her jeans before she
tried again." Accept any sensory pair and any body-detail rewrite.

**Exit check**

Sample: "'Her hands stopped shaking' is stronger because it gives
the reader evidence — the body — to conclude relief, while 'she
felt relieved' only names the feeling and leaves the reader
outside the moment."

## Lesson 06 — Transitions and conclusions

**Guided practice**

1. Time transition: "By seven o'clock." Place transition:
   "walking home across the footbridge." Transition clause:
   "Later, walking home across the footbridge under the stars"
   (time and place in one clause).
2. "By seven o'clock" is better because it names the exact time
   (seven) and the place shift (to the show). "And then" only
   says "next" — never when next or where next.
3. Three tests: (1) This story only — only this story has an
   empty case carried home and a borrowed guitar. (2) Matches
   what she learned — she learned she assumed the worst and was
   wrong, so "ask before I assumed" is exactly that lesson.
   (3) Closes the problem — the guitar is found, the show is
   played, and the case is a joke she makes about herself.
4. "And they all lived happily ever after" does not follow: it is
   a fairy-tale formula that could end any story, names no lesson
   from Maya's events, and does not settle the guitar or the
   show.

**Independent practice**

1. (a) "After" — names the time after the show. (b) "Inside" (or
   "In") — names the place, inside the garden. (c) "After" —
   names the time after an hour of searching. Accept any
   transition that correctly names the time or place shift.
2. Sample: "By seven o'clock, we were in the auditorium, the
   footbridge and its heron far behind us." (Time: seven o'clock.
   Place: the auditorium, away from the footbridge.)
3. Sample: "Maya learned to ask before she assumed — she had
   blamed the world for a guitar that Ana had borrowed to help
   her." (Names the lesson and ties it to one event.)
4. "He never forgot that day" cannot be judged yet: you would
   need to know what happened that day and what he learned from
   it. It only works if the story's events give "that day" a
   specific meaning and the not-forgetting connects to the lesson.
5. Order: "Maya found the pick." → "Maya opened the music-room
   door." → "Maya played at the show." Sample transitions: "After
   finding the pick, Maya opened the music-room door." / "By
   seven o'clock, Maya played at the show."
6. Sample: "From then on, Sam checked his own bag before blaming
   anyone — the book had been in his friend's backpack because
   Sam had lent it and forgotten." (Names what was learned and
   ties it to the event.)

**Applied task (sample)**

Conclusion paragraph: "The bus pulled away without me, but my
sister was already running down the street with the pass in her
hand. I had spent the morning sure I had lost it. I had only
lent it — and forgotten. From then on, I wrote down everything I
lent, and I asked before I blamed." Tests: (1) This story only —
a lent bus pass and a running sister. (2) What was learned — ask
before blaming; write down loans. (3) Problem closed — the pass
is back and the habit is named. Accept any 3–4 sentence
conclusion with three test sentences.

**Exit check**

Sample: "A conclusion follows when it grows out of the events —
it settles the problem the story opened and names what the
narrator learned, so it could only end this story."

## Practice A — Event sequences and orientation

1. Episodes in order: (1) The ribbon is empty one morning.
   (2) Ana searches her pockets, school bag, and kitchen drawer.
   (3) She remembers hanging the ribbon on the garden gate.
   (4) She runs to the gate. (5) The key is still there, swinging
   in the wind.
2. Sample: "One morning at the Riverbend school garden, Ana —
   told by a third-person narrator — finds the garden key missing
   from its blue ribbon." (Context: the garden, one morning.
   Narrator: third person. Character: Ana.)
3. "This happens because she remembered where she had left the
   ribbon — hanging on the garden gate while she watered the
   seedlings."
4. Turning point: remembering where she hung the ribbon. It is
   the moment the search changes from blind (pockets, bag,
   drawer) to directed (the gate).
5. Order: 1 (b) wakes up late; 2 (d) cannot find his shoes;
   3 (a) finds his shoes by the door; 4 (c) runs to school.
6. Missing episode: finding a candle and matches — the step
   between the lights going out and lighting a candle ("Maya
   finds the candles in the kitchen drawer").
7. Order X unfolds naturally: lose → search → find. Order Y
   breaks: Maya finds the pick before losing the guitar, so the
   reader does not know why the pick matters.
8. Sample: "On the first day of school, in the hallway of
   Riverbend School, I — the narrator, Sam — found a strange
   wooden box on the floor by the library door." Accept any
   two-sentence orientation with context, narrator, and character.

## Practice B — Narrator and character change

1. (a) is first person ("I opened… my heart sank"). (b) is third
   person ("Maya opened… her heart sank").
2. Sample: "Ana set the watering can down gently, checked the
   gate latch twice, and wrote the time on the garden log."
   (Action shows careful and helpful; neither word is used.)
3. Order: 1 (b) searches fast and loud like Ben; 2 (c) stops on
   the stage and asks where she last had the guitar; 3 (a) leads
   Ben to the footbridge with a plan.
4. A reader will not believe the change because there is no middle
   step — no pause, question, or small thing noticed that causes
   it. The missing middle step is the moment the character stops
   and thinks (or notices something) between the two behaviors.
5. Third person fits best: the story must show three friends at
   the same moment in different places, which a first-person "I"
   narrator cannot do — one character cannot see the other two.
6. Trait: impatient (or eager, hasty). The action shows it better
   than the word because the reader hears the rushing — shouting
   the same question every minute is impatience in action.
7. Sample: "'It's all right,' Maya said, her voice steady.
   'We'll check the footbridge. One place at a time.'" (Steady
   voice and a plan show calm; "calm" is never used.)
8. The shaking-hands pair shows the change in the body across two
   scenes, so the reader can track it; "Maya became brave" only
   announces a label with no evidence of how or when the change
   happened.

## Practice C — Dialogue that does work

1. "Did you find it?" Ben asked. / "Not yet," Maya said, "but I
   found this pick." (New paragraph per speaker; comma inside the
   closing quotation mark before the tag.)
2. Correctly paragraphed: "Where were you?" Ben asked. / "In the
   garden," Maya said. / "All afternoon?" Ben asked. / "Until
   dusk," Maya said. (Four paragraphs, one per speaker turn.)
3. Sample: "'I dropped it here.' Maya held up the pick."
   / "'Are you sure?' Ben leaned over the rail." (Spoken words
   kept; tags replaced by beats.)
4. Cross out (a) "Hi, Ben." and (c) "Nice weather today." — small
   talk that does no job. (b) "The pick was here all along."
   stays: it moves the plot (the pick is the clue).
5. Sample: Ana: "I noticed the buzz during your garden practice,
   so I took it to the music room." (Reveals character: Ana is
   attentive and caring.) Ana: "I left a note on your desk — did
   you see it?" (Moves the plot: the missing note.)
6. Three errors: (1) missing comma before the tag — "gone,"
   Maya said; (2) the question mark belongs inside the closing
   quotation mark — "when did you see it last?" (3) missing
   comma after "Yesterday" — "Yesterday," Maya said. Fixed:
   *"My guitar is gone," Maya said. Ben asked, "When did you
   see it last?" "Yesterday," Maya said.*
7. Sample: worried — "'The show starts at seven,' Maya said, her
   hands shaking." Excited — "'The show starts at seven!' Ben
   said, bouncing on his toes." Tired — "'The show starts at
   seven,' Maya said, sinking into a chair." (Tags and beats
   carry the feeling; no adverbs.)
8. (b) does the most work: it reveals the mistake (she carried an
   empty case and never looked inside) and the character change
   (she owns it). (a) only states the fact.

## Practice D — Pacing: scene vs. summary

1. (a) Summary. (b) Scene.
2. Sample: "Ben pulled the cupboard door. It stuck. He tugged
   again, and it opened with a squeak that made him jump."
   (Actions one at a time; one sense — the squeak.)
3. Sample: "That morning Maya opened the case, searched the
   house, and called Ben. By the time he arrived, she was
   waiting at the gate." (Time words: "That morning," "By the
   time"; list: "opened…, searched…, called…")
4. Scene: the minute the curtain opens — it is the climax the
   reader waited for, so the reader should be there for it.
   Summary: the week of rehearsals — repetition the reader only
   needs to know happened.
5. Sample: "Maya walked to school past the bakery, the park, and
   the library. By the time she reached the gate, Ben was
   already waiting." (A list and a time word replace seven
   sentences of walking.)
6. Sample: "Ana's hand shook as she held out the note. The paper
   was folded twice, and Maya's name was written on it in Ana's
   neat handwriting. Ana looked at her shoes and waited."
   (Actions one at a time; one sense — the folded paper.)
7. (a) "before" — the hall was empty *before* the show (not
   "suddenly," which names no time). (b) "after" — *after* an
   hour, he had opened every cupboard (a completed stretch of
   time, not simultaneous "meanwhile").
8. Lesson 04 slowed the footbridge minute because it is the
   turning point — the clue that changes the search — so the
   reader should be there for it. The morning search is
   repetition (cupboard after cupboard); the reader only needs to
   know it failed.

## Practice E — Precise words, transitions, and project plan

1. (a) "said something" → "whispered" — quiet and secretive,
   fitting a clue shared on a bridge. (b) "a good place" → "a
   quiet place" — the music room is quiet, at the end of the
   hall. (c) "a nice thing" → "a kind surprise" — Ana restrung
   the guitar to help, so the motive is named. Accept any precise
   replacement with a reason tied to meaning.
2. Sample: "Maya stood in the doorway of the music room, and the
   smell of wood polish and old rosin came out to meet her." It
   adds the reader's nose — the smell places them inside the
   room instead of outside it.
3. Sample: "Ana folded the note twice, then unfolded it to check
   her spelling, then folded it again." (Fussing with the note
   shows guilt and nervousness; "guilty" is never used.)
4. (b) "a kind surprise" has the right connotation: the note and
   the new strings show care. (a) "a sneaky trick" implies deceit,
   which contradicts the evidence — she left a note, restrung
   the guitar, and invited a duet.
5. (a) "By" dusk. (b) "Across" (or "Beyond") the footbridge.
   (c) "After" the show. Each names the time or place shift.
6. (b) follows: it names the lesson (ask before assuming) and the
   habit (check the case), both earned by the events. (a) "never
   left her guitar anywhere again" is a vague promise, not a
   lesson tied to what she learned — the guitar was not lost by
   leaving it; it was borrowed.
7. **Project plan (sample, Prompt A).** Beats: (1) opening — Maya
   kneels on the footbridge at dusk, the red pick in her hand;
   (2) rising — she retraces yesterday, garden to footbridge;
   (3) climax — the music-room door opens and the guitar stands
   there with new strings; (4) falling — Ana's note, the duet
   invitation; (5) resolution — the show, and Maya's reflection
   on the walk home. Narrator: first person — the story is about
   Maya's worry changing, and the reader must be inside her head.
   Opening line: "The talent show was tonight at seven, and my
   guitar was gone." Minute to slow: the moment Maya's fingers
   close around the pick — it is the turning point.
8. **Self-check (sample).** Objective 1 — the five beats in order
   with because-links. Objective 2 — the footbridge dialogue and
   the slowed pick scene. Objective 3 — "gleamed," "wood polish

## Project — A Riverbend Story (project.md) — 8 points

Score each criterion 2 / 1 / 0 against the rubric bands in
project.md. Guidance:

- **Sequence and orientation (2 points):** 2 = the five beats are
  in natural order with because-links and the orientation names
  context, narrator, and characters. 1 = one gap in the chain or
  one orientation part missing. 0 = episodes out of order or no
  working orientation.
- **Dialogue, pacing, description (2 points):** 2 = at least two
  dialogue lines that do a job, one slowed scene, and the rest
  summarized with clear pacing control. 1 = dialogue present but
  partly small talk, or one wrong speed. 0 = no working dialogue
  or everything one speed.
- **Precise words and sensory detail (2 points):** 2 = precise
  verbs and adjectives throughout, at least two sensory details,
  feelings shown. 1 = some precise words, one sensory detail.
  0 = vague words throughout, no sensory detail.
- **Transitions and conclusion (2 points):** 2 = transitions name
  time/place shifts (not only "and then") and the conclusion
  passes the three tests. 1 = mostly "and then," or a loosely
  connected conclusion. 0 = no working transitions, or a
  conclusion that contradicts the story.
- **Oral reading (complete / incomplete):** complete = the opening
  orients the reader (context, narrator, character audible) and
  the reading is clear. Incomplete = the opening does not orient,
  or the reading cannot be followed.
- **Revision note:** the note must name 3+ concrete changes
  (cut, slowed, replaced, added) with reasons. A note that only
  says "I fixed spelling" does not count toward portfolio
  readiness, even if the story scores 7–8.
