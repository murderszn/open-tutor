# Lesson 01 — Event Sequences That Unfold Naturally

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can open a story so the reader knows
where they are, who is telling it, and who is in it — and I can put
the events in an order that unfolds naturally, one thing leading to
the next."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** W.6.3.a (engage and orient the reader; organize an
event sequence that unfolds naturally and logically); RL.6.3
(plot unfolds in episodes).

## Launch (5 min)

"Think of a story that confused you. Usually the confusion is not
the words — it is the order. The writer told you the ending first,
or skipped the moment that explains everything, or introduced a
person you were supposed to already know. A story that unfolds
naturally feels like walking down a path: each step follows from
the one before it, and you can always see where you are. Today's
job is the path: where the story starts, who is telling it, and
what happens first, second, and third."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Here are three events from a story, out of order. Which order
unfolds naturally?

(a) Maya finds a red guitar pick on the footbridge.
(b) Maya opens her guitar case and finds it empty.
(c) Maya and Ben search the garden shed.

(The natural order is (b) → (c) → (a): the empty case is the
problem that starts the search, and the pick is found during the
search. Finding the pick first would leave the reader asking,
"Why is she on the footbridge, and why does a pick matter?")

## Explicit teaching

A narrative opens with an **orientation**: it **establishes a
context** (where and when the story happens), **introduces a
narrator** (who is telling the story — "I" or a voice outside the
story), and **introduces the characters** (who the story is about).
A reader who gets all three in the first paragraph can follow
everything after it. A reader who gets none of them spends the
whole story guessing.

An **event sequence** is the order the events happen in the story.
It **unfolds naturally and logically** when each event follows from
the one before it: a problem appears, someone reacts to it, the
reaction causes the next event. An **episode** is one chunk of that
sequence — a scene in one place at one time, like "the morning in
Maya's bedroom" or "the search in the garden shed." Most stories
move episode by episode; jumping between episodes without a bridge
is the most common way a story confuses its reader.

Two tests for your own sequence: the **because test** ("Did this
happen *because* the last thing happened?") and the **missing-step
test** ("Would a reader ask, 'Wait — how did we get here?'").

## The passage — "The Morning of the Show" (original)

This is Scene 1 of "The Missing Guitar," the story this unit
follows lesson by lesson. Maya is the narrator; she tells the
story as "I."

> **Paragraph 1.** The talent show was tonight at seven, and I
> was going to play "River Light," the song I wrote about our
> river. I opened my guitar case on my bedroom floor to tune up
> before school — and the case was empty.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** I stared at the blue lining for a whole minute.
> I had practiced in the garden yesterday after school, then
> carried the case home by its handle at dusk. It had felt light
> as a lunchbox. I had not thought about that until now.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** I looked under the bed, behind the door, and
> inside the closet. I checked the hallway, the kitchen, and the
> back step. The guitar was not in the house. My stomach felt
> like a stone in a sock.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** I called Ben. "My guitar is gone," I said.
> "The show is tonight." He said, "I'm coming over. We'll retrace
> your steps — garden, auditorium, library. Somewhere between
> yesterday and now, it has to be."

*All names, places, and events in this passage are invented
practice material for this unit. Riverbend School is fictional.*

## Worked example 1 — naming the orientation

**Model:** "The orientation is all in paragraph 1. Context: the
talent show is tonight at seven, and Maya is in her bedroom in
the morning — we know *when* (show tonight, morning now) and
*where* (her bedroom). Narrator: Maya tells it as 'I' — first
person. Characters: Maya, and her guitar, which is really the
second character of this story because everything happens to it.
A reader who reads only paragraph 1 already knows the problem:
the guitar is gone and the show is tonight. That is an
orientation doing its job."

## Worked example 2 — testing the sequence with "because"

**Model:** "Test the episodes with *because*. Maya opens the
case *because* the show is tonight and she needs to tune up. She
searches the house *because* the case is empty. She calls Ben
*because* the guitar is not in the house. Each episode happens
because of the one before it — the chain holds. Now imagine the
order scrambled: Maya calls Ben, then opens the case, then
practices in the garden. The chain breaks: she would be calling
Ben before she knows anything is wrong. That is what a sequence
that does *not* unfold naturally feels like."

## Guided practice

1. In one sentence each, name the context, the narrator, and the
   main character of "The Morning of the Show."
2. List the four episodes of the passage in order (one short phrase
   each: e.g., "opens the case").
3. For episodes 2, 3, and 4, write one sentence each starting
   "This happens because…" and finish it from the passage.
4. A classmate moves paragraph 3 (the house search) to before
   paragraph 1 (opening the case). Explain in two sentences why
   that breaks the sequence.

## Independent practice

1. Which sentence orients the reader to *when* the story happens?
   Quote it, and explain in one sentence what it tells you.
2. The narrator is first person ("I"). Rewrite the first sentence
   of paragraph 1 in third person ("Maya…"). What is lost, and
   what is gained? Answer in two sentences.
3. Write a two-sentence orientation for a story about a character
   who loses something important on the day of a big event. Your
   orientation must name the context, the narrator, and the
   character.
4. Put these four episodes in natural order and number them 1–4:
   (a) Maya carries the empty case home at dusk. (b) Maya
   practices in the garden after school. (c) Maya opens the case
   in the morning. (d) Maya calls Ben. Explain your order in one
   sentence using *because*.
5. A story begins: "The trophy was gone. Maya had won it last
   spring. She searched the auditorium." Which episode is missing
   between sentences 2 and 3? Name it in one sentence.
6. Reread paragraph 2. Why does the detail "light as a lunchbox"
   matter to the story? Answer in two sentences.

## Applied task

Write a five-episode plan for a short story of your own (a
character loses something on an important day). For each episode,
write one phrase naming what happens, then one sentence starting
"because" that links it to the episode before it. Then write the
orientation paragraph (3–4 sentences) that establishes context,
narrator, and character. You will use this plan again in the
project — keep it.

## Exit check

In one sentence, what does an orientation give the reader that a
story without one does not? (Answer in your own words.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** read the passage aloud together
  first; pre-teach *orientation* (the opening that sets where,
  when, and who), *episode* (one chunk of the story in one place
  at one time), and *retrace* (go back over your steps). A
  learner may answer guided practice orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** the episode order may be shown as
  a four-box chain (Episode 1 → 2 → 3 → 4) with a "because"
  arrow between boxes, instead of a written list.
- **Extension:** rewrite paragraph 4 so Ben arrives *before*
  Maya searches the house, and explain in two sentences how that
  one change breaks the because-chain.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners treat the orientation as "the
first sentence" rather than a job (context + narrator +
characters) that can take a whole paragraph, and they order
episodes by excitement instead of by cause. Reteach with the
because test: if two episodes cannot be joined by *because*,
something is missing or out of order. Answer key: see
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
