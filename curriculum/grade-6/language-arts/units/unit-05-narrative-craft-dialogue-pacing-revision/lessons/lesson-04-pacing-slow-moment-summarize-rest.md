# Lesson 04 — Pacing: Slow the Moment, Summarize the Rest

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can control the speed of my story —
slow down into a scene for the moment that matters, and summarize
quickly past the parts that do not."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** W.6.3.b (use pacing to develop experiences, events,
and/or characters).

## Launch (5 min)

"Yesterday Maya found a red pick on the footbridge. That moment
took about one minute of story time — but in a good story, the
most important minute can take a whole page, while a whole morning
can take four sentences. Writers control time the way a camera
controls a shot: close up for the moment that matters, wide and
fast for the rest. Today we learn the two speeds — scene and
summary — and when to use each."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which moment deserves a slow scene, and which deserves a quick
summary?

(a) Maya's whole morning of searching cupboards and sheds.
(b) The minute Maya kneels on the footbridge and picks up the
red pick.

((b) deserves the scene: it is the turning point, the clue that
changes the search. (a) deserves the summary: the searching
matters, but the reader does not need every cupboard. A writer
who slows down every cupboard bores the reader; a writer who
summarizes the turning point cheats them.)

## Explicit teaching

**Pacing** is how fast or slow a story moves. It has two speeds:

- A **scene** happens in real time, moment by moment, with action,
  dialogue, and sensory detail. It is slow: one minute of story
  time can take a page. Use a scene for the moment that matters
  most — the turning point, the discovery, the decision.
- A **summary** covers a stretch of time quickly, in a few
  sentences: "We searched the auditorium, the shed, and the
  library. Nothing." It is fast: a whole morning can take four
  sentences. Use a summary for travel, waiting, repetition, and
  everything the reader needs to know but does not need to live
  through.

To slow a moment down, add **one small action at a time** (kneel,
reach, turn the pick over), **one sense at a time** (the cold
planks, the glint of red), and **short sentences** that make the
reader wait with the character. To speed past time, use **time
words** ("then," "after that," "by noon"), **lists** ("the stage,
the shed, the library"), and **one sentence per place or hour**.

The test is the reader's question: "Would I rather *be there* for
this, or just *know* it happened?" If the answer is "be there,"
write a scene. If it is "just know," write a summary.

## The passage — "The Same Minute, Two Speeds" (original)

Scene 4 of "The Missing Guitar." First the footbridge minute in
slow motion, then the morning's search in summary.

> **Part A — the scene (slow).** I knelt. The planks were cold
> through my jeans, and one of them rocked a little under my knee.
> The pick lay between two boards, red against the grey wood, with
> the chip on one edge catching the last light. I reached for it
> slowly, as if it might blow away. My fingers closed around it.
> It was warm from the sun. Behind me, the river moved under the
> bridge, quiet as breathing. The heron did not move at all.
>
> **Part B — the summary (fast).** That morning we had searched
> the auditorium, the garden shed, and the library. Ben opened
> every cupboard twice. I checked under the stage and behind the
> curtains. By noon we had found nothing but dust, a lost mitten,
> and a pencil with no eraser.

*All names, places, and events in this passage are invented
practice material for this unit. Riverbend School is fictional.*

## Worked example 1 — naming what slows a scene down

**Model:** "Part A slows the minute down with three tools. First,
small actions one at a time: knelt, reached, closed my fingers —
each action gets its own sentence, so the reader feels the time
pass. Second, senses: cold planks, the red against grey wood, the
river 'quiet as breathing.' Third, short sentences: 'It was warm
from the sun.' 'The heron did not move at all.' Short sentences
make the reader pause, the way Maya pauses. The scene is about
seventy words for about one minute of story time. That is the
right speed for a turning point — the reader is kneeling on the
bridge with her."

## Worked example 2 — naming what speeds a summary up

**Model:** "Part B covers a whole morning — about four hours — in
four sentences. The tools are the opposite of the scene's: time
words ('That morning,' 'By noon'), a list ('the auditorium, the
garden shed, and the library'), and one sentence per place. The
funny little list at the end — 'dust, a lost mitten, and a pencil
with no eraser' — tells the reader the search failed without
spending a paragraph on each empty cupboard. The reader knows the
morning happened and knows it failed, and the story can move on
to the footbridge. If Part B had been written as a scene, the
reader would have spent a page watching Ben open cupboards —
and would have stopped caring before the pick appeared."

## Guided practice

1. In Part A, find one small action, one sense detail, and one
   short sentence. Quote each and explain in one sentence what it
   slows down.
2. In Part B, find the time words and the list. Quote them, and
   explain in one sentence how they speed the morning up.
3. Which part is a scene and which is a summary? Give one reason
   for each, using the reader's question ("be there" vs. "just
   know").
4. Rewrite this summary as the start of a scene (2–3 sentences):
   "Maya walked home across the footbridge at dusk." Slow down
   one detail — the light, the river, or the weight of the case.

## Independent practice

1. A classmate writes a scene about tying a shoe that takes a
   whole page, and a summary of the talent show in one sentence.
   Explain in two sentences what is wrong with the pacing.
2. Slow down this moment into a 3-sentence scene: "Ana opened the
   music-room door." Use one action at a time and one sense.
3. Summarize this morning in two sentences: Maya woke up, ate
   breakfast, packed her school bag, walked to school, and waited
   for Ben at the gate. Use a time word and a list.
4. Which moment in "The Missing Guitar" so far deserves a scene,
   and which deserves a summary? Name one of each and give a
   reason for each in one sentence.
5. Rewrite this sentence twice — once slowed down, once sped up:
   "The guitar was in the music room." (Slowed: at least two
   sentences, with a sense detail. Sped up: fold it into a
   summary sentence with two other facts.)
6. A story has ten episodes and the writer makes every one a
   scene. What will the reader feel by episode six, and why?
   Answer in two sentences.

## Applied task

Take the dialogue scene you wrote in Lesson 03. Choose the one
minute inside it that matters most. Rewrite that minute as a
slow scene (4–6 sentences: small actions, one sense, short
sentences). Then write a 2-sentence summary of everything in the
scene that happened before that minute. Label the two parts
"scene" and "summary."

## Exit check

In one sentence, how do you decide whether a moment deserves a
scene or a summary? (Answer in your own words.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** read the passage aloud together
  first, slowly for Part A and quickly for Part B, so the speed
  difference can be heard; pre-teach *pacing* (how fast or slow
  a story moves), *scene* (a moment told in real time), and
  *summary* (a stretch of time told quickly). A learner may
  answer guided practice orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** pacing may be shown on a timeline
  — a long bar for a summary stretch, a magnifying circle for a
  slowed scene — with one sentence under each.
- **Extension:** take Part B's summary and expand one item in it
  ("a lost mitten") into a 3-sentence scene, then explain in one
  sentence why the story is better with the mitten summarized
  instead.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners think "slow" means "more
adjectives" and "fast" means "shorter sentences," and they slow
down the wrong moments (the walk to school) while summarizing
the turning point. Reteach with the reader's question: "Would I
rather be there for this, or just know it happened?" Answer key:
see [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
