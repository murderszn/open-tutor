# Lesson 05 — Precise Words and Sensory Detail

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can choose precise words and sensory
details so the reader sees, hears, and feels the scene — and I can
show what a character feels instead of just naming the feeling."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** W.6.3.d (use precise words and phrases, relevant
descriptive details, and sensory language); L.6.5 (interpret
figures of speech; distinguish connotations and denotations;
analyze the impact of specific word choices).

## Launch (5 min)

"Yesterday we learned when to slow down. Today we learn what to
put in the slow moment. Compare these two sentences: 'Maya went
into the room and felt nervous.' 'Maya pushed the music-room door
open with one finger, and the smell of wood polish and old rosin
came out to meet her; her heart was beating in her throat.' The
first sentence tells you she was nervous. The second one makes
you nervous with her. The difference is precise words and
sensory detail — and it is the difference between a report and a
story."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which sentence shows, and which sentence tells?

(a) "Maya was scared."
(b) "Maya's hands shook so much she had to hold the case with
both arms."

((b) shows: the shaking hands let the reader feel the fear
without the word "scared." (a) tells: it names the feeling and
gives the reader nothing to picture. Showing takes more words,
but the reader remembers it longer.)

## Explicit teaching

**Precise words** are exact words: not "went" but "crept," "strode,"
or "hurried"; not "nice" but "warm," "steady," or "kind." A precise
verb or adjective does the work of a whole sentence. **Sensory
language** is detail for the five senses — sight, sound, smell,
taste, touch — that puts the reader inside the scene. One strong
sensory detail beats five vague ones: the smell of wood polish
tells you more about the music room than "it was a nice room."

**Show vs. tell:** *telling* names the feeling or judgment
("she was nervous," "it was beautiful"). *Showing* gives the
evidence and lets the reader conclude it (shaking hands, a room
that smells of polish and rosin). A story needs both — telling is
fine for small facts ("the show was at seven") — but feelings,
character, and important places should be shown.

Word choice also carries **connotation**: the feeling a word
brings with it. *Cheap* and *thrifty* both mean "spends little,"
but one insults and the other praises (Unit 04). In a narrative,
connotation is a tool: "the case felt light as a lunchbox"
suggests something wrong without saying "the guitar was gone."

## The passage — "The Music Room" (original)

Scene 5 of "The Missing Guitar." Maya narrates.

> **Paragraph 1.** The music room was at the end of the hall,
> past the auditorium. I pushed the door open with one finger.
> The smell came out first: wood polish and old rosin, and
> underneath it, the clean smell of new guitar strings.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** My guitar was standing on its stand in the
> middle of the room, catching the late light from the window.
> The strings gleamed. Someone had wiped the wood until it
> shone. A folded note leaned against the stand, with my name on
> it in Ana's neat handwriting.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** I did not run to it. I stood in the doorway
> and let my breathing slow down. The room was quiet except for
> the clock ticking and the far-off sound of chairs being set up
> in the auditorium. My hands had stopped shaking.

*All names, places, and events in this passage are invented
practice material for this unit. Riverbend School is fictional.*

## Worked example 1 — precise words doing the work

**Model:** "Look at paragraph 1: 'I pushed the door open with
one finger.' Not 'opened the door' — 'pushed… with one finger'
tells you Maya is being careful, almost afraid of what she will
find, without ever saying 'she was afraid.' And 'the smell came
out first' is precise: smells arrive before sights when a door
opens. In paragraph 2, 'gleamed' and 'shone' are doing character
work too — the guitar was cared for, not dumped. Someone loved
this guitar. That is the clue, before the note is even read, that
the guitar was borrowed by a friend, not taken by a thief. The
words are the evidence."

## Worked example 2 — showing a feeling

**Model:** "Paragraph 3 never says 'Maya felt relieved.' Instead
it shows relief three ways: 'I did not run to it' (her body
slows down), 'I stood in the doorway and let my breathing slow
down' (her breath changes), and 'My hands had stopped shaking'
(the shaking from Lesson 02 is gone). The reader feels the relief
because the body tells it. Compare with telling: 'Maya felt
relieved and happy.' That version is shorter, but it leaves the
reader outside the room. When a feeling matters, show the body."

## Guided practice

1. Find one precise verb and one sensory detail in paragraph 1.
   Quote each and explain in one sentence what it shows.
2. What does the word "gleamed" tell you that "was there" would
   not? Answer in two sentences.
3. Paragraph 3 shows relief without naming it. List the three
   body details that show it.
4. Rewrite this telling sentence as a showing sentence (one or two
   sentences): "Ben was excited about the show."

## Independent practice

1. Replace each vague word with a precise one, and explain your
   choice in one sentence each: (a) "Maya walked into the room."
   (b) "The guitar sounded good." (c) "Ana was a nice friend."
2. Write one sentence using smell, one using sound, and one using
   touch to describe the auditorium before the show. Do not use
   the word "nice" in any of them.
3. A classmate writes: "The room was very, very beautiful and
   amazing." Explain in two sentences why this sentence shows
   almost nothing, and rewrite it as one showing sentence.
4. Which has the stronger connotation for a borrowed guitar, and
   why? (a) "Someone had stolen it." (b) "Someone had borrowed
   it." Answer in two sentences.
5. Show, don't tell: write two sentences showing that Ana is
   proud of her surprise, without using the word "proud."
6. Reread paragraph 2. Which detail is the most important clue
   that the guitar was borrowed, not stolen? Explain in two
   sentences.

## Applied task

Take the dialogue scene you wrote in Lesson 03. Add a
two-sentence description of the place where it happens, using one
sensory detail (sound, smell, or touch) and one precise verb.
Then find one telling sentence in your own draft ("she was
nervous," "he was angry") and rewrite it as a showing sentence
using a body detail. Label the two rewrites "sensory" and
"showing."

## Exit check

In one sentence, why is "her hands stopped shaking" stronger
than "she felt relieved"? (Answer in your own words.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** read the passage aloud together
  first; pre-teach *precise* (exact — the right word, not a
  nearly-right word), *sensory language* (detail for sight,
  sound, smell, taste, touch), *rosin* (the sticky resin
  musicians rub on bows and strings), and *show vs. tell*
  (showing gives evidence; telling names the feeling). A learner
  may answer guided practice orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** sensory details may be collected
  in a five-senses table (see / hear / smell / touch / taste)
  for the music room, instead of in sentences.
- **Extension:** rewrite paragraph 1 from the guitar's point of
  view in two sentences (personification — Unit 04), keeping
  every event the same, and explain in one sentence what the
  personification adds.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners pile on adjectives ("very, very
beautiful") instead of choosing one precise word, and they
believe showing means "more description" rather than "evidence
the reader can conclude from." Reteach with the body test: "What
is the character's body doing? What would a camera see?" Answer
key: see [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
