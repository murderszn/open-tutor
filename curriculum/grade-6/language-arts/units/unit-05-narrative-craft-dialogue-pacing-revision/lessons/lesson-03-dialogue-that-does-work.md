# Lesson 03 — Dialogue That Does Work

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can write dialogue that sounds like
real people talking, punctuate it correctly, and make every line
do a job — reveal character, move the plot, or raise tension."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper;
the unit illustration (or its text-only alternative).
**Standards:** W.6.3.b (use dialogue to develop experiences,
events, and/or characters); L.6.1, L.6.2 (conventions applied in
drafting); RL.6.3 (character response).

## Launch (5 min)

"So far Maya's story has been mostly narration — Maya telling us
what happened. But the moment that matters most in a story is
often the moment people talk. Dialogue is not decoration. A good
line of dialogue does one of three jobs: it shows what a character
is like, it moves the plot forward, or it raises tension. A line
that does none of those three is small talk, and small talk
belongs in life, not in a story. Today we listen to Maya and Ben
on the footbridge — and we look at the moment itself."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which line does a job, and which line is small talk?

(a) "Hi." "Hi." "Nice weather." "Yes, very nice."
(b) "Ben, stop. I think I left it somewhere. We need the
footbridge, not the cupboards."

((b) does a job: it moves the plot (new plan: the footbridge)
and shows Maya changing (she is the one with the plan now). (a)
does nothing — it could be cut and the story would not notice.)

## Explicit teaching

**Dialogue** is the exact words characters say out loud, inside
**quotation marks**. Four rules keep it readable:

1. **New speaker, new paragraph.** Every time the speaker
   changes, start a new paragraph — even for one word.
2. **Quotation marks** go around the exact words spoken. The
   comma, question mark, or exclamation mark that ends the spoken
   words usually goes *inside* the closing quotation mark:
   "My guitar is gone," I said.
3. A **speaker tag** names who is speaking ("I said," "Ben
   asked"). Put the tag before, after, or in the middle of the
   spoken words — but vary it, and do not tag every line once a
   back-and-forth is clear.
4. An **action beat** is a small action instead of a tag: *Ben
   closed the cupboard.* Beats show feeling without naming it —
   a slammed case says more than "she said angrily."

Every line of dialogue should do at least one job: **reveal
character** (what this person is like), **move the plot** (change
what happens next), or **raise tension** (make the reader want to
know what happens next). Cut greetings, small talk, and lines
that repeat what the narration already said.

## The passage — "On the Footbridge" (original)

Scene 3 of "The Missing Guitar." Maya narrates; the dialogue
carries the scene.

> **Paragraph 1.** At dusk we reached the footbridge. The river
> was low and slow, and a heron stood in the reeds, not moving
> at all. My case was not there. But something small and red
> glinted between the planks, right where I had set the case
> down yesterday.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** I knelt and picked it up. It was my red guitar
> pick, with the chip on one edge. "Ben," I said, "look."
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Ben leaned over the rail. "A pick? That's it?
> We've been searching all day for a pick?"
>
> **Paragraph 4.** "No," I said. "It's proof I was here. I dropped
> it yesterday when I packed up. Which means I packed up here —
> and I carried the case home myself."
>
> **Paragraph 5.** Ben was quiet for a second. "So nobody took
> it," he said slowly. "Then where is it?"
>
> **Paragraph 6.** I turned the pick over in my fingers. "I don't
> know yet," I said. "But I know I wasn't robbed. I was careless.
> And careless I can fix."

*All names, places, and events in this passage are invented
practice material for this unit. Riverbend School is fictional.
The illustration is an AI-generated image for this lesson; it
names no one and proves nothing about any real event.*

## Worked example 1 — what each line does

**Model:** "Ben's line in paragraph 3 — 'A pick? That's it?
We've been searching all day for a pick?' — does two jobs at
once. It reveals character: Ben is impatient and wants a big
answer, not a small clue. And it raises tension: the reader
wonders whether Maya will give up. Maya's answer in paragraph 4
does the third job: it moves the plot. 'It's proof I was here'
turns a tiny object into the clue that changes the whole search.
Notice what the dialogue never does: nobody says 'hello,' nobody
repeats what the narration already told us ('the river was low'),
and nobody explains their feelings in a speech. The feelings are
in the beats — Maya kneeling, turning the pick over in her
fingers."

## Worked example 2 — fixing flat dialogue

**Model:** "Flat version: 'Hello, Ben,' Maya said. 'Hello, Maya,'
Ben said. 'Did you find the pick?' Maya asked. 'Yes,' Ben said.
'That is nice,' Maya said. Every line is tagged, nothing is
revealed, and the plot does not move. Fixed version: 'Ben,' I
said, holding up the pick. He leaned over the rail. 'A pick?
That's it?' Two tags are gone, the beat (holding up the pick,
leaning over the rail) does the work, and the question carries
Ben's impatience. Rule of thumb: if you can cut a line and lose
nothing, cut it."

## Guided practice

1. Copy one line of dialogue from the passage that reveals
   character, one that moves the plot, and one that raises
   tension. Label each with its job.
2. In paragraph 5, Ben says his line "slowly." What does that one
   word tell you about Ben in that moment? Answer in one
   sentence.
3. Find the action beat in paragraph 6. What does it show about
   Maya that the words alone do not? Answer in two sentences.
4. Rewrite this flat exchange so every line does a job (2–3 lines
   of dialogue, tags or beats as needed): "Hi," said Ana. "Hi,"
   said Maya. "How are you?" asked Ana. "I am fine," said Maya.

## Independent practice

1. Punctuate this dialogue correctly, with a new paragraph for
   each speaker: *where is the guitar Maya asked I don't know Ben
   said did you check the music room*
2. A classmate writes: "'I am very sad,' Maya said sadly. 'I am
   also sad,' Ben said sadly." Name two problems with these lines
   and fix them in two lines of dialogue.
3. Write two lines of dialogue for Ana arriving at the footbridge
   and seeing the pick. Her first line must reveal something
   about Ana; her second line must move the plot. (Ana has not
   told anyone yet that she borrowed the guitar.)
4. Which is the stronger speaker tag, and why? (a) "said Maya"
   (b) "Maya exclaimed loudly and angrily." Answer in two
   sentences.
5. Write one line of dialogue in which a character says one thing
   but means another (for example, "Great. Just great."). Then
   explain in one sentence what the character really means.
6. Look at the unit illustration again. Write two more lines of
   dialogue — Maya and Ben — about the kite caught in the branch.
   One line must raise a question the reader wants answered.

## Applied task

Write a short dialogue scene (6–8 lines) between two characters
from your Lesson 01 story: the moment your character tells
someone else what was lost. Use at least two action beats, vary
your tags, start a new paragraph for each speaker, and make sure
every line reveals character, moves the plot, or raises tension.
Underline the line that does the most work, and write one
sentence saying which job it does.

## Exit check

In one sentence, what is the difference between dialogue that
does a job and small talk? (Answer in your own words.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** read the passage aloud together
  first, with two voices for Maya and Ben; pre-teach *dialogue*
  (the exact words characters say), *speaker tag* (the "she
  said" that names the speaker), *action beat* (a small action
  that shows feeling), and *glinted* (shone with a quick flash
  of light). A learner may answer guided practice orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** dialogue lines may be acted out
  with the adult, or written as a two-column script (Speaker |
  Words) instead of paragraphs.
- **Extension:** rewrite paragraph 5–6 as a scene in which Ben
  is the one who finds the pick. Keep every event the same, and
  explain in two sentences how the dialogue changes when the
  finder changes.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners tag every line with "said" plus an
adverb ("said sadly"), and they write dialogue that repeats the
narration instead of adding to it. Reteach with the three-jobs
test: cover a line and ask, "What would the story lose without
this line?" If the answer is "nothing," cut it. Answer key: see
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
