# Lesson 06 — Transitions and Conclusions

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can move my reader through time and
place with transition words, phrases, and clauses — and I can end
a story with a conclusion that grows out of what actually
happened."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** W.6.3.c (use a variety of transition words, phrases,
and clauses to convey sequence and signal shifts from one time
frame or setting to another); W.6.3.e (provide a conclusion that
follows from the narrated experiences or events); W.6.5 (revise).

## Launch (5 min)

"We have the whole story now: the empty case, the search, the
pick, the music room. Two jobs remain. First, the bridges between
episodes — the words that carry the reader from morning to dusk,
from the garden to the footbridge, without a bump. Second, the
ending. A good ending does not arrive from nowhere, and it does
not just stop. It grows out of what happened — and it shows what
the narrator learned. Today we build the bridges and the ending."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which ending follows from Maya's story, and which does not?

(a) "From then on, Maya never trusted anyone again."
(b) "From then on, Maya asked before she assumed."

((b) follows: Maya learned she had assumed the worst and been
wrong — asking first is the lesson the events taught. (a) does
not follow: nobody betrayed Maya; Ana borrowed the guitar to
help. An ending that contradicts the story is worse than no
ending at all.)

## Explicit teaching

**Transitions** are words, phrases, and clauses that carry the
reader across a gap: a gap in time ("later," "by dusk," "the next
morning"), a gap in place ("across the footbridge," "at the end
of the hall"), or a gap between ideas ("meanwhile" — something
else happening at the same time). A good transition does two
things: it tells the reader *what changed* (time? place?) and it
keeps the sentence moving. A transition clause can do even more:
"By the time we reached the footbridge, the sun was low" tells
you the time, the place, and the light in one breath.

A **conclusion** is the ending of a narrative. It must **follow
from** the narrated experiences: the narrator's last thought, the
change, or the resolution should be something the events earned.
Three tests for a conclusion: (1) Could this ending happen after
*this* story and no other? (2) Does it match what the narrator
learned, not what the writer wishes they had learned? (3) Does it
close the problem the story opened? Maya's story opened with a
missing guitar and a show at seven; its conclusion must settle
both — the guitar found, the show played, and the lesson learned.

## The passage — "The Note" (original)

Scene 6 of "The Missing Guitar." Maya narrates.

> **Paragraph 1.** The note said: *"Maya — I borrowed your guitar
> to put on new strings. Yours were buzzing, and I wanted tonight
> to sound right. I left a note on your desk, but it must have
> slid off. Forgive me? — Ana."* Underneath, in smaller writing:
> *"P.S. Play 'River Light' with me tonight? I'll be on piano."*
>
> **Paragraph 2.** By seven o'clock, the auditorium was full. The
> chairs squeaked, the lights buzzed, and my hands — for the first
> time all day — were still. Ana sat at the piano and nodded.
> I played the first notes of "River Light," and the new strings
> rang clear.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** Later, walking home across the footbridge under
> the stars, I thought about the day. I had spent it sure someone
> had taken something from me. Nobody had. I had carried an empty
> case home and blamed the world for it. From then on, I decided,
> I would ask before I assumed — and I would check the case
> before I carried it home.

*All names, places, and events in this passage are invented
practice material for this unit. Riverbend School is fictional.*

## Worked example 1 — naming the transitions

**Model:** "Three transitions do three different jobs here.
'By seven o'clock' (paragraph 2) is a time transition: it jumps
the reader from the music room to the show, hours later, without
a gap. 'Later, walking home across the footbridge under the
stars' (paragraph 3) is a time *and* place transition in one
clause: it tells you when (later, after the show) and where (the
footbridge) and even the light (under the stars). And 'From then
on' (paragraph 3) is a time transition that points forward: it
carries the lesson out of the story and into Maya's future. Notice
that none of them is 'and then.' 'And then' only says 'next' — it
never says *when* next, or *where* next."

## Worked example 2 — testing a conclusion

**Model:** "Test Maya's conclusion with the three tests. (1)
Could this ending happen after this story and no other? Yes: only
this story has an empty case carried home and a borrowed guitar.
(2) Does it match what the narrator learned? Yes: she learned she
assumed the worst and was wrong — 'I would ask before I assumed'
is exactly that lesson. (3) Does it close the problem? Yes: the
guitar is found, the show is played, and the case is a joke she
makes about herself. Now test the wrong ending: 'From then on,
Maya never trusted anyone again.' It fails test 2 — Ana helped
her, so distrust is not what the events taught — and it fails test
1, because that ending could follow almost any story. A conclusion
that could follow any story follows no story."

## Guided practice

1. Find one time transition, one place transition, and one
   transition clause in the passage. Quote each and name its job.
2. Why is "By seven o'clock" a better transition than "And then"?
   Answer in two sentences.
3. Test Maya's conclusion with the three tests. Write one sentence
   for each test.
4. A classmate ends the story: "And they all lived happily ever
   after." Explain in two sentences why that conclusion does not
   follow from this story.

## Independent practice

1. Fill each blank with a transition that names the time or place
   shift. (a) "___ the show, we walked home." (b) "___ the
   garden, the shed was dark and quiet." (c) "___ an hour of
   searching, Ben sat down on the stage." Explain each choice in
   one sentence.
2. Write a transition clause that moves a reader from the
   footbridge at dusk to the auditorium at seven o'clock. It must
   name both the time and the place.
3. Rewrite this weak ending so it follows from the story: "Maya
   learned a lesson." (Name the lesson and tie it to one event.)
4. A story ends: "He never forgot that day." Is that a conclusion
   that follows? Explain in two sentences what information you
   would need to judge it.
5. Put these three sentences in order and add a transition to the
   second and third: "Maya found the pick." / "Maya opened the
   music-room door." / "Maya played at the show."
6. Write a one-sentence conclusion for a story about a character
   who loses a library book and finds it in a friend's backpack.
   The conclusion must name what the character learned.

## Applied task

Take the five-episode plan from Lesson 01 and the paragraphs you
have drafted since. Write the conclusion paragraph (3–4
sentences) for your story: settle the problem, name what the
narrator learned, and end with a sentence that could only follow
from *your* story. Then test it with the three tests and write one
sentence for each test. Keep this with your plan — the project
starts next session.

## Exit check

In one sentence, what makes a conclusion follow from a story
instead of just ending it? (Answer in your own words.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** read the passage aloud together
  first; pre-teach *transition* (a word, phrase, or clause that
  carries the reader across a gap in time or place), *conclusion*
  (the ending of a narrative), and *meanwhile* (at the same time
  as something else). A learner may answer guided practice orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** transitions may be collected on a
  two-column chart (Time | Place) before they are placed in
  sentences; conclusions may be tested with a three-box check
  (This story only? / What was learned? / Problem closed?).
- **Extension:** rewrite paragraph 3 so the reflection happens
  in the music room instead of on the footbridge, and explain in
  two sentences how the setting changes what the conclusion can
  say.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners use "and then" for every gap and
write conclusions that moralize ("Always be nice") instead of
conclusions that grow from the events. Reteach with the three
tests, and ask, "Could this ending follow a different story? If
yes, it follows none." Answer key: see
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (adult only).
