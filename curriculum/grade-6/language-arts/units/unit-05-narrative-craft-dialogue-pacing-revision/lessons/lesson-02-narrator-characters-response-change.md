# Lesson 02 — Narrator and Characters: Response and Change

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can choose a narrator on purpose,
show what my characters are like through what they do and say,
and show a character changing as the story moves toward its
resolution."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** W.6.3.a–b (introduce a narrator and characters;
use narrative techniques to develop characters); RL.6.3 (how
characters respond or change as the plot moves toward a
resolution).

## Launch (5 min)

"Yesterday Maya opened an empty case. Today two people search for
the guitar — and they search like two different people. One of
them changes during the search. A story is not just what happens;
it is what happens *to people*, and how they answer back. Today
we watch two characters answer the same problem differently, and
we watch one of them change."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Maya and Ben both want the guitar back. Which searcher is which?

(a) Opens every cupboard, shouts "Found it?" every minute, checks
the same shelf twice.
(b) Stands still, thinks about where she last had the guitar, and
checks one place at a time.

((a) is Ben — fast, loud, and hopeful. (b) is Maya — she is
learning to slow down and think. The difference is not who cares
more; it is how each character responds to the same problem.)

## Explicit teaching

The **narrator** is the voice telling the story. In **first
person**, the narrator is a character in the story ("I opened the
case"). In **third person**, the narrator stands outside the story
("Maya opened the case"). First person lets the reader feel what
the narrator feels and know only what the narrator knows; third
person can show what several characters are doing at once. Neither
is better — the choice should match what the story needs. "The
Missing Guitar" uses first person because the story is about
Maya's worry changing into something else, and we need to be
inside her head to feel that change.

Characters are developed — made real — by **what they do, what
they say, and how they respond** when things go wrong, not by a
list of adjectives about them. "Ben is impatient" tells the reader
something; Ben opening the same cupboard twice and shouting every
minute *shows* it, and the reader believes it more.

**Change** is what makes a character feel alive: as the plot moves
toward its **resolution** (how the problem gets settled), a
character responds differently than they did at the start. The
change must be **shown in steps**, not announced once at the end:
first the old response, then a moment that makes the character
stop, then the new response. If a character is frantic in scene
one and calm in scene two with nothing between them, the reader
will not believe the change.

## The passage — "Two Searches" (original)

Scene 2 of "The Missing Guitar." Maya narrates.

> **Paragraph 1.** Ben arrived with a flashlight, a spare key to
> the auditorium, and a plan that was mostly shouting. "We'll
> check everywhere!" he said. "Stage, shed, library, everywhere!"
> He ran ahead of me to the auditorium and started opening
> cupboards — the same one twice.
>
> **Paragraph 2.** I followed him, and for the first ten minutes I
> searched the way he did: fast, loud, and everywhere at once.
> I opened the piano bench. I looked behind the curtains. I
> checked under the stage. Nothing. My hands were shaking, and
> the shaking made me slower, not faster.
>
> **Paragraph 3.** So I stopped. I stood in the middle of the
> empty stage and asked myself the question I should have asked
> in my bedroom: *Where did I last have it?* Yesterday after
> school, I had practiced in the garden. Then I had walked home
> across the footbridge at dusk, carrying the case. I remembered
> the heron standing in the reeds. I remembered setting the case
> down on the planks to watch it.
>
> **Paragraph 4.** "Ben," I said, "stop. I think I left it
> somewhere — or I dropped something. We need the footbridge,
> not the cupboards." Ben closed the cupboard he was holding
> open. "The footbridge?" he said. "Okay. Lead the way." For the
> first time that day, I was the one with the plan.

*All names, places, and events in this passage are invented
practice material for this unit. Riverbend School is fictional.*

## Worked example 1 — naming the narrator's job

**Model:** "The narrator is Maya, first person. We know this
because she says 'I followed him' and 'my hands were shaking' —
we are inside her body, not watching from outside. The choice
matters here: the story's real subject is not the guitar, it is
Maya's worry turning into a plan. A third-person narrator could
tell us she calmed down; a first-person narrator lets us feel the
shaking hands stop. If the story needed to show what Ana was
doing at the same time, first person would be the wrong choice —
Maya cannot see Ana. The narrator should match the story's need."

## Worked example 2 — tracing a change in two steps

**Model:** "Maya's change has two visible steps. Step 1 — the old
response: she searches the way Ben does, 'fast, loud, and
everywhere at once,' and her hands shake. Step 2 — the turn: she
stops, stands still on the stage, and asks 'Where did I last have
it?' Then the new response: she leads Ben to the footbridge with
a plan. The middle step is what makes the change believable —
without the stopping and the question, 'calm Maya' would appear
out of nowhere. When you write a change, always write the middle
step: the pause, the question, or the small thing noticed."

## Guided practice

1. Name the narrator of "Two Searches" and give two pieces of
   evidence from the passage (words the narrator uses).
2. What does Ben's searching show about Ben? Name the trait and
   quote one action that shows it.
3. List Maya's change in three steps: (a) her first response,
   (b) the moment she stops and thinks, (c) her new response.
   Quote one phrase for each step.
4. Why is paragraph 3 the most important paragraph for Maya's
   change? Answer in two sentences.

## Independent practice

1. Rewrite the first sentence of paragraph 1 in third person
   ("Ben arrived…"). What does the reader gain, and what does the
   reader lose? Answer in two sentences.
2. Ben does not change in this scene. Write two sentences
   explaining why that is fine — not every character must change
   in every scene.
3. A classmate writes: "Maya was scared. Then she was brave."
   Explain in two sentences why this does *not* show a change,
   and name the missing middle step.
4. Choose one: write two sentences showing that Ana is careful,
   without using the word "careful" and without telling the
   reader she is careful. (Show it through an action.)
5. Which line shows Maya taking charge? Quote it, and explain in
   one sentence how her words are different from Ben's words in
   paragraph 1.
6. Imagine the same scene told by Ben in first person. Write his
   first sentence, then explain in one sentence what the story
   would gain and what it would lose.

## Applied task

Take the orientation paragraph you wrote in Lesson 01. Write the
next two paragraphs of that story: one paragraph that shows your
character's first response to the problem (through action and
dialogue, not adjectives), and one paragraph that shows the middle
step — the pause, the question, or the small thing noticed that
starts the change. Label the three steps (old response / middle
step / new response) in the margin.

## Exit check

In one sentence, why does a character's change need a middle step
instead of just a before and an after? (Answer in your own words.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extension

- **Reading/language access:** read the passage aloud together
  first; pre-teach *narrator* (the voice telling the story),
  *first person* ("I" — the narrator is in the story), *third
  person* ("he/she/they" — the narrator stands outside),
  *resolution* (how the problem gets settled), and *response*
  (what a character does when something happens). A learner may
  answer guided practice orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** Maya's change may be drawn as a
  three-box strip (Frantic → Stops and thinks → Leads the way)
  with one quoted phrase under each box.
- **Extension:** rewrite paragraph 4 from Ben's point of view in
  first person (3–4 sentences), keeping every event the same,
  and explain in one sentence how the scene feels different.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners announce change ("she became
brave") instead of showing it in steps, and they confuse the
narrator with the author (Maya is a character; the author is the
person who wrote Maya). Reteach by asking, "What did the
character *do* differently, and what happened right before that?"
