# Lesson 06 — Consistent Style, Tone, and Formal English

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can write and speak in formal English
when the situation calls for it, and keep one style and tone all
the way through a piece."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** L.6.3.b (consistency in style and tone), SL.6.6
(adapt speech; formal English when indicated or appropriate),
L.6.1.e (variations from standard English — introduced).

## Launch (5 min)

"Two messages, same request: 'Hey, thanks a bunch for letting us
use the gym!' and 'Thank you for allowing us to use the
gymnasium.' Both are friendly. Only the second belongs in a letter
to a principal. Formal English is not 'better' English — it is
the register a situation calls for. A text to a friend and a
letter to a principal can both be correct and still need
completely different words."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which message fits a letter to the principal, and which fits a
note to a friend? Why?

(a) "We would appreciate your help with the spring show."
(b) "Gimme a hand with the show, ok?"

((a) fits the principal: complete sentence, polite form
("would appreciate"), no slang. (b) fits a friend: shortened
verb ("Gimme"), casual check ("ok?"). The words are the giveaway
— register lives in word choice and sentence completeness.)

## Explicit teaching

**Formal English** uses complete sentences, full word forms
(*do not* instead of *don't* in the most formal writing, *as soon
as possible* instead of *ASAP*), polite request forms (*May I…?
Please let us know whether…*), precise vocabulary, and standard
grammar. **Informal English** uses contractions, slang, shortened
forms, and sentence fragments for effect — fine with friends,
wrong for a principal, a judge, or a formal presentation.

**Consistency in style and tone:** pick the register your
audience and purpose require, then stay in it. A **tone slip** is
one sentence in the wrong register: "We respectfully request the
use of the auditorium for our spring show. It'll be super fun,
trust me!" The second sentence breaks the formal tone the first
sentence built. Fix the slip, not the whole letter: "We hope it
will be an enjoyable evening for the school community."

**Tone** is the writer's attitude showing through word choice
(respectful, excited, apologetic, businesslike). **Style** is the
habitual way the sentences are built (short and plain, or long
and formal). Both must stay steady: a formal letter that suddenly
turns chatty confuses the reader about how seriously to take it.

**Formal speaking (SL.6.6):** the same rules apply aloud. When
presenting findings to the class or a guest, use complete
sentences, formal vocabulary, and a steady pace — no "like, you
know, stuff." Rehearse the first and last sentences; openings
and closings are where slips cluster.

## Worked example 1 — informal to formal

**Problem:** Rewrite formally: "Hey, thanks a bunch for letting
us use the gym!"

**Model:** "Thank you for allowing us to use the gymnasium."
Changes: greeting dropped (a letter opens with a salutation, not
"Hey"); *thanks a bunch* → *thank you*; *letting us use* →
*allowing us to use*; *gym* → *gymnasium*. Every change moves
the sentence toward the principal's register; none changes the
meaning.

## Worked example 2 — finding and fixing a tone slip

**Problem:** Find the slip and fix it: "We respectfully request
the use of the auditorium for our spring show. It'll be super
fun, trust me! The show will include music and short plays."

**Model:** The slip is sentence 2: contraction (*It'll*), slang
(*super fun*), and a chatty push (*trust me!*). The surrounding
sentences are formal and businesslike. Fix: "We hope it will be
an enjoyable evening for the school community." Now all three
sentences share one register, and the letter sounds like one
person wrote it.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Formal or informal? "We would appreciate your help."
   *Hint: check the verb form and the politeness.*
2. "Gimme the mic!" — formal or informal? Write the formal
   version. *Hint: "May I…"*
3. Spot the slip: "Dear Principal Alvarez, Thank you for
   meeting with us. It was awesome and stuff. We would like to
   request the auditorium." Which sentence slips, and why?
4. Rewrite formally: "The show was awesome and stuff."

## Independent practice

1. Rewrite formally: "We need the gym ASAP."
2. Rewrite formally: "Tell us if you can come, ok?"
3. Choose the formal closing for a letter to the principal:
   "Thanks!" or "Sincerely," — and say why.
4. Spot and fix the slip: "We respectfully invite you to the
   spring show. It'll be a blast! The program will begin at six
   o'clock."
5. Speaking task: write one formal sentence presenting this
   finding to the class: the garden grew twelve kinds of beans.
6. Tone match: write the principal version of this note to a
   friend: "Come see our show Friday — it'll be fun!"

## Application

Write a formal letter (4–6 sentences) to the principal requesting
the auditorium for a spring show. Keep one formal style and tone
throughout: salutation, request, two details (date or program),
and a formal closing. Read it back sentence by sentence and
circle any slip before you finish.

## Exit check

Rewrite this sentence formally: "The concert was really cool."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** keep a two-column "friend words /
  principal words" chart (gonna → going to, ASAP → as soon as
  possible, thanks a bunch → thank you) visible during revision.
- **Alternate response mode:** rehearse the formal version aloud
  to a partner, who listens for exactly one slip, before writing
  the final version.
- **Extension:** explain when informal English is the *right*
  choice (a note to a friend, dialogue in a story, a joke) and
  why "always formal" would be wrong there — register is a
  choice, not a ladder.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners equate formal with "longer and
harder words," producing stiff nonsense ("We hereby
utilization-request…"). Formal means complete, precise, and
polite — not inflated. Reward plain formal sentences over
pompous ones. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md)
