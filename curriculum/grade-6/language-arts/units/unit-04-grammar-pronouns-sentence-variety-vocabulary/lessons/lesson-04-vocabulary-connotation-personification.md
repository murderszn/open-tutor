# Lesson 04 — Vocabulary: Connotation, Personification, Word Choice

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell what a word means from its
dictionary definition, what feeling it carries, and what it does
to a reader — and I can use context clues and word parts to unlock
new words."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** L.6.5.a (figures of speech, e.g., personification),
L.6.5.c (connotations of words with similar denotations), L.6.4.a–d
(context clues, affixes/roots, reference materials, verification),
L.6.6 (grade-appropriate academic vocabulary).

## Launch (5 min)

"Three words for the same spending habit: *stingy, scrimping,
economical, thrifty*. A dictionary would say they all mean
'spending little.' But would you rather be called *thrifty* or
*stingy*? The dictionary meaning is only half the word. The other
half is the feeling it carries — and writers choose words for both
halves."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which word would a writer choose to praise a careful saver, and
which to criticize one — *thrifty* or *stingy*? Why?

(*Thrifty* praises: it suggests wise, careful spending. *Stingy*
criticizes: it suggests selfishly refusing to spend. Same
denotation — spending little — opposite connotations.)

## Explicit teaching

**Denotation** = a word's dictionary definition. **Connotation**
= the feelings and associations a word carries. Words with the
same denotation can push a reader in opposite directions:

| Denotation | Positive connotation | Negative connotation |
|---|---|---|
| spending little | thrifty, economical | stingy, cheap |
| wanting to know | curious, inquisitive | nosy, prying |
| firm, not giving in | determined, steadfast | stubborn, obstinate |
| small room | cozy, snug | cramped, tiny |

**Personification** is a figure of speech that gives human
qualities to something nonhuman: "The wind whispered through the
curtains." Wind cannot whisper, but the image makes the reader
*feel* a quiet, secretive night. To analyze personification, name
the human quality, then name the effect: what mood or picture does
it create? Personification is not a simile (a comparison with
*like* or *as*: "The drums thumped like a heartbeat" compares;
it does not give the drums a human action).

**Word-choice impact:** changing one word changes the sentence's
force. "The crowd cheered" reports a fact. "The crowd erupted"
makes the reader hear a sudden explosion of noise. Strong, precise
verbs beat vague ones (*went, got, was*) when the writer wants the
reader to feel something.

**Unlocking unknown words (L.6.4):** (a) use **context clues** —
the words around it ("After the long rehearsal, the performers
were weary, dragging their feet" → *weary* ≈ tired);
(b) use **affixes and roots** — *un-* (not) in *unprepared*,
*re-* (again) in *rewrite*, *dis-* (not) in *disagree*, *pre-*
(before) in *preview*; (c) **consult a dictionary** or glossary;
(d) **verify** — check your guess against the context or the
dictionary before using the word yourself.

## Worked example 1 — connotation changes the judgment

**Problem:** A writer describes the talent-show judge two ways:
"The judge was *strict*." "The judge was *harsh*." Both suggest
firm standards. How does each version make the reader judge the
judge?

**Model:** *Strict* is mostly neutral-to-positive: firm, fair,
holding a standard. *Harsh* is negative: severe, unkind, too
severe. The denotation overlaps (firm), but the connotation
decides whether the reader trusts or dislikes the judge. A writer
who wants a fair judge writes *strict*; a writer who wants an
unfair one writes *harsh*.

## Worked example 2 — personification and its effect

**Problem:** "The old school bell scolded late students." Name
the personification and its effect.

**Model:** A bell cannot scold — scolding is a human action.
Giving the bell that action makes it feel stern and alive, almost
like a watchful adult. Effect: the reader pictures students
hurrying, a little guilty, under a bell that seems to disapprove.
If the writer had written "The bell rang," the picture would be
flat and the mood would vanish.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. *Curious* vs. *nosy*: same denotation (wanting to know).
   Which connotation is positive, which negative? *Hint: which
   would you want as a compliment?*
2. "The thunder grumbled overhead." Personification? *Hint: can
   thunder grumble like a person?*
3. Use the context clue: "After the long rehearsal, the
   performers were weary, dragging their feet as they left."
   What does *weary* mean? *Hint: what do dragging feet show?*
4. Break apart *rewrite*: what does *re-* mean, and what does
   the whole word mean?

## Independent practice

1. *Stubborn* vs. *determined*: same denotation (not giving in).
   Which is the compliment, which the criticism? Explain in one
   sentence each.
2. "The garden slept under the snow." Personification? Name the
   human quality and the effect (quiet/still, waiting for spring).
3. "The drums thumped like a heartbeat." Personification or
   simile? Explain.
4. Use the context clue: "The principal's announcement was brief
   — just two sentences — so everyone listened carefully." What
   does *brief* mean?
5. Break apart *disagree*: what does *dis-* mean, and what does
   the whole word mean?
6. Compare: "The crowd cheered." vs. "The crowd erupted." Which
   fits a talent-show finale better, and what does the stronger
   verb add?

## Application

Write two sentences about the school garden:

(a) one sentence using personification (name the human quality
you gave the garden or a plant);
(b) one sentence in which you choose between two words with the
same denotation but different connotations (for example, *cozy*
vs. *cramped* for a small greenhouse) — and write one sentence
explaining why you chose the word you did.

## Exit check

"The old piano groaned when Maya pressed a key." Name the figure
of speech, the human quality, and the effect in one sentence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** sort words on a two-column
  "praise / criticize" chart before writing definitions; the
  chart makes connotation visible.
- **Alternate response mode:** act out the personification
  (groan like the piano, whisper like the wind) before naming
  its effect.
- **Extension:** explain the connotation shift in this pair:
  "The scientist *examined* the sample" vs. "The scientist
  *interrogated* the sample." (Examined = careful, neutral
  study; interrogated = aggressive questioning — wrong for a
  sample, and it changes the scientist's character.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners call every comparison
personification, or treat connotation as "my personal opinion."
Anchor connotation in shared associations (would most readers
hear praise or criticism?) and personification in a human action
done by a nonhuman subject. Answer-key reference:
