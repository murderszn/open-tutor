# Lesson 05 — Sentence Patterns and Variety

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can build simple, compound, and
complex sentences, combine choppy sentences, and repair fragments
and run-ons so my writing does not sound like a drumbeat."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** L.6.3.a (vary sentence patterns for meaning, reader
interest, and style).

## Launch (5 min)

"Read this paragraph aloud. Feel the drumbeat: 'Maya played the
guitar. Ben sang. The crowd clapped. The judges smiled.' Every
sentence is the same shape, the same length, the same beat. Now
hear it rebuilt: 'While Maya played the guitar and Ben sang, the
crowd clapped and the judges smiled.' Same facts, different music.
Sentence variety is not decoration — it tells the reader which
ideas belong together and which one matters most."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which of these is a fragment, and how would you fix it?

(a) "Because the lights went out."
(b) "The show paused."

((a) is a fragment: "Because the lights went out" is a dependent
clause — it leaves the reader waiting for what happened. Fix it
by adding an independent clause: "Because the lights went out,
the show paused." (b) is complete: subject + predicate.)

## Explicit teaching

A **clause** has a subject and a verb. An **independent clause**
can stand alone as a sentence. A **dependent clause** cannot —
it starts with a subordinating word (*because, when, although,
while, after, if*) and leaves the reader waiting.

| Pattern | Build | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | One independent clause (it may have a compound subject or predicate) | "Maya played." / "The judges watched and took notes." |
| Compound | Two independent clauses joined by a comma + coordinating conjunction (*and, but, or, so, yet*) or a semicolon | "Maya played, and Ben sang." |
| Complex | One independent clause + one or more dependent clauses | "When Maya played, Ben sang." / "Ben sang while Maya played." |

A **phrase** has no subject-verb pair ("in the morning," "on the
stage") and cannot be a sentence on its own.

**Combining choppy sentences:** look for ideas that belong
together and join them with the right tool — *and* for addition,
*but* for contrast, *because/when/although* for cause and time.
"The show started. The lights dimmed. The crowd cheered." →
"When the show started and the lights dimmed, the crowd
cheered." The *when* clause tells the reader the cheering
followed the dimming — the flat version never said that.

**Repairs:** a **fragment** is missing a subject, a verb, or a
complete thought — add what is missing. A **run-on** jams two
independent clauses together with no punctuation or conjunction —
split it into two sentences, join it with a comma + conjunction,
or subordinate one clause ("The show ended everyone cheered" →
"The show ended, and everyone cheered").

**Variety check:** read a paragraph aloud. If three sentences in
a row start the same way and run the same length, rebuild one:
combine two, subordinate one, or move a phrase to the front
("After the applause, Maya bowed").

## Worked example 1 — naming the pattern

**Problem:** Name each pattern: (a) "Maya played." (b) "Maya
played, and Ben sang." (c) "When Maya played, Ben sang."

**Model:** (a) Simple — one independent clause. (b) Compound —
two independent clauses ("Maya played" + "Ben sang") joined by a
comma + *and*. (c) Complex — "When Maya played" is dependent
(it cannot stand alone), "Ben sang" is independent. Trick case:
"The judges watched and took notes" is simple, not compound —
one subject, one compound predicate, one clause.

## Worked example 2 — combining and varying

**Problem:** Combine and vary: "The show started. The lights
dimmed. The crowd cheered." Then vary this flat trio: "Ben sang.
Maya played. The crowd clapped."

**Model:** "When the show started and the lights dimmed, the
crowd cheered." — the *when* clause subordinates the first two
facts and makes the cheering the main event. For the trio, one
good revision: "While Ben sang and Maya played, the crowd
clapped." The *while* clause joins the two performances and lets
the crowd's reaction land as the main clause. Other correct
combinations exist; the key test is that the revision is one
grammatical sentence and no fact is lost.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. "Ben sang while Maya played." Simple, compound, or complex?
   *Hint: can "while Maya played" stand alone?*
2. "The judges watched and took notes." Simple, compound, or
   complex? *Hint: count the subjects.*
3. Combine: "Maya practiced. She improved." *Hint: try *and* or
   *because*.*
4. Fix the run-on: "The show ended everyone cheered loudly."
   *Hint: two independent clauses need a join or a split.*

## Independent practice

1. "After the show ended, the students stacked the chairs."
   Simple, compound, or complex?
2. "The trophy shone, but the guitar gleamed." Simple, compound,
   or complex?
3. Combine with subordination: "The rain fell. The show went on."
4. Fix the fragment: "Because the lights went out."
5. Combine all three into one sentence: "Ben forgot his music.
   Maya shared hers. The duet went on."
6. Rewrite this flat trio with variety (at least one complex
   sentence, no fact lost): "Maya bowed. Ben bowed. The crowd
   stood up."

## Application

Revise "The Garden Log" draft paragraph below into a varied
paragraph. Use at least one simple, one compound, and one complex
sentence, and lose no fact. (The same draft returns in the
project, where you will also fix its pronouns, punctuation, and
tone.)

> "The garden grew beans. The beans grew tall. The students
> watered them. The students weeded them. The harvest came. The
> students cooked soup. The soup was good. Everyone ate it."

## Exit check

Combine into one varied sentence, losing no fact: "Maya played
the guitar. Ben sang. The crowd clapped."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** underline the subject once and
  the verb twice in each clause before naming the pattern; the
  visible clause count makes the pattern visible.
- **Alternate response mode:** clap the "beat" of a flat
  paragraph, then clap the rebuilt version, to hear variety
  before writing it.
- **Extension:** explain why starting three sentences in a row
  with "The" is a variety problem even when every sentence is
  grammatical, and revise a paragraph that does it.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners call any long sentence "complex"
and any sentence with *and* "compound" — including "The judges
watched and took notes" (simple: one subject, compound
predicate). Count clauses, not words or conjunctions. Also watch
comma splices presented as fixes ("The show ended, everyone
cheered" is still a run-on — it needs a conjunction or a split).
