# Lesson 03 — Nonrestrictive Punctuation and Spelling

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can set off extra information with
commas, parentheses, or dashes — and leave essential information
unpunctuated — and I can spell tricky words correctly."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** L.6.2.a (punctuation for nonrestrictive/parenthetical
elements), L.6.2.b (spell correctly).

## Launch (5 min)

"Read these two sentences and notice how the commas change who we
mean. 'The students who practiced daily performed well.' 'Maya,
who practiced daily, performed well.' In the first, the clause
picks out *which* students — take it away and the meaning
changes. In the second, the clause is extra information about one
already-identified person — take it away and Maya still performed
well. Punctuation is not decoration. It tells the reader what is
essential and what is extra."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Which sentence needs commas around the middle part, and why?

(a) "The guitar that Maya borrowed sounded bright."
(b) "Maya who plays guitar opened the show."

((b) needs commas: "Maya, who plays guitar, opened the show."
*Maya* already identifies the person, so the clause is extra. In
(a), *that Maya borrowed* identifies *which* guitar, so it is
essential and takes no commas.)

## Explicit teaching

A **nonrestrictive (nonessential) element** adds information about
a noun that is already identified. **Removal test:** take it out.
If the sentence still identifies the same person or thing and
keeps its core meaning, the element is nonrestrictive — set it
off with **commas**, **parentheses**, or **dashes**. A
**restrictive (essential) element** identifies *which* one you
mean; removing it changes the meaning or leaves the reader unsure.
It takes **no** commas.

- **Commas** are the everyday choice: "Maya, who plays guitar,
  opened the show."
- **Parentheses** mark a quiet aside: "The program (printed on
  yellow paper) listed twelve acts." Say it in a lower voice.
- **Dashes** mark emphasis or a sharp break: "The show — the first
  one of the year — filled the hall." Say it with a punch.

The same three marks set off **parenthetical elements** — words
that interrupt or comment, like appositives (a noun renaming
another noun): "Ben, our lead singer, forgot his music."

**Spelling (L.6.2.b)** is part of the same job: a misspelled word
misleads the reader just as a missing comma does. Three checks:
sound it out in syllables, check the pattern (possessive pronouns
never take apostrophes: *its, hers, theirs*), and verify a
homophone by meaning, not sound — *their* (ownership), *there*
(place), *they're* ("they are"); *its* (ownership), *it's* ("it
is"); *your* (ownership), *you're* ("you are"); *principal* (a
person in charge), *principle* (a rule or belief). When unsure,
look it up in a dictionary — checking is a writing skill, not a
failure.

## Worked example 1 — restrictive or not?

**Problem:** Punctuate or leave as is: (a) "Maya who plays guitar
opened the show." (b) "The students who practiced daily performed
well."

**Model:** (a) *Maya* already names the person. Remove "who plays
guitar" and the sentence still means the same thing: "Maya opened
the show." Nonrestrictive → commas: "Maya, who plays guitar,
opened the show." (b) Remove "who practiced daily" and we no
longer know *which* students performed well. Restrictive → no
commas: leave as is.

## Worked example 2 — choosing the mark

**Problem:** Add punctuation to: "The trophy a tall gold cup stood
on the table." Then to: "The show the first one of the year filled
the hall." Explain each choice.

**Model:** "The trophy, a tall gold cup, stood on the table." —
*a tall gold cup* renames *the trophy* and is removable, so commas
do the job. "The show — the first one of the year — filled the
hall." — the aside is a proud announcement, so dashes fit the
emphasis. Commas would also be correct here; dashes are a style
choice, and the key accepts either when the element is correctly
set off at both ends.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. "Ben our lead singer forgot his music." *Hint: is "our lead
   singer" removable?*
2. "The guitar that Maya borrowed sounded bright." *Hint: does
   the clause identify which guitar?*
3. "The talent show the first one of the year filled the hall."
   *Hint: pick commas or dashes — but set the element off at both
   ends.*
4. "My brother a sixth grader played drums." *Hint: "a sixth
   grader" renames "my brother."*

## Independent practice

1. "Mrs. Alvarez the music teacher announced the winners."
2. "The songs that the chorus sang were all original."
3. "The final act a surprise duet brought the crowd to its feet."
4. "The program printed on yellow paper listed twelve acts."
   (Punctuate, or leave as is and say why.)
5. Choose the spelling in each sentence: "The (principal /
   principle) praised the performers." / "The band followed the
   (principal / principle) of practicing daily."
6. Fill in the blanks with *their, there,* or *they're*: "___
   putting ___ instruments over ___." (Three blanks, in order.)

## Application

Punctuate this paragraph. It needs one nonrestrictive element set
off with commas and one parenthetical aside in parentheses.
(You choose which element goes in parentheses, but every element
you set off must pass the removal test.)

> "The garden club our newest school club planted beans. The beans
> which sprouted first grew fastest. The harvest festival a school
> tradition will feature the beans in soup."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** cover the middle element with a
  finger and read what is left; if the sentence still works and
  means the same thing, the element needs setting off.
- **Alternate response mode:** read the sentence aloud with a
  pause (commas), a quiet aside (parentheses), or a punch
  (dashes) to feel the difference before writing.
- **Extension:** explain why "My sister, who lives in Chicago,
  called" and "My sister who lives in Chicago called" mean
  different things. (With commas: I have one sister, and she
  happens to live in Chicago. Without commas: I have more than
  one sister, and the clause picks out the Chicago one.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners comma off *every* middle clause,
including restrictive ones ("The songs, that the chorus sang, …").
The removal test plus the "which one?" question fixes it. Also
watch one-sided punctuation — an element set off at the start
must also be set off at the end. Answer-key reference:
