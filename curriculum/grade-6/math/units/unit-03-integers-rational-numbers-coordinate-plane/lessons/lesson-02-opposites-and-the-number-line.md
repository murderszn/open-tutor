# Lesson 02 — Opposites and the Number Line

**My goal:** I can find the opposite of a number, and I can plot integers
and other rational numbers on horizontal and vertical number lines.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper, pencil, ruler; a number line from −10 to 10
(drawn or printed).

**Standards:** 6.NS.C.6.a, 6.NS.C.6.c · **Unit objectives:** UO8, UO9

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer before we start — this tells us what is already solid.

1. Write these in order from smallest to largest: 2, 7, 0, 5.
2. On a number line, where does 1/2 sit — between which two whole
   numbers? Where does 2.5 sit?
3. From Lesson 01: a fish swims at −4 m and a bird flies at +6 m. Which
   one is above zero, and how do you know?

## Explicit teaching

### The number line grows in both directions

You already know the number line for positive numbers: 0, then 1, 2, 3,
… marching to the right. Now extend it to the **left** of 0 by the same
steps: −1, −2, −3, … Each step is the same size; only the direction
changes. On a vertical number line, positives go **up** and negatives go
**down** — same idea, turned sideways.

A number line is not just for whole numbers. A **rational number** is
any number you can write as a fraction or a decimal — including
negatives. So −2.5, −3/4, 1/2, and 0.75 all have exact homes on the
line. To plot a fraction, split the step between two whole numbers into
equal parts: for quarters, each whole-number step holds 4 equal parts.

### Opposites: same distance, other side

The **opposite** of a number is the number the same distance from 0 but
on the other side of 0. To find an opposite, flip the sign:

- The opposite of 3 is **−3**. (3 is 3 steps right of 0; −3 is 3 steps
  left.)
- The opposite of −3 is **3**. (Start at −3, flip the sign.)
- The opposite of the opposite of a number is the number itself:
  **−(−3) = 3**. Flip twice and you are home.
- **0 is its own opposite.** It sits exactly on the dividing line, so
  there is no "other side" to go to: −0 = 0.

**Integers** are the whole numbers and their opposites:
…, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, … Fractions and decimals like −2.5 are
rational numbers but not integers — every integer is a rational number,
but not every rational number is an integer.

### Worked example 1 — plotting rationals on a horizontal line

Plot these on a number line from −5 to 5: −3, 1/2, −2.5, 4, −3/4.

1. **−3:** count 3 steps left of 0. Mark and label it −3.
2. **1/2:** halfway between 0 and 1. Mark it.
3. **−2.5:** halfway between −3 and −2 (2.5 steps left of 0). Note it
   sits *between* −3 and −2 — closer to 0 than −3 is.
4. **4:** count 4 steps right of 0.
5. **−3/4:** split the step from −1 to 0 into 4 equal parts; count 3
   parts left from 0 (or 1 part right from −1). Mark it −3/4, just left
   of 0.

Read the line left to right: −3, −2.5, −3/4, 1/2, 4. Left-to-right order
*is* smallest-to-largest order — a fact Lesson 03 will use.

**A common error:** plotting −2.5 between −2 and −1, or between −3
and −4. Say the value slowly: "negative two and a half" — it is 2 and a
half steps *left* of 0, so it lives between −3 and −2. The whole-number
part (2) tells you the step you are leaving; the fraction tells you how
far into the next step you go, in the negative direction.

### Worked example 2 — a vertical number line and opposites

A lift serves floors from −2 (two basement levels) to 5. Draw a vertical
line with 0 (ground) in the middle, positives up, negatives down.

- Plot floor **3** (3 up from 0) and floor **−2** (2 down from 0).
- The opposite of 3 is **−3** — but this building has no floor −3, so
  that point would fall one level below the lowest basement. Opposites
  always exist as numbers, even when the story has no matching floor.
- The opposite of −2 is **2**: floor 2, two levels above ground.
- Check: −(−2) = 2. Flipping twice returns the original number.

**Check the zero rule:** on a vertical line, "above 0" means up and
"below 0" means down. The direction changed (up/down instead of
right/left) but the rule did not: positives on one side of 0, negatives
on the other, equal steps throughout.

## Guided practice

Work these with your adult or partner. Draw the line before you answer.

1. Write the opposite of each: 7, −7, 0, 12, −1.5.
2. Plot on a horizontal line from −6 to 6: −5, −0.5, 3, −3.5, 5/2.
   (Hint: 5/2 = 2.5.)
3. A vertical thermometer shows −4 °C and 4 °C. Are those opposites?
   Explain with distance from 0.
4. True or false, with a reason: "−(−9) = −9." Then give the correct
   value.

## Independent practice

1. Write the opposite of each: 4, −11, 0, 2.5, −3/4.
2. Plot on a horizontal line from −5 to 5: −4, −1/4, 2.5, 0, −2.75.
   Label every point.
3. On a vertical number line from −6 to 6, plot −5, −1, 3, and 5.
   Which plotted point is farthest from 0? Which is closest to 0?
4. A diver is at −18 meters and a climber is at +18 meters above sea
   level. Are their positions opposites? Explain in terms of distance
   from sea level.
5. **Application — the freezer log:** A freezer log (fictional data)
   lists temperatures: −18 °C, −12 °C, −18 °C, −6 °C, 0 °C. (a) Plot
   them on a vertical number line. (b) Which temperature appears twice?
   (c) The opposite of −12 is 12. Does 12 °C appear in the log? What
   would 12 °C mean for a freezer — warmer or colder than the log's
   readings, and how do you know?

## Exit check

1. What is the opposite of −8? What is −(−8)?
2. Plot −1.5 on a number line: between which two integers does it sit?

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

**Reading/language access:** Say each rational number in words before
plotting ("negative two point five," "negative three quarters") so the
sign is heard, not just seen.

**Alternate response mode:** Walk a floor number line taped to the
ground — step right for positive, left for negative — or point to the
position on a large drawn line before marking it.

**Extension:** Plot −7/4 and 1.75 on the same line and explain which is
farther from 0. Then find a rational number between −1/4 and 0, and
explain why there is always one more number between any two numbers
you pick.

**Teacher note — likely misconception:** Learners often plot −2.5 as if
the ".5" moved it toward −2 (rounding toward zero) or place −3/4 on the
positive side because "3/4 is positive." Insist on the two-step read:
sign first (which side of 0), then size (how far). If the learner can
say "negative, so left of zero; two and a half steps" before marking,
the plot is almost always right. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-02) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02) (separate — do not show the key
to the learner).
