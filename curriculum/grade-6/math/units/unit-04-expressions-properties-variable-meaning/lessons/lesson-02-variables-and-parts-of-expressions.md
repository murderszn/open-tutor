# Lesson 02 — Variables and Parts of an Expression

**My goal:** I can write expressions that record operations with
numbers and letters standing for numbers, and I can name the parts of
an expression.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper, pencil;
[market-stall illustration](../assets/variable-bags-scene.png).

**Standards:** 6.EE.A.2.a–b · **Unit objectives:** UO14

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer before we start — this tells us what is already solid.

1. A bag holds 6 apples. How many apples do 3 bags hold? Write the
   calculation.
2. A bag holds some unknown number of apples. Can you write a
   calculation for 3 bags? What stops you?
3. In 2 × (8 + 7), which operation would you do first, and why?

*(Question 2 is today's whole lesson: a letter can stand for the
number we do not know yet.)*

## Explicit teaching

### A letter can stand for a number

Sometimes the number in a problem is not given — it varies, or we
simply do not know it yet. Mathematicians use a **variable**, a letter
that stands for a number. The letter is not mysterious: it is a
placeholder, and later (Lesson 03) we will give it a value and compute.

An **expression** is a piece of mathematical writing that records
operations: numbers, variables, and operation signs, with no equals
sign. (Once an equals sign appears, you have an equation — that is
Unit 05's business, not today's.) A **numerical expression** uses only
numbers, like 3 × 4 + 5. An **algebraic expression** uses at least one
variable, like 3n + 4.

### Worked example 1 — the market-stall picture

Look at the illustration:

*Caption: The market-stall scene. This illustration is an AI-generated
story prompt — not a photograph and not a measurement. The numbers in
this lesson are given in words; do not try to count apples from the
picture.*

**Text alternative:** Under a striped awning, a wooden market table
holds a basket of red apples on the left, three identical closed teal
cloth bags in a row in the middle, and a balance scale on the right.
(The numbers below are given in words — the picture contains no counts
or labels.)

The story gives the numbers in words: **each bag holds the same
unknown number of apples**. Call that number **n**. Then:

- One bag holds n apples.
- Three bags hold n + n + n apples, which we write as **3n**. (Writing
  3n means 3 × n; the multiplication sign is left out between a number
  and a letter.)
- Three bags plus 4 loose apples: **3n + 4**.

The letter n is doing real work here: the expression 3n + 4 describes
every possible bag size at once. If a bag holds 6 apples, the total is
3 × 6 + 4 = 22. If a bag holds 9, the total is 3 × 9 + 4 = 31. Same
expression, different values — that is the power of a variable.

### Writing expressions from words — order matters

Translate operations in the order the words say them, and watch out
for subtraction and division, where order changes the answer:

- "Subtract y from 5" → **5 − y** (start at 5, take y away). The
  reverse, y − 5, says something different.
- "7 more than twice m" → **2m + 7** (twice m first, then 7 more).
- "n groups of 8" → **8n**.
- "The quotient of 20 and q" → **20 ÷ q**.

### Worked example 2 — naming the parts

Every expression has parts with names. Learn them on two examples:

**Example A: 4x + 3.** This is a **sum** of two **terms**: 4x and 3.
The term 4x is a **product** of two **factors**, 4 and x. The number
4, multiplying the variable, is the **coefficient** of x. The term 3,
with no variable, is a constant term.

**Example B: 2(8 + 7).** This is a **product** of two **factors**:
2 and (8 + 7). Now look at (8 + 7) two ways at once: it is a **sum**
of two terms, 8 and 7 — and it is also a **single entity**, one factor
being multiplied by 2. Seeing a bracketed part as one thing is a skill
this unit returns to again and again: in 2(8 + 7), the parentheses
tell you to treat (8 + 7) as a single quantity first.

Summary of part names: a **term** is a piece added or subtracted in
a sum; a **factor** is a piece multiplied in a product; a
**coefficient** is the number multiplying a variable; a **quotient**
is the result of a division (in 20 ÷ q, the whole expression is a
quotient).

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Write an expression for "n groups of 8." *(Hint: groups of means
   multiplication.)*
2. Write an expression for "12 less than p." *(Hint: less than
   reverses the order — start at p.)*
3. Write an expression for "the quotient of 20 and q."
4. In 6a + 2, name the terms and the coefficient of a.
   *(Hint: the sum has two terms; the coefficient is the number
   multiplying a.)*
5. In 5(t + 2), name the factors. Is (t + 2) a sum, a single entity,
   or both? Explain.

## Independent practice

1. Write an expression for each phrase:
   (a) 9 more than k (b) twice s, then 5 less (c) the product of 7
   and m (d) 15 divided by r (e) 4 fewer than twice t.
2. For 3y − 5: name the terms and the coefficient of y.
3. For 5(t + 2): name the factors, and describe (t + 2) as both a sum
   and a single entity.
4. For 8x + 2x + 6: how many terms are there? Which terms contain x?
5. A stall sells 2 bags of n oranges plus 3 loose oranges. Write the
   expression for the total. Then evaluate it (Lesson 03 skill, preview
   here) for n = 8.

## Application

Back to the market stall: the stall now sells bags of n apples plus
4 loose apples, written 3n + 4. (a) Write the expression for 5 bags
plus 4 loose apples. (b) If each bag holds 9 apples, how many apples
are there in all for 3 bags plus 4 loose? Show the substitution.

## Exit check

Write an expression for "7 more than twice m." In that expression,
what is the coefficient of m?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Translate each phrase twice: once in
  words ("twice m, then 7 more") and once in symbols (2m + 7). Keep a
  running word-to-symbol list on the page.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may describe parts orally
  ("the sum of two terms…") while the adult writes the symbols, or
  point to each part on a printed expression.
- **Extension:** Explain why 3n means 3 × n but 3 + n means something
  different, using n = 10 as a test: 3 × 10 = 30, but 3 + 10 = 13.
  Then write an expression for "n bags with 3 apples each, plus n
  loose apples" (3n + n) and simplify it in words to "4 bags' worth"
  (4n) — a preview of Lesson 05.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: reading "subtract y from 5" as y − 5; reading
3n as "three and n" (addition) instead of "three times n"; treating
(8 + 7) as two separate factors instead of one entity. See the teacher
guide and the answer-key reference.
