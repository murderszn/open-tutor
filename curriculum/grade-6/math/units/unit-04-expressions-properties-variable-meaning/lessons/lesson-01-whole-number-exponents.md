# Lesson 01 — Whole-Number Exponents

**My goal:** I can write and evaluate numerical expressions with
whole-number exponents, and I can explain why an exponent is not
multiplication by the exponent.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper, pencil; multiplication table (optional, for
checking products only).

**Standards:** 6.EE.A.1 · **Unit objectives:** UO13

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer before we start — this tells us what is already solid.

1. What is 3 × 3 × 3 × 3? (Multiply step by step.)
2. What is 3 × 4? How is that different from question 1?
3. A square garden has side 5 meters. Its area is 5 × 5 square meters.
   What is that area?

*(If #1 felt long to write out, good — today's shorthand exists
exactly for that problem.)*

## Explicit teaching

### A shorthand for repeated multiplication

Writing 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 is correct, but it is long, and it is easy to
lose count of the 3s. Mathematicians shorten it with an **exponent**:

- 3⁴ means 3 used as a factor **4 times**: 3 × 3 × 3 × 3.
- The big number, 3, is the **base** — the number being multiplied.
- The small raised number, 4, is the **exponent** — how many times
  the base is used as a factor.
- The whole thing, 3⁴, is a **power**. Its **value** is the product:
  3⁴ = 81.
- 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 is called the **expanded form**; 3⁴ is the
  **exponential form**.

Read 3⁴ as "three to the fourth power." Two special readings come up
constantly: 5² is "five squared" (a square of side 5 has area 5²), and
5³ is "five cubed" (a cube of side 5 has volume 5³).

### The one mistake to avoid

An exponent is **not** multiplication by the exponent:

- 3⁴ = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81, but 3 × 4 = 12. Those are very different.
- 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, but 2 × 3 = 6.

Whenever you see a power, first rewrite it in expanded form, then
multiply. That one habit prevents the most common exponent error.

### Worked example 1 — evaluate 3⁴

Rewrite in expanded form: 3⁴ = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3.

Multiply from left to right, two factors at a time:

- 3 × 3 = 9.
- 9 × 3 = 27.
- 27 × 3 = 81.

So 3⁴ = 81. Check the count: the expanded form has four 3s, matching
the exponent 4. If you had written three 3s, you would have computed
3³ = 27 instead — counting the factors matters as much as multiplying
them.

### Worked example 2 — compare 2³ and 3²

A bigger base does not always win, and a bigger exponent does not
always win. Compute both before comparing:

- 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.
- 3² = 3 × 3 = 9.

So 3² > 2³, because 9 > 8 — even though 2³ has the larger exponent.
The only safe way to compare powers is to evaluate them.

A surprise pair worth noticing: 4² = 16 and 2⁴ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16.
Different-looking powers can have the same value. That idea returns
in Lesson 05, when we ask when two expressions are equivalent.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Evaluate 5². *(Hint: expanded form is 5 × 5.)*
2. Evaluate 2⁵. *(Hint: count five 2s: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2.)*
3. Evaluate 10³. *(Hint: 10 × 10 × 10. What pattern do you see in
   powers of 10?)*
4. Write 6 × 6 × 6 in exponential form, then evaluate it.
5. Write 7² in expanded form, then evaluate it.
6. Evaluate 1⁶. *(Hint: 1 × 1 × 1 × 1 × 1 × 1. What is 1 times
   anything?)*

## Independent practice

1. Evaluate: (a) 3³ (b) 8² (c) 2⁶ (d) 5³ (e) 10² (f) 9².
2. Write 4⁵ in expanded form and evaluate it.
3. Write 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 in exponential form and evaluate it.
4. Which is greater, 2⁴ or 4²? Evaluate both, then compare.
5. Which is greater, 2⁵ or 5²? Evaluate both, then compare.
6. Error analysis: Sam says 4³ = 12 because 4 × 3 = 12. Explain
   Sam's error and give the correct value.

## Application

A square garden bed has side 9 feet and is planted with 1-foot-square
lettuce squares, one plant per square foot. Write the number of plants
as a power, then evaluate it. How many lettuce plants fit in the bed?

## Exit check

Write 5 × 5 × 5 × 5 in exponential form and evaluate it. In one
sentence, explain what the exponent 4 tells you.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Say every power aloud before writing
  it ("four to the third power"), and always expand before
  multiplying. Pair each power with its expanded form on the page.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may answer with repeated
  multiplication chains and a final product, without using exponent
  notation at first, then translate their chain into exponential form.
- **Extension:** Evaluate 2¹⁰ by doubling (2⁵ = 32, so 2¹⁰ = 32 × 32).
  Then explain why 2¹⁰ is much larger than 10², even though 10 is the
  larger base.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: 3⁴ read as "three times four." Insist on the
expanded-form rewrite until it is automatic; the error disappears once
the learner counts factors. See the teacher guide for the full
misconception list and the answer-key reference.
