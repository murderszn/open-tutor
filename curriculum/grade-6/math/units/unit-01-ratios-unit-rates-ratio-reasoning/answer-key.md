# Answer Key — Unit 01: Ratios, Unit Rates, and Ratio Reasoning

Every question in the unit's lessons, practice, project, and assessments is
solved here independently. Keep this file away from the learner.

## Lesson 01

**Readiness:** 1) blue marbles (6 > 4). 2) Accept "4:6 red to blue" or any
equivalent shortening that keeps both quantities and the order. 3) 8 red,
12 blue (both doubled — same mix).

**Guided:** 1) 4:7. 2) 7:4 — it answers the opposite question (oranges
named first). 3) 6:2; then 2:6. 4) 5:8.

**Independent:** 5) Example: 6 circles, 4 triangles → 6:4 (circles to
triangles), 4:6 (triangles to circles), 6:10 (circles to total). Any
correct triple from the learner's drawing. 6) Flour:sugar = 3:2;
sugar:flour = 2:3. Swapping makes it sweeter — 3:2 sugar:flour means more
sugar per flour than 2:3 did. 7) Dana is right. Theo wrote the numbers in
the order he saw them (yellow:green = 9:6) instead of the asked order
(green:yellow = 6:9). 8) Disagree. Example: Amara's 2:3 (2 concentrate to
3 water) vs 3:2 (3 concentrate to 2 water) — the second is far stronger;
same numbers, different recipe, because order is part of the claim.

**Application:** Recipes are 2:3 (yours) vs 3:2 (friend's); doubled: 4:6 vs
6:4. Yours keeps Amara's taste — 4:6 scales 2:3 by 2; 6:4 is the flipped
relationship, twice as strong.

**Exit:** a) 12:18 (accept 2:3 simplified). b) 18:30 — bicycles to total
(12 + 18 = 30). c) 12:18 is cars:bicycles, not bicycles:cars. The numbers
are right but the order answers the wrong question; correct is 18:12.

## Lesson 02

**Readiness:** 1) 4 cups concentrate, 6 cups water. 2) No — adding to each
part changes the relationship; only scaling both by the same factor keeps
it.

**Guided:** 1) 3:4 → 6:8, 9:12, 12:16, 15:20. 2) The diagram shows 3:5;
scaled by 3 → 9:15. 3) 9 cups oil → scale factor 3 → 12 cups vinegar.
4) 5:7 = 20:28; scale factor 4.

**Independent:** 5) 3:1 → 6:2, 9:3, 12:4; 12 cups tea → factor 4 →
4 cups lemonade. 6) The reasoning is unsound: adding different amounts is
still additive thinking, and it only "worked" by accident of the chosen
numbers. The correct move is scaling by 5 (2 × 5 = 10, 3 × 5 = 15).
7) Same scale factor is the better description: 8:12 is 2:3 scaled by 4,
so it *is* the same relationship — the tape diagram shows the rhythms
lining up. "Same numbers" alone says nothing about the relationship.
8) 1:25 = 4:100; 4 cm (scale factor 4).

**Application:** 40 servings × 5 cups = 200 cups total; scale factor 40 →
80 cups concentrate, 120 cups water. Table sentence: "each row scales 2:3
by 40, so the totals keep the relationship." Tape sentence: "the 80-part
and 120-part tapes line up in the same 2:3 rhythm." Double-number-line
sentence: "60 batches lands at 120 above 180, confirming the table."

**Exit:** a) 4:5 = 12:15; scale factor 3. b) 4:5 tape scaled by 3 → 12:15.
c) 15 (scale factor 3).

## Lesson 03

**Readiness:** 1) $3 per notebook (12 ÷ 4). 2) 50 mph (150 ÷ 3). 3) "Per
notebook" says what the $3 is *for each one of* — $3 alone could be a
total, a tax, anything.

**Guided:** 1) $3.00 ÷ 6 = $0.50 per apple. 2) $3.00 ÷ 12 = $0.25/oz;
$4.00 ÷ 16 = $0.25/oz — a tie; neither is the better buy. 3) 42 ÷ 3 =
14 km/h; 7 × 14 = 98 km. 4) $2.40 ÷ 8 = $0.30 per eraser; 8 × $0.30 =
$2.40 ✓.

**Independent:** 5) 120 ÷ 4 = 30 pages per minute; 10 × 30 = 300 pages;
check 300 ÷ 10 = 30 ✓. 6) X: $4.50 ÷ 5 = $0.90 per bar; Y: $2.85 ÷ 3 =
$0.95 per bar — Store X is lower. 7) The mistake is comparing total prices
instead of unit prices; the learner should compute price per battery for
each pack ($0.80 vs $0.90) and compare those. 8) 240 ÷ 8 = 30 miles per
gallon — each gallon of gas takes the car 30 miles; a 100-mile trip needs
100 ÷ 30 ≈ 3.33 gallons.

**Application:** $5.12 ÷ 64 = $0.08 per oz; $2.72 ÷ 32 = $0.085 per oz —
the 64-oz bottle wins. Poster sentence (example): "The big bottle costs 8
cents per ounce and the small one costs 8.5 cents per ounce, so the big
bottle saves money on every ounce."

**Exit:** a) $27.00 ÷ 15 = $1.80 per pound. b) 48 ÷ 6 = 8 minutes per mile;
6 ÷ 0.8 = 7.5 mph. Minutes per mile is more useful for pacing — it tells
the runner what each mile should feel like. c) A unit rate claims an
amount *per one* of something; without the units you cannot tell what the
"one" is, so the number cannot be used or checked.

## Lesson 04

**Readiness:** 1) "Per hundred" (cent = hundred). 2) 30/100 = 0.30.
3) It would let you compare anything "out of 100" directly.

**Guided:** 1) 40/100 = 0.40. 2) 25% of 80 = 25/100 × 80 = 1/4 × 80 = 20.
3) 10% of 45 = 4.5; 20% = 9.0. 4) Table: 100 → 60 is factor 0.6; 35 × 0.6
= 21. (Fraction check: 60/100 × 35 = 2,100/100 = 21 ✓.)

**Independent:** 5) 14 walk. 50% means half, and half of 28 is 14 — no
computation beyond halving. 6) (a) 75/100 × 120 = 9,000/100 = 90;
(b) 3/4 × 120 = 360/4 = 90. Same answer because 75/100 simplifies to 3/4 —
same relationship, simpler numbers. 7) The learner forgot the ÷ 100 that
% carries: 30 × 90 = 2,700 is 30 *times* 90, not 30 *percent of* 90.
Correct: 30/100 × 90 = 27. 8) 20/100 × $35 = $7.00 discount.

**Application:** 35/100 × 200 = 7,000/100 = 70 people. Table: 100 → 35 is
factor 0.35; 200 × 0.35 = 70 ✓. Why they agree: both methods scale the
same relationship — the table pins one row to 100 and the fraction writes
the same pinning as 35/100; multiplying by the quantity is the same
scaling either way.

**Exit:** a) 17 out of 100; 17/100; 0.17. b) 40/100 × 55 = 2,200/100 = 22.
c) Accept any reasonable "we'd work backwards from the part to the 100"
answer — e.g., the table would run in reverse (part → ? at the 100-row).
Full method arrives in U05/U08.

## Lesson 05

**Readiness:** 1) 12 in; 24 in; 6 in. 2) Scale factor 3. 3) It compares
two quantities (a length in two different units) in order — a relationship
between feet and inches.

**Guided:** 1) 4 × 36 = 144 inches. 2) 3,000 ÷ 1,000 = 3 kilometers
(dividing — meters are smaller, so the number gets smaller). 3) 7 × 12 =
84 inches; 84 ÷ 36 = 7/3 = 2⅓ yards. 4) 2 × 1,000 = 2,000 mL.

**Independent:** 5) 5 mi × (5,280 ft / 1 mi) = 26,400 ft — miles cancel,
feet remain. 6) 2.5 × 1,000 = 2,500 g. 7) Two identifications: (i) size —
1,080 feet is absurd (that's 3 football fields from 90 inches); the units
got bigger so the number must get smaller: 90 ÷ 12 = 7.5 ft. (ii) units —
90 in × (12 in / 1 ft) = 1,080 in²/ft, nonsense units that don't cancel to
feet; the correct factor is (1 ft / 12 in). 8) Kim's setup divides by the
conversion ratio (48 oz ÷ 16 oz/lb = 3 lb — ounces cancel). Lee's setup,
48 oz × (16 oz / 1 lb), leaves oz²/lb — the units don't cancel to pounds,
so the method is wrong before any arithmetic.

**Application:** (a) 2 gal × (16 cups / 1 gal) = 32 cups. (b) 10 ft ×
(12 in / 1 ft) = 120 inches. (c) 45 min × (1 h / 60 min) = 45/60 h = 3/4
hour. Sentences (examples): (a) "I multiplied because cups are smaller
than gallons, so the number grows." (b) "I multiplied because inches are
smaller than feet." (c) "I divided (via the 1/60 factor) because hours are
bigger than minutes, so the number shrinks."

**Exit:** a) 9 pt × (1 qt / 2 pt) = 4.5 quarts. b) 1,750 ÷ 1,000 = 1.75
liters. c) The answer is right, but the reasoning is incomplete for this
unit: full credit needs the ratio/unit reasoning (1 km : 1,000 m, so 5 ×
1,000) that *explains* the decimal move — the shortcut alone doesn't show
the relationship.
