# Lesson 03 — Unit Rates: Pricing and Speed

**My goal:** I can shrink a ratio's second quantity to one, compute the
**unit rate** with correct units, and interpret it — for prices and for
constant speed.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** grocery flyers or a short adult-verified price list
(adult confirms any current prices used), calculator (check only), paper.

**Standards:** 6.RP.A.2, 6.RP.A.3.b · **Unit objectives:** UO2

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. 4 notebooks cost $12. What does one notebook cost? Show the division.
2. A car travels 150 miles in 3 hours. How many miles per hour? (Starter
   lesson Session 3 — no key beside the question here.)
3. What do the units "dollars per notebook" tell you that "$3" alone does
   not?

## Explicit teaching

### Shrink the second quantity to one

A **unit rate** is a ratio with the second quantity equal to **one**:
miles *per* hour, dollars *per* notebook, cups *per* batch. "Per" is the
spoken word for the slash in a rate — it means "for each one."

To find it, **divide both quantities** so the second becomes 1 (scale factor
1 ÷ b):

> **Unit rate = a ÷ b**, with units "first-units per one second-unit."

The starter lesson's three unit rates are worth re-deriving here:

- 42 km in 3 h → 42 ÷ 3 = **14 km per hour**. Check: 14 × 3 = 42. ✓
- 150 mi in 3 h → 150 ÷ 3 = **50 miles per hour**. Check: 50 × 3 = 150. ✓
- $12 for 4 notebooks → 12 ÷ 4 = **$3 per notebook**. Check: 3 × 4 = 12. ✓

### Worked example 1 — unit pricing (the better buy)

Two battery offers (adult-verified flyer prices; prices change — always
re-verify before using real ones):

- Offer A: 10-pack for **$8.00**
- Offer B: 4-pack for **$3.60**

Which is the better buy? Totals mislead (B is cheaper overall but gives
fewer batteries). Compare *price per battery*:

- A: $8.00 ÷ 10 = **$0.80 per battery**
- B: $3.60 ÷ 4 = **$0.90 per battery**

Offer A is the better buy: $0.80 < $0.90 per battery. **Interpretation:**
"Each battery in pack A effectively costs 80 cents, so the same battery
costs less." Note the units on the answer — "$0.80" alone does not say per
what.

**Check with multiplication:** 10 × $0.80 = $8.00 ✓; 4 × $0.90 = $3.60 ✓.

### Worked example 2 — constant speed

A train travels **150 miles in 3 hours** at constant speed.

- Unit rate: 150 ÷ 3 = **50 miles per hour** (mph).
- **Interpretation:** "Every hour, the train covers 50 miles — so after 5
  hours it has covered 5 × 50 = 250 miles."

**Going further — time from a rate:** How long to travel 400 miles at 50
mph? Divide distance by the rate: 400 ÷ 50 = **8 hours**. Check: 8 × 50 =
400 ✓. (Units guide the operation: miles ÷ (miles per hour) = hours.)

**A common error:** dividing the wrong way — 3 ÷ 150 ≈ 0.02 "hours per
mile." That *is* a valid rate, but it answers a different question ("how
many hours per mile?") and the tiny number should trigger suspicion. Always
ask: *what is "per one" of?*

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. 6 apples cost $3.00. Find the price per apple. *(Hint: dollars ÷ apples.)*
2. Two juice offers: 12 oz for $3.00 and 16 oz for $4.00. Find each unit
   price (price per ounce) and name the better buy.
3. A cyclist rides 42 km in 3 hours. What is the speed in km per hour?
   How far in 7 hours at the same speed?
4. 8 erasers cost $2.40. What is the price per eraser? Check by
   multiplying back.

## Independent practice

5. A printer prints 120 pages in 4 minutes. Find pages per minute, and use
   the rate to predict pages in 10 minutes. Check your prediction.
6. Store X sells 5 granola bars for $4.50; Store Y sells 3 for $2.85. Which
   store has the lower price per bar? Show both unit rates with units.
7. **Error analysis:** "The 4-pack at $3.60 is cheaper than the 10-pack at
   $8.00, so it's the better buy." Explain the mistake and name what the
   learner should have compared instead.
8. A car travels 240 miles on 8 gallons of gas. Find miles per gallon and
   explain in a sentence what this rate means for a 100-mile trip.

## Application

**Amara's price poster.** Amara buys concentrate two ways: a 64-oz bottle
for $5.12 and a 32-oz bottle for $2.72. Compute the unit price (dollars per
ounce) for each, decide which she should buy for the stand, and write a
two-sentence justification a customer could read on her poster. (All prices
are fictional practice data, not real store prices.)

*Check: $5.12 ÷ 64 = $0.08/oz; $2.72 ÷ 32 = $0.085/oz — the 64-oz bottle
wins.*

## Exit check

a. 15 pounds of dog food cost $27.00. Find the price per pound.
b. A runner covers 6 miles in 48 minutes. Find minutes per mile and miles
   per hour (48 min = 0.8 h). Which rate is more useful for a race plan?
c. Explain why a unit rate must always name its units to be useful.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** sentence frames — "The unit rate is ___
  (quantity 1) per one (quantity 2), which means ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** sort pre-printed price cards into "better
  buy / worse buy" piles, then state the unit rate orally.
- **Extension:** gas-mileage puzzle — Car A: 320 mi on 10 gal; Car B: 285
  mi on 9.5 gal. Which is more fuel-efficient? Also: convert 50 mph to
  feet per second as a setup (preview of Lesson 05 unit factors).

## Teacher note (misconceptions; key reference)

- **Unit-dropping:** learners compute the number and stop. Withhold full
  credit until units are attached — the number without units is not a rate.
- **Reversed division:** 3 ÷ 150 instead of 150 ÷ 3. Ask "how many miles
  would that rate give in 3 hours?" — 0.02 × 3 = 0.06 miles exposes the
  absurdity.
- **Total-price comparison** instead of unit-rate comparison; always demand
  the per-unit evidence.
