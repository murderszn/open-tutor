# Lesson 01 — Ratio Language and Order

**My goal:** I can describe a ratio relationship between two quantities in
order, and explain why changing the order changes the meaning.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** two-color counters or beads (red/blue), paper, pencil;
[lemonade-stand illustration](../assets/lemonade-stand-illustration.png).

**Standards:** 6.RP.A.1 · **Unit objectives:** UO1

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer before we start — this tells us what is already solid.

1. A box holds 4 red marbles and 6 blue marbles. Which are there more of?
2. Write "4 red marbles for every 6 blue marbles" in your own shorter words.
3. If you doubled the marbles and kept the same *mix*, how many of each
   color would you have?

*(If #3 felt tricky, stay with us — today builds exactly that idea.)*

## Explicit teaching

### What a ratio is

A **ratio** compares two quantities and tells how they relate **in a given
order**. Order is not decoration — it is part of the claim.

Say you count a bead tray: **3 red beads for every 5 blue beads**. The ratio
of red beads to blue beads is written **3:5** and read "3 to 5." That is a
claim about *red compared to blue*. Flip it — 5:3 — and you are now claiming
"5 blue for every 3 red," a completely different tray.

There are three standard ways to write a ratio:

- **3:5** (colon form)
- **3 to 5** (words)
- **3/5** (fraction form) — only when both parts refer to the same whole,
  e.g. 3 of the 8 beads are red. Use this form carefully; it is *not* a
  division problem.

### Worked example 1 — naming a ratio from objects

Lay out 3 red beads and 5 blue beads.

- The ratio of **red to blue** is **3:5**. In words: "3 red for every 5
  blue."
- The ratio of **blue to red** is **5:3**. In words: "5 blue for every 3
  red."
- The ratio of **red to total** is **3:8**, because 3 + 5 = 8 beads total.
  In words: "3 red for every 8 beads altogether."

**Check the order rule:** if someone asks for "red to blue" and you answer
5:3, you have described a different tray. Always ask yourself: *which
quantity was named first?*

**A common error:** writing 3:5 for the ratio of *blue to red* because the
numbers on the table are 3 and 5. The numbers don't order themselves — you
order them.

### Worked example 2 — the lemonade-stand story prompt

Look at the illustration:

pitchers of yellow lemonade, a stack of paper cups, and a red bicycle
nearby.](../assets/lemonade-stand-illustration.png)

*Caption: Amara's lemonade stand. This illustration is an AI-generated story
prompt for ratio work — not a photograph of a real stand.*

**Text alternative:** The picture shows a wooden lemonade stand with a
red-and-white striped awning. Two large pitchers of yellow lemonade sit on
the counter next to a stack of paper cups; a red bicycle leans against the
stand. (The recipe numbers below are given in words — the picture does not
contain any counts or labels.)

Amara's recipe for one batch of lemonade is:

> **2 cups of lemon concentrate for every 3 cups of water.**

- The ratio of **concentrate to water** is **2:3**.
- The ratio of **water to concentrate** is **3:2** — a different recipe
  entirely (more water than concentrate, flipped).
- The ratio of **concentrate to the whole batch** is **2:5**, because one
  batch is 2 + 3 = 5 cups of liquid.

If Amara wants a bigger batch with the *same taste*, she must keep the
relationship 2:3 — she cannot just "add a little of each." (Next lesson
shows how to grow it fairly.) A frequent mistake is growing 2:3 to 4:5 by
adding 2 to each part — Lesson 02 proves why that changes the taste.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. A fruit bowl has 4 apples and 7 oranges. Write the ratio of apples to
   oranges. *(Hint: which fruit was named first?)*
2. In that bowl, write the ratio of oranges to apples. How is it different
   from #1?
3. A team scored 6 goals and allowed 2 goals. Write the ratio of goals
   scored to goals allowed. Then write the ratio of goals allowed to goals
   scored.
4. Look at the lemonade-stand picture. Amara sells a small cup and a large
   cup. Suppose 5 small cups are sold for every 8 large cups. Write the
   ratio of small to large. *(The picture is a story prompt; the 5-and-8
   numbers are given here.)*

## Independent practice

5. Draw a tray of your own: some circles (blueberries) and some triangles
   (strawberries). Write three ratios: part-to-part in both orders and one
   part-to-whole.
6. A recipe uses 3 cups flour for every 2 cups sugar. Write the ratio of
   flour to sugar and the ratio of sugar to flour. Which one would taste
   sweeter if you swapped the numbers — and why?
7. **Error analysis:** Two learners describe a jar with 6 green beads and 9
   yellow beads. Dana writes 6:9 (green to yellow). Theo writes 9:6 (green
   to yellow). Who is right, and what did the other person likely mix up?
8. **Explain your reasoning:** A learner says "2:3 and 3:2 are the same
   ratio because the numbers are the same." Agree or disagree, and give a
   real example showing why.

## Application

**Stand talk.** You run Amara's stand with a friend. You say "the recipe is
2:3" and your friend hears "3:2." Your friend doubles the batch their way.
Write the two recipes side by side (concentrate:water), and explain in one
or two sentences which batch keeps Amara's taste and how you can tell from
the order.

## Exit check

A parking lot has 12 cars and 18 bicycles.

a. Write the ratio of cars to bicycles.
b. Write the ratio of bicycles to the total number of vehicles.
c. A friend writes "the ratio of bicycles to cars is 12:18." What is wrong?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** use the sentence frame "The ratio of ___
  (first quantity) to ___ (second quantity) is ___ : ___." Point to the
  objects while saying each part.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the ratios with counters and point to
  which group goes first; the adult scribes the written sentences.
- **Extension:** A triple ratio describes three quantities in order — e.g.,
  a trail-mix recipe of 2 cups oats : 3 cups cereal : 1 cup raisins (2:3:1).
  Write three pairwise ratios from it, and explain what 2:3:1 claims that
  three separate pair ratios do not.

## Teacher note (misconceptions; key reference)

- **Order blindness** is the signature error of Lesson 01: learners record
  the two numbers they see rather than the two quantities in the asked order.
  Insist on reading the question's order aloud before writing.
- **Fraction-form confusion:** some learners will compute 3/5 as 0.6 and
  lose the ratio meaning. Remind them that in 3:5, the 3 and 5 name *parts
  of a relationship*, not a division to perform — though 3 out of 5 and
  3 out of 8 are legitimate fraction readings when the whole is named.
