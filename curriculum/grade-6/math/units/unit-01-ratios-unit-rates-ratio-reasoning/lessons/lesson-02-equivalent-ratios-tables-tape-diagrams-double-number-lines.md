# Lesson 02 — Equivalent Ratios: Tables, Tape Diagrams, and Double Number Lines

**My goal:** I can build equivalent ratios by scaling both quantities by the
same factor, using ratio tables, tape diagrams, and double number lines —
and find missing values.

**Time:** about 45 minutes × 2 concept sessions (S03: tables + tape; S05:
double number lines), plus guided practice. This page covers both sessions;
S05 is marked below.

**Materials:** grid paper, ruler, colored pencils, ratio-table tray or
paper; printed or on-screen tape diagram below.

**Standards:** 6.RP.A.1, 6.RP.A.3.a · **Unit objectives:** UO1

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Amara's recipe: 2 cups concentrate for every 3 cups water (2:3). If she
   makes a double batch, how many cups of concentrate? How many of water?
2. A different cook "doubles" 2:3 by adding 2 to each part: 4:5. Would that
   taste the same? Guess first — today proves it.

## Explicit teaching

### The equal-scaling rule

**Equivalent ratios** describe the *same relationship* with different numbers.
You make them by **multiplying (or dividing) both quantities by the same
factor**. That factor is called the **scale factor**.

- 2:3 scaled by 2 → **4:6** (2 × 2 = 4, 3 × 2 = 6). Same taste.
- 2:3 scaled by 4 → **8:12**. Same taste.
- Adding the same number to both parts — 2:3 → 4:5 — does **not** preserve
  the relationship. 4/6 = 2/3, but 4/5 ≠ 2/3. This is the additive-scaling
  trap from the starter lesson.

### Worked example 1 — a ratio table

Build Amara's recipe table. Each row scales 2:3 by a whole-number factor:

| Batch | Concentrate (cups) | Water (cups) | Scale factor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 3 | × 1 |
| 2 | 4 | 6 | × 2 |
| 3 | 6 | 9 | × 3 |
| 5 | 10 | 15 | × 5 |

Every row is an equivalent ratio: 2:3 = 4:6 = 6:9 = 10:15. Reading *down a
column* also works: concentrate values 2, 4, 6, 10 and water values 3, 6, 9,
15 grow by the same factor each step.

**Missing-value move:** Amara has 9 cups of water. How much concentrate does
she need? Find the row with water = 9: scale factor 3, so concentrate =
2 × 3 = **6 cups**. The ratio 6:9 simplifies back to 2:3 (divide both by 3).

### Worked example 2 — a tape diagram

A tape diagram draws each quantity as a bar split into equal parts. Parts
are equal *within* a tape but the tapes only match up as a pair:

![Tape diagrams: the ratio 2:3 shown as a 2-part concentrate tape above a
3-part water tape, then the same tapes doubled to 4 parts and 6
parts.](../assets/tape-diagram-2-to-3.svg)

Read it this way: one concentrate part lines up with one-and-a-half water
parts — the relationship is 2:3. Double every part (scale factor 2) and you
get 4 concentrate parts against 6 water parts: **4:6**, visibly the same
relationship, just more of it.

**Check against the trap:** adding 2 to each part would give a 4-part
concentrate tape against a 5-part water tape — the tapes no longer line up
in the same rhythm. The diagram *shows* the broken relationship.

### S05 — Worked example 3 — a double number line

A **double number line** is two number lines drawn together, one for each
quantity, with equivalent-ratio pairs aligned vertically.

Imagine Amara's line: the top line counts concentrate (0, 2, 4, 6, 8 cups),
the bottom line counts water (0, 3, 6, 9, 12 cups), aligned so that 2 sits
above 3, 4 above 6, and so on. Every vertical pair is an equivalent ratio.

**Missing-value move on the line:** Amara has 8 cups of concentrate. Walk
the top line to 8; read straight down: the bottom line says **12 cups of
water**. Check in the table: 8:12, scale factor 4 from 2:3. ✓

Double number lines shine when the missing value sits *between* whole-number
rows — you can read the in-between alignment, then confirm by computing the
scale factor.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Complete this ratio table for 3:4 (oil to vinegar) through scale factor
   5. *(Hint: multiply both parts each row.)*
2. A tape diagram shows 3 shaded parts out of a 5-part bar for ingredient A
   and a 5-part bar fully matched for ingredient B. What ratio does it show?
   Draw the tape scaled by 3. What is the equivalent ratio?
3. **(S05)** Draw a double number line for the 3:4 recipe from #1 with oil
   marked 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 and vinegar marked 0, 4, 8, 12, 16. If you have 9
   cups of oil, how much vinegar? Read it from the line, then check with
   the scale factor.
4. Find the missing value: 5:7 = 20:___. Name the scale factor.

## Independent practice

5. Amara's stand also sells iced tea: 3 cups tea for every 1 cup lemonade
   (3:1). Build a ratio table through scale factor 4, then find how much
   lemonade is needed for 12 cups of tea.
6. **Error analysis:** "To get from 2:3 to 10:15 I added 8 to the first
   part and 12 to the second part, so it must be equivalent." The final
   ratio *is* equivalent — but is the reasoning sound? Explain what the
   correct scale factor is and why "adding different amounts" is still
   wrong thinking even when the answer matches.
7. Which is the better description of "same relationship": equal ratios
   written with the same numbers, or ratios built by the same scale factor?
   Defend your choice with the 2:3 → 8:12 example.
8. A map uses 1 cm : 25 km. Find the missing value: __ cm : 100 km.
   (Preview: this is unit-conversion thinking, coming in Lesson 05.)

## Application

**Batch planning.** Amara expects 40 customers; each gets a 5-cup serving
(the whole 2:3 batch). Build the ratio table to the batch size she needs,
read the concentrate and water totals, and write one sentence for each of
the three models (table, tape, double number line) explaining *how you know*
your totals keep the taste.

*Check: 40 servings × 5 cups = 200 cups; scale factor 40; concentrate =
80 cups, water = 120 cups.*

## Exit check

a. Complete: 4:5 = 12:___ (name the scale factor).
b. Draw a tape diagram for 4:5 and shade it to show the scale-factor-3
   version. What is the equivalent ratio?
c. On a double number line for 4:5 (first line 0, 4, 8, 12; second line 0,
   5, 10, 15), what aligns with 12 on the first line?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** pre-drawn tape frames with labeled parts;
  sentence frame "I scaled both parts by ___, so ___ : ___ is equivalent
  to ___ : ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** build tapes with paper strips cut to equal
  lengths; point to aligned pairs on the double number line while the adult
  scribes.
- **Extension:** non-whole scale factors — 2:3 scaled by 1.5 gives 3:4.5;
  explain why the relationship still holds and where you would see this
  (e.g., one-and-a-half batches). Also: prove generally that scaling by n/n
  preserves the part-to-whole fraction.

## Teacher note (misconceptions; key reference)

- **Additive scaling** is the unit's deepest trap; it resurfaces on every
  assessment. The tape diagram is the best antidote because the broken
  rhythm is visible.
- **Column confusion:** learners sometimes scale down one column and up the
  other. Insist the factor applies to the whole row.
- **Double number line alignment errors:** learners misalign the zero marks.
  Always anchor 0-to-0 first.
