# Lesson 04 — GCF, LCM, and the Distributive Property

**My goal:** I can find the greatest common factor and least common
multiple of two whole numbers by listing factors and multiples, and I can
use the distributive property to rewrite a sum as a multiple of a sum.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** hundred chart (optional), paper, pencil.

**Standards:** 6.NS.B.4 · **Unit objectives:** UO7

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. List all the factors of 12. (How do you know you found them all?)
2. Write the first five multiples of 6.
3. Compute 4 × (9 + 2) two ways: add first, then distribute.

## Explicit teaching

### Factors and multiples — the listing strategy

A **factor** of a number divides it evenly (12's factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6,
12). A **multiple** of a number is that number times 1, 2, 3, …
(multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, …).

To be sure you listed *all* factors, work in pairs from the outside in:
1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9, 6 × 6 — when the pairs meet in the middle,
you are done. Factors of 36: **1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36**.

### Worked example 1 — GCF and the distributive property

Rosa is packing **36** mini muffins and **48** conchas into identical gift
boxes, using *every* pastry and the same mix in each box. What is the
greatest number of boxes she can make, and what goes in each?

The question is the **greatest common factor** of 36 and 48 — the largest
number that divides both.

- Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36
- Factors of 48: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48
- Common factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 → **GCF(36, 48) = 12**

12 boxes: 36 ÷ 12 = **3 muffins** and 48 ÷ 12 = **4 conchas** per box.

Now the distributive property in action — the total pastries, two ways:

> 36 + 48 = 12(3 + 4) = 12 × 7 = **84**

Check: 36 + 48 = 84 ✓. Writing the sum as **a multiple of a sum** (with
3 and 4 sharing no common factor) is exactly what 6.NS.B.4 asks for —
the standard's own example is 36 + 8 = 4(9 + 2).

### Worked example 2 — LCM

Two bus routes leave Rosa's stop every **6 minutes** and every **8
minutes**. They just left together. In how many minutes will they next
leave together?

The question is the **least common multiple** — the smallest positive
number that is a multiple of both.

- Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, **24**, 30, …
- Multiples of 8: 8, 16, **24**, 32, …
- **LCM(6, 8) = 24** minutes.

**GCF or LCM — how to choose?** Ask what the answer must satisfy:

- *Splitting things into equal groups* (boxes, teams, shares) → the
  answer must **divide** both numbers → **GCF** (it is *smaller* than or
  equal to both).
- *Repeating events lining up* (buses, flashing lights, schedules) →
  the answer must **be a multiple of** both numbers → **LCM** (it is
  *larger* than or equal to both).

**A common error:** answering the bus question with 48 (a common
multiple, but not the *least*) or with 2 (a common factor — buses do not
leave every 2 minutes on *both* schedules together after time 0).
"Least" and "greatest" are doing real work in the question.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Find GCF(24, 36) by listing factors. Then write 24 + 36 as a multiple
   of a sum. *(Hint: pair factors from the outside in.)*
2. Find LCM(4, 10) by listing multiples. *(Hint: stop at the first
   number on both lists.)*
3. Rosa has 28 stickers and 42 stamps for goodie bags — identical bags,
   everything used. How many bags? What is in each? *(GCF or LCM? Why?)*
4. A red light blinks every 9 seconds, a blue light every 12 seconds.
   They blink together now. When next? *(GCF or LCM? Why?)*

## Independent practice

5. Find: (a) GCF(30, 45); (b) LCM(9, 12); (c) GCF(16, 40); (d) LCM(5, 7).
6. Rewrite each sum as a multiple of a sum with no common factor inside:
   (a) 25 + 15; (b) 16 + 24; (c) 28 + 42. Check each by computing both
   sides.
7. **Error analysis:** "Dev says GCF(18, 24) = 6 and LCM(18, 24) = 6,
   because 6 is the biggest number that works for both." What is right,
   what is wrong, and what is the correct LCM?
8. Two trays hold 20 and 30 pastries. Rosa wants identical serving plates
   with no pastry left over and the same mix per plate — what is the
   greatest number of plates she can make? How many pastries per plate?
   Justify with GCF or LCM language.

## Application

**The festival schedule.** For the street festival, the band plays every
**15 minutes** and the dance troupe performs every **20 minutes**. Both
start at noon.

a. When is the next time both perform at the same time? (Show the
   multiple lists.)
b. Rosa sells pastry boxes during the festival: she has **54** empanadas
   and **81** cookies to pack into identical boxes with nothing left over.
   What is the greatest number of boxes, and what is in each? Write the
   total as a multiple of a sum.
c. Explain in two sentences how you decided which of (a), (b) needed
   the LCM and which needed the GCF.

*Check: (a) LCM(15, 20) = 60 → 1:00 PM. (b) GCF(54, 81) = 27 → 27 boxes;
2 empanadas + 3 cookies each; 54 + 81 = 27(2 + 3) = 27 × 5 = 135 ✓.*

## Exit check

a. GCF(14, 35) = ? (List to be sure.)
b. LCM(3, 8) = ?
c. 18 + 27 = __(__ + __) with no common factor inside the parentheses.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** factor-pair T-chart template; sentence
  frame — "I need the (GCF/LCM) because the answer must (divide/be a
  multiple of) both numbers."
- **Alternate response mode:** hundred-chart coloring — color multiples
  of each number in two colors; the LCM is the first double-colored
  number; factors circled in the factor rows.
- **Extension:** GCF(48, 72) and LCM(48, 72); notice that
  GCF × LCM = 48 × 72. Test whether this always works on (6, 8) and
  (12, 18), and state the pattern as a conjecture (no proof required).

## Teacher note (misconceptions; key reference)

- **GCF/LCM swap** is the dominant error; the "divide both vs multiple
  of both" decision sentence is the durable fix — drill the *choice*,
  not just the listing.
- **Stopping the multiple list too early** (picking a common multiple
  that is not least): require both lists written until the first match
  is *circled*.
- **Distributive rewrite with a common factor left inside** (e.g.,
  36 + 48 = 6(6 + 8)): insist on the "no common factor inside" check —
  6 and 8 share 2, so keep going to 12(3 + 4).
