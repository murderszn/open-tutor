# Lesson 02 — Why Invert-and-Multiply Works

**My goal:** I can compute a fraction divided by a fraction, and I can
explain — with a common denominator or a multiplication check — why
"flip the second fraction and multiply" gives the right answer.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** fraction bars or grid paper, pencil.

**Standards:** 6.NS.A.1 · **Unit objectives:** UO5

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Rewrite 2/3 and 3/4 with the common denominator 12.
2. (1/2) ÷ (1/4): draw the model from Lesson 01 and give the quotient.
3. If □ ÷ (3/4) = 8/9, what multiplication does that suggest? (Hint:
   division undoes multiplication.)

## Explicit teaching

### Same-size pieces divide cleanly

The shortcut "invert and multiply" is not magic — it is a faster way of
doing something you already understand: **dividing same-size pieces**.

Take the standard's own example: **(2/3) ÷ (3/4)** — "how many 3/4-cup
servings are in 2/3 of a cup of yogurt?"

**Step 1 — common denominator.** 2/3 = 8/12 and 3/4 = 9/12. Now the
question is (8/12) ÷ (9/12): *how many 9-twelfth servings fit in 8
twelfths?*

**Step 2 — divide the counts.** The pieces are the same size (twelfths),
so the twelfths cancel and we just divide the numbers of pieces:
8 ÷ 9 = **8/9**.

So (2/3) ÷ (3/4) = **8/9** — less than one full serving, which makes
sense: the serving (3/4 cup) is bigger than the amount we have (2/3 cup).

### The shortcut computes the same thing

Now watch what the common-denominator work does in general:

> (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (ad/bd) ÷ (bc/bd) = (ad) ÷ (bc) = **ad/bc**

The common denominator bd cancels, leaving ad ÷ bc. And ad/bc is exactly
what you get from "flip and multiply":

> (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c) = **ad/bc** ✓

So the rule is not a trick to memorize — it is the common-denominator
division with the middle step skipped. When you forget the rule, rebuild
it: common denominator, then divide the numerators.

### Worked example 1 — fraction ÷ fraction, two ways

**(4/5) ÷ (2/3)** — "4/5 of a pan of brownies; each serving is 2/3 of a
pan. How many servings?"

**Way A (common denominator):** 4/5 = 12/15, 2/3 = 10/15.
(12/15) ÷ (10/15) = 12 ÷ 10 = 12/10 = **6/5** = 1 1/5 servings.

**Way B (invert and multiply):** (4/5) × (3/2) = 12/10 = **6/5** ✓.
Same answer — Way B skipped the 15ths.

**Verify by multiplication** (the readiness-check idea): division undoes
multiplication, so divisor × quotient must equal the dividend:
(2/3) × (6/5) = 12/15 = **4/5** ✓. The answer checks out.

### Worked example 2 — a story with an answer smaller than 1

**The yogurt question** from the standard: 2/3 cup of yogurt, 3/4-cup
servings.

(2/3) ÷ (3/4) = (2/3) × (4/3) = 8/9 of a serving.

**Sense check:** the serving is larger than the supply, so fewer than one
serving — 8/9 < 1 ✓. And the multiplication check: (3/4) × (8/9) =
24/36 = 2/3 ✓ — three-quarters of the quotient rebuilds the original
2/3, exactly as the standard describes.

**A common error:** flipping the *first* fraction — (3/2) × (2/3) = 1 —
or flipping both. The rule touches **only the divisor** (the second
fraction, the group size). When in doubt, redo Way A: only the
common-denominator numerators divide.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. (5/6) ÷ (2/3): rewrite with denominator 6 (or 18), divide the
   numerators, then confirm with invert-and-multiply.
   *(Hint: 2/3 = 4/6.)*
2. (3/8) ÷ (1/2): which is larger, the dividend or the divisor? Predict
   whether the quotient is above or below 1, then compute.
3. Rosa has 7/8 lb of dough. Each roll uses 1/4 lb. How many rolls?
   Solve with a picture AND with invert-and-multiply; do they agree?
4. **Error analysis:** "Maya computed (1/2) ÷ (3/4) as (2/1) × (3/4) =
   3/2." Name what she flipped wrong, compute the correct quotient, and
   verify it with multiplication.

## Independent practice

5. Compute: (a) (2/5) ÷ (3/10); (b) (7/9) ÷ (2/3); (c) (5/4) ÷ (1/2).
   Simplify each answer.
6. A 3/4-mile trail is marked every 1/8 mile. How many markers? (Think:
   is this "how many groups?" or "how much per group?")
7. Write a story for (5/6) ÷ (1/2), solve it, and verify with a
   multiplication check.
8. **Explain your reasoning:** in two or three sentences, explain to a
   5th grader why (2/3) ÷ (3/4) = 8/9, using either the common-denominator
   idea or the multiplication check — your choice.

## Application

**The deterministic model.** Open
[assets/fraction-division-model.svg](../assets/fraction-division-model.svg)
(a precise, reproducible bar diagram — not a generated picture).

a. The bar is split into 12 equal cells. How many cells are shaded, and
   what fraction of the whole is that?
b. The shaded part is grouped into quarters (3 cells each). How many
   full quarters fit, and what fraction of a quarter is left over?
c. Use the diagram to write the division equation it models and its
   quotient. Then confirm the quotient with invert-and-multiply.

*Check: (a) 8 cells = 2/3. (b) 2 full quarters + 2 cells = 2/3 of a
quarter left → 2 2/3. (c) (2/3) ÷ (1/4) = (2/3) × 4 = 8/3 = 2 2/3 ✓.*

**Text-only alternative:** the same questions are answerable from the
cell counts alone (12 cells, 8 shaded, groups of 3) — no image needed.

## Exit check

a. (3/4) ÷ (2/5) = ? Show the flip-and-multiply step.
b. Complete: (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (__/__).
c. Why is (3/4) ÷ (1/2) larger than (3/4) ÷ 2? Answer with the
   question-type language from Lesson 01.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** word bank — dividend, divisor, quotient,
  reciprocal, verify. Sentence frame: "I flipped ___ because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** sort worked cards into "flipped the
  divisor" vs "flipped the dividend" piles and fix the wrong ones orally.
- **Extension:** (7/6) ÷ (2/3) — predict whether the quotient exceeds 2
  (sense check), compute, and verify. Then try a division where the answer
  is exactly 1 and explain why.

## Teacher note (misconceptions; key reference)

- **Flipping the dividend** is the signature error; the common-denominator
  rebuild (Way A) is the reteach, not more drill on the rule.
- **Skipping simplification:** 12/10 left unsimplified loses the
  "clean answer" habit the assessment rewards; require simplest form.
- **Quotient-size blindness:** learners who can compute but cannot predict
  whether the answer is above/below 1 need more Lesson-01 question-type
  work before fluency practice.
