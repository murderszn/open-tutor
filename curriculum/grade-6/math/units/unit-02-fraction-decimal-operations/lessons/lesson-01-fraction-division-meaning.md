# Lesson 01 — Fraction Division: What the Question Means

**My goal:** I can read a fraction division as a real question about groups
or sharing, solve it with a picture or a story, and say what the quotient
means.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper strips or fraction bars, paper, pencil. Optional:
measuring cups with water or rice (adult supervises).

**Standards:** 6.NS.A.1 · **Unit objectives:** UO5

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Draw 3 wholes. Split each whole into halves. How many halves in all?
2. 1/2 of a chocolate bar is shared equally among 3 people. Is each
   person's share more or less than 1/2? Sketch it.
3. Write one question the calculation 6 ÷ 2 could answer.

## Explicit teaching

### Division asks about groups

Every division calculation is one of two questions:

- **"How many groups?"** — I have an amount; each group takes a fixed
  size. How many groups can I fill? Example: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 ("how many
  2s are in 6?").
- **"How much in one group?"** — I have an amount; it is shared into a
  known number of groups. How much per group? Example: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 again,
  but now meaning 6 shared among 2 → 3 each.

Fractions change nothing about this — they only change the *sizes*.
"3 ÷ (1/2)" asks: **how many half-size groups fit in 3?** "(1/2) ÷ 3"
asks: **half a whole shared among 3 — how much per person?** The two
questions look alike and mean completely different things. Reading the
question correctly is half the work.

### Worked example 1 — whole divided by a fraction ("how many groups?")

Rosa has **3 cups** of trail mix. Each snack bag holds **1/2 cup**.
How many bags can she fill?

The question: 3 ÷ (1/2) — *how many halves are in 3 wholes?*

Draw 3 whole bars. Split every bar into 2 halves:

```
|‾‾‾|‾‾‾|   |‾‾‾|‾‾‾|   |‾‾‾|‾‾‾|
  1     1     2     2     3     3   (each half = one bag)
```

Count the halves: **6**. So 3 ÷ (1/2) = **6 bags**.

**Sense check:** each bag is *smaller* than a cup, so the answer should be
*bigger* than 3. 6 > 3 — the answer makes sense. Whenever you divide by a
fraction smaller than 1, expect the quotient to be *larger* than the
dividend.

### Worked example 2 — fraction divided by a whole ("how much per group?")

Three friends share **1/2 lb** of chocolate equally. How much does each
person get?

The question: (1/2) ÷ 3 — *half a whole, shared among 3*.

Draw the half of a bar. Split that half into 3 equal pieces:

```
 whole bar:  |████████████████|                |
             (half = ████) split into 3  → each piece is 1/6 of the whole
```

Each person gets **1/6 lb**. So (1/2) ÷ 3 = **1/6**.

**Sense check:** the share must be *smaller* than the half we started with.
1/6 < 1/2 ✓. And 3 × 1/6 = 3/6 = 1/2 — the three shares rebuild the half. ✓

**A common error:** answering (1/2) ÷ 3 with 3/2. That confuses "shared
among 3" with "3 groups of a half." Always match the question type to the
picture: sharing *shrinks* each piece; the quotient must be smaller than
the starting fraction.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. 4 pizzas are each cut into thirds. How many third-slices in all?
   *(Hint: 4 ÷ (1/3) — draw the 4 wholes first.)*
2. 2/3 cup of yogurt is shared equally between 2 people. How much per
   person? *(Hint: draw the 2/3, then split it in half — what fraction of
   the whole cup is one piece?)*
3. How many 1/4-cup servings are in 2 cups of rice? Write the division,
   draw it, and give the quotient.
4. Write a story question for (3/4) ÷ 2. Then solve it.

## Independent practice

5. Rosa has 5 cups of flour. Each loaf uses 1/2 cup. How many loaves?
   Check your answer against the sense-check rule.
6. 1/4 gallon of paint is shared equally among 2 rooms. How much paint
   per room? Draw the model.
7. **Error analysis:** "Leo says 2 ÷ (1/4) = 1/2 because division makes
   things smaller." Find two mistakes in his thinking and give the correct
   quotient with a picture.
8. 3/5 liter of juice is poured equally into 3 glasses. How much per
   glass? Show the whole-bar model.

## Application

**The panadería tray.** Rosa's display tray holds exactly **2 1/2 cups**
of sliced almonds for decorating. Each concha needs **1/4 cup**.

a. Write the division question and solve it: how many conchas can she
   decorate?
b. Draw the model: start with 2 1/2 wholes, mark every quarter, count.
c. Suppose each concha needed **1/2 cup** instead. Without computing,
   would the answer be larger or smaller than in (a)? Explain, then
   compute to confirm.

*Check: (a) (5/2) ÷ (1/4) = (5/2) × 4 = 10 conchas. (c) Larger? No —
smaller: bigger servings means fewer conchas. (5/2) ÷ (1/2) = 5.*

## Exit check

a. 2 ÷ (1/3): write the "how many groups?" question in words and solve.
b. (2/3) ÷ 2: write the "how much per group?" question in words and solve.
c. Dividing 4 by 1/2 gives 8. Explain in one sentence why the quotient
   is *larger* than 4.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** sentence frames — "The question __ ÷ __
  asks ___," and "I know my answer is reasonable because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** build the models with paper strips or
  measuring cups (adult-supervised) and state each quotient orally before
  writing.
- **Extension:** (5/6) ÷ (1/3) — draw a 6-cell bar, shade 5/6, group the
  shaded cells into thirds; predict, then verify with the count. (Preview
  of Lesson 02's fraction ÷ fraction work.)

## Teacher note (misconceptions; key reference)

- **Question-type blindness:** learners compute without identifying which
  question the division asks. Insist on the one-sentence "this asks ___"
  before any arithmetic — it is the skill UO5 measures.
- **"Division always makes smaller":** directly contradicted by Worked
  example 1. The sense-check rule (dividing by a fraction < 1 grows the
  quotient) is worth rehearsing aloud until it is automatic.
- **Flipped dividend/divisor in stories:** learners write (1/2) ÷ 3 when
  they mean 3 ÷ (1/2). Ask: "which number is the *amount you have*, and
  which is the *group size*?"
