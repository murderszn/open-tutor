# Lesson 03 — Decimal Operations Fluency

**My goal:** I can add, subtract, multiply, and divide multi-digit
decimals — and divide multi-digit whole numbers — with the standard
algorithm, and I estimate first so I can catch a misplaced decimal point.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper, pencil. Calculator for checking only.

**Standards:** 6.NS.B.2, 6.NS.B.3 · **Unit objectives:** UO6

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. What is the value of the 4 in 12.45? In 0.04?
2. 32 × 45 = ? (You will need this product in Worked example 1.)
3. Estimate: 48.6 ÷ 6 is about ___ ÷ 6 = ___.

## Explicit teaching

### The estimate-first rule

Every decimal computation in this lesson follows the same discipline:
**estimate first, compute, compare.** The estimate is your lie detector —
a decimal point in the wrong place almost always contradicts the estimate.

### Worked example 1 — decimal multiplication

**3.2 × 4.5**

**Estimate first:** 3.2 ≈ 3 and 4.5 ≈ 4.5, so about 3 × 4.5 = 13.5.
The real answer should be near 13–14.

**Compute:** ignore the decimal points and multiply as whole numbers —
you did this in the readiness check: 32 × 45 = **1440**.

**Place the point:** 3.2 has 1 decimal place, 4.5 has 1 decimal place, so
the product needs 1 + 1 = **2 decimal places**: 1440 → **14.40 = 14.4**.

**Compare:** 14.4 sits right next to the estimate 13.5 ✓. (The estimate
is low because we rounded 3.2 down — expected and fine.)

Why it works: 3.2 = 32/10 and 4.5 = 45/10, so 3.2 × 4.5 =
(32 × 45)/(10 × 10) = 1440/100 = 14.40. Counting decimal places is
counting the tens in the denominator.

### Worked example 2 — decimal division

**3.45 ÷ 1.5**

**Estimate first:** 3.45 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 3.5 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 2.3. Expect an answer a
little above 2.

**Compute:** shift the decimal point in *both* numbers until the divisor
is whole: 3.45 ÷ 1.5 = **34.5 ÷ 15**. (Multiplying both by 10 does not
change the quotient — the same scaling idea as equivalent ratios in
Unit 01.)

Now divide: 15 goes into 34 twice (2 × 15 = 30), remainder 4; bring down
the 5 → 45; 15 goes into 45 three times. **Quotient: 2.3.**

**Compare and verify:** 2.3 ≈ estimate 2.3 ✓. Verify by multiplication:
2.3 × 1.5 = 3.45 ✓.

### Adding, subtracting, and whole-number division

**Line up the decimal points** — always. 20.00 − 7.36: the 0s hold the
hundredths and tenths places while you borrow.

```
  20.00
−  7.36
────────
  12.64        (estimate: 20 − 7 ≈ 13 ✓)
```

**Multi-digit whole division** uses the same long-division algorithm from
grade 5, extended: **1,435 ÷ 5**.

5 into 14 → 2 (2 × 5 = 10), remainder 4; bring down 3 → 43; 5 into 43 →
8 (8 × 5 = 40), remainder 3; bring down 5 → 35; 5 into 35 → 7.
**Quotient: 287.** Check: 287 × 5 = 1,435 ✓.

**A common error:** dividing 3.45 ÷ 1.5 as 345 ÷ 15 by shifting only the
*dividend's* point. Shift **both** — scaling only one number changes the
quotient. Another: adding 12.45 + 3.8 without alignment and getting
12.83. The aligned version: 12.45 + 3.80 = **16.25**.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Estimate, then compute: 12.45 + 3.8. *(Hint: line up the points;
   3.8 = 3.80.)*
2. Estimate, then compute: 0.6 × 0.7. *(Hint: whole-number product first;
   how many decimal places total?)*
3. Estimate, then compute: 7.2 ÷ 0.9. *(Hint: shift both points — what
   whole-number division is this?)*
4. 952 ÷ 7 with the standard algorithm. Check by multiplying back.

## Independent practice

5. Estimate first, then compute: (a) 24.8 + 3.65; (b) 40 − 12.75;
   (c) 2.6 × 3.4; (d) 9.6 ÷ 2.4. For each, write the estimate beside
   the exact answer.
6. 3,276 ÷ 12 with the standard algorithm. Check: does 12 × (your
   quotient) = 3,276?
7. **Error analysis:** "Priya computed 4.8 ÷ 1.2 = 0.4. Her estimate was
   'about 4.' What did she do wrong, and how does her own estimate expose
   it?" (Correct: 48 ÷ 12 = 4.)
8. A ribbon is 8.4 meters long. Rosa cuts it into 6 equal pieces. How
   long is each piece? Estimate first, then divide: 8.4 ÷ 6.

## Application

**The market receipt.** Rosa buys: 2.5 kg of flour at $1.80/kg, 0.75 kg
of sugar at $2.40/kg, and 3 dozen eggs at $4.25/dozen. (Prices are
fictional practice data.)

a. Estimate the total bill before computing.
b. Compute each line (2.5 × 1.80; 0.75 × 2.40; 3 × 4.25) and the total.
c. She pays with a $50 bill. Compute her change with decimal subtraction.
   Does the change make sense against your estimate?

*Check: (b) $4.50 + $1.80 + $12.75 = $19.05; (c) $50 − $19.05 = $30.95.*

## Exit check

a. 6.05 + 2.9 = ? (Show the alignment.)
b. 48.6 ÷ 6 = ? (You estimated this in the readiness check.)
c. In one sentence: why do you shift the decimal point in *both* numbers
   when dividing decimals?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** place-value chart printed on the desk;
  sentence frame — "My estimate was ___, my answer is ___, so it is
  (reasonable/not reasonable) because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** estimate-and-check orally with the adult
  for each problem before writing the algorithm.
- **Extension:** 8,544 ÷ 24 with the standard algorithm (two-digit
  divisor); then 86.4 ÷ 1.8 — notice how the decimal version reuses the
  same digits. (Preview: decimal division *is* whole-number division
  after scaling.)

## Teacher note (misconceptions; key reference)

- **Point-placement by feel** in multiplication/division is the #1 error;
  the estimate-first discipline is the fix, not more reminders.
- **Misaligned addition/subtraction** persists from grade 5 — one
  misaligned problem in guided practice is worth catching live.
- **Division with a decimal divisor** where learners shift only one
  number: re-anchor to "multiplying both by the same power of 10 keeps
  the quotient" (a Unit 01 scaling idea).
