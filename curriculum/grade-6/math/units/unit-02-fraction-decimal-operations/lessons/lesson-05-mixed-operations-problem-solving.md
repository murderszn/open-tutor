# Lesson 05 — Mixed Operations Problem Solving

**My goal:** I can look at a multi-step problem, decide which operations
it needs and in what order, carry them out with fractions and decimals,
and check that the answer is reasonable.

**Time:** about 45 minutes (5 readiness · 15 teaching · 10 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper, pencil. Calculator for checking only.

**Standards:** 6.NS.A.1, 6.NS.B.2, 6.NS.B.3 · **Unit objectives:** UO5, UO6

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. (3/4) ÷ (1/2) = ?  (Simplify.)
2. 2.5 × 1.8 = ?  (Estimate first.)
3. Which is larger: GCF(18, 24) or LCM(18, 24)? No computation — use the
   size rule from Lesson 04.

## Explicit teaching

### Step 1: name the operations before computing

Multi-step problems punish rushing. Read the whole problem, then write a
**plan line** naming each operation in order *before* you touch numbers:

> "First add the fractions (common denominator), then multiply by 3,
> then subtract the decimal."

The order of operations still rules: parentheses first, then
multiplication/division left to right, then addition/subtraction left to
right. Fractions and decimals obey the same order — they are just numbers
wearing different clothes.

### Worked example 1 — unit pricing with fractions (a U01 reunion)

Two juice offers at the market (fictional practice prices):

- Offer A: a **3/4-liter** bottle for **$2.10**
- Offer B: a **5/6-liter** bottle for **$2.40**

Which is the better buy? Unit 01 taught us: compare **price per liter**.

**Plan line:** for each offer, divide price by liters (fraction division),
then compare the two decimals.

- A: 2.10 ÷ (3/4) = 2.10 × (4/3) = 8.40/3 = **$2.80 per liter**.
  (Estimate: 2.10 ÷ 0.75 ≈ 2.8 ✓.)
- B: 2.40 ÷ (5/6) = 2.40 × (6/5) = 14.40/5 = **$2.88 per liter**.
  (Estimate: 2.40 ÷ 0.83 ≈ 2.9 ✓.)

**$2.80 < $2.88 — Offer A is the better buy.** The fraction division from
Lesson 02 and the decimal sense from Lesson 03 had to work together;
either one alone could not finish the problem.

### Worked example 2 — order of operations across forms

Compute: **(1/2 + 1/4) × 3 − 0.5**

**Plan line:** parentheses first (add the fractions), then multiply,
then subtract the decimal.

1. Parentheses: 1/2 + 1/4 = 2/4 + 1/4 = **3/4**.
2. Multiply: (3/4) × 3 = **9/4** = 2.25.
3. Subtract: 9/4 − 0.5 = 9/4 − 1/2 = 9/4 − 2/4 = **7/4** = 1 3/4.

**Check in decimals:** 0.75 × 3 − 0.5 = 2.25 − 0.5 = 1.75 = 7/4 ✓.
Two forms, same answer — that agreement is the check.

**A common error:** computing left to right as ((1/2 + 1/4) × 3 − 0.5)
is fine here, but many learners compute 1/2 + 1/4 × 3 − 0.5 *without*
parentheses as ((3/4) × 3)… which happens to work, then conclude order
never matters. Contrast: 1/2 + (1/4 × 3) − 0.5 = 1/2 + 3/4 − 1/2 = 3/4,
while (1/2 + 1/4) × 3 − 0.5 = 7/4. Same numbers, different grouping,
different answer — **parentheses are decisions, not decoration.**

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Write a plan line, then compute: (2/3 + 1/6) × 2 − 0.25.
   *(Hint: common denominator 6 in the parentheses.)*
2. Rosa's two flour offers: a 2/5-kg bag for $1.20 and a 1/2-kg bag for
   $1.45. Find each price per kilogram and name the better buy.
   *(Hint: price ÷ kilograms, as in Worked example 1.)*
3. 8,544 ÷ 24: estimate first (about ___ ÷ 24 ≈ ___), then divide.
   *(Hint: Lesson 03 extension — the digits match 86.4 ÷ 1.8.)*
4. **Error analysis:** "Sam computed (5/6) ÷ (2/3) = 5/6 × 2/3 = 10/18.
   He says his method is fine because he multiplied." What did he
   actually do wrong, and what is the correct quotient?

## Independent practice

5. Compute with a plan line first: (a) (7/8 − 1/4) ÷ (1/2) + 0.5;
   (b) 3.6 ÷ 0.9 × (1/2 + 1/3).
6. A 2.5-lb bag of flour; each batch of pan dulce uses 3/4 lb. How many
   *full* batches can Rosa make? (How much flour is left over?)
7. Write a two-step word problem that requires one fraction division
   AND one decimal multiplication. Solve it, showing the plan line.
8. **Explain your reasoning:** a friend says "dividing by 1/2 and
   multiplying by 2 are the same, so dividing by 2/3 and multiplying by
   3/2 are the same." Is the reasoning correct? Use Lesson 02's
   common-denominator idea in your answer.

## Application

**The festival order.** (Fictional practice prices.) A customer orders
for a party: 4 boxes of empanadas at $6.75 per box, plus a 3/2-kg
(1.5-kg) cake at $9.60 per kilogram. Rosa gives a 10% neighborhood
discount on the empanadas only. (U01: 10% of a quantity = 10/100 of it.)

a. Write the plan line.
b. Compute the empanada subtotal, the discount, and the discounted
   empanada price.
c. Compute the cake price and the final total.
d. The customer pays with two $20 bills. Estimate the change, then
   compute it exactly.

*Check: (b) 4 × 6.75 = $27.00; discount 0.10 × 27 = $2.70; discounted
$24.30. (c) 1.5 × 9.60 = $14.40; total $38.70. (d) $40 − $38.70 =
$1.30.*

## Exit check

a. (5/6) ÷ (2/3): give the quotient and the multiplication check.
b. 2.4 × 3.5: estimate, then compute.
c. In one sentence: what is the first thing you do when a problem mixes
   fractions, decimals, and more than one operation?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** plan-line template — "First I will ___,
  then ___, then ___, because ___." Operation word bank on the desk.
- **Alternate response mode:** act out the plan line with number cards
  (operation cards in order) before writing any computation.
- **Extension:** design a "trap" problem where left-to-right computing
  gives a different answer than correct order of operations; trade with
  the adult, solve each other's, and explain the trap.

## Teacher note (misconceptions; key reference)

- **Operation choice** (not computation) is where most points are lost;
  the plan line makes the choice visible and gradable — score it.
- **Form-switching avoidance:** learners who refuse to convert 0.5 to 1/2
  (or vice versa) do twice the work; Worked example 2 models switching
  as a *strategy*, and the check rewards it.
- **"Same numbers, same answer"** thinking about parentheses: the
  contrast pair in Worked example 2 is the reteach — keep it handy.
