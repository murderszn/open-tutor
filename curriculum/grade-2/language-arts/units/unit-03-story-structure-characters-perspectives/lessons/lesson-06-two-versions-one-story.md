# Lesson 06 — Two Versions of One Story

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare two versions of the same story and say what is the same and what is different."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the two versions below; a two-column chart labeled SAME / DIFFERENT.
**Standards:** RL.2.9, RL.2.2 (retell as the comparison base).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If two friends both tell about the same lost kite, will
their stories be word-for-word the same?" (No — each person remembers
different details, even when the main events match.) "Today we compare
two tellings of one sunflower story and sort what matches from what
changes."

## Version A — "Tomas and the Tall Sunflower" (school garden)

> Tomas planted a sunflower seed in the school garden. He watered it
> every day after lunch. His classmates helped him pull weeds. The
> sunflower grew and grew until it was taller than the garden fence.
> The class measured it with a long tape. Then Tomas shared the seeds
> with his classmates, and they planted seeds of their own.

## Version B — "Tomas and the Tall Sunflower" (Grandma's yard)

> Tomas planted a sunflower seed in Grandma's yard. He forgot to water
> it one week, so Grandma helped him water it every morning. The
> sunflower grew and grew until it was taller than Grandma. Then Tomas
> shared the seeds with his family at dinner, and they saved seeds to
> plant next year.

## Explicit teaching

A **version** is one telling of a story. Two versions of the same story
share the core: the same main character, the same main events, the same
kind of ending. They differ in details: place, helpers, small events,
and sometimes who hears the ending. Comparing means sorting, not just
saying "they are kind of the same":

- **Same:** Tomas; a sunflower seed; a sunflower that grows very tall;
  Tomas shares seeds at the end.
- **Different:** place (school garden vs. Grandma's yard); helper
  (classmates pull weeds vs. Grandma helps water after Tomas forgets);
  how tall is measured (taller than the fence, measured with a tape, vs.
  taller than Grandma); who receives seeds (classmates vs. family at
  dinner).

Rule: a "same" must be true in *both* versions. "Tomas waters every
day" is not a same — in Version B he forgets one week and Grandma
helps. A "different" must name both sides: not just "the place is
different," but "school garden in A, Grandma's yard in B."

### Modeled example 1 — Finding a same

"Is 'Tomas plants a seed' a same? Version A: 'Tomas planted a sunflower
seed in the school garden.' Version B: 'Tomas planted a sunflower seed
in Grandma's yard.' Both plant a seed — same, even though the place
differs. Same core, different detail. That is the whole skill in one
sentence."

### Modeled example 2 — Finding a different, both sides named

"Helper: in A, classmates help pull weeds. In B, Grandma helps water
after Tomas forgets. So the different is: helper — classmates in A,
Grandma in B. If I only said 'the helpers are different,' I have not
proved it. Name both sides every time."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Is 'the sunflower grows tall' a same or a different?" (Same — taller than the fence in A, taller than Grandma in B; tall in both.)
2. "Name the place different, both sides." (School garden in A; Grandma's yard in B.)
3. "Name one ending different, both sides." (Seeds shared with classmates who plant their own in A; seeds shared with family at dinner and saved for next year in B.)
4. "Is 'Tomas never forgets' a same?" (No — it is false in B, where he forgets one week. A same must be true in both.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult says, "The stories are totally different because one is at school and one is at Grandma's." "Is that right?" (No — the core is the same: Tomas, seed, tall sunflower, sharing seeds. Different details do not make a different story.)

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. Write or dictate two sames, each true in both versions.
2. Write or dictate two differents, each naming both sides.
3. Which version has a measuring scene? What is measured with? (A — the class measures the sunflower with a long tape.)
4. In which version does Tomas forget something? What does he forget, and who helps? (B — he forgets to water for one week; Grandma helps water every morning.)
5. Retell Version A in two sentences, then Version B in two sentences. Circle the part that is the same in both retells.
6. If a third version put Tomas on a farm with his uncle, what would stay the same and what would change? (Same: Tomas, seed, tall sunflower, sharing; change: place and helper — a prediction, not a fact from either version.)

## Applied task

The learner tells the adult their own two-sentence version of the
sunflower story in a new place (a park, a balcony pot, a friend's
yard), keeping the core (Tomas, seed, tall sunflower, sharing seeds)
and changing at least two details. The adult checks: is the core intact,
and are the changes named? This is the seed of the project book's
"my version" page.

## Exit check

"Tell me one same and one different between the two sunflower
versions, with both sides for the different." (Any valid pair from the
key passes; "Tomas waters every day" as a same fails — see the answer
key.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep both versions visible side by side; use the SAME /
  DIFFERENT chart with one example already filled in; adult scribes.
- **Extension:** the learner compares Milo's kite story with Ana's
  rainy-day view: same Saturday? No — different days and stories; the
  comparison skill is to notice that *not* every pair shares a core, and
  to say so instead of forcing sames.
- **Language access:** color-code: underline sames in one color in both
  versions, circle differents in another; the colors make the sort
  visible before any writing.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) calling a detail that appears in only one
version a "same"; (2) naming a different with only one side; (3) judging
versions as "totally different" from one changed detail, or "exactly
the same" from a shared core. The unit assessment tests all three traps.
