# Lesson 04 — Two Points of View: The Same Rain, Two Feelings

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell how two characters see the same thing differently — and read their words in two different voices."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the picture in
[assets/two-views-rainy-day.png](../assets/two-views-rainy-day.png) (and
its text-only alternative below); the story below.
**Standards:** RL.2.6, RL.2.7.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Show the picture (or read the text-only alternative aloud) and ask:
"Who is happy here, and who is sad? How can you tell?" (Faces, bodies,
and what each child is doing: frowning at a soggy picnic vs. laughing in
a puddle.) "Same garden, same rain — so why do they feel different?
That is point of view."

## The story — "A Rainy Day, Two Ways" (original)

> On Saturday it rained and rained in the garden. Ana had planned a
> picnic. She set out her blanket and her basket. Then the rain soaked
> the blanket, and the bread got wet. "Oh no!" Ana said in a low, slow
> voice. "My picnic is spoiled. This is the worst day."
> Ben ran out in his red coat. He jumped in the biggest puddle he could
> find. Splash! "Hurray!" Ben said in a high, happy voice. "This is the
> best day! The rain made the best puddles!"
> Same garden. Same rain. Ana saw a spoiled picnic. Ben saw perfect
> puddles. They both went inside, and Ana laughed at Ben's wet boots.
> "Maybe rain is not all bad," Ana said.

## Explicit teaching

A character's **point of view** is how that character sees and feels
about what happens. It is not the same as what *really* happened — the
rain is the same rain for both children. Point of view comes from what
the character wanted, what they lost, and what they gained:

- Ana wanted a picnic. The rain took it away. So her view is: rain is bad.
- Ben wanted puddles. The rain gave them to him. So his view is: rain is great.

When you read dialogue aloud, use a **different voice for each
character**: Ana low and slow (sad), Ben high and happy (excited). The
voice is not decoration — it shows you understand the point of view.
If you read both lines in the same flat voice, the listener cannot hear
that the characters disagree.

Pictures and words work together (RL.2.7): the words say Ana's picnic
is spoiled, and the picture shows the wet blanket and spilled food; the
words say Ben loves puddles, and the picture shows the splash. If the
picture and the words disagreed, you would have to stop and ask which
one to trust — here they agree, so each one proves the other.

### Modeled example 1 — Ana's voice

Adult reads Ana's lines low and slow: "'Oh no! My picnic is spoiled.
This is the worst day.' Hear how my voice droops? That is Ana's point
of view: the rain ruined what she planned. Her proof is in the story
and the picture: wet blanket, wet bread."

### Modeled example 2 — Ben's voice, then the contrast

Adult switches to high and happy: "'Hurray! This is the best day! The
rain made the best puddles!' Same rain — opposite voice. Now the
contrast in one sentence: Ana thinks the rain is the worst because it
spoiled her picnic; Ben thinks it is the best because it made puddles.
If I said 'Ana loves the rain,' I would be wrong — that is Ben's view,
not Ana's."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Read Ana's lines in a low, slow voice. Then read Ben's lines in a high, happy voice. (Adult coaches: slower + quieter for sad; faster + brighter for happy.)
2. "Point to the picture detail that proves Ana is sad." (Frown, crossed arms, soggy blanket, spilled food.)
3. "Point to the picture detail that proves Ben is happy." (Laughing face, arms wide, big splash.)
4. "Why does Ana change her mind a little at the end?" (She laughs at Ben's wet boots; seeing his fun softens her view — "Maybe rain is not all bad." She does not suddenly love rain; her view shifts only a little.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult reads Ben's line in a sad voice. "Did that voice fit Ben?" (No — Ben is delighted; a sad voice would tell the listener the wrong point of view.)

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. Write or dictate Ana's point of view in one sentence ("Ana thinks… because…").
2. Write or dictate Ben's point of view in one sentence.
3. What is the same for both children? Name two things (same garden, same rain, same Saturday).
4. What is different? Name two things (what each wanted; how each feels).
5. Draw Ana's face and Ben's face (two small circles) and label each with one feeling word.
6. If Ana told the whole story, what would she call it? If Ben told it, what would he call it? (Accept any title that matches the view, e.g., "The Spoiled Picnic" vs. "The Best Puddles.")

## Applied task

The learner re-tells Milo's kite story twice, once as Milo ("I was so
sad when my kite…") and once as Ana ("My brother was sad, but I knew
we could ask for help…"). Each telling must include one feeling and one
thing that character did. The adult notes whether the two tellings
actually differ — if they are word-for-word the same, the point of view
has not changed yet, and the learner tries again with the feeling word
changed first.

## Exit check

"Read Ben's line 'This is the best day!' in Ben's voice, then tell me
why Ben thinks so." (Happy voice + because the rain made the best
puddles.) Voice and reason both required.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** use the text-only alternative for every picture task;
  let the learner point to faces instead of naming feelings; adult
  models each voice first and the learner echoes.
- **Extension:** the learner writes two lines of dialogue for a new
  shared event (a lost ball found in a tree) — one character relieved,
  one still worried — and reads them in two voices.
- **Language access:** pair each voice with a hand signal (thumb down +
  slow for sad; arms up + fast for happy) so the voice choice is visible
  as well as audible.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) saying the characters disagree about *facts*
("Ana thinks it is sunny") — they agree it rains; they disagree about
whether rain is good; (2) reading all dialogue in the narrator's voice;
(3) claiming Ana fully changes her mind at the end — she only softens
("Maybe rain is not all bad"). See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md)
and the [answer key](../answer-key.md) (adults only).
