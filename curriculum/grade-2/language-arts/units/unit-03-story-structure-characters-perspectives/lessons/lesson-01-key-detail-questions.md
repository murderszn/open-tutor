# Lesson 01 — Key-Detail Questions: Who, What, Where, When, Why, How

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can ask and answer who, what, where, when, why, and how questions about a story — and point to where the story answers me."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson's story (below); six question cards the adult writes by hand (WHO, WHAT, WHERE, WHEN, WHY, HOW); a pencil or pointer.
**Standards:** RL.2.1.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks about yesterday (not about a book): "Who did you play with?
Where did you play? What did you do?" If the learner answers in full
sentences, say: "You just answered key-detail questions. Stories work the
same way." If answers are one word, model one full sentence and have the
learner repeat it: "I played with Sam at the park."

## The story — "Milo and the Runaway Kite" (original; adult reads aloud first, learner re-reads)

> On Saturday morning, Milo took his new red kite to the park with his
> sister Ana. The wind was strong, and the kite flew high over the grass.
> "Hold on tight!" Ana said. But the string slipped from Milo's hand, and
> the kite sailed away. It caught in the branches of a tall oak tree.
> Milo felt sad. He thought his new kite was lost. Ana said, "Do not
> worry. Let us ask for help." They found Mr. Lee, the park keeper. Mr.
> Lee used a long pole to lift the kite down. The kite had a small tear.
> Milo put tape on the tear. Then Milo flew the kite again, holding the
> string tight with both hands. "I will not let go this time!" he said.

## Explicit teaching

A **key detail** is a fact the story itself tells you — not something you
guess. Six question words catch almost every key detail:

- **Who** — the people (or animals) in the story.
- **What** — what happens.
- **Where** — the place.
- **When** — the time.
- **Why** — the reason something happens or someone feels a way.
- **How** — the way something happens or gets fixed.

Rule: after every answer, point to the proof. The proof is a sentence in
the story or a detail in a picture. "I think so" is not proof; "the story
says…" is proof.

### Modeled example 1 — Who and where

Adult points to the first sentence and thinks aloud: "Who? The story
says *Milo* took his kite, *with his sister Ana*. So who: Milo and Ana.
Where? The same sentence says *to the park*. So where: the park. I found
both answers in one sentence, and I can point to them."

### Modeled example 2 — Why and how

"Why did Milo feel sad? The story says: *He thought his new kite was
lost.* That is the reason — why. How did the kite come down? Not by
magic. The story says: *Mr. Lee used a long pole to lift the kite down.*
That is the how. Then Milo fixed the small tear with tape — that is a
second how, for the fix."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "What did Milo take to the park?" (A new red kite.) "Point to the sentence."
2. "When did this happen?" (Saturday morning.)
3. "What happened to the kite?" (The string slipped; it sailed away and caught in a tall oak tree.)
4. "Why did Ana say, 'Let us ask for help'?" (Because Milo was sad and thought the kite was lost; asking for help was the plan.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult says, "Milo's kite caught in a pine tree." "Did I read that right?" (No — the story says an oak tree. Point to *oak*.) "What does that teach us?" (Check the story, do not trust memory alone.)

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. Who are the three people in the story?
2. Where does the kite get caught?
3. When does Milo fly the kite again — before or after Mr. Lee helps?
4. Why does Milo hold the string with both hands at the end?
5. How does Mr. Lee get the kite down?
6. Draw or point to the moment Milo feels sad, and say why in one sentence.

## Applied task

The learner asks the adult two new key-detail questions about the story
that were not asked above, and the adult must answer with proof. Then
they swap: the adult asks one, the learner answers with proof. (Example
learner question: "What color was the kite?" — proof: "new red kite.")

## Exit check

The adult asks one unpractised question: "How did Milo fix the tear?"
(Correct: with tape.) If the learner answers without proof, prompt once:
"Where does the story say that?" — then record whether the proof was
found alone or with the prompt.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** cover all but one sentence at a time; let the learner
  point instead of reading aloud; adult scribes answers the learner
  dictates.
- **Extension:** the learner writes one why-question and one how-question
  of their own about a book at home, with the answer and its proof.
- **Language access:** act out *slipped*, *caught*, and *tear* before
  reading; repeat the six question words with hand signs (point to self
  for who, point around for where, tap a watch for when).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: answering *why* with *what* ("Why was Milo sad?
Because the kite flew away" is actually a what-answer; the story's
reason is *he thought it was lost*). Accept the event first, then ask:
"And what did Milo *think*?" See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md)
and the [answer key](../answer-key.md) (adults only — do not show the
learner).
