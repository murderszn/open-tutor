# Lesson 05 — Rhythm, Repeated Lines, and What Pictures Add

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can hear how repeated words and rhymes make a story fun to say — and I can tell what a picture adds that the words alone do not say."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the chant below; the picture from
[Lesson 04](lesson-04-two-points-of-view.md); a hand drum or table to tap (optional).
**Standards:** RL.2.4, RL.2.7.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult claps a steady beat and says: "Rain, rain, go away —" and
stops. Most learners will want to finish it. Ask: "Why did you want to
finish it?" (The beat and the rhyme pull you forward.) "That pull is
rhythm. Today we find it in our own stories."

## The chant — "Kite in the Tree" (original; clap or tap on each beat)

> Kite in the tree, kite in the tree,
> High in the oak where the wind blows free.
> Pull, pull, pull — it will not come down.
> Ask, ask, ask — help comes to town.
> Tape, tape, tape — the tear is small.
> Fly, fly, fly — we hold it all!

## Explicit teaching

**Rhythm** is the beat of words: regular beats (tap-tap-tap),
**alliteration** (same starting sound: *silly, soggy, Saturday*),
**rhymes** (tree/free, down/town, small/all), and **repeated lines**
(the same words coming back, like "kite in the tree" and "pull, pull,
pull"). They do three jobs:

1. They make a story easier to remember and retell — the beat carries
   the words.
2. They make a feeling stronger — fast repeated words feel hurried or
   excited; slow repeated words feel sad or careful.
3. They signal what matters — a line that comes back twice is usually
   important (the kite stuck, then the kite flying again).

Pictures do a fourth job: they show what words leave out. The words in
"A Rainy Day, Two Ways" say Ana's picnic is spoiled, but the picture
shows *how* spoiled — the blanket dark with water, bread spilled, an
apple rolling away. The words say Ben jumps in a puddle, but the picture
shows *how big* the splash is — water flying higher than his boots.
That is RL.2.7: use the illustration *and* the words together.

### Modeled example 1 — Finding rhythm in the chant

Adult taps the beat while reading: "Hear it? *Kite in the tree, kite in
the tree* — the line repeats, so your ear expects it again. *Pull, pull,
pull* — three pulls in a row sound like trying, trying, trying. Then
the rhyme: *tree/free*, *down/town*, *small/all*. If I read it flat
with no beat and no repeats, it would still tell the facts — but it
would not feel like trying hard and then flying again."

### Modeled example 2 — What the picture adds

"Words alone: 'Ben jumped in the biggest puddle.' Picture adds: the
splash reaches above his knees, his arms are wide, his mouth is open in
a laugh — so we know he is not just wet, he is delighted. Words alone:
'Ana's picnic is spoiled.' Picture adds: her arms are crossed, her
blanket is dark blue with puddles on it, food has spilled. If a learner
only read the words, they would miss how sad Ana looks and how happy
Ben looks. Together, words + picture give the full point of view."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Clap the chant's beat while the adult reads it; point to each rhyming pair (tree/free, down/town, small/all).
2. "Which repeated line tells us the kite is stuck?" ("Kite in the tree, kite in the tree.")
3. Alliteration practice (clearly labeled practice phrase, not story text): the adult says "silly, soggy Saturday." "What sound starts all three words?" (/s/.) "Now make your own: three words that all start with /b/ about Ben's puddle." (Example: "big, blue, bouncy puddle.")
4. Look at the Lesson 04 picture: "Name one thing the picture shows that the story's words do not spell out." (The apple rolling away; the splash height; Ana's crossed arms.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult says, "The picture is just decoration; the words say everything." "Is that right?" (No — the picture shows feelings on faces and the size of the splash, which the words do not fully describe.)

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. Circle or point to the rhyming words in the chant's last two lines (small/all).
2. Write or dictate one repeated line of your own for Milo's story (e.g., "Hold on tight, hold on tight!").
3. Say whether "pull, pull, pull" feels fast or slow, and why (fast — short words, three in a row, like tugging).
4. Draw a puddle splash as big as Ben's, and dictate one sentence the picture adds that words alone would miss.
5. Which picture detail best shows Ana's point of view — the wet blanket or Ben's splash? Why? (The wet blanket — it belongs to Ana's picnic.)
6. Listen as the adult reads Milo's story flat, then with expression: which reading helped you feel Milo's sadness more, and what did the reader change? (Voice, speed, pauses.)

## Applied task

The learner makes a two-line chant for "The Two Builders" with one
repeated word and one rhyme, for example: "Build it slow, build it
slow, / check each block before you go." (Go/slow is a slant rhyme —
accept it, and note that exact rhymes like "stand/hand" also work.)
The learner performs it with a beat, then explains what the repeated
word does (it makes "slow" feel important — which is the lesson).

## Exit check

"Say the chant's first line with its beat, then tell me one rhyme and
one thing the Lesson 04 picture adds." (Line + beat; one pair such as
tree/free; one picture detail such as the spilled bread or the high
splash.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** tap the beat on the table for every line; let the
  learner point to rhymes instead of naming them; use the text-only
  alternative for all picture tasks.
- **Extension:** the learner writes a four-line chant for the sunflower
  story (Lesson 06) with a repeated line, and reads it aloud with a beat.
- **Language access:** define rhyme by ear first ("words that sound
  the same at the end"), spelling second; never require the learner to
  spell a rhyme to prove they heard it.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) calling any repeated word "rhyme" — rhyme
is end-sound match, repetition is same-word return (praise a learner
who says "fly, fly, fly is repetition, not alliteration"); (2) treating
the picture as decoration; (3) forcing alliteration into the chant —
the chant's power is repetition and rhyme, and task 3's alliteration
comes from a labeled practice phrase, not from story text. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and the
[answer key](../answer-key.md) (adults only).
