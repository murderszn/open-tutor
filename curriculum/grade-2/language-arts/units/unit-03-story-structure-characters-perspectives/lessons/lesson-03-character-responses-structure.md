# Lesson 03 — How Characters Respond, and How a Story Is Built

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can describe how a character responds when something big happens — and I can show how the beginning starts a story and the ending finishes it."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the Milo and Two Builders stories from Lessons 01–02; a two-column chart (CHARACTER / WHAT THEY DID + SAID).
**Standards:** RL.2.3, RL.2.5.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "In Milo's story, what was the big trouble, and what did
Milo do first when it happened?" (The kite caught in the tree; Milo felt
sad and thought it was lost.) "And what did Ana do?" (She told him not
to worry and to ask for help.) Same trouble, two different responses —
that is today's work.

## Explicit teaching

A character **responds** to a major event with feelings, words, and
actions. To describe a response well, name all three when the story
gives them:

- **Feels:** Milo felt sad.
- **Says:** Ana said, "Do not worry. Let us ask for help."
- **Does:** Milo tapes the tear and flies the kite again, holding tight.

Saying only "Milo was sad" misses half the response: the important part
is what Milo *does next* — he accepts help, fixes the kite, and tries
again. A response is a chain: trouble → feeling → words → action.

A story's **structure** is its shape:

- **Beginning introduces:** who the characters are, where they are, and
  what is starting. (Milo and Ana, the park, a new red kite, Saturday
  morning.)
- **Middle:** the trouble and the tries to fix it.
- **Ending concludes:** the trouble is settled and the action wraps up.
  (The kite flies again; Milo holds tight.) An ending is not just "the
  last sentence" — it must *finish* the trouble. If the kite were still
  in the tree, the story would not be concluded.

### Modeled example 1 — Milo's response, step by step

"Trouble: the string slips and the kite catches in the oak tree. Milo
responds: he feels sad (feeling), he thinks the kite is lost (thought),
and then — with Ana's help — he asks Mr. Lee for help (action), tapes
the tear (action), and flies again holding tight (action). Notice the
change: sad and stuck at first, calm and trying again at the end. That
change is the character's response."

### Modeled example 2 — Maya's response, and a beginning vs. ending check

"Maya's trouble: her fast tower falls with a crash. She responds: she
feels cross (feeling), she watches Jon (action — she learns by watching),
and she tries his careful way (action). Her second tower stands.
Beginning check: 'Maya and Jon loved building with blocks' introduces
who and what — that is a beginning. Ending check: 'Her second tower
stood tall too' concludes the action — the tower problem is solved.
If I swapped them, the story would start with a standing tower and end
with an introduction. It would not make sense."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "How does Ana respond to Milo's trouble? Name a feeling, a word, and an action if you can." (Feeling: not stated as sad — she stays calm; words: "Do not worry. Let us ask for help"; action: finds Mr. Lee with Milo.)
2. "How does Jon respond when Maya's tower falls?" (He does not laugh or rush; he keeps building carefully, and his steady example shows Maya a better way. Note: the story does not say Jon speaks — do not invent words for him.)
3. "Which sentence introduces Milo's story?" (The Saturday-morning park sentence.)
4. "Which sentence concludes it?" (Milo flies the kite again, holding tight, saying he will not let go.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult says, "Maya's response was that she was cross, so she quit." "Is that right?" (No — she felt cross, but she did not quit; she watched Jon and tried again. A feeling is not the whole response.)

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. Describe Milo's response in two sentences (feeling + what he does next).
2. Describe Maya's response in two sentences.
3. Write one beginning sentence for a new story about a lost library book (must introduce who and where).
4. Write one ending sentence for that story (must conclude the trouble — the book is found or replaced, not still lost).
5. Sort these Milo events into beginning / middle / end: (a) Milo tapes the tear; (b) Milo takes the kite to the park; (c) the string slips.
6. Draw Milo at the end of the story (flying the kite, holding tight) and write or dictate one sentence about how he changed from the middle.

## Applied task

The learner picks one character (Milo, Ana, Maya, or Jon) and tells the
adult: "If I were [character], I would have felt ___ because ___,
and I would have ___." Then the learner must say whether their idea
matches the story or is a new idea — both are welcome, but they must not
be mixed up. (Example: "If I were Milo, I would have felt scared because
the tree was tall, and I would have asked Mr. Lee — that matches the
story.")

## Exit check

"How did Maya respond when her tower fell — what did she feel, and what
did she do?" (Felt cross; watched Jon; tried the careful way; second
tower stood.) One feeling + one action passes; feeling + watching +
retrying is full marks.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** use the chart with three rows (feels / says / does) and
  fill it together before any writing; drawing counts as a response
  description when dictated captions accompany it.
- **Extension:** the learner writes a middle for their library-book
  story in which the character responds in two steps (a feeling, then a
  helpful action), and reads it aloud.
- **Language access:** act out *respond* (show a feeling face, then do
  the next action); keep the words *introduces* and *concludes* on cards
  with a picture of an opening door and a closing door.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) reporting only the feeling; (2) inventing
dialogue or actions the story never gives (Jon speaking, Mr. Lee
climbing the tree — he uses a pole, he does not climb); (3) calling any
last sentence an ending even when the trouble is unresolved. The answer
key marks invented details as incorrect even when they sound plausible.
