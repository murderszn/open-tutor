# Lesson 02 — Retell in Order and Find the Lesson

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can retell a story in order — beginning, middle, end — and say its lesson in my own words."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** "Milo and the Runaway Kite" from [Lesson 01](lesson-01-key-detail-questions.md); the new story below; three cards labeled BEGINNING, MIDDLE, END.
**Standards:** RL.2.2, RL.2.5 (beginning/end preview).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Tell me what happened in Milo's story — first, next,
last — in three short parts." If the learner skips the middle (the kite
in the tree and asking for help), lay out the three cards and say: "The
middle is where the trouble happens. What was Milo's trouble?"

## Explicit teaching

**Retell** means telling a story again in your own words, in the order it
happened — not copying every sentence, and not jumping to the end. A
good retell has three parts:

- **Beginning:** who, where, and what starts. (Milo takes his new red
  kite to the park on Saturday morning.)
- **Middle:** the trouble and what people try. (The string slips, the
  kite catches in the oak tree, Milo is sad, Ana says to ask for help,
  Mr. Lee lifts it down with a long pole.)
- **End:** how it finishes. (Milo tapes the tear and flies the kite
  again, holding tight with both hands.)

A **lesson (moral)** is what the story teaches about how to live or act.
It is not the same as the retell. The retell says *what happened*; the
lesson says *what we learn*. Milo's lesson: *when something goes wrong,
stay calm and ask a grown-up for help.* You will not find those exact
words in the story — you have to think them from what Milo does.

### The story — "The Two Builders" (original fable-style story)

> Maya and Jon loved building with blocks. Maya built fast, fast, fast!
> Her tower went up in a hurry — and then it wobbled and fell down with a
> crash. Maya felt cross. Jon built slowly. He put each block down with
> care, and he checked that it sat flat before he added the next one.
> His tower stood tall. Maya watched Jon. Then she tried his careful way.
> She put each block down slowly, and she checked that it sat flat. Her
> second tower stood tall too. "Slow and careful works," Maya said, and
> Jon smiled.

### Modeled example 1 — Retelling Milo in order

Adult models, holding up one card per part: "Beginning: Milo and Ana
take a new red kite to the park on Saturday morning. Middle: the string
slips, the kite catches in the oak tree, and Mr. Lee lifts it down with
a long pole. End: Milo tapes the tear and flies the kite again, holding
tight. Three parts, in order, nothing skipped."

### Modeled example 2 — Finding a lesson (not guessing one)

"Watch how I find Maya and Jon's lesson. Maya's fast tower falls. Jon's
careful tower stands. Maya copies the careful way, and her tower stands
too. The pattern is: careful wins, fast falls. So the lesson is: *slow
and careful work lasts.* I did not guess it from one sentence — I found
the pattern across the whole story. A wrong guess would be 'blocks are
fun' — true, but it is not what the story teaches, because both towers
were fun to build and only one stood."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Retell Milo's story in three parts, using the cards. (Adult supplies
   a missing part only after the learner tries.)
2. "What is Milo's lesson, in your own words?" (Accept: ask for help when
   something goes wrong; stay calm and get help.)
3. Retell "The Two Builders" in three parts.
4. "What did Maya do differently the second time?" (She built slowly and
   checked each block sat flat, like Jon.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult retells Milo's story out of order (end
   first). "Did that retell work?" (No — a retell must go in order, or
   the listener cannot follow the trouble and the fix.)

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. Write or dictate Milo's retell in three sentences (one per part).
2. Write Milo's lesson in your own words (one sentence).
3. Write "The Two Builders" retell in three sentences.
4. Write its lesson in your own words.
5. Which story's lesson is about asking for help — Milo's or the
   builders'? How do you know?
6. Draw the middle of one story (the trouble) and label it in one sentence.

## Applied task

The adult reads aloud the public-domain fable "The Hare & the Tortoise"
from the Library of Congress (link in the
[Resource Pack](../resource-pack.md); adult reads it, the learner does
not need the page). The learner retells it in three parts and names its
lesson. The source's own moral is "The race is not always to the swift";
accept the learner's own words for the same idea (for example, "keep
going, even if you are slow" or "do not show off and stop"). Compare:
Maya/Jon's lesson (slow and careful work lasts) is close to the
Tortoise's lesson — stories from different times can teach the same
thing.

## Exit check

"Retell 'The Two Builders' without the cards, then tell me its lesson."
Correct retell keeps fast-fall, careful-stand, second-try-stand in order;
correct lesson is any own-words version of *slow and careful work lasts*.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** retell with the three cards in view; act out each part;
  adult scribes while the learner speaks.
- **Extension:** the learner retells a familiar picture book in three
  parts and names a lesson the adult has not named first.
- **Language access:** sentence starters on cards: "At the beginning…",
  "Then the trouble was…", "At the end…".

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: naming a *detail* as the lesson ("The lesson is
that Milo had a red kite") or naming a *feeling* ("Milo was sad"). If
this happens, ask: "What did Milo *learn to do*?" — lessons are about
actions and choices. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
the [answer key](../answer-key.md) (adults only).
