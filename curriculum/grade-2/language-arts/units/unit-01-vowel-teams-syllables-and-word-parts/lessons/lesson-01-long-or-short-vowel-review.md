# Lesson 01 — Long or Short? A Vowel Sound Review

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell if the vowel in a word is long or short, and I can read both."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the unit word sets ([unit README](../README.md#unit-word-sets-authored-for-this-unit-reused-across-lessons-and-practice)); word cards the adult writes by hand; two trays labeled LONG and SHORT.
**Standards:** RF.2.3.a.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult says three words slowly — *cat*, *cake*, *bed* — and asks: "Which
two have vowels that sound the same?" (*cat* and *bed* — both short.) Then:
"Which one says its letter name?" (*cake* — the a says its name, /ā/, so it
is long.) If the learner cannot hear it yet, stretch the vowel:
"c-ăăă-t … c-āāā-ke."

## Explicit teaching

Every vowel has two famous voices. The **short** voice is the quick, clipped
sound: /ă/ in *cat*, /ĕ/ in *bed*, /ĭ/ in *pin*, /ŏ/ in *hop*, /ŭ/ in *tub*.
The **long** voice says the letter's own name: /ā/ in *cake*, /ē/ in *these*,
/ī/ in *pine*, /ō/ in *hope*, /ū/ in *tube*.

Here is the grade-2 detective rule for one-syllable words: when a word ends
in **one vowel, one consonant, and a silent e** — vowel-consonant-e, like
*cape*, *pine*, *hope*, *tube* — the first vowel almost always says its name
(long), and the final e stays quiet. It is called the "magic e," but nothing
is magic: the e marks the pattern.

Without the silent e — one vowel between two consonants, like *cap*, *pin*,
*hop*, *tub* — the vowel is almost always short.

Say the pair aloud and feel the difference: *cap* (quick /ă/) vs. *cape*
(stretched /ā/, the a says its name).

### Modeled example 1 — cap vs. cape

*Adult writes* cap *and* cape *on cards, pointing left to right:*
"First card: c-a-p. One vowel between two consonants — short pattern.
Sound it out: /k/ /ă/ /p/ — *cap*, like a hat."
"Second card: c-a-p-e. Vowel, consonant, silent e — long pattern. The a
says its name: /k/ /ā/ /p/ — *cape*, like a superhero wears."
"The only change is the quiet e at the end, but the vowel's voice
changes. That is the whole trick of this lesson."

### Modeled example 2 — pin vs. pine, then hop vs. hope

*Adult writes* pin / pine: "*pin* — short /ĭ/, a straight pin. *pine* —
long /ī/, a pine tree. Same quiet-e trick."
*Adult writes* hop / hope: "*hop* — short /ŏ/, like a bunny hop. *hope* —
long /ō/, wishing for something. Say both pairs with me slowly, and tap
the table once for short, twice for long."
"Detectives, notice: we never guessed. The pattern told us."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Read *kit*." (/k/ /ĭ/ /t/ — *kit*.) "Long or short?" (Short.)
2. "Read *kite*." (Long /ī/ — *kite*.) "What told you?" (The quiet e.)
3. "Read *tub*." (Short /ŭ/.) "Read *tube*." (Long /ū/.) "Tap once or twice for each."
4. **Odd one out (ear only, no cards):** "Which is long — *met*, *mate*, *pen*?" (*mate*.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult reads *hope* as "hop" on purpose. "Did I
   read that right?" (No — the quiet e makes it *hope*, long /ō/.) "What
   pattern did I ignore?" (vowel-consonant-e.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Sort these 6 word cards into LONG and SHORT trays: *cap, cape, pin, pine, hop, hope*. (Long: cape, pine, hope. Short: cap, pin, hop.)
2. Read the SHORT tray aloud. Then read the LONG tray aloud.
3. "Write the long partner of *tub*." (*tube* — adult scribes if the learner prefers to dictate; spelling is not graded here, reading is.)
4. Ear check: the adult says *bed, beet, bet*. "Which two rhyme?" (*bed, bet*.) "Which is the odd vowel out?" (*beet* — long.)
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why is the vowel in *kite* long?" (Because
   of the quiet e — vowel-consonant-e makes the first vowel say its name.)

## Application

"Vowel voice cards." The learner picks any 4 pairs from the unit word sets,
writes each word on a card (adult scribes as needed), and draws a one-tap
dot under short vowels and a two-tap line under long vowels. Then the
learner reads all 8 cards to the adult, tapping while reading. One card
must be a word the learner got wrong today.

## Exit check

1. Read *cube*. (Long /ū/ — the quiet e.)
2. Read *cub*. (Short /ŭ/.)
3. "What does the quiet e do?" (It tells the first vowel to say its name.)
4. The adult says *fin* and *fine*: "Which is long?" (*fine*.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** practice the five short vowels with the learner's own
  spoken words first ("a short-a word you know…") before word-list words.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may tap once (short) or twice
  (long) instead of naming the vowel — then read the word.
- **Seated-table variant:** two trays labeled with a one-tap dot and a
  two-tap line instead of the words LONG/SHORT.
- **Floor-play variant:** two floor spots — one tap spot, two-tap spot —
  the learner stands on the spot and reads the card.
- **Extension:** find the quiet-e pattern in a picture book (5 examples);
  test whether it ever breaks ("Have you found *have*? Why is that one
  tricky? — hold that question for Lesson 06").

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *Every long vowel needs a team:* some learners will hunt for two vowel
  letters. Point back: "One vowel plus the quiet e is a long vowel too —
  teams come in Lesson 02."
- *Reading the quiet e aloud:* "*cap-ee*" — cover the e with a finger:
  "Quiet e never makes a sound. It only bosses the first vowel."
