# Lesson 06 — Word Puzzles: Compound Words and Tricky Spellings

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can use word parts to guess what a compound word means, and I can read the tricky words that break the patterns."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the unit word sets (compounds,
tricky list); word cards; a "tricky word wall" space (paper on the wall).
**Standards:** L.2.4.d, RF.2.3.e, RF.2.3.f, L.2.2.d.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

"Say *bird*. Say *house*. Now smash them together: *birdhouse*."
"What is a birdhouse?" (A house for birds.) "You just solved a compound
word — two whole words holding hands, and the meaning is hiding in the
parts. But some words do NOT play fair today — the tricky ones."

## Explicit teaching

**Part 1 — compound words.** A **compound word** is two (or more) whole
words joined into one: *bird* + *house* = *birdhouse*. The meaning
prediction trick: say each part, then guess — "a house for birds," "a
light for a house" (*lighthouse* — close! a tower with a light that
guides ships; check with the adult). Read them with clap-and-break from
Lesson 04: *sun-shine, rain-bow, base-ball, cow-boy, back-pack, pop-corn,
foot-ball, mail-box, tooth-brush, bed-room*.

**Part 2 — tricky spellings.** Some teams spell more than one sound
(RF.2.3.e — inconsistent but common correspondences):
- **ea** is usually /ē/ (*leaf, sea*) but sometimes /ĕ/ (*head, bread, dead*).
- **oo** is usually "moon" /oo/ but sometimes "book" /ŏŏ/ (*book, look, foot*).
- **ow** is usually "snow" /ō/ but sometimes "cow" /ou/ (*cow, down, brown*).
Try-and-flip from Lesson 03 handles all of these.

**Part 3 — irregular words** (RF.2.3.f). A few common words ignore the
patterns entirely and must be learned by heart: *said, have, of, was,
were, some, come, what, where, again, laugh, would, could, should,
friend, people, water, only, because*. Notice the pattern-breakers: *said*
spells its vowel with *ai* but says /ĕ/; *have* ends in quiet-e but the a
stays short. These go on the tricky word wall — read them every session
until they are instant.

**Part 4 — spelling by pattern** (L.2.2.d). When you WRITE a new word,
borrow a pattern you know: *cage* → *badge* (same -age), *boy* → *boil*
(same -oy), *rain* → *train* (same -ain). You do not memorize every
word — you generalize the team.

### Modeled example 1 — toothbrush

*Adult writes* toothbrush *and thinks aloud:*
"Two whole words: *tooth* + *brush*. Clap-and-break: tooth-brush. Guess
the meaning: a brush for teeth! Check: yes — you brush your teeth with
it. The parts told me."

### Modeled example 2 — said (tricky word)

*Adult writes* said: "Pattern check: *ai* in the middle — my brain wants
to say /ā/, 'sayed'. But this word breaks the pattern: it says *said*
(/sĕd/). No sounding out will save it — it goes on the tricky word wall,
and I learn it by heart. Reading *said* correctly is pure memory, and
that is a real reading skill."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Break *mailbox* into its words. Guess the meaning." (*mail* + *box* =
   a box for mail.)
2. "Break *sunshine*." (*sun* + *shine* = light from the sun.)
3. "Read *head*." (Try /ē/ first → "heed"? Not a word. Flip → *head*,
   /hĕd/.) "Which team, which sound?" (**ea** saying /ĕ/ — the
   inconsistent one.)
4. Tricky-word wall drill: the adult flashes *said, have, of, was, were*
   — the learner reads each instantly. Any miss goes back on the wall.
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult reads *cowboy* as "cow-boy" with a long
   o in *boy* ("cow-boy" rhyming with "toy"). "Did I read that right?"
   (No — *boy* here keeps its /oi/ sound: *cowboy*.) "What parts did I
   ignore?" (The compound's real pronunciation — check: is it a real
   word I know?)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 6 tasks)

1. Break and define 4 compounds: *lighthouse, popcorn, bedroom, football*.
   ("A tower with a light… corn that pops…")
2. Sort 8 word cards — FAIR TEAMS vs. TRICKY: *leaf, head, moon, book,
   snow, cow, said, have*. (Fair: leaf, moon, snow. Tricky: head, book,
   cow, said, have.)
3. Read the tricky list aloud twice: *said, have, of, was, were, some,
   come, what*. Star the ones read instantly.
4. Spelling by pattern (adult dictates, learner writes or tiles):
   "Spell *badge* — borrow from *cage*." / "Spell *boil* — borrow from
   *boy*." / "Spell *train* — borrow from *rain*."
5. Read this original sentence set: "The cowboy found popcorn in the
   mailbox." / "I said I would come, but the rain came down."
6. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why can't you sound out *said*?"
   (Because *ai* usually says /ā/, but *said* breaks the pattern — it
   must be memorized.)

## Application

"Tricky word wall + compound riddles." The learner writes (or dictates
for the adult to write) all 19 tricky words onto the wall in big letters.
Then the learner invents 3 compound riddles for the adult to solve:
"I'm thinking of a compound word: it is a room for a bed…" (*bedroom*).
The adult must break the word and explain the meaning to "win." The
riddles go in the learner's notebook for the review week.

## Exit check

1. "What two words make *birdhouse*, and what does it mean?" (*bird* +
   *house* — a house for birds.)
2. Read *bread*. (Flip **ea** to /ĕ/ — *bread*.)
3. Read *of*. (Instant — tricky word, /ŏv/.)
4. "When you write a new word, what do you borrow?" (A pattern you know —
   *cage* → *badge*.)

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** build compounds from real objects first (a toy box
  + a toy = "toybox" — is it a real word? check together) before cards.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may draw the two parts of a
  compound instead of defining it in words.
- **Seated-table variant:** compound puzzles — two half-cards the learner
  physically joins, then reads.
- **Floor-play variant:** two word mats on the floor — the learner stands
  on each part, says it, then jumps to the joined word.
- **Extension:** collect 5 more compounds from a picture book; find one
  compound whose meaning is NOT in its parts (*butterfly* is not butter +
  fly — words can be sneaky) and discuss.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *Every compound's meaning is literal:* *lighthouse* is close but not
  exact — always run the check step ("does my guess make sense?").
- *Tricky words can be sounded out "if you try harder":* they cannot —
  insisting builds frustration. Wall + repetition is the method.
- *Memorizing means no thinking:* wrong — choosing try-and-flip vs.
  wall-memory IS the thinking.

  all guided/independent items solved and reconciled.
