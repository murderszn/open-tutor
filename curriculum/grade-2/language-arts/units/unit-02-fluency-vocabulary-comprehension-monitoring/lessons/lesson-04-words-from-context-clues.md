# Lesson 04 — Clue Words: Figuring Out New Words from Context

**Goal (learner hears):** "When I meet a word I don't know, I can use the
words around it as clues."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** Passage B — "How Kites Fly"
([unit README](../README.md)); word cards the adult writes by hand.
**Standards:** L.2.4.a.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult says: "My soup was too **scalding** to eat." Ask: "I bet you
have never cooked with the word *scalding* — so how do you know what it
means?" (*Too hot to eat — 'too ___ to eat' tells you it is about heat.*)
"You just used a **context clue**: the words around a new word whispering
what it means."

## Explicit teaching

A **context clue** is a hint hiding in the same sentence — sometimes in
the sentence before or after. Four hiding spots to check, in order:

1. **A definition right there:** "A **gust** is a sudden burst of wind."
   The sentence tells you flat-out.
2. **An example:** "It moves air fast." — *fast* movement shows what wind does.
3. **A contrast:** "If the wind stops, the kite **drifts** down." Drifting
   is the opposite of flying up — the sentence contrasts them.
4. **The whole scene:** "Wind can also **tangle** the string. A **tangle**
   is a messy knot of string." The next sentence explains the word.

The detective routine: **Circle the new word → read the whole sentence →
ask 'what would make sense here?' → check the sentence before and after →
say your guess in your own words.**

You do not need the dictionary's exact words. Your own words that fit the
sentence count.

### Modeled example 1 — *invisible*

*Adult reads:* "The wind is a strong, **invisible** force."

"Circle *invisible*. Read the whole sentence. What would make sense? Wind
is strong — but can I **see** wind? No! I can only see what it moves. So
*invisible* must mean **cannot be seen**. Check: 'a strong, cannot-be-seen
force' — that fits perfectly. My own words: *invisible* = you can't see
it."

### Modeled example 2 — *flutters*

*Adult reads:* "If the wind stops, the kite **drifts** down. It **flutters**
to the ground."

"Two new words. *Drifts*: the wind stopped, so the kite cannot fly up —
it must come **down slowly**. Drifts = moves down slowly and gently.
*Flutters*: the next sentence shows the kite coming down — kites do not
fall like rocks; they wobble side to side in the air. *Flutters* = moves
with quick little wobbles. Scene clue: the whole sentence paints the
picture."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "A **gusty** day is the best day to fly a kite." What's the clue, and
   what does *gusty* mean? (Clue: the next sentence defines *gust* as a
   sudden burst of wind. *Gusty* = full of gusts / very windy.)
2. "Wind can also **tangle** the string." Find the hiding spot. (The next
   sentence: "A **tangle** is a messy knot of string" — a definition right
   there.)
3. **Contrast hunt:** "The wind is a strong, invisible force. It moves air
   **fast**." If the wind moved air *slowly*, would kites fly? (No — *fast*
   contrasts with slow; the clue tells you wind must move quickly to work.)
4. **Odd one out:** which is NOT a context clue — "the sentence before,"
   "a picture in your head from the words," or "the first letter of the
   word"? (The first letter alone is not a meaning clue.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult guesses "*drifts* means *flies up*."
   "Check my guess against the sentence." (Wrong — "the kite drifts **down**";
   the sentence says down, so *drifts* cannot mean up.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Read Passage B sentence by sentence; circle (or point to) the five bold
   clue words: *invisible, gusty, tangle, drifts, flutters*.
2. For *gusty*: name the clue type (definition in the next sentence) and
   give the meaning in your own words.
3. For *tangle*: name the clue type and the meaning.
4. For *drifts* and *flutters*: name the clue type (scene/contrast) and the
   meaning in your own words.
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why is 'say it in your own words' better
   than memorizing a dictionary definition?" (Because your own words prove
   you really understand it — and you can use it when you talk and write.)

## Application

"Clue-word cards." For each of the five bold words, the learner makes a
card: the word on the front; on the back, the meaning in their own words
**plus** the exact clue words from the passage that gave it away (e.g.,
back of *gusty*: "full of sudden bursts of wind — clue: 'A gust is a
sudden burst of wind'"). Cards go into the practice deck for Session 13's
race.

## Exit check

The adult reads a fresh sentence: "The kite string got a **snarl** in it,
a messy knot the wind had twisted." The learner defines *snarl* (a messy
twisted knot) and names the clue (definition right there: "a messy knot").

## Supports and alternate response modes

- The adult may read the passage aloud while the learner follows with a
  finger and calls out when a bold word appears.
- The learner may draw the meaning instead of writing it (draw wind you
  cannot see for *invisible*).
- Reduce to three clue words for a learner still securing decoding;
  *gusty*, *tangle*, and *flutters* carry the lesson.

## Extension

The learner writes two original sentences with a made-up word (e.g.,
"The **zibber** was too heavy to lift, so we pushed it.") and the adult
must use context clues to define *zibber* — then they switch.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner guesses from the first letter or from
one nearby word instead of the whole sentence. Insist on the routine:
whole sentence first, then before/after. A one-word "clue" is often a
trap. Answers: see [answer-key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
