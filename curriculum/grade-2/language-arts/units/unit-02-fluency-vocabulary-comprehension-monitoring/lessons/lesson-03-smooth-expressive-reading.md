# Lesson 03 — Smooth and Full of Feeling: Expressive Reading

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can read the same passage again and again,
each time smoother and with more feeling."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** Passage A — "The Lost Red Kite"
([unit README](../README.md)); the generated illustration
[fluency-expression-readers.png](../assets/fluency-expression-readers.png);
a pencil for marking.
**Standards:** RF.2.4.b.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

Show the generated illustration. "What is this reader doing with their
voice?" (Reading aloud with big feeling, like a storyteller.) "Who is
listening?" (A dog — listening closely.) "Today you are that reader. The
secret: nobody reads with feeling the **first** time. Feeling comes on the
second and third reads."

## Explicit teaching

Fluency has three parts:

- **Accuracy** — the words are right.
- **Rate** — the speed is just right: not racing, not crawling. Conversational, like talking to a friend.
- **Expression** — your voice shows the meaning: excitement goes up, sad
  parts go soft, questions lift at the end.

The trick is **successive readings**: read the same text again and again,
and each time it gets smoother. First read: get the words right. Second
read: find a good speed. Third read: add the feeling. This is why actors
rehearse.

**Echo reading** is the fastest way to learn the music of a sentence: the
adult reads one line with full expression, and the learner reads the same
line back, copying the voice exactly — the pauses, the rises, the
emphasis. Then the learner owns it.

Punctuation is the composer's marking: a period means a full stop and a
falling voice; a comma is a short pause; **!** means big feeling; **?**
means your voice lifts. Quotation marks mean a character is talking — give
them a voice.

### Modeled example 1 — three successive readings

*The adult reads the line "'Where is my kite?' he asked." three times:*

1. Slow and flat: "'Where… is… my… kite?' he asked." — "That was accurate
   but robot-like. First read: words right."
2. Smooth and conversational: "'Where is my kite?' he asked." — "Better
   rate. But Sam is worried — where is the feeling?"
3. Worried and lifted: "'**Where** is my kite?!' he asked." — "Now Sam
   sounds lost and worried. The **?** lifts my voice; 'where' gets the
   push."

### Modeled example 2 — echo reading with expression marks

*The adult marks the line on the learner's copy:*

> "I see **red**!" she said. ⬆ (voice jumps on *red*)

*Adult reads it with the jump; learner echoes, copying exactly. Then:*

> "You are the **best** kite **ever**," he said. 😊 (happy face — proud voice)

*Adult models a proud, smiling voice; learner echoes. "Expression marks are
your rehearsal notes — actors write them in the script."*

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. **Echo:** adult reads "Then — **snap**! The string broke." with a sharp
   gasp on *snap*; learner echoes twice until it matches.
2. **Mark it:** learner puts an expression mark (⬆, 😊, 😟, or !) on "Look
   by the fence!" and reads it with that feeling.
3. **Rate check:** adult reads a line racing-fast, then crawling-slow.
   Learner picks which rate is "just right" and explains why. (Conversational
   — fast enough to follow the story, slow enough to get every word.)
4. **Punctuation hunt:** learner finds one **?**, one **!**, and one set of
   quotation marks in Passage A and reads each line with the matching voice.
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult reads the whole passage accurately but in
   a flat monotone. "Is that fluent?" (Not fully — accuracy yes, but no
   expression; the listener cannot feel Sam's worry or joy.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. First read: read Passage A aloud for **accuracy only**; adult notes any
   errors without interrupting.
2. Second read: read it again, aiming for a **conversational rate**.
3. Third read: the "performance read" — add expression to the three marked
   lines.
4. Adult scores the third read with the fluency rubric (see
   [teacher-guide](../teacher-guide.md#fluency-rubric)).
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why did your third read sound better than
   your first?" (Because rereading freed my brain from decoding — I could
   spend it on feeling.)

## Application

"Illustration storytime." With
[fluency-expression-readers.png](../assets/fluency-expression-readers.png)
on screen, the learner reads the passage's three dialogue lines — "Where
is my kite?", "I see red!", "You are the best kite ever" — **to** the dog
in the picture, using a different voice for Sam, Lee, and the narrator.
Then answer the picture question: "What in the picture shows the reader is
reading with feeling?" (Open mouth mid-story, wide storytelling eyes —
the listener leans in.)

**Text-only alternative:** the three dialogue lines above, with the prompt:
"Read each line three ways — worried, excited, proud. Which feeling matches
each character, and what punctuation told you?"

## Exit check

The learner echo-reads two new lines from Passage A after the adult's
model, matching phrasing and expression; then reads them solo once. Adult
checks: accuracy, conversational rate, and at least one expression choice
that fits the meaning.

## Supports and alternate response modes

- The learner may echo line-by-line for the whole passage instead of
  soloing the second read.
- Mark expression spots with stickers or colors instead of symbols.
- If reading aloud is hard, the learner may "whisper-read" the performance
  read to a stuffed listener first.

## Extension

The learner writes expression marks for an unseen paragraph (from a
library book at the right level) and explains two of their choices before
performing it.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner thinks expression means being loud or
silly. Anchor every choice to meaning: "Would Sam sound silly here, or
worried?" Punctuation is evidence, not decoration. Answers: see
[answer-key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
