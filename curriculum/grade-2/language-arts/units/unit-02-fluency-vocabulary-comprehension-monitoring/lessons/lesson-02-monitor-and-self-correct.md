# Lesson 02 — Catch It and Fix It: Monitoring and Self-Correcting

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can notice when my reading does not sound
right or make sense, and I can fix it by checking the words and rereading."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** Passage A — "The Lost Red Kite"
([unit README](../README.md)); a "fix-it" card the adult writes: *Stop →
Check → Reread*.
**Standards:** RF.2.4.c.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult reads aloud: "It was a **wimpy** day. Sam ran to the park with a
big red kite." Then: "Did that sound right?" (*No — 'wimpy' does not make
sense and does not match the letters: windy.*) "How did you know?" (The
sentence means a breezy day for kites; the letters say w-i-n-d-y.) That
catch-and-fix feeling is today's whole lesson.

## Explicit teaching

Good readers are **monitors**: they watch their own reading like a
lifeguard watches a pool. A monitor asks two silent questions, all the
time:

1. **Does it sound right?** (Do the letters I read match the word I said?)
2. **Does it make sense?** (Would the story really go this way?)

When the answer is no, the reader runs the **fix-it steps**:

- **Stop** — pause; do not keep going past the broken part.
- **Check** — look at the word again: what do the letters really say? Read
  the sentence around it: what would make sense here?
- **Reread** — read the sentence over, correctly, at least once.

Context — the words around a tricky word — is your second clue. If you
read "Sam pulled the kite out of the **glass**," the letters are close to
*grass*, but "glass" makes no sense: kites fall in grass, and earlier the
passage said "tall grass." Letters + sense together confirm the fix.

### Modeled example 1 — the adult plants an error

*The adult reads Passage A, "accidentally" reading the line as: "Sam
pulled the kite out of the **grass**… er, **glass**."*

"Stop — that did not sound right. **Check:** the letters are g-r-a-s-s —
*grass*, not glass. And it does not make sense: the kite fell into the
tall **grass** two lines ago. **Reread:** 'Sam pulled the kite out of the
grass.' Fixed. Did you see my three steps? Stop, check, reread."

### Modeled example 2 — a sense error, not a letter error

*The adult reads: "'Up went the kite. Sam **frowned**.' Stop — check.
**Frowned** means a sad face. Does that make sense? Sam just got his kite
back and called it the best kite ever. A smile fits; a frown does not. The
word really says **smiled**. Reread: 'Sam **smiled**.' Two clues agreed:
the letters and the sense."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. The adult reads "It was a **wintry** day" for "windy." Learner calls
   "Stop!" and fixes it. (Letters: w-i-n-d-y → *windy*; sense: kites need
   wind.)
2. The adult reads "'Lee tied the **swing** back on" for "string." Learner
   fixes it. (Letters: s-t-r-i-n-g → *string*; sense: you tie a string to
   a kite, not a swing.)
3. **Context-only check (ear only):** the adult says, "The wind blew
   again. Up went the **shoe**." The learner says which clue fails: letters
   or sense? (Sense — kites go up, not shoes. Even without seeing letters,
   the story would not say that.)
4. The learner reads the first paragraph aloud; the adult planted one
   written error on the learner's copy ("Sam ran to the **dark** with a big
   red kite"). The learner must catch and fix it. (*park* — letters p-a-r-k;
   sense: you fly kites at the park.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult reads a hard word wrong, shrugs, and keeps
   going. "What did I skip?" (The fix-it steps — you must stop, check, and
   reread, not plow past.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

The learner reads Passage A from a copy with **four planted errors** the
adult wrote in lightly (crossed out after the fix): *dark* for *park*,
*glass* for *grass*, *frowned* for *smiled*, *swing* for *string*.

1. Read and stop at the first wrong-feeling word. (dark → park)
2. Say the two clues that prove the fix: letters and sense.
3. Fix it, reread the sentence.
4. Continue; catch the remaining three errors, fixing each with the
   Stop → Check → Reread steps.
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Which of your two clues — letters or sense —
   caught the *frowned/smilied* mix-up? Why?" (Sense: Sam was happy. The
   letters also differ, but the feeling-mismatch is what flags it.)

## Application

"Fix-it detective report." The learner writes (or dictates) a two-line
report on the card: the word they misread, what they said, and which clue
caught it — e.g., "I said *swing*. Letters said s-t-r-i-n-g. I fixed it to
*string*." The report goes on the project wall.

## Exit check

The adult reads aloud a fresh sentence: "The kite fell into the tall
**grass**, and Sam felt **proud**… er, I mean **loud**." The learner must
catch it (*proud*, not *loud* — sense: proud fits getting the kite back)
and state the fix-it steps used.

## Supports and alternate response modes

- Planted errors may be read aloud by the adult while the learner follows
  the print with a finger and calls "Stop!"
- The learner may point to the clue sentence instead of explaining it
  verbally.
- Reduce planted errors to two for a learner still securing Unit 01
  decoding; accuracy of the fix matters more than the count.

## Extension

The learner plants three errors in a fresh copy of Passage A for the adult
to catch — and must defend each plant with both clues when the adult
"misses" one on purpose.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner equates self-correcting with "being bad
at reading." Reframe explicitly: fixing is what strong readers do; the
best readers catch the most. Never grade speed here. Answers: see
[answer-key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
