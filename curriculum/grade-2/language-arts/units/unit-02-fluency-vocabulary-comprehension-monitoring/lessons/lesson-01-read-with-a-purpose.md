# Lesson 01 — Read with a Purpose

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can pick a reason to read, and I can tell if
my reading answered it."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** Passage A — "The Lost Red Kite"
([unit README](../README.md)); paper for the purpose note.
**Standards:** RF.2.4.a.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "When you pick up a book, what are you usually trying to
find out?" Accept anything — a funny part, how it ends, a fact. Then:
"Today we read for one reason on purpose. That reason is called your
**purpose**. At the end we will check: did our reading answer it?"

## Explicit teaching

Fluent readers do not just start reading. First they ask themselves: *What
am I trying to find out?* A purpose can be tiny:

- "I want to find out **who** helps Sam."
- "I want to find out **what** happens to the kite."
- "I want to find out **where** the kite lands."

Reading **with purpose** means your brain hunts for that one thing while
your eyes move. It is like going to the kitchen with a job: you do not
wander — you get the spoon and come back. At the end, you check: *Did I
find what I was hunting for?* That check is how you know you read with
understanding.

### Modeled example 1 — purpose: who helps Sam?

*The adult reads the purpose aloud, then reads Passage A aloud at a
natural pace, pointing to each line:*

"My purpose: **who helps Sam?** While I read, I am listening for a helper.

'It was a windy day. Sam ran to the park with a big red kite…' — no helper
yet. 'His friend Lee came running. "I see red!" she said. "Look by the
fence!"' — there! Lee helps. She finds the kite. Later Lee ties the string
back on. Purpose check: my purpose was 'who helps Sam?' The answer is
**Lee**. My reading worked."

### Modeled example 2 — purpose: where does the kite land?

"New purpose: **where does the kite land?** Listen for the place.

'Then — snap! The string broke. The red kite fell into the **tall
grass**.' The place is the tall grass. Purpose check: where does the kite
land? **In the tall grass.** Found it."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "New purpose: **what** breaks?" (The string snaps/breaks.)
2. "New purpose: **how** does Sam feel at the end?" (Happy — he smiles and
   calls it the best kite ever. Evidence: "Sam smiled.")
3. The adult reads the first three sentences slowly; the learner raises a
   hand the moment the purpose answer ("who helps") appears. (At "His
   friend Lee came running" — later in the passage; the learner waits.)
4. **Odd one out:** which is a reading purpose — "find out why the string
   broke," "read really fast," or "find out what color the kite is"? (The
   first and third; "read really fast" is not a purpose — it is just speed.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult says, "My purpose was 'who helps Sam?' and
   I answer 'the wind.'" ("Wrong — the wind is not a helper. The answer
   must come from the text: Lee helps.")

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Learner picks a purpose from the adult's list of three: *who / what /
   where*, and says it aloud before reading.
2. Learner reads Passage A aloud (help with hard words is fine — this is
   not a fluency test yet).
3. Learner answers the purpose question in one full sentence.
4. Learner sets a **second** purpose of their own ("I want to find out
   ___") and rereads just for that.
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why is it easier to understand a story when
   you pick a purpose first?" (Because your brain knows what to hunt for;
   you notice the important parts instead of drifting.)

## Application

"Purpose notes." The learner draws a small kite on paper, writes (or
dictates for the adult to scribe) one purpose inside it — "I want to find
out ___" — then reads Passage A one more time and writes the answer under
the kite. The adult saves the kite card for the project wall.

## Exit check

The adult names a fresh purpose — "find out **when** this happens" — and
the learner reads Passage A aloud and answers in one sentence. ("It
happens on a windy day." Accept "during the day / at the park on a windy
day.")

## Supports and alternate response modes

- Pre-teach: before reading, the adult and learner walk through the
  passage's pictures or imagine the park scene together.
- The learner may answer purpose checks by pointing to the sentence that
  holds the answer, then the adult reads it aloud.
- Adult-scribed writing for the kite card; spelling is not graded here.

## Extension

The learner invents a purpose the passage **cannot** answer ("What is
Sam's last name?") and explains how they know it is unanswerable (the text
never says).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner treats "reading fast" as the goal.
Redirect every speed comment to the purpose check: "Did your reading
answer the question? Then it worked." Answers: see [answer-key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
