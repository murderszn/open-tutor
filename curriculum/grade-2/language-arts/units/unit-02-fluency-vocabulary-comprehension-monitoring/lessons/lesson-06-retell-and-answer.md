# Lesson 06 — Tell It Back: Recounting Key Ideas and Details

**Goal (learner hears):** "After someone reads to me, I can tell back the
important ideas and details in order."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** Passage B — "How Kites Fly"
([unit README](../README.md)); the learner's describing-word wall from
Lesson 05.
**Standards:** SL.2.2.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult says: "Pretend a friend missed today. In three sentences, tell
them what 'The Lost Red Kite' was about." Listen: does the learner give
the **key ideas** (lost kite, friend helps, fixed, flies again) or wander
into tiny details (the fence, the color of the grass)? "Today we practice
telling back the important parts — the skeleton, not every freckle."

## Explicit teaching

**Recounting** means telling back what you heard or read — the key ideas
and details, in an order that makes sense. Strong retells have:

1. **The big idea first:** what is this mostly about? ("How Kites Fly" is
   mostly about how wind lifts a kite and what can go wrong.)
2. **Key details in order:** the steps or facts that matter (wind pushes →
   string holds → gusts help → tangles happen → no wind means down).
3. **Your own words:** not memorizing sentences — rebuilding the meaning.
4. **A check:** ask yourself the **5 Ws + H** — *who, what, where, when,
   why, how* — and see if you can answer them.

Listening counts as reading here: when someone reads aloud **to** you,
your job is to catch the ideas as they fly past, the way you would catch a
ball. You cannot reread a voice — so you listen for the big idea and hold
onto key details.

### Modeled example 1 — the adult models a retell

*The adult reads Passage B aloud once, at a natural pace, then models:*

"Big idea: this tells **how kites fly and what can go wrong**. Key
details, in order: wind pushes against the kite and lifts it up. The string
holds it so it does not fly away. A gusty day — full of sudden bursts — is
best. But wind can tangle the string into a messy knot. If the wind stops,
the kite drifts and flutters down. My own words, in order — and I can
answer: **what** lifts the kite (wind), **why** the string matters (holds
it), **when** is best (a gusty day)."

### Modeled example 2 — fixing a wandering retell

*The adult gives a bad retell on purpose:* "It was about wind, and knots,
and… uh… the sky is blue, and kites are fun, and my cousin has a kite…"

"Stop — I wandered. The 5 Ws + H check catches it: I never said the **big
idea** (how kites fly), and I added stuff from my own life (my cousin)
that is not in the text. Fix: big idea first, key details in order, only
what the text said. Watch: 'This tells how kites fly. Wind pushes the kite
up. The string holds it. Gusty days are best. Wind can tangle the string.
No wind — the kite comes down.'"

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. The adult reads Passage B aloud; the learner states the **big idea** in
   one sentence. ("It tells how kites fly.")
2. **5 Ws + H:** the adult asks *what* holds the kite in place? (The
   string.) *Why* is a gusty day best? (Sudden bursts of wind lift the kite.)
3. The learner retells the middle of the passage (gust → tangle) in order,
   in their own words.
4. **Odd one out:** which detail does NOT belong in a retell of Passage B —
   "the string holds the kite," "a gust is a sudden burst," or "kites come
   in many colors"? (The third — the passage never says that.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult retells: "The wind stops, so the kite
   flies higher." ("Wrong order and wrong fact — when the wind stops, the
   kite drifts **down**. Check the text, not your guess.")

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

The adult reads Passage B aloud **once** (no reread — this is listening).

1. State the big idea in one sentence.
2. Tell back three key details in order.
3. Answer: **who** would like this text? (Someone learning to fly a kite.)
4. Answer: **how** does the wind help, and **what** can go wrong? (Pushes
   the kite up; it can tangle the string / stop and let the kite fall.)
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why is it harder to retell something you
   only *heard* than something you *read*?" (You cannot go back and reread
   a voice — you have to catch the big idea while listening.)

## Application

"News report." The learner pretends to be a TV reporter: "Breaking news
from Kite Park!" They report the key ideas of Passage B in 4–6 sentences,
using at least two describing words from the word wall (*gusty, messy,
bright*). The adult is the audience and may ask one follow-up question
the report must answer from the text.

## Exit check

The adult reads the first three sentences of Passage A aloud. The learner
retells them with the big idea and two details, in order, in their own
words. (Big idea: Sam takes his kite to the park on a windy day. Details:
wind pulls it up over the trees; the string snaps; it falls in the tall
grass.)

## Supports and alternate response modes

- The learner may retell while pointing to simple sketches they draw
  (kite up → string → knot → kite down).
- Sentence starters: "This is mostly about…", "First…", "Then…",
  "A problem is…".
- The adult may read Passage B in two halves with a pause for a mid-point
  retell.

## Extension

The learner compares the two passages' big ideas: "How is a story's big
idea different from an information text's big idea?" (A story's big idea is
what happens and how it turns out; an information text's big idea is what
you learn — here, how kites fly.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner retells every sentence or recites
memorized lines. Praise the skeleton: "You got the bones! Now which bones
matter most?" Memorizing is not understanding — own-words retells are.
