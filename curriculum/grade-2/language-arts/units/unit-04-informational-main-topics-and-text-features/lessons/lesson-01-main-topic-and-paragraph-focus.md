# Lesson 01 — Main Topic and Paragraph Focus

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can name what a whole information text is
mostly about, and what each paragraph is mostly about — and point to
where the text shows me."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the text below; a pencil or
pointer.
**Standards:** RI.2.2, RI.2.1.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks about something the learner already knows: "Tell me two
true things about dogs." Then: "If I wrote a whole book about dogs, the
book's main topic would be dogs. A paragraph about what dogs eat would
have a smaller focus: dog food. Main topic is the whole text; focus is
one paragraph." If the learner cannot name two true things, the adult
models one ("Dogs bark") and the learner repeats and adds one.

## The text — "Beavers: Nature's Builders" (original; adult reads aloud first, learner re-reads)

Beavers are large rodents that live near streams and ponds. A rodent is
an animal with big front teeth that keep growing. Beavers use their
teeth, their flat tails, and their strong bodies to change the land
around them. They are nature's builders.

### A Beaver's Body

A beaver has brown fur, a wide flat tail, and big orange front teeth.
Its teeth never stop growing, so a beaver must gnaw, or chew, on wood
to keep them the right length. A beaver is a strong swimmer. It can
close its nose and ears under water and see with clear lids over its
eyes, like goggles.

### Building a Dam

A beaver builds a **dam** across a narrow stream. A dam is a wall made
of sticks, branches, and mud. The beaver piles sticks across the stream
and packs mud between them. The wall slows the water down, and a pond
forms behind it. The pond must be deep enough that it does not freeze
all the way in winter.

### A Home Called a Lodge

In the pond, beavers build a **lodge**. A lodge is a dome-shaped home
made of branches and mud. The doors of the lodge are under the water,
so beavers swim in and out safely. Inside, the room is dry and above
the water. A beaver family lives in the lodge together.

### What Beavers Eat

Beavers eat only plants. They eat bark, twigs, and leaves. In fall,
beavers store branches under the water near the lodge. In winter, they
swim under the ice, pull out a branch, and eat the soft bark. Baby
beavers are called **kits**. Kits stay with their parents for about
two years and learn to build and swim.

**Glossary (at the end of the text):** **dam** — a wall built across a
stream to slow water and make a pond. **lodge** — a dome-shaped home
made of branches and mud. **kit** — a baby beaver. **gnaw** — to chew
on something hard, again and again.

## Explicit teaching

An information text teaches true facts about a real topic. Two words
run this lesson:

- **Main topic** — what the *whole* text is mostly about. Ask: "If I
  had to tell a friend what this whole text teaches, in five words or
  fewer, what would I say?" The title is a clue, not proof: check that
  every paragraph connects to it.
- **Focus of a paragraph** — what *one* paragraph is mostly about.
  Ask: "What do most sentences in this paragraph talk about?" The first
  sentence often says it, and the heading above it says it too.

Rule: after every answer, point to the proof — a sentence, a heading,
or a glossary entry. "I guessed from the title" is not proof.

### Modeled example 1 — Main topic

Adult thinks aloud: "The title says *Beavers: Nature's Builders*. Is
the whole text about beavers? Paragraph 1 is about beavers in general.
Paragraph 2 is a beaver's body. Paragraph 3 is a beaver's dam.
Paragraph 4 is a beaver's lodge. Paragraph 5 is beaver food and babies.
Every paragraph is about beavers — so the main topic is beavers, and
more exactly, how beavers build and live."

### Modeled example 2 — Paragraph focus

"Look at the paragraph under *Building a Dam*. Most sentences talk
about the wall: what it is made of, how the beaver packs mud, what
happens to the water. One sentence mentions the pond, but the pond is
the *result* of the dam. So the focus is building a dam — not ponds in
general. The heading agrees: *Building a Dam*."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "What is the main topic of the whole text?" (Beavers — how they
   build and live.) "Point to one sentence in each paragraph that is
   about beavers."
2. "What is the focus of the paragraph under *A Beaver's Body*?" (What
   a beaver's body is like: fur, tail, teeth, swimming.)
3. "What is the focus of the paragraph under *A Home Called a
   Lodge*?" (The beaver's home: what it is made of, its underwater
   doors, the dry room inside.)
4. "What is the focus of the paragraph under *What Beavers Eat*?"
   (Beaver food and babies: plants, stored branches, kits.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult says, "The main topic is ponds,
   because paragraph 3 talks about a pond." "Is that right?" (No —
   only one paragraph is mostly about the pond, and it is about the
   pond a dam makes. Four of five paragraphs are about beavers
   themselves. A main topic must fit the whole text.)

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. In your own words, what is the main topic of "Beavers: Nature's
   Builders"?
2. Which paragraph tells you what a beaver's teeth do? Say its heading.
3. Name one key detail from the "Building a Dam" paragraph.
4. Who lives in the lodge? (Answer with a sentence from the text.)
5. Why must a beaver gnaw on wood? (Use the word *growing* in your
   answer.)
6. Draw or point to the paragraph about beaver food, and say its focus
   in one sentence.

## Applied task

The learner asks the adult two new questions about the text that were
not asked above — one who/what/where question and one why/how
question — and the adult must answer with proof. Then they swap.
(Example learner question: "Where are the lodge doors?" — proof: "The
doors of the lodge are under the water.")

## Exit check

"Tell me the main topic in five words or fewer, and tell me the focus
of one paragraph I pick." Ready to move on: main topic named, paragraph
focus named, and at least one proof pointed to.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** adult reads aloud first; learner re-reads one
  paragraph at a time. Bold words are pre-taught from the glossary.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the paragraph, draw its focus,
  or dictate the answer for the adult to scribe.
- **Extension:** the learner writes a new heading for the first
  paragraph (which has none) and defends it: "Beavers Change the Land"
  or "Meet the Beaver."

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: naming the title as the main topic without
checking the paragraphs, or naming one paragraph's focus as the main
topic. Reteach with the "every paragraph?" test from Modeled example 1.
