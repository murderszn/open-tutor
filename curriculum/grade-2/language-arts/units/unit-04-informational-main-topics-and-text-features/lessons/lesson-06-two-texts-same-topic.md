# Lesson 06 — Two Texts, Same Topic

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare two texts about the same
topic and say what is the same and what is different — with proof from
both texts."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** Lesson 01's text and the text
below; a pencil or pointer.
**Standards:** RI.2.9, RI.2.2.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Two friends both tell you about their cats. One says
her cat is orange and sleeps all day. The other says his cat is gray
and chases toys. What is the same? What is different?" (Same: both
about cats, both pets. Different: color, behavior.) If the learner
answers only "same" or only "different," model the other side once.

## The text — "Beaver Families" (original; adult reads aloud first, learner re-reads)

A beaver family lives and works together in one lodge. The family is
made of a mother and father, last year's young, called yearlings, and
this year's babies, called kits.

The parents do the biggest jobs. They cut down trees and build the dam
and the lodge. The yearlings help. They drag branches, pack mud, and
watch the kits swim.

Kits stay in the lodge for their first month. Then they swim with the
family and learn to find food. Kits stay with their parents for about
two years. Then they leave to build their own dams and lodges.

A beaver family is a team. Every beaver has a job, and every job helps
the family live safely in the pond.

## Explicit teaching

**Compare** means find what is the same. **Contrast** means find what
is different. When two texts share a topic, they will agree on some
points and differ on others — usually because they have different
focuses.

Method, in three moves:

1. **Name each text's main topic and focus.** "Beavers: Nature's
   Builders" focuses on bodies, dams, lodges, and food. "Beaver
   Families" focuses on family life and jobs.
2. **Match points across texts.** Same point in both? (Both say kits
   stay about two years. Both say beavers build dams and lodges.)
3. **Name what only one text says.** (Only "Nature's Builders" says
   teeth keep growing and look orange. Only "Beaver Families" says
   yearlings help drag branches and watch kits swim.)

Rule: every same and every different needs proof from both texts (for
a "same") or from the one text that says it (for a "different"). "I
remember it" is not proof — point to the sentence.

### Modeled example 1 — One same point

Adult thinks aloud: "'Nature's Builders' says kits 'stay with their
parents for about two years.' 'Beaver Families' says 'Kits stay with
their parents for about two years.' Same words, same fact, two texts.
That is a same point, and I can point to both sentences."

### Modeled example 2 — One different point

"'Nature's Builders' has a whole paragraph about a beaver's body —
teeth, tail, swimming. 'Beaver Families' never talks about teeth or
tails at all. That is a difference in focus: one text teaches bodies,
the other teaches family jobs. Different does not mean one text is
wrong — it means the authors chose different focuses."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "What is the main topic of both texts?" (Beavers.)
2. "Name one point both texts make." (Kits stay about two years;
   beavers build dams and lodges; families live in the lodge — any
   one with two proofs.)
3. "Name one point only 'Nature's Builders' makes." (Teeth grow all
   their lives; beavers eat bark, twigs, leaves; lodge doors are under
   water — any one.)
4. "Name one point only 'Beaver Families' makes." (Yearlings help and
   watch kits; kits stay in the lodge for the first month; parents do
   the biggest jobs — any one.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult says, "'Beaver Families' is wrong
   because it never mentions orange teeth." "Is that fair?" (No — a
   text is not wrong for leaving something out. Different focus is not
   a mistake. It would be wrong only if it said something false.)

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. Write two points that are the same in both texts, with a proof
   word or sentence for each text.
2. Write two points that are different, naming which text says each.
3. Which text would you use to answer "What do beavers eat?" Why?
4. Which text would you use to answer "What jobs do yearlings do?"
   Why?
5. Do the two texts disagree about anything? Answer yes or no, and
   defend it in one sentence.
6. Draw or point to one same point and one different point on a
   two-circle chart (the adult draws two overlapping circles; shared
   points go in the middle, text-only points go on the sides).

## Applied task

The learner picks a new topic they know well (for example, dogs or
bicycles) and dictates two tiny texts about it with different focuses
(one about bodies, one about families or care). Then the learner names
one same point and one different point between their own two texts.

## Exit check

"Tell me two same points and two different points between the two
beaver texts, with proof." Ready to move on: 2 same + 2 different,
each with its sentence pointed to.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** adult reads both texts aloud; the learner tracks
  with a pointer and uses the two-circle chart as a manipulative.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort sentence cards into "both,"
  "Builders only," and "Families only" piles; draw the chart; dictate.
- **Extension:** the learner writes one sentence that combines a same
  point from both texts ("Both texts agree that ___") and one that
  states a difference ("Only ___ says ___").

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: calling a missing fact a disagreement, or naming
vague sameness ("both about animals") instead of specific points.
Reteach with the proof rule and Modeled example 2. Answer-key
