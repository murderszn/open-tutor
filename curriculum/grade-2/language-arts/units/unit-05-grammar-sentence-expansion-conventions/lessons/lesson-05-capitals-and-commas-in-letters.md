# Lesson 05 — Capitals and Commas in Letters

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can use capital letters for holidays,
products, and places — and put commas in the right spots in a letter."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the model letter below; a
pencil or pointer.
**Standards:** L.2.2a, L.2.2b.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Which words do we already capitalize?" (The first word
of a sentence; names of people; days and months.) "Today we add three
more: holidays, product names, and place names — and we learn the two
commas a letter needs."

## The text — "A Letter from Grandma Rosa" (original model letter; adult reads aloud first, learner re-reads)

Dear Grandma Rosa,

Thank you for the red kite. Sam and I flew it in the park on Saturday.
It went up high, and we ran fast to keep it in the sky. At
Thanksgiving, we will bring it to Chicago and fly it near Lake
Michigan. I will pack Crayola markers so we can draw the kite, too.

Love,

Ana

## Explicit teaching

**Part 1 — Three new capital letters.**

- **Holidays** — the name of a special day: Thanksgiving, Halloween,
  Christmas, New Year's Day, Fourth of July, Valentine's Day.
- **Product names** — the brand name of a thing you buy: LEGO, Crayola,
  Cheerios, Popsicle, Nike. (The brand name is capitalized; the thing
  itself is not: "LEGO blocks," "Crayola markers.")
- **Geographic names** — the name of a place: Chicago, Texas, Lake
  Michigan, Rocky Mountains, Main Street, Africa.

Rule: if it is the special name of a holiday, a product, or a place,
capitalize it. "thanksgiving" is a mistake; "Thanksgiving" is right.
"I want lego blocks" is a mistake; "I want LEGO blocks" is right.

**Part 2 — Two commas in a letter.** A friendly letter needs two
commas:

- After the **greeting**: "Dear Grandma Rosa," — the comma goes right
  after the name.
- After the **closing**: "Love," — the comma goes right after the
  closing words ("Love," "Your friend," "Sincerely,").

### Modeled example 1 — Capitals

Adult thinks aloud: "'We will visit chicago at thanksgiving.' *Chicago*
is a place — a city — so it needs a capital: *Chicago*. *Thanksgiving*
is a holiday, so it needs a capital: *Thanksgiving*. Fixed: 'We will
visit Chicago at Thanksgiving.' Now: 'I want cheerios for breakfast.'
*Cheerios* is a product name, so: 'I want Cheerios for breakfast.'"

### Modeled example 2 — The two commas

"Look at Grandma Rosa's letter. The greeting says 'Dear Grandma Rosa,'
— there is the first comma, right after the name. The closing says
'Love,' — there is the second comma, right after *Love*. Without them,
the letter looks unfinished: 'Dear Grandma Rosa' with no comma is a
mistake a reader will notice."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. Fix the capitals: "We swam in lake michigan." ("We swam in Lake
   Michigan.")
2. Fix the capitals: "I want lego blocks for christmas." ("I want
   LEGO blocks for Christmas.")
3. Add the comma: "Dear Mr. Chen" ("Dear Mr. Chen,")
4. Add the comma: the closing "Love Ana" ("Love, / Ana" — the comma
   goes after *Love*.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult shows: "dear ana, thank you for the
   crayola markers." "What needs fixing?" (*Dear* needs a capital —
   first word of the greeting — and *Crayola* is a product name:
   "Dear Ana, thank you for the Crayola markers.")

## Independent practice (adult-scribed or written; 6 tasks)

1. "My birthday is on halloween." (Halloween)
2. "We drove to texas." (Texas)
3. "She wore nike shoes." (Nike)
4. Write the greeting for a letter to Ms. Patel. ("Dear Ms. Patel,")
5. Write the closing for a letter to a friend. (Accept: "Your friend,"
   or "Love," — with the comma.)
6. Fix the whole note: "dear sam, happy fourth of july! love, ana"
   ("Dear Sam, Happy Fourth of July! Love, Ana")

## Applied task

The learner writes a two-line note to someone in the family: one line
about a holiday, product, or place (with the capital), and one line
that is the greeting or closing of a letter (with the comma). The adult
checks both capitals and the comma.

## Exit check

"Capitalize a holiday, a product, and a place, and show me the two
commas a letter needs." Ready to move on: three capitals named and
both commas placed.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the model letter aloud first;
  the learner points to each capital letter and each comma.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the capital or the comma spot,
  or dictate for the adult to scribe.
- **Extension:** the learner sorts six words into "needs a capital"
  and "does not" — christmas, dog, nike, park, lake michigan, book
  (Christmas, Nike, Lake Michigan need capitals; dog, park, book do
  not — unless they are part of a name).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: capitalizing common nouns that feel important
("Dog," "Park") and forgetting that the comma follows the greeting
name, not the word *Dear* ("Dear, Grandma Rosa" is wrong). Also watch
for lowercase brand spellings copied from packaging (some brands style
themselves lowercase); in school writing, brand names take a capital.
