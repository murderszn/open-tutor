# Lesson 05 — Picture Graphs, Bar Graphs, and Evidence Claims

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
ruler, the learner's data table from Lesson 04 or the project, graph paper
(optional — squared notebook paper works)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will turn data into **graphs**
— pictures of numbers — and use the graph to make a **claim** supported
by **evidence**.

**Readiness check:** "What does our data table tell us about the smooth
paper and the towel?" Any answer that uses a number ("the paper numbers
are bigger") shows readiness. If the learner only says "paper is better,"
ask, "How do you *know*? Show me the numbers."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A table holds data, but a **graph** *shows* it. Two graphs scientists use
a lot:

- A **picture graph**: one picture stands for one (or a set) of things.
  Three car pictures in the "smooth paper" row = three trials.
- A **bar graph**: bars made of squares. Taller bar = bigger number. In
  grade 2 we use a **single-unit scale**: each square is 1 unit (here, 1 cm).

A **claim** is what you say is true. **Evidence** is the data that backs
it up. The sentence frame scientists use is: **"My claim is ___. My
evidence is ___."** A claim without evidence is just an opinion.

**Worked example 1 — table to bar graph, modeled:**

> Adult takes the Lesson 04 practice table (smooth paper: 42, 40, 41 cm;
> towel: 25, 24, 26 cm) and builds a bar graph of the *best single number*
> for each condition — the middle value (median): smooth paper 41 cm, towel
> 25 cm. Adult draws two bars, one square per cm, labels the bars "smooth
> paper" and "towel," labels the axis "distance (cm)," and colors the taller
> bar. "Look — the smooth-paper bar towers over the towel bar. The picture
> of the data tells the same story as the table, faster."
>
> Why the middle value? "Three bars per surface would be six bars — messy.
> Scientists often show one number that represents the set. I chose the
> middle of the three trials. You could also show all three — either is
> honest, as long as you say which you did."

**Worked example 2 — reading a graph and making a claim:**

> Adult draws a fresh bar graph: "Paper towel A" bar = 6 squares, "Paper
> towel B" bar = 3 squares, axis labeled "spoons of water held."
> Adult reads it aloud: "The bar for towel A is taller — 6 squares to 3.
> Each square is one spoon of water. So my **claim** is: towel A holds more
> water than towel B. My **evidence** is: towel A's bar shows 6 spoons and
> towel B's bar shows 3 spoons. Claim + evidence — that's how a scientist
> talks."

Key idea to say plainly: **Graphs are pictures of data. Read the graph,
then say your claim and point to your evidence.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner reads and builds)

1. Adult shows the worked-example-2 graph. Learner points to the taller bar
   and names the winner.
2. "How many more spoons did A hold than B?" (3 more — compare problem,
   counting squares.)
3. Learner builds a **picture graph** of the Lesson 04 trials: one car
   picture per 10 cm per condition (4 pictures vs. 2.5 — discuss: "we can't
   draw half a car neatly, so picture graphs are rough; bar graphs are
   exact"). Adult scribes the key: "1 car picture = 10 cm."
4. Learner reads their own picture graph back: "Which condition won?"
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "A friend says, 'Towel B is better because I
   like blue.' Is that a claim with evidence?" (No — liking is not
   evidence; evidence is data.)
6. Learner completes the sentence frame about the Lesson 04 data: "My claim
   is ___. My evidence is ___."

## Independent practice

Using the practice table from [practice.md](../practice.md) task 5 (plant
heights: 5 cm, 8 cm, 6 cm), the learner draws a **bar graph** with a
labeled axis ("height in cm") and writes one claim-evidence sentence
beneath it. Adult checks: one square per cm, bars labeled, axis labeled.

## Application

Graph detective: the learner finds a graph in a book, newspaper, or
classroom poster and answers: "What is being compared? Which bar/picture
is biggest? What claim could I make from it?" Record the claim in the
journal.

## Exit check

Adult draws two bars (no labels at first): "Red = 7, Blue = 4." Learner
states a claim with evidence using the frame. Then: "What makes a claim
scientific?" (It points to evidence — data — not just an opinion.)

**Table variant:** Preprinted graph grid with the axis pre-labeled "cm";
the learner colors squares to the correct heights and dictates the
claim-evidence sentence for the adult to scribe.

**Active variant:** "Human bar graph": the learner stands on floor tape
marked in "cm squares" — their height in squares becomes a bar; compare
with a sibling/object's "bar." (Fun, approximate — label it as play, not
precise data.)

**Reading/language support:** The claim-evidence frame is on a permanent
word-wall card. Color is never the only cue: bars get pattern or letter
labels (A/B), and the learner always reads the *numbers*, not just "the
tall one."

**Extension:** The learner re-graphs the Lesson 04 data showing **all
three trials** per condition (six bars, grouped by color/pattern) and
explains why the single-representative graph and the full graph tell the
same story — and what the full graph shows that the simple one hides
(how close the trials were).

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners often treat the tallest bar as "the best" regardless of the
question — e.g., in the absorbency project, taller = holds more water =
wins, but in a "which dries fastest" question, the *shortest* bar would
win. Always ask: **"What does taller mean *in this graph*?"** Tie the bar
back to the unit (cm, spoons) before any claim. This habit is the seed of
2-PS1-2's "best suited for an intended purpose" reasoning.
