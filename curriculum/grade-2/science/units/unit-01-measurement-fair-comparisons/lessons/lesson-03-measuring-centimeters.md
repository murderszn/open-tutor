# Lesson 03 — Measuring Length to the Nearest Centimeter

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, a ruler
marked in centimeters (adult checks it shows cm), five household objects of
different lengths (pencil, crayon, spoon, book, shoe), a strip of paper
marked "estimate"

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how to measure length
with a ruler — starting at the **0**, and reporting to the **nearest
centimeter**.

**Readiness check:** Adult holds the pencil and asks, "Is this longer or
shorter than the crayon?" and "How could we find out *exactly* how long?"
Any comparing answer ("use the ruler," "line them up") shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Scientists **measure** so they can compare fairly. "About this big" is not
enough — numbers let everyone agree. In this unit we measure length in
**centimeters**, written **cm**. A centimeter is about the width of your
little fingernail.

Three rules for honest measuring:

1. **Estimate first.** Before the ruler, make a careful guess. Estimating
   trains your eyes — and later, if your measurement is wildly different
   from your estimate, you know to check again.
2. **Start at the 0, not the edge.** Line the object's end up with the
   ruler's **0 mark**. The edge of the ruler is not zero — starting there
   steals or adds length.
3. **Read to the nearest centimeter.** Find the centimeter mark *closest*
   to the object's other end. If the end falls between 6 and 7 but is
   closer to 7, you write **7 cm**. If it lands halfway, we round up to the
   larger number (adult demonstrates: "halfway between 6 and 7 → 7 cm").

**Worked example 1 — modeled measuring, slowly:**

> Adult places a crayon on the ruler: "First I estimate: about 9 cm. Now
> I line one end of the crayon up with the **0** — see, not the edge of
> the ruler. The other end reaches past the 9 mark but doesn't reach 10.
> It is closer to 9. So I write: **crayon = 9 cm.** My estimate was 9, my
> measurement is 9 — they agree, so I trust it."

**Worked example 2 — the edge-starting mistake, caught:**

> Adult deliberately starts the same crayon at the ruler's **edge** (the
> 1-cm mark area on many rulers, or the physical end): "Look — now it
> reads 10 cm. But the crayon didn't grow! Starting at the edge added an
> extra centimeter. That's why rule 2 matters: start at the **0**. The
> right answer is still 9 cm. Always check: *did I start at zero?*"

Key idea to say plainly: **Estimate first, start at the 0, and read the
nearest centimeter — then your measurements are honest and comparable.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner measures)

Adult estimates aloud for the first object, then the learner leads.

1. **Spoon:** learner estimates, then measures from the 0; adult checks the
   start point. Record: "spoon ≈ ___ cm (estimate), ___ cm (measured)."
2. **Pencil:** learner estimates and measures independently; adult only
   verifies the 0-start. Discuss: was the estimate close?
3. **Book (width):** the book is longer than the learner expects — practice
   holding the ruler steady across the full width; measure twice to check
   the reading repeats.
4. **Shoe:** awkward shape — model measuring the *longest* straight part;
   discuss that measuring the same way each time is part of a fair test.
5. **Error check:** adult measures the crayon starting at the ruler's edge
   on purpose. Learner must spot the mistake and re-measure correctly.

## Independent practice

The learner measures **three self-chosen objects** from the room, recording
estimate and measured length for each in the journal. Adult reviews the
same day: check one random start-point alignment per object.

## Application

"Measure five" challenge: the learner predicts which of the five unit
objects is closest to **10 cm**, then measures to check. Record the
winner and how close the prediction was.

## Exit check

Adult hands the learner the spoon. "Show me where you start measuring.
Now read it — to the nearest centimeter." Correct start at 0 and a
nearest-cm reading passes. Then: "Why do we estimate first?" (Accept: to
check our measurement makes sense / to train our eyes.)

**Table variant:** A preprinted table with columns "Object | My estimate |
Measured | Check (same day)". The learner points to the 0 on the ruler
before each measurement instead of holding it.

**Active variant:** "Human ruler": the learner lies next to a taped paper
strip while the adult marks and measures their arm length in cm; the
learner reads the nearest cm aloud.

**Reading/language support:** Teach *centimeter* by touch — a cm cube or
the fingernail comparison — before the symbol "cm". Every measurement is
read aloud with its unit: "nine centimeters," never "nine" alone.

**Extension:** The learner measures one object in centimeters and in
*paper-clip units* (laying clips end to end), and explains why the numbers
are different even though the object is the same ("the units are different
sizes — that's why scientists agree on units").

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Two classics: (a) starting at the ruler's **edge** instead of 0, and (b)
reading the number at the object's end as a count of *spaces* — e.g.,
reading "9" when the object spans 8 full cm. Worked example 2 addresses (a)
directly. For (b), have the learner count the centimeter *spaces* the object
covers and compare with the endpoint reading; they should match — if not,
re-check the start.
