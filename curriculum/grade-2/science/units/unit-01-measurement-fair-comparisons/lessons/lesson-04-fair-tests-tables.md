# Lesson 04 — Fair Tests, Repeated Trials, and Data Tables

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil,
[assets/fair-test-comparison.png](../assets/fair-test-comparison.png),
toy car or ball, book-and-board ramp, smooth paper, towel, ruler

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will run a fair test with
**repeated trials** and record the data in a **table** — and you will use
a picture to spot a fair test and an unfair one.

**Readiness check:** "What makes a test fair?" (One change; everything else
the same.) If the learner can't answer, replay Lesson 01's Worked example 1
in 60 seconds.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A fair test needs one more ingredient: **repeated trials**. One try could
be a lucky accident — the car hit a bump, you released it crooked. So
scientists repeat the same fair test **three times** and look at all three
results. If the three results are close together, the test is trustworthy.

And scientists keep their data in a **table**: columns say *what* was
tested, rows say *which trial*. A table lets anyone read your evidence at
a glance.

**Worked example 1 — three trials, recorded:**

> Adult runs the Lesson 01 surface test: same ramp, same car, same release;
> smooth paper for three trials, then the towel for three trials. After each
> run the adult measures and calls out the distance:
>
> | Surface | Trial 1 | Trial 2 | Trial 3 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Smooth paper | 42 cm | 40 cm | 41 cm |
> | Towel | 25 cm | 24 cm | 26 cm |
>
> "Look: the three smooth-paper numbers are close to each other — 42, 40,
> 41. The three towel numbers are close too — 25, 24, 26. Nothing weird
> happened in any single trial. And in *every* trial, the smooth paper was
> farther. That's a trustworthy result. (These are our practice numbers —
> yours will be your own.)"

**Worked example 2 — the image error-analysis:**

> Adult shows [fair-test-comparison.png](../assets/fair-test-comparison.png):
> "Two friends each tested which setup lets a toy travel farther. Look at
> the LEFT station: two ramps at the **same** height, the same red car on
> each — the only difference is that one ramp is covered with a blue towel.
> One change! That's a **fair test**. Now the RIGHT station: one ramp is
> **tall** and one is **short** — and one has a red car while the other has
> a yellow ball. **Two** changes. Unfair! If the car goes farther, we can't
> tell whether it was the height or the toy. The right station is a good
> example of what NOT to do."
>
> *Text-only alternative (same information as the image):* The picture shows
> two side-by-side test stations. Left station: two identical wooden ramps
> propped at exactly the same height; one ramp is bare wood and the other is
> covered with a soft blue towel; a small red toy car sits at the top of each
> ramp. Right station: two different ramps — one noticeably taller than the
> other; a small red toy car sits at the top of the tall ramp and a small
> yellow toy ball sits at the top of the short ramp.

Key idea to say plainly: **Repeat your fair test three times, keep every
trial in a table, and check: are the trials close together?**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. Look at the image again. Learner points to the fair-test station and
   says *what stays the same* (ramps, cars) and *what changes* (surface).
2. Learner points to the unfair-test station and names **both** changes
   (ramp height; the toy).
3. "How would you fix the right station to make it fair?" (Accept any
   answer that leaves exactly one change: same-height ramps with different
   toys, or same toy on different-height ramps with the question changed to
   "does ramp height change the distance?")
4. Adult runs one real trial with the class ramp; learner records the
   distance in a blank three-row table the adult draws.
5. **Tally practice:** Adult asks, "How many trials have we done?" Learner
   updates a tally (|| = 2). "How many more until three?" (one more).
6. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "A friend did one trial and said the towel is
   slower. Should we believe them yet? Why not?" (One trial could be luck;
   we need repeated trials close together.)

## Independent practice

The learner designs a **data table on paper** for a fair test they invent
(question from Lesson 01, e.g., "does a taller ramp make the car roll
farther?"): one column for each condition, three rows for trials, and a
space for the measurement unit (cm). They do not need to run it today —
the table is the product. Adult checks: does the plan change only one thing?

## Application

Fair-test detective: the learner looks around the room and finds one
*unfair* comparison someone might make ("this book is heavier than that
feather — but the book is also bigger!"), names the two changes, and says
how to make it fair.

## Exit check

Show the image one more time. Learner states: which station is fair, which
is unfair, and **both** changes in the unfair station. Then: "Why do we do
three trials instead of one?" (To make sure the result wasn't an accident.)

**Table variant:** Preprinted table with "Trial 1 / Trial 2 / Trial 3"
rows and a "cm" label; the learner dictates numbers while the adult writes,
or stamps tally marks.

**Active variant:** The learner physically sets up the fair station from the
image with real materials (two equal-height ramps, bare vs. towel) and runs
the three trials while the adult records.

**Reading/language support:** Sentence frame for the table reading: "In
trial ___, the ___ went ___ cm." Word wall: *trial* (three tally marks),
*table* (drawn grid), *fair* (balance).

**Extension:** The learner adds a fourth row, "My prediction," to the
table *before* running trials, then compares prediction to data and
explains any surprise — the unit's first prediction-vs-evidence reflection.

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners often think repeating a trial means *changing* something each time
("let's try the towel now, then the paper, then the towel — that's three
trials!"). Clarify: repeated trials repeat the **same** conditions; the
surface *condition* changes only when you move to the next column. Also,
learners may average mentally and report "about 25" instead of all three
numbers — insist the table keeps every trial, because a single odd trial
(a bump, a push) is information, not embarrassment.
