# Lesson 01 — Questions You Can Test (Fair-Test Questions)

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
a toy car or ball, a book (to make a ramp), two surface cloths (smooth paper
and a towel)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn what makes a question
one you can *test*, and what makes a test *fair* — a test that changes only
one thing.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What is one question you wonder about?"
Any question shows readiness. If the learner has none, model one: "I wonder
which surface a toy car rolls farther on."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Scientists don't just wonder — they turn some wonderings into **questions
they can test**. A question you can test is one you could explore by looking,
touching, measuring, or trying — and that would give you an answer.

But testing has a rule. Imagine I ask: *"Does the surface change how far the
toy rolls?"* I could test that. But if I roll the car on the smooth paper
from a high ramp **and** on the towel from a low ramp, I changed **two**
things — the surface AND the ramp height. If the car rolls farther, I don't
know which change did it. That's an **unfair test**.

A **fair test** changes only **one thing** and keeps everything else the
same. Then, whatever changes in the result must be because of the one thing
I changed.

**Worked example 1 — explored out loud, slowly:**

> Adult: "My question: *Does the surface change how far the toy car rolls?*
> I can test this — I can try it! What is the **one thing** I will change?
> The surface: smooth paper, then a towel. What must I keep the **same**?
> The same car, the same ramp, the same ramp height, the same release — no
> pushing. One change, everything else the same. That is a fair test."

Adult demonstrates the two trials, keeping everything visibly identical
except the surface.

**Worked example 2 — a testable question done unfairly, then fixed:**

> Adult: "New question: *Does a bigger push make the ball roll farther?*
> First I tried it wrong: I pushed the ball gently on the rug and hard on
> the smooth floor. Two changes — push AND floor. Unfair! I learned nothing.
> So I fixed it: same smooth floor both times, only the push changes. Now
> the test is fair, and if the ball rolls farther, I know it was the push."

Key idea to say plainly: **A fair test changes one thing and keeps the
rest the same. Then the result tells you about the thing you changed.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner says whether the test is fair or not,
and names the problem if there is one.

1. "Which surface is slipperier? I slide the same block on wood and on
   carpet, starting the block the same way." *(fair — only the surface changes)*
2. "Which toy is faster? I roll a marble down a tall ramp and a toy truck
   down a short ramp." *(unfair — ramp height AND toy change)*
3. "Which container holds more? I pour the same cup of water into a tall
   glass and a short wide cup." *(fair — only the container changes)*
4. "Which paper towel absorbs more? I pour one spoon of water on towel A and
   three spoons on towel B." *(unfair — the amount of water changes too)*
5. "Do plants need light? I put one plant by the window and one in the
   closet, and I water both the same." *(fair — only the light changes)*
6. "Which ramp is faster? I roll the same car from the same height twice."
   *(fair — but also: this test changes nothing; it just checks the result
   repeats. Ask: "What would that tell us?" — that the measurement is
   trustworthy, i.e., a repeated trial.)*

For each unfair test, have the learner say how to fix it: "Change only one
thing — keep the ___ the same."

## Independent practice

The learner picks one question from these and says the **one change** and
**two things to keep the same** (adult scribes):

- "Which ball bounces higher — the rubber ball or the tennis ball?"
- "Does a taller ramp make the car roll farther?"
- "Which crayon color shows up best on brown paper?"

## Application

Start the unit's "Question Board" page in the journal. The learner dictates
two wonderings: one testable ("Does … ?") and one not testable ("Why is …
my favorite?"). The adult scribes both. Star the testable one: that is this
unit's kind of question.

## Exit check

Adult describes one fair and one unfair test from guided practice #2 and
#3. The learner points or says which was fair and names what changed in the
unfair one.

**Table variant:** The learner sorts 4 index cards with pictures (car on two
ramps of same height / different heights) into "fair" and "not fair" piles.

**Active variant:** The learner acts out an unfair test with the toy car
(helper changes two things) and then fixes it while narrating.

**Reading/language support:** Vocabulary cards with objects: *fair* (a toy
balance), *change* (a flipped card), *same* (two matching blocks). Pair
every vocabulary word with the object first, the word second.

**Extension:** The learner designs their own fair-test question about
something in the room, naming the one change and three things kept the same.

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners often believe "more changes = a better test" ("let's change the
car AND the ramp so it's more fun"). Confront it directly with Worked
example 2's fix: ask, "If the car went farther, was it the car or the ramp?
We can't tell — so we learned nothing." The "we can't tell" line is the
core insight; repeat it across the unit.
