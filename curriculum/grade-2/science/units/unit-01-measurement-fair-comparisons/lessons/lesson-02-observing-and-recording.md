# Lesson 02 — Observing Carefully and Recording What You See

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
hand lens, one interesting object per person (pinecone, leaf, shell, or
feather — handled gently), flashlight

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how to observe like a
scientist — looking closely at details — and how to record what you observe
so someone else could picture it.

**Readiness check:** Adult holds up the object. "Tell me three things you
notice about this." Any three observations show readiness. If the learner
struggles, model one observation with each sense: "I *see* bumps. I *feel*
roughness. I *smell* nothing strong."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

An **observation** is something you notice with your senses. Scientists
don't rush past the details — they slow down. A hand lens (magnifier) helps
your eyes see details that are too small to notice, like the ridges on a
pinecone or the tiny hairs on a leaf.

And scientists **record** — they write, draw, and count what they observe.
Why? Because memory fades, but a record lets you (and other people) see the
same evidence later. A good record has three parts: **words** (what I
noticed), a **labeled drawing** (a picture with name tags), and **tallies**
or counts.

**Worked example 1 — modeled observation, out loud:**

> Adult picks up a pinecone and narrates slowly while the learner watches:
> "I observe with my eyes first: the pinecone is brown and oval, with flat
> scales that overlap like shingles. Now my hand lens: I see tiny light-brown
> tips on each scale, and dust between the scales. With my fingers — gently —
> the scales feel hard and prickly. I hear nothing when I hold it still.
> That is five observations. Now I record: I draw the pinecone, and I label
> 'scales,' 'tips,' and 'dust.'"

Adult draws in the journal while narrating each label, using a leader line
from label to part.

**Worked example 2 — a weak record made strong:**

> Adult shows a quick scribble of a leaf with no labels: "This was my
> record yesterday. Can you tell which part I meant was the vein? Neither
> can I. So I redraw it — same leaf — and this time I add labels: 'pointed
> tip,' 'smooth edge,' 'center vein,' 'stem.' Now someone else could look at
> my drawing and know exactly what I observed. Labels are what turn a
> picture into evidence."

Key idea to say plainly: **Slow down, use your senses and tools, and
record with words, labeled drawings, and counts — that's how observations
become evidence.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner observes)

1. Learner observes the pinecone (or chosen object) with eyes only for 30
   seconds, then reports **two observations** aloud.
2. Learner uses the hand lens and reports **one new detail** the lens revealed.
3. Learner adds **one touch observation** (gentle) and **one smell
   observation** (no mouth contact — safety rule: we never taste or put
   science objects near the mouth).
4. Adult scribes the learner's best three observations as a labeled drawing:
   the learner points to each part while the adult writes the label; then
   the learner copies or traces one label themselves.
5. Count practice: the learner counts the pinecone's visible scales (or the
   leaf's veins) and records the count as a **tally**: "|||| ||| = 8."
   Confirm: "What does the tally tell a reader that the drawing doesn't?
   — how many."

## Independent practice

The learner chooses a *different* object, observes it for two minutes with
the hand lens, and makes their own record: a labeled drawing with at least
**three labels** (adult may scribe labels the learner dictates) and a count
of one feature (e.g., ridges, spots, bumps) written as a tally.

## Application

Science journal entry: the learner draws the object **at its real size next
to their hand** and writes (or dictates) one sentence: "I observed ___."
This size-reference drawing is reused in Unit 02's material collection.

## Exit check

Adult covers the object. The learner points to one label on their drawing
and says what they observed there. Then: "Name one thing the hand lens
showed you that your eyes alone didn't."

**Table variant:** The learner records on a preprinted observation form
(box for drawing, lines for labels, a tally row) instead of a blank page.

**Active variant:** "Observation scavenger": the learner finds something in
the room that is *smooth*, *rough*, and *bumpy*, touches each, and reports
one observation per item — then records all three on one labeled page.

**Reading/language support:** Teach *observe* with the action first
("Watch me observe — I look closely"), then the word. Word wall card shows
a hand lens + eye. Home-language labels for parts are welcome next to
English labels.

**Extension:** The learner makes a "zoom sequence": three drawings of the
same small area — with eyes, with the hand lens, and an imagined closer
view — labeling one new detail in each.

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners often record what they *think* should be there ("the leaf is
green" for a brown autumn leaf) instead of what they see. When it happens,
point at the object and ask, "Are your eyes telling you green, or are you
remembering green?" Teach: **record what your senses find, not what you
expect.** This is also the unit's first honesty-in-data lesson — return to
it in Week 6's re-measure challenge.
