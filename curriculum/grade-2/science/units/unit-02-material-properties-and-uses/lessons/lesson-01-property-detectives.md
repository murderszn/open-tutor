# Lesson 01 — Property Detectives

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons, a
"material collection" tray: 8–10 everyday objects made of different
materials (e.g., wooden block, metal spoon, cotton ball, plastic cup, piece
of sandpaper, rubber band, rock, sponge, paper straw, aluminum-foil square)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn what a *property* is —
something you can observe about a material — and sort objects by their
properties the way a scientist sorts evidence.

**Readiness check:** Adult holds up two objects (e.g., the cotton ball and
the rock) and asks, "What is different about these two?" Any observed
difference (soft/hard, light/heavy, white/gray) shows readiness — the
learner already notices properties.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Everything is made of some **material** — the stuff an object is made of.
This spoon is made of *metal*. That ball is made of *cotton*. A
**property** is something you can observe about a material: what it looks
like, how it feels, what it does when you push on it.

Scientists sort by properties. Four properties we will use all unit:

- **Color** — what color is it? (You can see this one.)
- **Texture** — how does it feel? Smooth, rough, bumpy, soft, sticky?
- **Hardness** — is it hard to scratch or dent, or easy? (Test with a
  fingernail, gently — never with teeth or tools.)
- **Flexibility** — does it bend easily without breaking, or does it stay
  stiff?

**Worked example 1 — sorting by one rule, out loud:**

> Adult (places the wooden block, rock, metal spoon, and rubber band in a
> row): "My rule is *flexibility*: bends easily, or does not bend. Watch.
> The wooden block — I push the ends — it does not bend. Not-bends pile.
> The rock — does not bend. Not-bends pile. The metal spoon — a tiny bit,
> but basically no. Not-bends pile. The rubber band — stretches and bends!
> Bends pile. I used ONE rule for every object. If I switched rules halfway
> — color for two objects, bendiness for the rest — my sort would be a
> mess, and nobody could check my work."

**Worked example 2 — the same objects, a different rule:**

> Adult (same four objects): "New rule: *texture* — rough or smooth. The
> wooden block: a little rough. Rough pile. The rock: rough. Rough pile.
> The metal spoon: smooth! Smooth pile. The rubber band: smooth. Smooth
> pile. Same objects, different rule, different piles. That is allowed —
> as long as you say your rule first and use it for every object."

Key idea to say plainly: **A property is something you can observe about a
material. When you sort, say your rule first and use the same rule for
every object.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each; the learner points or says the answer.

1. "I sorted these by *color*: red things here, blue things there. Is color
   a property?" *(Yes — you can observe it.)*
2. "I sorted by *my favorite*: I like this one, I don't like that one. Is
   'favorite' a property?" *(No — it is an opinion, not something everyone
   can observe.)*
3. Adult holds the sponge and the foil square: "Which one is more
   flexible?" *(The sponge bends easily; the foil bends but then stays
   bent — talk through what "bends without breaking" means.)*
4. Adult holds the rock and the cotton ball: "Which is harder?" *(The rock;
   hardness is about scratching or denting.)*
5. "I sorted 5 objects by texture, then for the last one I switched to
   color because it was prettier. Good sorting?" *(No — one rule, every
   object.)*
6. "Name one property of your shirt." *(Accept: color, soft texture,
   flexible fabric — any observable property.)*

## Independent practice

The learner chooses ONE property (color, texture, hardness, or
flexibility), states the rule, and sorts all 8–10 tray objects into two
piles. The adult scribes the rule in the journal: "I sorted by ___."
Learner draws or dictates one object from each pile with its property
("The spoon is smooth and hard").

## Application

Property detective walk: the learner picks 3 objects in the room, names the
material each is made of (wood, metal, fabric, plastic, paper, glass) and
one property of each. Adult scribes; the learner points to or touches the
property as they say it.

## Exit check

Adult mixes the two piles back together and asks the learner to re-sort 4
objects by a *different* property, naming the new rule first. Correct rule
statement + consistent sorting = ready to move on.

**Table variant:** The learner places picture cards of the tray objects
into two labeled columns ("bends" / "does not bend") instead of handling
objects.

**Active variant:** "Property statues" — the adult calls a property ("Show
me something ROUGH!"), and the learner runs to touch a rough object in the
room and freezes like a statue beside it.

**Reading/language support:** Vocabulary cards with objects: *property* (a
magnifying glass over a block), *texture* (sandpaper square), *hardness*
(an unscratchable rock), *flexibility* (a rubber band). Object first, word
second; home-language labels welcome beside the English word.

**Extension:** The learner invents a new sorting rule scientists could use
(e.g., "shiny or not shiny," "see-through or not") and sorts the tray by
it, then argues whether it is a property everyone could observe or an
opinion.

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners often sort by *what the object is for* ("toys here, kitchen things
there") instead of by an observable property. Name it directly: "You sorted
by *job*, not by *property*. A scientist sorting by properties would put
the wooden spoon with the wooden block — same material — even though one
is for cooking." Revisit in Lesson 04, where jobs finally matter.
