# Lesson 05 — Fair Tests for a Purpose

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
the Lesson 03 data table (for reference), two candidate materials for a
new job (adult's choice, e.g., foil vs. paper for wrapping a sandwich, or
sponge vs. rag for wiping)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will put the whole unit together:
**plan a fair test that compares two materials for one job**, decide what
evidence would pick the winner, and read data like a scientist.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What three things stayed the same in our
absorbency test?" (Same-size pieces, same spoon of water, same wait time.)
Naming two shows readiness — the fair-test habit is transferring.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Choosing the best material for a job takes three steps:

1. **Name the job and the property that matters.** (Lesson 04)
2. **Test both materials the SAME way** — one change (the material),
   everything else the same. (Lessons 02–03, and Unit 01)
3. **Read the data and make a claim with evidence.** (Unit 01's graphs and
   claims, now with materials)

**Worked example 1 — planning a fair purpose test, out loud:**

> Adult: "New job: a wrapper that keeps a sandwich fresh — the job is
> 'keep air and water out.' Property that matters: low absorbency, and it
> should not tear easily. Two materials: aluminum foil and paper.
> Now the fair test plan. The ONE change: the material — foil vs. paper.
> What stays the same? Same-size squares. Same spoon of water dripped on
> each. Same wait — count to 30. Same check — lift and look underneath:
> is it dry or wet? I write the plan BEFORE I test, so I don't change the
> rules halfway."

**Worked example 2 — reading data and claiming:**

> Adult (shows a small table: Foil — Trial 1: dry underneath; Trial 2: dry
> underneath; Trial 3: dry underneath. Paper — Trial 1: wet underneath;
> Trial 2: wet underneath; Trial 3: wet underneath): "Three trials each —
> that's repeated trials, like Unit 01. Foil: dry, dry, dry. Paper: wet,
> wet, wet. The pattern is clear. My claim: foil is the better sandwich
> wrapper, because in all three trials the water stayed on top and the
> underside stayed dry, while the paper let water through every time. My
> evidence is the table — six trials, same result each time. If one trial
> had disagreed, I would say so honestly and run it again."

Key idea to say plainly: **Plan the fair test first. Test the same way
every time. Then let the data — not your favorite — pick the winner.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Job: a bib that keeps a shirt dry. Two materials: plastic and felt.
   What is the ONE change?" *(The material.)*
2. "Name two things that must stay the same." *(Same-size pieces; same
   spoon of water; same wait; same dryness check — any two.)*
3. "How many trials should we run, and why more than one?" *(Three, like
   our other tests — one try could be a fluke; repeated trials show a
   pattern.)*
4. Adult shows a new mini-table (Sponge: soaked 3/3 trials; Rag: soaked
   2/3 trials, one trial the water ran off the edge): "Which material
   wins the wipe-up job?" *(The sponge — 3/3 beats 2/3.)*
5. "What should we do about the rag's odd trial?" *(Say it honestly; run
   that trial again. Scientists don't hide odd results.)*
6. "Your friend says 'I just like the sponge better, so it wins.' What do
   you tell them?" *(The data picks the winner, not favorites — point to
   the table.)*

## Independent practice

The learner picks a job from Lesson 04's job cards, chooses two materials
from the samples, and dictates a 4-line test plan (job / one change /
stays the same / how I'll check). Adult scribes; the learner reads it back
and corrects any unfair part themselves.

## Application

Data detective: the adult "accidentally" shows a flawed plan ("I'll test
a big towel against a tiny plastic square with different amounts of
water") and the learner plays teacher — finding every unfair part and
fixing it. Role reversal cements the fair-test habit.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "In one sentence, how does a scientist choose the best
material for a job?" (Accept any version of: test both the same way,
look at the data, pick the winner with evidence.)

**Table variant:** The learner arranges pre-written plan cards (JOB,
CHANGE, SAME, CHECK) into a plan strip for the sandwich-wrapper test.

**Active variant:** "Court of materials" — two learners (or learner +
adult) each argue for one material using the data table as their only
allowed evidence; the "judge" (adult) rules for the better evidence, not
the louder arguer.

**Reading/language support:** Plan strip with icons: 🎯 job, 🔄 one change,
🔒 stays the same, 👀 how to check. Sentence frame for claims: "___
is better for ___ because in my tests ___."

**Extension:** The learner adds a *second* property to the decision — e.g.,
the sandwich wrapper must also not tear: run the bend/tear test on foil
vs. paper and decide whether the winner changes when the job has two
requirements.

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners often "decide" the winner before testing and then test
carelessly, or change the procedure mid-way to favor their pick. The plan-
before-testing ritual is the antidote: once the plan is written, it is a
promise. If a learner's data contradicts their prediction, celebrate it
out loud — "Your test just taught you something your guess didn't know!"
— because evidence-over-opinion is the unit's deepest lesson and the
engine of 2-PS1-2.
