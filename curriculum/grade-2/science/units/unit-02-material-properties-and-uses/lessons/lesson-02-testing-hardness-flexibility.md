# Lesson 02 — Testing Hardness and Flexibility

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons, 4
test materials (e.g., a wooden craft stick, a piece of chalk, a rubber
band, a strip of cardboard), a hand lens, a simple data table (adult draws
it or uses [practice.md](../practice.md) task 3's table)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn to *test* two
properties — hardness and flexibility — by doing the **same test** on every
material and writing down what happens.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Show me with your hands: what does
*flexible* mean?" Bending hands show readiness. If the learner is unsure,
hand them the rubber band to stretch.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Yesterday we *observed* properties. Today we *test* them. A test is a
small, careful try — and here is the rule from Unit 01 that comes back:
**test every material the same way**, or the comparison is unfair.

**The hardness test (scratch test):** Drag one fingernail gently across the
material ONE time. Ask: *Did it leave a mark or a scratch?* No mark = hard.
A mark = softer. (Safety: fingernails only — never teeth, never sharp
tools; materials stay away from the mouth.)

**The flexibility test (bend test):** Hold the material at both ends and
bend it gently. Ask: *Did it bend without breaking?* Bends = flexible.
Stays stiff or snaps = not flexible.

Record every result in a table — scientists write down what happened so
they can compare later.

**Worked example 1 — testing the wooden craft stick, out loud:**

> Adult: "Hardness test first. One gentle fingernail drag… no mark. I
> write: *wood — hardness: no mark, hard.* Now flexibility: I hold both
> ends and bend gently… it bends a little, then — stop, I don't want to
> snap it. It bends a little but not easily. I write: *wood — flexibility:
> bends a little, not very flexible.* I will do EXACTLY the same two tests
> on the next material."

**Worked example 2 — testing the chalk, and a fix:**

> Adult: "Chalk. Hardness: one fingernail drag… it left a white mark, and
> a little dust. Softer than wood. Flexibility: I bend it… SNAP! It broke.
> Oh no — I bent too hard. That's a mistake, and scientists fix mistakes:
> I will test a *new* piece of chalk and bend *gently* this time. Gentle
> bend… it still snaps, even gently. So: chalk is soft and not flexible.
> The broken piece goes in the trash — the result still counts, because
> the test was the same."

Key idea to say plainly: **Test every material the same way, and write
down what happens. If you change how you test, the comparison is unfair.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult and learner test the rubber band and cardboard strip together; the
learner predicts first, then tests.

1. "Before we test: will the rubber band leave a scratch mark?" *(Predict,
   then test: no mark — the nail slides off; it is hard to scratch, even
   though it is soft to squeeze. Name the surprise: hardness and
   softness-to-squeeze are different.)*
2. "Flexibility test on the rubber band?" *(Bends easily — very flexible.)*
3. "Predict the cardboard strip: scratch mark?" *(Test: the nail may dent
   it slightly — softer than wood, harder than chalk.)*
4. "Flexibility of the cardboard?" *(Bends, but creases and stays bent —
   flexible, but different from the rubber band, which springs back. Note
   this in the table.)*
5. "I tested the chalk with one gentle drag but the wood with ten hard
   scratches. Fair?" *(No — same test, every material. This is Unit 01's
   fair-test rule wearing a new hat.)*
6. "Why write results in a table instead of remembering?" *(Memory fades;
   the table lets us compare all four materials side by side tomorrow.)*

## Independent practice

The learner tests one new material (adult's choice: sponge, foil square,
or plastic lid) with both tests, exactly as modeled, and records the two
results in the journal table (adult scribes dictated words; learner draws
a check or cross).

## Application

"Material report": the learner picks their favorite material from today's
four and dictates a two-sentence report: "The ___ is ___. It ___ when I
tested it." Adult scribes; the learner reads it back.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "You want to know which is harder — a rock or a piece of
bread. How will you test them fairly?" Learner must name the same test
for both (fingernail drag, once, gently) — the procedure, not just the
answer.

**Table variant:** The learner sorts pre-filled result cards ("left a
mark" / "no mark"; "bent" / "did not bend") into the right material rows
of a table.

**Active variant:** "Test-and-freeze" — the learner acts out the scratch
and bend tests on giant imaginary materials while the adult calls
"harder!" or "softer!" and the learner freezes in a pose showing the
result.

**Reading/language support:** Keep the two test names on word cards with
gestures: *scratch test* (dragging finger in the air), *bend test* (hands
bending). Sentence frame: "The ___ is ___ because when I ___, it ___."

**Extension:** The learner designs a *third* test for a new property —
e.g., a "stretch test" (pull both ends: does it get longer?) — and runs it
the same way on two materials.

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners conflate "hard" with "strong" and "soft" with "weak": a rubber
band is soft to squeeze but resists scratching; chalk is hard to squeeze
but crumbles. Teach the precise test language — "hard to *scratch*"
vs. "easy to *bend*" — and accept "it depends on the test" as a
sophisticated answer. Also watch for testing harder on disliked materials;
the "same test" rule is the unit's fairness spine.
