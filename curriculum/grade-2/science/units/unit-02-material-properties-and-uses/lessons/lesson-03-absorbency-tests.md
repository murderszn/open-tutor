# Lesson 03 — Absorbency Tests

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
4 test materials cut to the same size (paper towel square, plastic-bag
square, aluminum-foil square, felt square — adult pre-cuts), a spoon, a
cup of water, a tray, a timer or slow count to 30, paper towels for
cleanup

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will test which materials
*soak up* water — that's called **absorbency** — and you will keep the test
fair so you can believe the results.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "If you spill water, do you grab a towel
or a plastic bag to wipe it up? Why?" Choosing the towel for its
soak-up-ability shows readiness — the learner already uses absorbency
knowledge.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

**Absorbency** is how much water a material soaks up. Some materials drink
water in; some let it slide right off. To find out which is which, we test
— and we test *fairly*: same-size pieces, the **same spoon of water** on
each, the **same wait time**, and we watch the **same way**.

The procedure (adult demonstrates on the paper towel first):

1. Lay the material flat on the tray.
2. Pour **one spoon of water** right in the middle.
3. Count slowly to 30 together (the wait time — same for all).
4. Lift the material and look: Is the tray underneath wet? Is the material
   soggy? Where did the water go?

**Worked example 1 — the paper towel, modeled out loud:**

> Adult: "One spoon of water in the middle… now we count to 30 — same
> count for every material. Time! I lift the towel… the water is GONE from
> the top. The towel is heavy and drippy. The tray underneath is almost
> dry. My observation: the paper towel *soaked up* the water. High
> absorbency. I write it in the table."

**Worked example 2 — the plastic square, and a prediction check:**

> Adult: "Before I test: what do you predict — will the plastic soak up
> water like the towel did? … You predicted no — let's see. One spoon,
> count to 30, lift… look! The water is sitting in a puddle ON TOP of the
> plastic. The tray underneath is dry, and the plastic is not soggy at
> all. The water did not go IN — it stayed ON. Low absorbency. Your
> prediction was right — and even when predictions are wrong, we keep
> them, because the test decides, not our guess."

Key idea to say plainly: **Same size, same spoon of water, same wait
time, same way of looking. Then the difference in the results must be
because of the material — that's a fair test.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

The learner predicts, then the adult (or learner, supervised) runs the
test on the foil and felt.

1. "Predict: will the foil soak up water?" *(Test: water beads and sits on
   top, like plastic — low absorbency.)*
2. "Predict the felt." *(Test: soaks it up, like the towel — high
   absorbency.)*
3. "I poured one spoon on the towel but three spoons on the felt. Can I
   compare them?" *(No — same spoon, every material. Unit 01's rule again.)*
4. "I waited 30 counts for the towel but only 5 for the plastic. Fair?"
   *(No — same wait time.)*
5. "The towel square is big and the plastic square is tiny. Problem?"
   *(Yes — same size pieces; more material could soak more water just by
   being bigger.)*
6. "Look at our table: which two materials soaked up water, and which two
   did not?" *(Towel and felt soaked; plastic and foil did not. Name the
   pattern: materials made for wiping — towels, felt — absorb; materials
   made to keep water out — plastic, foil — do not.)*

## Independent practice

The learner chooses a fifth material from the house (adult-approved: e.g.,
a cotton ball, a napkin, a wax-paper square) and runs the same 4-step
test, dictating observations for the adult to scribe, then places it in
the table as "soaks" or "does not soak."

## Application

Spill-rescue plan: "A cup of water tipped over on the table. From our
table, which TWO materials would you grab to clean it up, and why?" The
learner points to the table and explains using the results.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Tomorrow your friend wants to test which material soaks up
the MOST water. What three things must stay the same?" (Same-size pieces,
same spoon of water, same wait time — any two of three with explanation
counts as ready.)

**Table variant:** The learner matches picture cards of each test step (1
spoon, count to 30, lift and look) in order, then points to the "soaks" /
"does not soak" column for each material.

**Active variant:** "Be the water drop" — the learner is a water drop;
the adult holds up each material, and the learner acts out "soak IN"
(melting into a crouch behind the material) or "sit ON TOP" (perching on
tiptoes on it).

**Reading/language support:** *Absorbency* is a long word — teach it as
"soak-up-ability" first, then attach the science word: "Scientists call
soak-up-ability *absorbency*." Sentence frame: "The ___ soaked up the
water, so it has high absorbency."

**Extension:** The learner re-tests the winner with *two* spoons of water
to see if it can soak up more — introducing the idea that absorbency has a
limit (the towel gets full), previewing "how much" questions for later
grades.

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners often think "the water disappeared" means it vanished. Name where
it went: "It is *inside* the towel now — the towel got heavier. Water
doesn't disappear; it moves into the material." Weigh the dry vs. wet
towel in your hand to make this concrete. Also watch for "bigger piece =
more absorbent" confusion — that is exactly why the same-size rule exists;
name it when a learner notices the towel square was the biggest.
