# Lesson 04 — Which Property for Which Job

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
the generated illustration
[rain-cover-comparison.png](../assets/rain-cover-comparison.png), 4 "job
cards" (adult draws or writes: RAINCOAT, TOWEL, BRIDGE, CUSHION), material
samples from Lessons 01–03

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn the most important
idea in this unit: **different properties are good for different jobs**.
You will decide which property matters for each job — and use a picture to
prove it.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Would you rather wear a raincoat made of
paper towel, or dry your face with a plastic bag?" Laughing at both wrong
answers shows readiness — the learner already feels that jobs need the
right material.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A property is what a material is *like*. A **purpose** is the *job* the
material needs to do. The big rule: **match the property to the job.**

- A **raincoat's** job is to keep water OUT. The property that matters is
  **low absorbency** — water slides off instead of soaking in.
- A **towel's** job is to soak water UP. The property that matters is
  **high absorbency** — the opposite!
- A **bridge's** job is to hold heavy things up without bending. The
  property that matters is **hardness and stiffness**.
- A **cushion's** job is to be soft to sit on. The property that matters
  is **softness and flexibility**.

Same word — *absorbency* — but the raincoat wants LOW and the towel wants
HIGH. The job decides which property wins.

**Worked example 1 — the raincoat, modeled out loud:**

> Adult: "Job: raincoat. What must it do? Keep the rain off me. Which
> property decides that? Absorbency — but which *amount*? If the raincoat
> soaks up water like our paper towel did, I get wet AND heavy. So the
> raincoat needs LOW absorbency — water stays on top and rolls off, like
> our plastic square. My claim: plastic is a better raincoat material than
> paper towel, because its absorbency is low. The property matches the
> job."

Key idea to say plainly: **The job decides which property matters. The
best material is the one whose property matches the job.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult holds up each job card; the learner names the property that matters
and picks the better material from the samples.

1. "Job: TOWEL. Which property matters?" *(High absorbency — paper towel
   or felt beats plastic.)*
2. "Job: BRIDGE for toy cars. Which property matters?" *(Hardness/stiffness
   — wood or metal beats rubber band.)*
3. "Job: CUSHION. Which property matters?" *(Softness/flexibility —
   sponge or cotton beats wood.)*
4. "Job: UMBRELLA. Which property matters — and is it the same as the
   raincoat?" *(Low absorbency, same as the raincoat — water must stay out.)*
5. "A boat's hull — the bottom of the boat. Should it soak up water or keep
   water out?" *(Keep water out — low absorbency, like plastic or foil.
   This previews the project.)*
6. "Could one material be the BEST for every job?" *(No — that is the
   whole point. Plastic wins raincoats; towel wins wipe-ups. There is no
   champion material, only the right material for the job.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws two columns in the journal — "Job" and "Property that
matters" — and fills 3 rows of their own choosing (adult scribes words;
learner may draw the objects). Example rows: "soup spoon → hardness,"
"winter coat → keeps warmth in," "shoelace → flexibility."

## Application

Wrong-material comedy: the learner picks a job card and deliberately
chooses the WORST material from the samples, then explains to the adult
why it fails ("A bridge made of rubber bands would wobble and drop the
cars because it is too flexible"). Laughter is encouraged; the explanation
is the learning.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Your little brother wants a bib that keeps his shirt dry at
lunch. Which property matters, and which of our materials would you pick?"
(Low absorbency; plastic or foil — with the reason "water stays on top"
stated.)

**Table variant:** The learner matches job picture cards to property word
cards (LOW ABSORBENCY, HIGH ABSORBENCY, HARD, FLEXIBLE) in a two-column
table instead of answering aloud.

**Active variant:** "Job interview" — the learner pretends to be a material
("I am Plastic!") and the adult interviews them for a job ("Why should I
hire you as a raincoat?"), with the learner answering in property
language.

**Reading/language support:** The sentence frame of the unit: "The ___
is ___, so it is good for ___." Keep the four job cards with pictures
(raincoat, towel, bridge, cushion) on the word wall all week.

**Extension:** The learner finds a real object at home whose material is a
*compromise* — good at one property, bad at another (e.g., a glass cup:
hard and see-through, but breakable) — and explains the trade-off.

## Teacher note — likely misconception

Learners often pick a *favorite* material ("I like plastic best") and then
justify every job with it, or assume expensive = better. Confront it with
the image: "Plastic wins the rain cover. But would you dry your hands
with plastic? The job changed, so the winner changed." Keep a running
"winners board" where each job crowns a different material — visible proof
that no material wins everything.
