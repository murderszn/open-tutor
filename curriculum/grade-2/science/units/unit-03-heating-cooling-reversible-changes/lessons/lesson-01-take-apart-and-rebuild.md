# Lesson 01 — Take Apart and Rebuild

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
a small building-brick model the adult built earlier (6–10 bricks), a
take-apart toy or a set of nesting cups, a piece-count card

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will take apart an object made
of a small set of pieces and rebuild the *same* pieces into something
new. Then you will tell what changed and what stayed the same.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What is something at home that is made
of pieces?" Any answer (blocks, a puzzle, a toy car) shows readiness. If
the learner has none, model one: "My bookshelf is made of boards and
screws — those are its pieces."

**Safety note:** Building bricks only. No small pieces for a learner who
still mouths objects; the adult checks piece size first. Count pieces
together at the start so none go missing.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Lots of things are made of a small set of **pieces**. A toy tower made of
blocks. A puzzle made of puzzle pieces. A simple toy car made of a body,
four wheels, and two axles. The pieces are the parts. The object is what
the pieces make when they are put together.

Here is the big idea of this lesson: **the same pieces can make more than
one object.** If you take an object apart and rebuild its pieces into
something new, the pieces stay the same — but the object changes.

Adult shows the pre-built brick model (say, a small house: 8 bricks) and
counts the pieces aloud with the learner: "One, two, three… eight pieces."

**Worked example 1 — modeled out loud, slowly:**

> Adult: "My model is a house made of 8 bricks. Watch me take it apart.
> Now I have 8 loose bricks — no house, just pieces. Now I rebuild the
> *same 8 bricks* into a bridge. What changed? The shape changed, and
> what it is changed — house to bridge. What stayed the same? The pieces:
> still 8 bricks, same colors, same sizes. Nothing was added, and
> nothing was taken away."

Adult draws the house and the bridge in the journal, side by side, and
labels each drawing "8 bricks."

**Worked example 2 — a second object, with a counting check:**

> Adult: "Here is a toy car. Its pieces are: 1 body, 4 wheels, 2 axles.
> That is 7 pieces. I take it apart — body here, wheels here, axles
> here. I rebuild the same 7 pieces into a little cart: body flat, wheels
> on the sides, axles holding them. What changed? It is a cart now, not
> a car, and it looks different. What stayed the same? 7 pieces — the
> same body, the same 4 wheels, the same 2 axles. If I counted 6 pieces
> after rebuilding, I would know one piece was missing. Counting is how
> I check."

Key idea to say plainly: **Take apart, rebuild, count. Same pieces, new
object. Say what changed and what stayed the same.**

**Original reading passage (adult reads aloud; learner retells):**

> "Mira builds a tower from ten blocks. She takes the tower apart, one
> block at a time, and counts: ten blocks. Then she builds a wall from
> the same ten blocks. The tower is gone. The wall is new. But the blocks
> are the same ten blocks. Mira says, 'What changed is the shape. What
> stayed the same is the blocks.'"

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
drawing. Adult notes are in parentheses.

1. Adult builds a 6-brick car, takes it apart, rebuilds the 6 bricks as
   a boat. "What changed? What stayed the same?" *(Changed: shape and
   what it is — car to boat. Stayed the same: 6 bricks, same colors.)*
2. "I took apart a puzzle with 12 pieces and rebuilt it, but now I count
   11 pieces. What might have happened?" *(One piece is missing — or
   still in the box. Count again to check.)*
3. Learner names the pieces of a nesting-cup stack: "How many cups? What
   else could the same cups make?" *(Any new arrangement counts, as
   long as the cup count stays the same.)*
4. "A tower has 5 red blocks and 3 blue blocks. After rebuilding, it has
   5 red blocks and 3 blue blocks. Did any piece change color?" *(No —
   the same pieces kept their colors; only the arrangement changed.)*
5. Error-analysis: "Sam says, 'I rebuilt my 9 bricks into a castle, and
   now I have 10 bricks!' What is wrong with Sam's count?" *(He added a
   brick, or counted one twice. Same pieces means the same count.)*

## Independent practice

The learner takes apart the adult's second model (or their own small
model), counts the pieces, rebuilds them into something new, counts
again, and draws both objects with labels: "Before: ___ pieces. After:
___ pieces. Changed: ___. Stayed the same: ___." The adult scribes
dictated words.

## Application

Start the unit's "Pieces Journal" page. The learner finds one object at
home made of a small set of pieces, draws it, and lists its pieces with
counts. This page will be reused in the project poster.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "If you rebuild the same pieces into something new, what
stays the same?" Acceptable: "The pieces" / "The number of pieces" /
"The same blocks." Then: "What changes?" Acceptable: "The shape" / "What
it is."

**Table variant:** The learner sorts 4 picture cards (house/bridge from
the same bricks; tower/wall from the same blocks) into "same pieces"
pairs.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Pre-counted piece bags (6 bricks in a bag) remove counting
  load; the adult scribes all labels.
- **Language:** Point to the pieces while saying "same pieces, new
  object" — the sentence frame stays on the wall all unit.
- **Extension:** Rebuild the same pieces into *two* different new
  objects and compare all three; or write one sentence: "I changed
  ___ into ___ with the same ___ pieces."

**Misconception note:** Learners often think rebuilding *changes the
pieces themselves* ("the bricks became a bridge, so they are bridge
bricks now"). Counter with the count: "Count them. Same bricks — new
arrangement." See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (Lesson 01).
