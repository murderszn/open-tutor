# Lesson 05 — Building an Argument with Evidence

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
the unit's melt table (from Lesson 02 / project), the sort cards from
Lesson 04, the sentence frame card: "My claim is ___. My evidence is
___. This shows ___."

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will build an **argument**:
a claim, plus evidence that supports it. Your argument will be: *some
changes caused by heating or cooling can be reversed, and some cannot.*

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What is one change that can go back,
and one that cannot?" Any correct pair (ice/egg, butter/leaf) shows
readiness. If the learner has none, replay the Lesson 04 sort quickly
with two cards.

**Safety note:** No materials are heated or cooled in this lesson. It
is a talking, drawing, and writing lesson built on observations already
made. If the learner wants to re-check an observation, use the journal
drawings and the melt table — not a new heating step.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

An **argument** in science is not a quarrel. It is a claim plus the
evidence that supports it:

1. **Claim** — what you say is true.
2. **Evidence** — what you observed or measured that supports the claim.
3. **Why it counts** — one sentence saying how the evidence supports
   the claim.

A strong argument for this unit needs **two** pieces of evidence: one
reversible change and one irreversible change. One example alone cannot
show "some can and some cannot" — you need one of each.

**Worked example 1 — modeled out loud, slowly (reversible half):**

> Adult: "My claim is: *some changes caused by heating or cooling can
> be reversed.* My evidence is: *ice melted into water in the sunny
> cup — 4 of 4 cubes melted in 30 minutes — and when water like that
> goes into the freezer, it becomes ice again.* This shows my claim
> because the water went solid → liquid → solid. It went back. That is
> one half of the argument."

**Worked example 2 — modeled out loud, slowly (irreversible half):**

> Adult: "My claim is also: *some changes caused by heating or cooling
> cannot be reversed.* My evidence is: *an egg cooked in a pan became
> white and firm, and cooling it did not make it raw again; and a leaf
> that froze stayed brown and limp after it thawed.* This shows my
> claim because neither change could be undone by warming or cooling.
> Put both halves together and the full argument is: *some changes can
> be reversed, and some cannot — ice and butter show the first half,
> and the cooked egg and the frozen leaf show the second half.*"

Key idea to say plainly: **Claim + evidence + why it counts. One
reversible example and one irreversible example together prove "some
can and some cannot."**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
drawing. Adult notes are in parentheses.

1. "Claim: 'Ice melting can be reversed.' Give one piece of evidence."
   *(Water from melted ice freezes into ice again in the freezer.)*
2. "Claim: 'Cooking an egg cannot be reversed.' Give one piece of
   evidence." *(The cooked egg stays white and firm when cooled; it
   never becomes raw again.)*
3. "Is this an argument or just a claim? 'Butter can go back.'"
   *(Just a claim — it has no evidence yet. Add: 'Soft butter hardened
   in the refrigerator.')*
4. "Finish this argument: 'My claim is that some changes cannot be
   reversed. My evidence is that a frozen leaf ___.'" *(Stayed brown
   and limp after it thawed; it did not become fresh and green again.)*
5. Error-analysis: "Sam's argument is: 'Ice melts, so all heating
   changes can be reversed.' What is wrong with Sam's argument?"
   *(One example cannot prove 'all.' And the cooked egg disproves it:
   one irreversible example breaks an 'all' claim. Sam needs 'some
   can, some cannot' — with one example of each.)*

## Independent practice

The learner writes or dictates a full argument using the sentence
frame, with one reversible and one irreversible example, and draws a
small picture for each piece of evidence. The adult scribes as needed
and checks that both halves are present.

## Application

The learner's argument goes on the project poster (see
[project.md](../project.md) step 6) as the poster's closing talk: the
learner presents the claim, points to the melt data, and names the
irreversible example from the adult demonstrations.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Tell me your argument in three parts: claim, evidence,
why it counts." Acceptable: any claim of the "some can, some cannot"
form with at least one reversible and one irreversible piece of
evidence and a linking sentence ("This shows… because…").

**Table variant:** The learner picks one "CAN GO BACK" card and one
"CANNOT GO BACK" card and places them under the words CLAIM and
EVIDENCE on a two-column mat.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** The sentence frame stays visible; the learner may
  answer entirely orally while the adult scribes. Evidence pictures
  may stand in for evidence sentences.
- **Language:** Teach *argument* as "claim + evidence" with the two
  hand gestures from Lesson 04 (circle for reversible, one-way arrow
  for irreversible) plus a "plus" gesture joining them.
- **Extension:** "Convince a skeptic": the learner answers, "But what
  if someone says the melted ice is gone forever?" with the freezer
  evidence. Or: write the argument as a 4-sentence paragraph instead
  of the frame.

**Misconception note:** "More examples always make a better argument."
Counter: "Two well-chosen examples — one of each kind — beat ten
examples of the same kind. What matters is that the evidence matches
the claim." See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (Lesson 05).
