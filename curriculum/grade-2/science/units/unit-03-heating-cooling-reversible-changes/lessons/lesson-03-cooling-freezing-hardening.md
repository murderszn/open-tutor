# Lesson 03 — Cooling: Freezing and Hardening

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
two small cups, water, a freezer, a refrigerator, the soft butter from
Lesson 02 (or a fresh soft butter pat), an ice-cube tray

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will watch what **cooling**
does: water freezes and soft butter hardens again. You will describe
what each thing looked like before cooling and after cooling.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Where does water go when it becomes
ice?" Any answer involving a freezer, cold, or winter shows readiness.
If the learner has none, model one: "Water in a tray in the freezer
becomes ice cubes."

**Safety note:** The adult handles the freezer and refrigerator items
and any icy trays. The learner observes, draws, and touches only items
the adult says are safe to touch (a cold cup, a firm butter pat). No
stove, no hot water, nothing sharp.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

**Cooling** means making something colder. Shade cools things. A
refrigerator cools things. A freezer cools things even more. When some
liquids and soft things get cold enough, they change:

- **Freeze** means a liquid turns into a solid. Liquid water, when
  cooled in a freezer, freezes into solid ice.
- **Harden** means something soft becomes firm again. Soft butter, when
  cooled in a refrigerator, hardens back into firm butter.

Cooling is warming in reverse — and for water and butter, the change
goes in reverse too. That round trip is the heart of this unit.

**Worked example 1 — water freezes (prepared the day before):**

> Adult: "Yesterday I poured water into this tray and put it in the
> freezer. Yesterday it was liquid: it poured, it splashed, it took the
> shape of the tray. Today I take it out. Now it is solid: it holds its
> shape, it does not pour, and I can lift out ice cubes. What changed?
> Liquid → solid. What caused it? Cooling in the freezer. And here is
> the round trip: if I leave this ice in a warm room, it will melt back
> into liquid water. Water → ice → water. Cooling froze it; warming
> would melt it again."

Adult and learner draw the round trip as three boxes with arrows:
liquid water → (cooling) → ice → (warming) → liquid water.

**Worked example 2 — soft butter hardens again (the reverse of Lesson 02):**

> Adult: "Remember the soft butter from Lesson 02? It spread easily and
> its edges drooped. I put that same dish in the refrigerator. An hour
> later, look: the butter is firm again. It holds its shape, and the
> knife cannot spread it easily — just like before it warmed. What
> changed? Soft → firm. What caused it? Cooling. So butter can go
> firm → soft → firm. That is a change that goes back. Keep that idea
> — it gets a name in the next lesson."

Key idea to say plainly: **Cooling can freeze a liquid into a solid
(water → ice) or harden something soft (soft butter → firm butter).
Water and butter can go back and forth when warmed and cooled.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
drawing. Adult notes are in parentheses.

1. "Liquid water goes into the freezer overnight. In the morning it is
   ice. What happened, and what caused it?" *(It froze; cooling caused it.)*
2. "Soft butter goes into the refrigerator. Later it is firm. What
   happened?" *(It hardened; cooling caused it.)*
3. "Ice left in a warm room becomes water. Is that warming or cooling?"
   *(Warming — melting is a warming change.)*
4. "Put these in order: liquid water, ice, liquid water again. What
   caused each step?" *(Cooling: water → ice. Warming: ice → water.)*
5. Error-analysis: "Sam says, 'Freezing makes water disappear, because
   the ice looks different.' What would you say?" *(It did not
   disappear; it is the same water, now solid. It still weighs about
   the same and it can melt back.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws the butter round trip (firm → soft → firm) with
arrows labeled "warming" and "cooling," and dictates or writes one
sentence: "Cooling made the soft butter ___ again." The adult scribes
as needed.

## Application

Project step 4 (see [project.md](../project.md)): the learner checks
the ice-melt race cups, records the melt table, and starts the
melt-and-freeze journal page — including the plan to refreeze one cup
of meltwater overnight so the class can watch water → ice → water.

## Exit check

Adult shows a cup of water and a tray of ice. "Which one was cooled?
How do you know?" Acceptable: "The ice — water freezes when it gets
cold" / "The solid one, because cooling turns water into ice."

**Table variant:** The learner points to the "after cooling" picture in
each of two before/after pairs (water/ice, soft butter/firm butter).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Three-box round-trip template with arrows pre-drawn;
  the learner draws only the contents. Sentence frame: "Cooling made
  ___ into ___."
- **Language:** Act out *freeze* (stand stiff like ice) and *harden*
  (press arms firm) before writing; home-language labels welcome.
- **Extension:** "What else freezes?" — the learner predicts and the
  adult checks one safe item (for example, juice in a small cup in the
  freezer) and compares it with water. Note: juice freezing is an
  extension observation, not a framework example, and it is labeled as
  such in the journal.

**Misconception note:** "Cold makes things" — learners sometimes think
the freezer *creates* ice from nothing. Counter: "The water was already
there. Cooling changed it, the way warming changed the ice back."
