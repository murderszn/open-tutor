# Lesson 02 — Warming: Melting and Softening

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
two clear cups, 8 same-size ice cubes, a sunny windowsill and a shady
spot, one firm butter pat and one small dish, a timer or clock

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will watch what **warming**
does: ice melts and butter softens. You will describe what each thing
looked like before warming and after warming.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What happens to an ice cube if you hold
it in your hand for a long time?" Any answer involving melting, wetness,
or getting smaller shows readiness. If the learner has none, model one:
"Ice in my hand gets wet and smaller — my warm hand melts it."

**Safety note:** The learner handles only ice, sunlight, shade, and cool
water. The adult handles any warm water in this lesson (a bowl of warm —
not hot — tap water). No stove, no kettle, no hot water for the learner.
If warm water is unavailable, sunlight alone is the whole lesson, and
that is fine.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

**Warming** means making something warmer. Sunlight warms things. A warm
room warms things. Warm water warms things. When some solids get warm
enough, they change:

- **Melt** means a solid turns into a liquid. Ice is solid water. When
  ice warms up, it melts into liquid water.
- **Soften** means something firm becomes soft and easy to spread or
  bend. Firm butter, when warmed, softens.

The change is something you can *see*: solid ice becomes liquid water;
firm butter becomes soft butter. Before and after look different — and
that difference is your observation.

**Worked example 1 — ice in sun and shade (a fair comparison, adult-led):**

> Adult: "I put 4 same-size ice cubes in a cup on the sunny windowsill
> and 4 same-size ice cubes in a cup in the shade. Same cubes, same cups
> — only the place is different. After 30 minutes we look. Sunny cup:
> all 4 cubes are gone; the cup holds liquid water. Shady cup: 3 cubes
> are still solid, and 1 is only partly melted. What warmed the sunny
> ice? The sunlight. What is my evidence? The sunny cup melted fully
> and the shady cup did not. So: warming made the ice melt faster."

Adult and learner record this in the journal as a two-row table
(sunny: 4 of 4 melted; shady: 1 of 4 melted) with a before/after drawing
of one cup.

**Worked example 2 — butter, firm to soft (adult handles warm water):**

> Adult: "Here is a firm butter pat on a dish. It holds its shape, and
> a butter knife cannot spread it easily. I set the dish in a bowl of
> warm water — I am the only one who touches the warm water — and we
> wait 5 minutes. Now look: the butter is soft. It spreads easily, and
> its edges have gone droopy. What changed? Firm to soft. What caused
> it? Warming. Did the butter disappear? No — it is still butter, just
> soft butter. That matters for later: a change that keeps the material
> the same stuff may be a change we can undo."

Key idea to say plainly: **Warming can melt a solid into a liquid
(ice → water) or soften something firm (firm butter → soft butter).
Describe before and after, and name warming as the cause.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
drawing. Adult notes are in parentheses.

1. "An ice cube sits in the sun. After 20 minutes it is a puddle. What
   happened, and what caused it?" *(It melted; warming from the sun
   caused it.)*
2. "Two cups of ice: sunny cup, 4 of 4 melted; shady cup, 1 of 4 melted.
   Which cup warmed more?" *(The sunny cup — its ice melted more.)*
3. "Firm butter sits in a warm room. An hour later it spreads easily.
   What changed?" *(Firm → soft; it softened because it warmed.)*
4. "Which is the fair test: same-size cubes in sun vs. shade, or one big
   cube in sun vs. one small cube in shade?" *(Sun vs. shade with
   same-size cubes — only the place changes. Different cube sizes would
   change two things.)*
5. Error-analysis: "Sam says, 'The ice disappeared — it is gone
   forever.' What would you say to Sam?" *(It did not disappear; it
   became liquid water in the cup. Melted ice is still water.)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws a before/after pair for one warming change they
observed today (melting ice or softening butter), labels both drawings,
and dictates or writes one sentence: "Before: ___. After: ___. Warming
caused ___." The adult scribes as needed.

## Application

Start the project ice-melt race (see [project.md](../project.md) steps
1–3): the learner helps place same-size cubes in sun and shade and
records the first observation. This is the same fair comparison as
Worked example 1, run by the learner.

## Exit check

Adult shows two cups (or pictures): melted-ice water and a solid ice
cube. "Which one was warmed? How do you know?" Acceptable: "The water —
the ice melted" / "The liquid one, because warming melts ice."

**Table variant:** The learner points to the "after warming" picture in
each of two before/after pairs (ice/water, firm butter/soft butter).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Pre-drawn before/after boxes; the learner draws only the
  change. Sentence frame: "Before ___. After ___. Warming caused ___."
- **Language:** Teach *melt* and *soften* with the objects in hand
  before any writing; home-language labels welcome.
- **Extension:** Predict, then check: "Which will melt first — one big
  cube or four small cubes in the same sun?" (Small cubes melt first:
  more surface meets the warmth. Test only if time allows; the claim
  needs its own fair test.)

**Misconception note:** "Melted means gone." Counter by pointing to the
liquid in the cup: "It is still here — it changed form." See
[teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (Lesson 02).
