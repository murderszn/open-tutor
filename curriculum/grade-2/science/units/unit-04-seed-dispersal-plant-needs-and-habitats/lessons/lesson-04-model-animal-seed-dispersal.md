# Lesson 04 — Model Animal Seed Dispersal

**Student goal:** I can build a simple model that acts like an
animal carrying seeds, and explain what each part of my model
stands for.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 7 of 16**
**Adult directions are part of the lesson.** The model uses safe
household materials only. Do not use real burrs with sharp hooks.
Wash hands after handling the model materials.

## Materials

- A piece of fleece, felt, or a fuzzy sock — the "fur"
- Small felt balls or pom-poms with a strip of hook-side Velcro
  stuck on, or Velcro dots pressed onto felt balls — the "burr
  seeds" (about 10)
- A tray or table as the "field," a second tray as the "new place"
- A tally sheet for 3 trials

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Yesterday's picture: how does a hooked seed travel? What does
the hook do?" (Expected: it catches on animal fur and rides
along until it drops off. If this is shaky, re-read the Lesson 03
middle-panel example before building.)

## Teaching

We cannot easily follow a real animal around for days to watch
seeds drop off its fur. So we build a **model**: a simple copy
that acts like the real thing in one important way. A model is
not the real thing — it is a tool for thinking.

Our model: the fleece is the animal's fur. The felt balls with
Velcro are burr seeds — the Velcro hooks act like a burr's hooks.
The tray is the field where the parent plant grows. We drag the
"fur" across the field, count how many "seeds" stick, carry the
fur to the "new place" tray, and shake it gently. The seeds that
fall off have been "dispersed" — carried to a new place, just
like real burr seeds on real fur.

What each part stands for:

| Model part | Real thing |
|---|---|
| Fleece / fuzzy sock | An animal's fur |
| Felt ball with Velcro | A burr seed with hooks |
| Dragging the fur across the tray | An animal walking past the parent plant |
| Seeds falling off at the new tray | Seeds dropping off fur in a new place |

And one honest difference: real fur is attached to a living
animal that walks, eats, sleeps, and grooms; our fleece does none
of that. The model shows only the carrying part.

**Key vocabulary:** *model*, *hitchhiker seed*, *dispersal*.
A model acts like the real thing in one important way.

## Worked example 1 — Running three trials (modeled reasoning)

> "Trial 1: I drag the fur across the field once. 6 seeds stick.
> I carry the fur to the new place and shake gently. 6 seeds fall
> off. Trial 2: 5 seeds stick, 5 fall off. Trial 3: 7 seeds stick,
> 7 fall off. Total stuck: 6 + 5 + 7 = **18 seeds** across 3
> trials. Most trials picked up 5 or more seeds. My claim: the
> model works — fur can carry hooked seeds to a new place."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** one trial could be luck. Three
trials show a pattern: seeds stick almost every time. Counting
each trial separately (not just "a lot") is what makes the claim
believable.

## Worked example 2 — Naming a model difference (modeled reasoning)

> "Is my fleece a real animal? No. A real animal moves on its
> own, and it might groom the seeds off or eat them. My fleece
> only moves when I drag it. So my model shows the *carrying*
> part well, but it does not show the *animal* part. That is
> fine — a model only has to act like the real thing in the way
> we are studying."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** every model is wrong in some ways
and useful in one way. Saying the difference out loud is part of
doing science, not a failure of the model.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. "Build your model. Point to each part and tell me what real
   thing it stands for." (Expected: fur → animal fur; Velcro
   ball → burr seed; tray → field; new tray → new place.)
2. "Run Trial 1. How many seeds stuck? How many fell off at the
   new place?" (Expected: learner counts and records both
   numbers; they should match unless a seed was lost — see key
   note.)
3. "Run Trials 2 and 3. What pattern do you see?"
   (Expected: seeds stick in most or all trials.)
4. "What would happen if the seeds had no Velcro — smooth balls
   only?" (Expected: few or none would stick; the hook is what
   makes the ride work. Test it if time allows.)
5. Error analysis: the adult drags the fur *over* the seeds
   without touching them and says, "See, no seeds traveled!"
   Ask: "Is that a fair test of the model?" (Expected: no — the
   fur must brush past the seeds the way an animal walks through
   plants; hovering is not how animals meet burrs.)
6. "Name one way your model differs from a real animal."
   (Expected: any real difference — it does not walk, eat, groom,
   or choose where to go.)

## Independent practice

The learner runs the 3-trial record on the tally sheet alone
(adult nearby), writes the total, and finishes the sentence:
"My model shows that ___." (Adult scribes if needed.)

## Application

"Think of a dog walk: seeds stick to a dog's fur and to your
socks. Where might those seeds end up? Why is that good for the
plant?" (Expected: somewhere new — a park, a garden, home — where
the seed gets its own space, sunlight, water, and soil.)

## Exit check

"What does the fleece stand for? What does the Velcro stand for?
Name one way the model is different from the real thing."
(Expected: fleece → animal fur; Velcro → burr hooks; any honest
difference.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** model-part cards the learner matches to
  real-thing cards.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may demonstrate and
  tell instead of writing the tally; the adult records numbers
  the learner counts aloud.
- **Seated-table variant:** all trays on the table.
- **Floor-play variant:** the learner walks the "fur" along a
  floor path from field to new place.

## Extension

"Change one thing in the model — use a smooth cloth instead of
fleece — and run 3 trials. Does the smooth cloth carry as many
seeds? What does that tell us about why real fur works?"
(Expected: fewer seeds stick; the fuzziness, like fur, is what
the hooks catch on.)

## Teacher note

If trial counts differ between "stuck" and "fell off" (a seed
rolls away), treat it as data, not a mistake: record both numbers
and note "1 seed lost on the way" — real dispersal loses seeds
too. Do not let the model lesson drift into "animals plant seeds
on purpose": animals do not intend to help plants; the plant
benefits from the animal's ordinary walking. Correct purpose
language gently when it appears ("the plant *uses* the ride" is
fine as shorthand; "the dog *decided* to plant it" is not).
