# Lesson 05 — Model Pollination and Good Places to Grow

**Student goal:** I can build a simple model that acts like a bee
carrying pollen between flowers, and I can judge whether a place
gives a seed a good chance to grow.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**
**Adult directions are part of the lesson.** Model "pollen" is a
dry powder (yellow cornmeal or chalk dust) that is dabbed with a
pom-pom "bee," never blown and never tasted. Wash hands after.
Check for corn or chalk-dust sensitivities; use colored paper
dots instead if needed.

## Materials

- 2 paper flowers (cupcake liners or cut paper), a yellow pom-pom
  "bee" on a craft stick or clothespin
- A small dish of yellow cornmeal or chalk dust — the "pollen"
- Paper and crayons; 2 place cards (sunny garden / dark dry closet)

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"In Lesson 03's picture, what was the bee doing at the yellow
flower? Was it carrying seeds?" (Expected: visiting the flower;
no — bees carry pollen, not seeds. If this is shaky, re-read the
Lesson 03 teacher note aloud before building.)

## Teaching

Flowers make a fine yellow powder called **pollen**. For a flower
to make seeds, pollen must move from one flower to another flower.
Flowers cannot walk, so animals help: a bee visits a flower for
nectar, pollen sticks to its fuzzy body, and when the bee visits
the next flower, some pollen rubs off there. That moving of pollen
is called **pollination**. Without pollination, many flowers would
never make seeds at all — and without seeds, there would be no
seed travelers from Lessons 03 and 04.

Our model: the pom-pom is the bee. The yellow powder is pollen.
Flower A is the first flower; Flower B is the second flower. Dab
the bee in the powder at Flower A (pollen sticks to the bee),
then dab the bee on Flower B (pollen rubs off). Pollen has moved
from one flower to another — pollination, modeled.

What each part stands for:

| Model part | Real thing |
|---|---|
| Pom-pom bee | A real bee (or other pollinating animal) |
| Yellow powder | Pollen |
| Paper flowers | Real flowers |
| Dabbing bee from A to B | A bee visiting one flower, then another |

And one honest difference: a real bee flies on its own and visits
flowers because it is looking for food; our pom-pom only moves
when we move it, and it does not eat anything.

Then the second half of today's lesson: **where a seed lands
matters**. A seed that travels far has still only won half the
battle. To grow, it needs what our bean plants needed: sunlight,
water, and soil. A place that offers those things is a good
**habitat** for a seed. A sunny garden bed with soil and water
gives a seed a good chance. A dark, dry closet gives it almost
none — no sunlight, no soil, no water. A seed on bare concrete
has sunlight but no soil or steady water, so its chance is poor.

**Key vocabulary:** *pollen*, *pollination*, *habitat*. Pollen
moves from flower to flower; a habitat is a place that offers
what a living thing needs.

## Worked example 1 — Moving model pollen (modeled reasoning)

> "Flower A has a dish of yellow powder. I dab the bee in it.
> Look — the pom-pom is dusty yellow now. That dust is our
> pollen, stuck to the bee the way real pollen sticks to a bee's
> fuzzy body. Now I dab the bee on Flower B. Look — yellow dust
> is on Flower B now. Pollen moved from A to B. That is
> pollination, in model form. The bee did not *decide* to help
> the flower; it was visiting for food, and the pollen hitched a
> ride."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the model works because the same
two steps happen as in real life: pick up, then drop off. If the
bee never touched Flower B, no pollination would happen — the
second visit is the whole point.

## Worked example 2 — Judging two places (modeled reasoning)

> "Place 1: a sunny garden bed with soil. A seed lands there.
> Sunlight? Yes. Water? Rain and watering can give it water.
> Soil? Yes. My judgment: **good chance** — the seed has what
> plants need. Place 2: a dark, dry closet floor. Sunlight? No.
> Water? No. Soil? No. My judgment: **poor chance** — the seed
> has almost nothing plants need. Notice I checked all three
> needs, not just one. A place with sun but no soil is still a
> poor place for a seed."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** judge a place by *all* the needs,
not the first one you notice. One missing need can be enough to
stop growth.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. "Build your model. Point to each part and tell me what real
   thing it stands for." (Expected: pom-pom → bee; powder →
   pollen; paper flowers → real flowers.)
2. "Move pollen from Flower A to Flower B. Show me the pollen on
   the bee, then on Flower B." (Expected: bee dusty after A;
   dust visible on B after dabbing.)
3. "What would happen if the bee visited only Flower A and then
   flew home?" (Expected: no pollination — pollen never reaches
   another flower.)
4. "Name one way your model differs from a real bee."
   (Expected: any real difference — it does not fly, eat, or
   choose flowers.)
5. "Judge this place: a sunny windowsill pot with soil, watered
   when dry. Good or poor chance? Why?" (Expected: good — sun,
   soil, and water are all present.)
6. Error analysis: the adult says, "This seed landed on a sunny
   rock, so it will grow well — it has sun!" Ask: "Is sun
   enough? What else does it need?" (Expected: no — it also
   needs water and soil; a bare rock gives neither.)

## Independent practice

The learner draws the pollination model in two steps (bee at
Flower A with pollen; bee at Flower B leaving pollen) and writes
or dictates one sentence: "Pollination is ___." Then the learner
judges two new places from place cards (for example, a pond edge
with soil and sun, and a dark basement floor) with a reason each.

## Application

"Why do gardeners plant flowers that bees like? Use pollination
in your answer." (Expected: bees visiting flowers move pollen,
which lets flowers make seeds and fruit; more pollination means
more seeds and more new plants.)

## Exit check

"What does the yellow powder stand for? What is pollination?
Name one place that gives a seed a good chance, and say why."
(Expected: powder → pollen; pollination → pollen moving from one
flower to another; any good place with sunlight, water, and soil
named.)

## Supports

- **Language access:** model-part cards matched to real-thing
  cards; place cards with sun / water / soil symbols (not color
  alone).
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may demonstrate and
  tell instead of drawing; the adult scribes.
- **Seated-table variant:** all modeling at the table.
- **Floor-play variant:** the learner "flies" the bee between
  two floor flowers.

## Extension

"Some flowers are pollinated by wind, not bees. How would you
change the model to show wind pollination? What would carry the
pollen then?" (Expected: blow gently or shake powder over the
flowers from above; the wind carries it — and much of it lands
nowhere, which is why wind-pollinated flowers make so much
pollen.)

## Teacher note

Watch for two errors. First, "the bee makes seeds": correct
gently — the bee moves pollen; the *flower* makes the seeds after
pollination. Second, purpose language: bees visit flowers for
food, not to help plants on purpose; plants benefit from the
visit. Keep the habitat judgment inside this unit's scope:
sunlight, water, and soil only. Do not turn it into Unit 05's
tally-and-compare work (counting kinds of living things in two
habitats) — that comparison belongs to Unit 05.
