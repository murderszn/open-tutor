# Lesson 05 — Why Do More Kinds Live Here?

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
the two tally charts from Lessons 02–03, the comparison poster draft

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will look for a **pattern**:
why does one habitat hold more kinds of living things than another?
You will explain the pattern with things you observed.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Which habitat held more kinds in
your counts — A or B? What were the two numbers?" (The learner's own
totals.) Then: "What four things does a habitat give?" (food, water,
shelter, space.) If the learner cannot recall their totals, open the
two charts together and read the totals aloud first.

**Safety note:** Today is a talking-and-drawing lesson. The care rule
from all unit: living things are observed, never collected; plants are
not picked.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **pattern** is something that happens again and again in the same
way. Scientists look for patterns because a pattern helps explain
*why* something happens — not just *what* happened.

Here is the pattern to look for: **a habitat that offers more food,
more water, more shelter, and more space usually holds more kinds of
living things.** And there is a second pattern inside the first one:
**plants give animals food and shelter.** A place with many kinds of
plants often holds many kinds of animals, because the plants feed them
and hide them.

But careful — a pattern is not a rule that is always true. A desert
holds few kinds, but the kinds that live there are exactly right for
the desert. "More kinds" is not "better." It is just what we observed
in these two habitats.

**Worked example 1 — the garden and the lawn, modeled out loud:**

> Adult: "Garden: 8 kinds. Lawn: 4 kinds. Why? Look at what each
> habitat offers. The garden has four kinds of plants — sunflower,
> tomato, carrot, bean. Each plant is food or shelter for something:
> the bee visits the sunflower, the ladybug hunts on the tomato
> leaves, the sparrow eats seeds, the worm lives in the damp soil.
> The lawn has two kinds of plants — grass and one dandelion. The
> robin finds worms in the grass, and the ants march across it. So
> the pattern is: more kinds of plants, more kinds of animals. The
> garden's plants give more food and more shelter, so more kinds can
> live there."

**Worked example 2 — water and shelter, with a second pattern:**

> Adult: "Now look at a pond edge. It has water all the time, reeds
> for shelter, and mud for animals to hide in. I would expect it to
> hold many kinds — more than a dry, bare patch of dirt. Why? Water is
> one of the four things. Shelter is another. A place that gives all
> four things generously usually holds more kinds than a place that
> gives only a little of one thing. That is the pattern: more of the
> four things, more kinds. Test it with your own two habitats: which
> one gave more food, water, shelter, and space? Is that the one with
> more kinds?"

Key idea to say plainly: **More food, water, shelter, and space
usually goes with more kinds. Plants give animals food and shelter.**

**Original reading passage (adult reads aloud; learner retells):**

> "Ana looks at her poster. Garden: eight kinds. Lawn: four kinds.
> 'The garden has more plants,' she says. 'The plants feed the bee.
> The plants hide the ladybug. The soil hides the worm. More plants,
> more animals.' She draws a sunflower and a bee beside her claim.
> Then she writes: 'I will not pick the flowers. The bee needs them.
> A habitat is a home — you visit it, you do not carry it away.'"

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
drawing. Adult notes are in parentheses.

1. "The garden has 4 kinds of plants and the lawn has 2 kinds of
   plants. The garden also has more kinds of animals. What pattern
   do you see?" *(More kinds of plants goes with more kinds of
   animals — the plants give food and shelter.)*
2. "A pond edge has water, reeds for shelter, and mud to hide in. A
   bare dirt patch has almost none of those. Which would you expect
   to hold more kinds?" *(The pond edge — it gives more of the four
   things.)*
3. "A desert holds few kinds of living things. Does that mean the
   desert is a bad habitat?" *(No — the kinds that live there need
   very little water, and the desert gives them what they need. "More
   kinds" is not "better.")*
4. "What do plants give animals in a habitat?" *(Food and shelter —
   for example, flowers feed bees, leaves hide ladybugs.)*
5. Error-analysis: "Sam says, 'The garden holds more kinds because
   gardens are better than lawns, always.' What is wrong?" *(He turned
   a pattern from two habitats into an always-rule. A pattern is
   "usually," not "always" — and a lawn is a habitat too.)*

## Independent practice

The learner writes or dictates one pattern sentence about their own
two habitats: "Habitat ___ holds more kinds because ___." They must
name at least one of the four things (food, water, shelter, space) or
one way plants help animals. The adult scribes dictated words.

## Application

Finish the project poster: add the pattern sentence and one drawing
showing a plant giving an animal food or shelter. The poster is ready
to share in Session 14.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Why might a habitat with many kinds of plants also hold
many kinds of animals?" Acceptable: "The plants give them food" /
"The plants give them shelter." Then: "Does more kinds mean a better
habitat?" Acceptable: "No — just more kinds" / "Every habitat gives
what its living things need."

**Table variant:** The learner sorts 4 picture cards (a flowering
plant with a bee; a bare dirt patch; a pond with reeds; a shady tree
with a nest) into "likely many kinds" and "likely few kinds" and says
why for one card.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** The four word cards (food, water, shelter, space) stay
  visible; the pattern sentence frame "___ holds more kinds because
  ___" stays on the wall; the adult scribes.
- **Language:** Teach *pattern* with a repeated example first (the
  garden's plants feeding animals), the word second; home-language
  labels welcomed.
- **Extension:** Predict the kinds in a third habitat before visiting
  it, then check the prediction; or write two sentences: the pattern
  and one way to care for a habitat (do not pick plants, do not chase
  animals, leave logs where they lie).

**Misconception note:** Learners often turn a pattern into an
always-rule ("more kinds is always better," "gardens always beat
lawns"). Counter with the desert: few kinds, but exactly right for
the living things there. A pattern explains what we saw; it does not
judge habitats. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (Lesson 05).
