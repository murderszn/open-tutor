# Lesson 02 — Counting Kinds in One Habitat

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
a hand lens, a tally-chart card (see below), a timer or clock

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will visit one habitat and
count how many **different kinds** of living things live there. You
will count kinds, not how many of each kind.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What four things does a habitat
give?" (food, water, shelter, space — Lesson 01). Then: "If I see
three dandelions and one daisy, how many *kinds* of flowers did I
see?" Two kinds is the ready answer; if the learner says "four," model
it: "Three dandelions are one kind. One daisy is a second kind. Two
kinds."

**Safety note:** Observe, never collect. Plants are not picked. Stay
on paths. Do not touch an animal you do not know. Wash hands after
outdoor work. If going outside is not possible today, use the picture
tally in the guided practice instead — the counting rules are the same.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Scientists who compare habitats do not count *how many* living things
they see. They count **how many different kinds**. That number is
called **biodiversity** — a big word that means "lots of different
kinds of living things in one place."

Why kinds and not how many? Because one ant hill can hold a hundred
ants, and a hundred ants are still just **one kind**. A garden with
one sunflower, one tomato plant, one bee, and one worm holds **four
kinds** — more kinds than the ant hill, even though it holds fewer
animals. Counting kinds tells you how *varied* a habitat is. That is
what we are comparing in this unit.

Here is the tally chart we will use. Each row is one kind. Each tally
mark means "I saw this kind." We write the kind's name or draw it.

| Kind I saw | Tally | How many kinds? |
|---|---|---|
| (draw or write each kind) | \|\|\|\| | 1 kind per row |

Rules for a fair count — the same rules for both habitats:

1. Look for the same amount of time in each habitat (10 minutes).
2. Look in the same way: stand still, look slowly, use the hand lens.
3. Write down what you **actually see** — not what you think lives there.
4. One kind gets one row, no matter how many of that kind you see.
5. If you are not sure what something is, draw it and write "not sure"
   — it still counts as one kind.

**Worked example 1 — a garden bed, modeled out loud, slowly:**

> Adult: "I stand at the garden bed for 10 minutes. I see: a
> sunflower, a tomato plant, carrot tops, a bean plant, a bee, a
> ladybug, a sparrow, and an earthworm. Now I count the *kinds*.
> Plants: sunflower, tomato, carrot, bean — 4 kinds. Animals: bee,
> ladybug, sparrow, earthworm — 4 kinds. Four plus four is 8. This
> garden bed holds **8 kinds** of living things. Did I count the three
> carrots as three kinds? No! Three carrots are one kind — carrot.
> That is the rule that matters most."

Adult fills in the tally chart in the journal as the learner watches,
one row per kind, and counts the rows aloud: "1, 2, 3… 8 kinds."

**Worked example 2 — the same garden, counted wrong on purpose:**

> Adult: "Now watch me count the wrong way. I see 3 carrots, 3
> tomatoes, 1 sunflower, 1 bean plant. If I count *how many*, I get
> 3 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 8 plants. But that is not 8 kinds — it is 4 kinds
> with 8 plants. And the bee? The bee is 1 animal, but it is also 1
> kind. If I mix up 'how many' and 'how many kinds,' my two habitats
> cannot be compared fairly. So I always ask: 'Is this a new kind, or
> one I already wrote down?'"

Key idea to say plainly: **Count kinds, not how many. One row per
kind. Three carrots are one kind.**

**Original reading passage (adult reads aloud; learner retells):**

> "Ana sits beside the garden bed. She does not pick anything. She
> looks. A bee hums past. A ladybug walks on a leaf. A worm curls in
> the soil. Ana draws a line for each new kind she sees. Bee. Ladybug.
> Worm. Sunflower. Tomato. Carrot. Bean. Sparrow. 'Eight kinds,' she
> whispers. 'I did not count how many. I counted how many kinds.'"

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
drawing. Adult notes are in parentheses.

1. "I see 5 ants and 2 robins on the lawn. How many kinds of animals
   is that?" *(2 kinds — ant and robin. Five ants are one kind.)*
2. "I see 1 dandelion, 1 daisy, and 1 clover. How many kinds of
   plants?" *(3 kinds.)*
3. Picture tally (use the generated image in
   [../assets/garden-and-lawn-habitats.png](../assets/garden-and-lawn-habitats.png),
   left side only): "Count the kinds in the garden picture. How many
   plant kinds? How many animal kinds?" *(Plants: sunflower, tomato,
   carrot, bean = 4 kinds. Animals: bee, ladybug, sparrow, earthworm =
   4 kinds. Total 8 kinds.)*
4. "I see a sparrow, then later I see the same sparrow again. Do I
   start a new row?" *(No — it is the same kind, already counted.
   Seeing it twice does not make it two kinds.)*
5. Error-analysis: "Sam counts 10 kinds in the garden. His chart has
   a row for 'carrot,' a row for 'orange carrot,' and a row for 'carrot
   tops.' What is wrong?" *(He split one kind into three rows. A
   carrot, its orange root, and its green tops are the same kind —
   one row.)*

## Independent practice

The learner makes a tally chart for a habitat they can see from a
window or in the yard (or the garden picture, if outdoors is not
possible), using the five rules. The adult scribes dictated words and
checks that each row is a different kind.

## Application

Start the unit's "Habitat A" tally chart — the first half of the
project. The learner chooses Habitat A (the garden bed, the lawn, or
the pond edge), writes the plan (10 minutes, same rules), and records
the first observations.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Do we count how many, or how many kinds?" Acceptable:
"How many kinds." Then: "I see 4 daisies. How many kinds is that?"
Acceptable: "One kind."

**Table variant:** The learner sorts 6 picture cards into "same kind"
pairs (two dandelions together, a dandelion and a daisy apart).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Pre-drawn tally chart with 8 blank rows; the adult
  scribes; the learner points and draws.
- **Language:** The sentence frame "This is a new kind" / "I already
  wrote this kind" stays on the wall; home-language names for living
  things are welcomed next to the English name.
- **Extension:** Count the kinds in a second picture habitat and
  compare the two totals; or write one sentence: "My habitat holds
  ___ kinds."

**Misconception note:** Learners almost always count individuals
instead of kinds ("I saw 5 ants, so that's 5"). Counter every time
with the question: "Is this a new kind, or one I already wrote
down?" See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (Lesson 02).
