# Lesson 04 — Compare Two Habitats

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
the generated picture below, the two real tally charts from Lessons
02–03

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will compare two habitats
side by side and say which one holds more kinds of living things —
using the counts as your evidence.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Garden: 8 kinds. Lawn: 4 kinds.
Which holds more?" (The garden.) "How do you know?" (8 is more than
4 — the counts are the evidence.) If the learner answers from a
feeling ("the garden looks busier") instead of the counts, model it:
"Busy is a feeling. The counts are the evidence. 8 is more than 4."

**Safety note:** Today is a picture lesson — no outdoor work. The
picture is an AI-made drawing, not a photograph of a real place; the
kinds in it are for counting practice, not real observations.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Comparing is easy once both habitats were counted the same way. You
put the two charts side by side, read the two totals, and say which
number is bigger. Then you say it as a **claim with evidence**: "My
claim is ___. My evidence is ___."

Look at the picture below. It shows two habitats side by side: a
garden bed on the left and a lawn on the right. Count the kinds in
each picture the way you counted the real habitats — one row per kind.

*Caption: Two habitats, drawn side by side so their kinds can be
counted the same way. Left: garden bed. Right: lawn. This is an
AI-generated illustration for counting practice, not a photograph of a
real place.*

**Text-only alternative (the same information, in words):** Garden
(left): plants — sunflower, tomato, carrot, bean (4 kinds); animals —
bee, ladybug, sparrow, earthworm (4 kinds); total 8 kinds. Lawn
(right): plants — grass, dandelion (2 kinds); animals — ant, robin
(2 kinds); total 4 kinds. A learner using only this text can answer
every question in this lesson.

**Worked example 1 — counting the garden picture, modeled out loud:**

> Adult: "Left side, the garden. Plants first. I see a sunflower —
> one kind. A tomato plant — a second kind. Carrot tops — a third kind.
> A bean plant — a fourth kind. Four plant kinds. Animals next. A bee
> — one kind. A ladybug — a second kind. A sparrow — a third kind. An
> earthworm — a fourth kind. Four animal kinds. Four plus four is 8.
> The garden picture holds **8 kinds**."

**Worked example 2 — counting the lawn picture, and the comparison:**

> Adult: "Right side, the lawn. Plants: grass — one kind. A dandelion
> — a second kind. Two plant kinds. Animals: ants — one kind, even
> though I see three ants. A robin — a second kind. Two animal kinds.
> Two plus two is 4. The lawn picture holds **4 kinds**. Now compare:
> garden 8, lawn 4. Eight is more than four. My claim: the garden
> picture holds more kinds of living things than the lawn picture. My
> evidence: I counted 8 kinds in the garden and 4 kinds in the lawn."

Key idea to say plainly: **Compare with counts, not feelings. Claim,
then evidence: "My claim is ___. My evidence is ___."**

**Original reading passage (adult reads aloud; learner retells):**

> "Ana puts her two charts side by side. Garden: eight kinds. Lawn:
> four kinds. 'The garden holds more kinds,' she says. 'My evidence
> is my count. Eight is more than four.' Her friend asks, 'Is the lawn
> bad?' Ana shakes her head. 'No. The lawn is a habitat too. It gives
> food, water, shelter, and space — just to fewer kinds.'"

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
drawing. Adult notes are in parentheses.

1. "How many plant kinds are in the garden picture?" *(4 — sunflower,
   tomato, carrot, bean.)* "How many animal kinds?" *(4 — bee,
   ladybug, sparrow, earthworm.)*
2. "How many kinds are in the lawn picture in all?" *(4 — grass,
   dandelion, ant, robin.)*
3. "Which pictured habitat holds more kinds, and what is your
   evidence?" *(The garden — 8 kinds against the lawn's 4 kinds.)*
4. "Your real Habitat A chart has 7 kinds and your Habitat B chart
   has 7 kinds. Which holds more?" *(Neither — they hold the same
   number of kinds. Saying "the same" is a real answer.)*
5. Error-analysis: "Sam says, 'The garden holds more kinds because
   it is prettier.' What is wrong with Sam's evidence?" *(Pretty is
   not a count. The evidence must be the numbers: 8 kinds and 4
   kinds.)*

## Independent practice

The learner compares their two real tally charts from Lessons 02–03
(or two practice charts from [practice.md](../practice.md)) and writes
or dictates one claim-with-evidence sentence: "My claim is ___ . My
evidence is ___ ." The adult scribes dictated words.

## Application

Start the comparison poster (project step 4): the two tally charts,
the two totals, and the claim-with-evidence sentence go on the poster
side by side.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Garden picture: 8 kinds. Lawn picture: 4 kinds. Which
holds more kinds?" Acceptable: "The garden." Then: "What is your
evidence?" Acceptable: "I counted 8 and 4" / "8 is more than 4."

**Table variant:** The learner points to each kind in the picture in
turn while the adult makes the tally marks, then points to the bigger
total.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** The text-only alternative above gives every count in
  words; the learner can answer from the text without seeing the
  picture. The adult scribes the claim sentence.
- **Language:** The sentence frame "My claim is ___. My evidence is
  ___." stays on the wall; home-language names welcomed.
- **Extension:** Compare the plant kinds alone (garden 4, lawn 2) and
  the animal kinds alone (garden 4, lawn 2); or write one sentence:
  "The ___ holds more kinds because I counted ___ and ___."

**Misconception note:** Learners often give feelings as evidence
("prettier," "busier," "I like it"). Counter by asking for the
numbers every time: "How many kinds did you count in each?" See
[teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (Lesson 04).
