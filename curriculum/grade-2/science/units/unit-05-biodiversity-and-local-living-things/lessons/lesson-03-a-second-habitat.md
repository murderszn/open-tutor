# Lesson 03 — A Second Habitat

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil/crayons,
a hand lens, the Habitat A tally chart from Lesson 02, a timer or clock

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will visit a **second**
habitat and count its kinds the same way you counted the first one.
Same time, same rules — so the two habitats can be compared fairly.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "How do we count — how many, or how
many kinds?" (How many kinds.) "How long do we look in each habitat?"
(10 minutes — the same time in both.) If the learner cannot recall the
time rule, model it: "If I look at the garden for 10 minutes and the
lawn for 2 minutes, is that fair? No — I gave the garden more time to
show me its kinds."

**Safety note:** Same rules as Lesson 02: observe, never collect;
plants are not picked; stay on paths; do not touch an animal you do
not know; wash hands after outdoor work. If going outside is not
possible, use the picture tally in the guided practice.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

One habitat tells you how many kinds live in *that* place. But our
question is a comparing question: **which habitat holds more kinds?**
To answer it, we need a second habitat counted the **same way**:

- The same 10 minutes of looking.
- The same slow, careful looking — stand still, use the hand lens.
- The same rule: one row per kind, no matter how many of that kind
  you see.
- The same honest rule: write down what you actually see.

If the rules change between habitats, the counts cannot be compared.
That is the fair-test idea from Unit 01, used again: change one thing
(the habitat), keep everything else the same.

**Worked example 1 — the lawn, counted the same way, modeled out loud:**

> Adult: "Yesterday I counted the garden bed: 8 kinds. Today I stand
> at the lawn for the same 10 minutes and look the same way. I see:
> grass, a dandelion, ants, and a robin. Now I count the kinds.
> Plants: grass, dandelion — 2 kinds. Animals: ant, robin — 2 kinds.
> Two plus two is 4. The lawn holds **4 kinds** of living things. I
> saw 3 ants — but 3 ants are one kind, ant. I looked for 10 minutes,
> the same as the garden. So now I can compare: garden 8, lawn 4."

Adult fills in the Habitat B tally chart next to the Habitat A chart
in the journal, so the two charts sit side by side.

**Worked example 2 — an unfair comparison, spotted out loud:**

> Adult: "Now watch me compare unfairly. I counted the garden for 10
> minutes and found 8 kinds. Then I counted the pond edge for 30
> minutes, with a friend helping me look, and found 12 kinds. Can I
> say the pond holds more kinds than the garden? Not yet! I gave the
> pond more time and more eyes. Maybe the garden would show 12 kinds
> too, if I looked for 30 minutes with a friend. A fair comparison
> needs the same time and the same way of looking. So I go back, count
> the garden for 30 minutes with my friend, and *then* I compare."

Key idea to say plainly: **Same time, same rules, both habitats. Then
the counts can be compared.**

**Original reading passage (adult reads aloud; learner retells):**

> "Ana walks to the lawn. She sets her timer for ten minutes — the
> same ten minutes as the garden. She looks slowly. Grass. A
> dandelion. Ants, marching in a line. A robin, listening for worms.
> 'Four kinds,' she says. She does not say the lawn is worse than the
> garden. She says, 'The garden holds more kinds. The lawn holds
> fewer kinds. Both are habitats. Both give food, water, shelter, and
> space.'"

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
drawing. Adult notes are in parentheses.

1. "I counted the garden for 10 minutes and the lawn for 10 minutes.
   Is that fair?" *(Yes — same time, same rules.)*
2. "I counted the garden in the morning and the lawn at night, when
   most animals are asleep. Is that fair?" *(No — different times of
   day change what you can see. Count both at about the same time of
   day.)*
3. Picture tally (use the generated image in
   [../assets/garden-and-lawn-habitats.png](../assets/garden-and-lawn-habitats.png),
   right side only): "Count the kinds in the lawn picture. How many
   plant kinds? How many animal kinds?" *(Plants: grass, dandelion =
   2 kinds. Animals: ant, robin = 2 kinds. Total 4 kinds.)*
4. "My Habitat A chart has 8 kinds. My Habitat B chart has 4 kinds.
   Which habitat holds more kinds?" *(Habitat A — 8 is more than 4.
   The evidence is the two counts.)*
5. Error-analysis: "Sam counted the garden with his friend and the
   lawn alone. He found 9 kinds in the garden and 3 in the lawn. What
   is unfair about his comparison?" *(Two people looking find more
   kinds than one person. Both habitats need the same number of
   lookers — or the comparison is not fair.)*

## Independent practice

The learner completes the Habitat B tally chart for the second real
habitat (or the lawn picture, if outdoors is not possible), using the
same 10 minutes and the same rules as Habitat A. The adult checks that
each row is a different kind and that the time matched.

## Application

Finish the project's survey step: both tally charts now sit side by
side in the journal. The learner writes the two totals under the
charts: "Habitat A: ___ kinds. Habitat B: ___ kinds."

## Exit check

Adult asks: "What must stay the same when you count a second
habitat?" Acceptable: "The time" / "The rules" / "How I look." Then:
"The garden has 8 kinds and the lawn has 4 kinds. Which holds more?"
Acceptable: "The garden — 8 is more than 4."

**Table variant:** The learner matches 4 "fair" and "unfair" picture
cards (same timer vs. different timer; one looker vs. two lookers) to
the words FAIR and NOT FAIR.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** The two tally charts sit side by side on one page;
  the adult scribes; the timer is visible so the 10 minutes are
  concrete.
- **Language:** The sentence frame "I looked for ___ minutes in both
  habitats" stays on the wall; home-language names welcomed.
- **Extension:** Count a third habitat the same way and rank all
  three; or write one sentence: "Habitat A holds ___ kinds. Habitat
  B holds ___ kinds."

**Misconception note:** Learners often think "more" always means
"better" ("the garden wins"). Counter early: "More kinds is not a
prize — it is a fact about the place. A lawn is a habitat too, and
some animals need a lawn." See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (Lesson 03).
