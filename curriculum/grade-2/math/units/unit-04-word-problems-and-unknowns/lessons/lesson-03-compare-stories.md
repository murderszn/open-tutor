# Lesson 03 — Compare Stories: Difference, Bigger, Smaller

**Student goal:** I can solve compare stories — how many more, how
many fewer, and the missing bigger or smaller amount.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**

## Materials

- Counters in two colors
- Paper and crayons
- Number-line strip 0–100

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Build 52 with one color and 37 with another. Line them up. Which
pile is bigger, and by how many?" (Expected: 52 is bigger; 52 − 37 =
15 more.) If lining up is new, start both piles at the same edge so
the extra part sticks out.

## Teaching

Compare stories do not move anything and do not combine groups. They
hold two amounts side by side and ask about the **gap** between them.
Draw two bars starting at the same left edge: the longer bar sticks
out, and the sticking-out part is the difference.

There are three compare questions:

1. **Difference unknown:** "How many more does A have than B?"
   Equation: `smaller + □ = bigger`, solved by `bigger − smaller = □`.
2. **Bigger unknown:** "A has this many more than B. How many does A
   have?" Equation: `smaller + difference = □`.
3. **Smaller unknown:** "A has this many more than B. How many does B
   have?" Equation: `□ + difference = bigger`, solved by
   `bigger − difference = □`.

**Key vocabulary:** *compare*, *difference* (the gap), *bigger
unknown*, *smaller unknown*. Warning, said out loud every lesson:
"more" in a compare story does **not** automatically mean add. It
depends on which amount is missing.

## Worked example 1 — difference unknown

"Maya has 52 ribbons. Jon has 37 ribbons. How many more ribbons does
Maya have than Jon?"

Draw the bars from the same edge: Maya's bar runs to 52, Jon's to 37.
The sticking-out part is the question.

> "Smaller + □ = bigger: 37 + □ = 52. Subtract: 52 − 37 = □.
> 52 − 30 = 22, 22 − 7 = **15**. Check: 37 + 15 = 52 — the gap closes
> the bars. Maya has **15 more ribbons**. Note: we did not add the two
> amounts. 52 + 37 = 89 would be a total, and this story never asks
> for a total."

## Worked example 2 — bigger unknown

"Ben has 28 bottle caps. Ana has 19 more bottle caps than Ben. How
many bottle caps does Ana have?"

> "Ben is the smaller amount (28); the difference is 19; Ana is the
> bigger amount, unknown. Equation: 28 + 19 = □. Break apart: 20 + 10 =
> 30, 8 + 9 = 17, 30 + 17 = **47**. Check: 47 − 28 = 19 — Ana really
> has 19 more. Ana has **47 bottle caps**."

## Worked example 3 — smaller unknown

"Rosa has 63 stickers. Rosa has 24 more stickers than Tim. How many
stickers does Tim have?"

> "Careful: Rosa is named first, but Rosa is the **bigger** amount
> (63), and 24 is the gap. Tim is the smaller amount, unknown.
> Equation: □ + 24 = 63. Subtract: 63 − 24 = □. 63 − 20 = 43,
> 43 − 4 = **39**. Check: 39 + 24 = 63. Tim has **39 stickers**.
> Adding here (63 + 24 = 87) would make Tim bigger than Rosa, which
> contradicts the story."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** in all three examples the same two
operations appear, but which amount is missing decides the equation.
First ask: "Who has more? What is missing — the gap, the bigger pile,
or the smaller pile?" Only then compute.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds with bars.

1. "Sam scored 71 points. Alex scored 48 points. How many more points
   did Sam score?" (Expected: difference unknown, 71 − 48 = **23**.)
2. "Lena has 56 shells. Lena has 18 more shells than Omar. How many
   shells does Omar have?" (Expected: smaller unknown, 56 − 18 =
   **38**.)
3. "Noah has 44 marbles. Priya has 27 more marbles than Noah. How
   many marbles does Priya have?" (Expected: bigger unknown,
   44 + 27 = **71**.)
4. Error analysis: the adult shows "Kim has 17 fewer stickers than
   Dan. Dan has 62 stickers. Kim has 62 + 17 = 79 stickers." and
   asks: "Is it?" (Expected: wrong — "fewer" means Kim is the smaller
   amount: 62 − 17 = **45**. Adding made Kim bigger, contradicting
   "fewer.")
5. "Why can't 'more' alone tell you to add?" (Expected: "Because in
   a compare story 'more' can describe the gap, and the missing amount
   might be the smaller pile.")

## Independent practice

The learner draws bars and writes, without hints: "A red tower has 84
blocks. A blue tower has 59 blocks. How many more blocks does the red
tower have?" (84 − 59 = **25**); "Eli has 36 pencils. Mia has 23 more
pencils than Eli. How many does Mia have?" (36 + 23 = **59**);
"Lena has 92 points. Lena has 35 more points than Sam. How many points
does Sam have?" (92 − 35 = **57**).

*Alternate response mode:* build both piles with counters, line them
up from the same edge, and count the sticking-out part aloud.

## Application

Tower story: "A block tower with 67 blocks stands next to a tower
with 49 blocks. How many more blocks does the taller tower have?"
(Expected: difference unknown, 67 − 49 = **18 more blocks**. Check:
49 + 18 = 67.)

## Exit check

"Draw the bars: 78 points and 53 points. How many more is 78 than
53?" Expected: 78 − 53 = **25**. Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** always say "who has more?" and point to the
  longer bar before writing any equation; rephrase "fewer than" as
  "not as many as" when needed.
- **Alternate response mode:** use two strips of paper cut to the two
  amounts and lay them side by side; the overhang is the difference.
- **Floor-play variant:** stand two rows of counters on the floor from
  the same starting line and walk the gap.

## Extension

"Write a compare story where the smaller amount is the unknown and
the difference is a two-digit number (like 28). Solve it, then check
by adding the gap back onto the smaller amount." (Example: "Zoe has
81 ribbons, 28 more than Kai. Kai has 81 − 28 = **53** ribbons;
check: 53 + 28 = 81.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) adding the two amounts for a
difference question — the bars show why: a total is not a gap;
(2) adding when the story says "more" but asks for the smaller amount
— always identify who is bigger first; (3) reading "A has 24 more
than B" as if A were the smaller amount — rephrase as "A's pile is
bigger by 24" before computing.
