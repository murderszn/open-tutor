# Lesson 06 — Check: Estimate, Inverse, Story Fit

**Student goal:** I can check any story answer three ways — estimate,
inverse operation, and "does it fit the story?" — and catch my own
mistakes.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Unit:** [U04](../README.md) · **Session 11 of 16**

## Materials

- Counters
- Paper and crayons
- Number-line strip 0–100

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Estimate 47 + 36 before solving: round each number to the nearest
ten, then add." (Expected: 50 + 40 = **90**, so the answer should be
near 90.) If rounding is new, use the number line: 47 is closer to 50
than to 40; 36 is closer to 40 than to 30.

## Teaching

An answer is not finished until it is checked. Three checks, in order
of speed:

1. **Estimate.** Round each number to the nearest ten and compute
   roughly. The real answer should land near the estimate — not
   exactly on it, but in the same neighborhood. An answer far away
   (like 173 for 47 + 36) is certainly wrong.
2. **Inverse operation.** Addition is checked by subtraction, and
   subtraction by addition. If 47 + 36 = 83, then 83 − 36 must be 47.
   If the inverse does not return the starting number, something is
   off.
3. **Story fit.** Ask what the story did: did the pile grow or
   shrink? Is the answer bigger or smaller than the start, as the
   story requires? Could the answer exist at all — for example, a
   take-from answer bigger than the start is impossible, and a
   part can never be bigger than its whole.

A check that fails means: re-read the story, re-check the equation,
then re-compute — in that order. Most story mistakes are equation
mistakes, not arithmetic mistakes.

**Key vocabulary:** *estimate* (a rough nearby answer), *round to
the nearest ten*, *inverse operation*, *story fit*, *reasonable*
(an answer that could be true for this story).

## Worked example 1 — estimate catches a wild answer

"Tom has 47 stickers. He gets 36 more. How many stickers does Tom
have now? A classmate answers 173. Is that reasonable?"

> "Estimate first: 47 ≈ 50, 36 ≈ 40, 50 + 40 = 90. The answer should
> be near 90. 173 is nowhere near 90 — and it is bigger than 100,
> while both piles together cannot exceed 47 + 36, which is under
> 100. So 173 is impossible. Solve properly: 47 + 36 = □. 40 + 30 =
> 70, 7 + 6 = 13, 70 + 13 = **83**. Inverse check: 83 − 36 = 47 —
> the start returns. Story fit: 83 is bigger than 47, and Tom gained
> stickers. **83 stickers**."

## Worked example 2 — story fit catches an impossible direction

"Lena had 58 beads. She gave some beads away. Now she has 71 beads.
A classmate says she gave away 13 beads. Does that fit the story?"

> "No — and no computation is needed to see it. Giving away shrinks
> a pile, so the end must be smaller than the start. But 71 is bigger
> than 58. The story as told cannot happen with a give-away: either
> the numbers are swapped (she gave away some and now has fewer), or
> the action was 'got more' (58 + 13 = 71 fits a getting story).
> The check is: **does the direction match the action?** If not, the
> equation is wrong, whatever the arithmetic says."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** checks are not extra work for tidy
students — they are how mathematicians trust their own answers. In
this unit, every quiz and assessment item asks for a check, and the
project rubric scores the check separately from the answer.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds.

1. "Estimate, then solve: 62 + 29." (Expected: estimate 60 + 30 =
   90; actual 62 + 29 = **91** — near 90, sensible. Inverse: 91 − 29
   = 62.)
2. "Estimate, then solve: 85 − 38." (Expected: estimate 90 − 40 =
   50; actual 85 − 38 = **47** — near 50, sensible. Inverse: 47 + 38
   = 85.)
3. Error analysis: the adult shows "74 − 29 = 55" and asks: "Check
   it." (Expected: estimate 70 − 30 = 40 — 55 is far from 40, so
   suspect it. Inverse: 55 + 29 = 84, not 74 — confirmed wrong.
   Correct: 74 − 29 = **45**; inverse 45 + 29 = 74.)
4. "A story says: 'Kim had 41 marbles. She found 16 more. Now she
   has 25 marbles.' What is wrong, without computing?" (Expected:
   story fit fails — finding more must end bigger than 41, but 25
   is smaller. The end number cannot be 25 for this story.)
5. "Which check do you run first, and why?" (Expected: "Estimate —
   it is fastest and catches wild answers before I spend time on the
   exact computation.")

## Independent practice

The learner estimates, solves, and inverse-checks, without hints:
"53 + 28" (estimate 50 + 30 = 80; actual **81**; inverse 81 − 28 =
53); "96 − 47" (estimate 100 − 50 = 50; actual **49**; inverse
49 + 47 = 96); "A story answer of 64 for 38 + 17" (estimate 40 + 20 =
60; actual 38 + 17 = **55** — 64 is too high; inverse of the claim
fails: 64 − 17 = 47, not 38).

*Alternate response mode:* say the estimate aloud and hold up "near"
or "far" hands before computing exactly.

## Application

Shop story: "A shop sells 43 apples in the morning and 38 oranges in
the afternoon. A classmate says the shop sold 81 pieces of fruit in
all. Check it." (Expected: estimate 40 + 40 = 80 — 81 is near 80,
sensible. Exact: 43 + 38 = **81**; inverse 81 − 38 = 43. The claim is
correct, and the checks prove it.)

## Exit check

"Estimate 67 + 24, solve it, and inverse-check it. Is the answer
sensible?" Expected: estimate 70 + 20 = 90; actual 67 + 24 = **91**;
inverse 91 − 24 = 67; 91 is near 90 — sensible. Record: secure /
developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "estimate" as "a friendly guess with
  tens" and always compute the estimate before the exact answer, so
  the habit has a fixed place.
- **Alternate response mode:** mark the estimate on the number line
  with a crayon dot and the exact answer with a second dot; "near"
  means the dots sit close together.
- **Floor-play variant:** step the estimate on a floor number line
  (big ten-steps), then step the exact answer (small steps).

## Extension

"Find a story answer that passes the estimate check but fails the
inverse check, and explain which mistake each check is built to
catch." (Example: for 86 − 39, the wrong answer 57 passes a rough
estimate (90 − 40 = 50, within the neighborhood) but fails the
inverse (57 + 39 = 96, not 86); the correct answer is **47**, inverse
47 + 39 = 86. Estimates catch wild errors; inverses catch small
slips.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) treating the estimate as the answer
— the estimate is a neighborhood, not an address; always follow it
with the exact computation; (2) running the inverse with the same
mistake repeated — compute the inverse from scratch, not by copying
digits; (3) checking only the arithmetic while the equation is wrong
— story fit comes first when the direction is impossible, because no
arithmetic can rescue a wrong equation.
