# Lesson 01 — Odd and Even: Pairing Tells the Story

**Student goal:** I can tell whether a group of objects is odd or even
by pairing them up, and I can say what is left over.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- 20 counters (buttons, beans, or blocks)
- Two paper plates or sorting trays, labeled "EVEN" and "ODD" by the adult

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Count these 6 counters by 2s with me: 2, 4, 6." Then: "Show me
what a pair is." (Expected: two counters pushed together.) If counting
by 2s to 6 is shaky, count pairs of hands, shoes, and socks first —
pairs are everywhere before they are numbers.

## Teaching

Some numbers split neatly into pairs. Some always leave one alone.

1. Take a group of counters. Push them into **pairs** — two counters
   side by side, again and again.
2. If every counter finds a partner, the number is **even**. No one is
   left over.
3. If one counter is left alone at the end, the number is **odd**. The
   leftover is the whole story: one left over means odd.
4. Say it both ways: "6 is even — 3 pairs, none left over." "7 is odd
   — 3 pairs, 1 left over."

**Key vocabulary:** *pair*, *leftover*, *odd*, *even*. A pair is two
things that go together. Even means "pairs all the way down, nothing
left over." Odd means "one left over."

## Worked example 1 — 6 counters

Push 6 counters into pairs: pair, pair, pair. Three pairs, nothing
left over.

> "6 is **even**. Every counter has a partner: 3 pairs and 0 left
> over."

## Worked example 2 — 7 counters

Push 7 counters into pairs: pair, pair, pair — and one counter sits
alone.

> "7 is **odd**. Three pairs, and 1 left over. That one leftover
> counter is what makes 7 odd. If I add one more counter, I get 8 —
> 4 pairs, none left over — even again."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** odd and even are not about big or small.
7 is odd and 20 is even. The test is always the same: pair them up and
look for a leftover.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds with the counters.

1. "Pair 10 counters. Odd or even?" (Expected: even — 5 pairs, none
   left over.)
2. "Pair 13 counters. Odd or even?" (Expected: odd — 6 pairs, 1 left
   over.)
3. "Pair 20 counters. Odd or even?" (Expected: even — 10 pairs, none
   left over.)
4. Error analysis: the adult pairs 9 counters as 4 pairs with 1 left
   over and says, "9 is even because it has 4 pairs." Ask: "Is it?"
   (Expected: wrong — the leftover matters. 9 is odd because 1 is
   left over. The pairs do not decide it; the leftover does.)
5. "Say each number two ways: the pairs, then odd or even." (Use
   round 2: "6 pairs and 1 left over — 13 is odd.")

## Independent practice

The learner pairs and sorts, without hints: 8, 11, 15, 18 counters —
one pile at a time — onto the EVEN or ODD plate, saying the pairs and
the leftover aloud. The adult checks that every counter is actually
paired before the sort, not guessed from the number.

*Alternate response mode:* point to each pair and hold up one finger
for a leftover; the adult says the words and writes "odd" or "even."

## Application

Snack plates: "You put 12 crackers on a plate. Can you share them
fairly between 2 plates, 6 and 6, with none left over?" (Expected: yes
— 12 is even, 6 pairs, so 2 plates of 6 works with nothing left over.)
"Now 11 crackers?" (Expected: no — 11 is odd, 5 pairs and 1 left
over, so one plate would get the extra cracker.)

## Exit check

"Pair 17 counters and tell me: odd or even, and how do you know?"
Expected: odd — 8 pairs and 1 left over. Record: secure / developing /
not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** always say the number two ways ("8 pairs and 1
  left over — 17 is odd"); never let "odd" or "even" be a bare label.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to each pair and hold up one
  finger for a leftover; the adult says the words and writes the label.
- **Floor-play variant:** lay the counters in a long line on the floor
  and step on them two at a time — the last step lands on a pair (even)
  or on one alone (odd).

## Extension

"Is 0 even or odd? Pair zero counters." (0 pairs, nothing left over —
even. The adult may note that grown-up math agrees: 0 is even.) Or:
"Find the next odd number after 17, and the next even number after 18,
using pairs." (19 is odd — 9 pairs, 1 left over; 20 is even — 10
pairs.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) deciding odd/even from the pairs and
ignoring the leftover — always ask "and what is left over?" before
accepting an answer; (2) thinking odd means "a big number" or even
means "a small number" — use 7 (odd, small) against 20 (even, big) to
break it; (3) counting by 1s instead of pairing — pairing is the test
in this lesson; counting by 2s arrives in Lesson 02.
