# Lesson 05 — Tile the Rectangle: Partition into Squares

**Student goal:** I can split a rectangle into rows and columns of
same-size squares, count the squares, and write the total as a
repeated-addition equation.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Unit:** [U05](../README.md) · **Session 9 of 16**

## Materials

- Graph paper (large squares)
- Crayons and a ruler
- 25 counters

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Build a 3-by-4 array with counters. How many counters, by rows?"
(Expected: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12.) If arrays are shaky, replay Lesson 04
with 3 rows of 4 before starting — this lesson draws the same array as
squares inside a rectangle.

## Teaching

A rectangle can be tiled — filled — with same-size squares in rows and
columns. The tiled rectangle IS an array, drawn instead of built.

1. Draw a rectangle on the graph paper, following the grid lines.
2. **Partition** it: draw lines across and down so the whole rectangle
   is split into same-size squares, with no gaps and no overlaps. Every
   square must be the same size as every other square.
3. Count the squares by rows: each row has the same number of squares,
   so write the repeated-addition equation — one addend per row.
4. Check by columns: each column has the same number of squares, so
   the column equation must give the same total.

**Key vocabulary:** *partition*, *same-size squares*, *tile*. To
partition is to split into parts. Tiling means filling a shape with
same-size squares edge to edge.

## Worked example 1 — A 3-by-4 rectangle

Draw a rectangle 4 squares wide and 3 squares tall. Partition it: 2
lines across, 3 lines down. Count the squares by rows — 4 in each row.

> "By rows: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 squares. By columns: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12
> squares. The rectangle holds 12 same-size squares, and the two
> equations agree."

## Worked example 2 — A 2-by-5 rectangle

Draw a rectangle 5 squares wide and 2 squares tall. Partition it into
same-size squares.

> "By rows: 5 + 5 = 10 squares. By columns: 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 10
> squares. Ten squares either way."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the squares must all be the same size —
that is what makes the rows equal and the columns equal. If one square
were bigger, the rows would not match and the repeated addition would
be wrong. Same-size squares are the whole rule.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 4–6 short rounds on graph paper.

1. "Draw a rectangle 3 squares wide and 5 squares tall. Partition it
   and count the squares by rows." (Expected: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 15.)
2. "Draw a rectangle 2 squares wide and 4 squares tall. Partition it
   and count the squares by columns." (Expected: 4 + 4 = 8.)
3. "Draw a rectangle 5 squares wide and 4 squares tall. Count by rows,
   then check by columns." (Expected: rows 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20; columns
   4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 20.)
4. Error analysis: the adult draws a rectangle split into squares of
   two different sizes and writes "3 + 3 + 3 = 9." Ask: "Is it?"
   (Expected: the count cannot be trusted — the squares are not the
   same size, so the rows are not equal groups. Re-partition with
   same-size squares first, then count.)
5. "Say it two ways: rows, then columns." (Use round 1: "5 rows of 3 —
   3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 15; 3 columns of 5 — 5 + 5 + 5 = 15.")

## Independent practice

The learner draws and partitions, without hints: a 4-by-3 rectangle
(rows equation and total); a 2-by-2 rectangle (columns equation and
total); a 5-by-3 rectangle (rows equation and total). The adult checks
that every square is the same size before the equation is written.

*Alternate response mode:* place one counter in each square and count
the counters row by row, saying each row total aloud; the adult writes
the equation.

## Application

Garden bed: "A garden bed is a rectangle 4 squares wide and 3 squares
tall. How many same-size squares fit inside it?" (Expected: 3 rows of
4 — 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 squares.) "If one seed goes in each square, how
many seeds?" (Expected: 12 seeds — one per square.)

## Exit check

"Draw a rectangle 3 squares wide and 2 squares tall, partition it, and
tell me how many squares and the row equation." Expected: 2 rows of 3 —
3 + 3 = 6 squares. Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** always say the tiling two ways ("3 rows of 4 —
  4 + 4 + 4 = 12 squares"); never let a bare equation be the only answer.
- **Alternate response mode:** place a counter in each square and count
  by rows, pointing; the adult writes the equation.
- **Floor-play variant:** tile a floor rectangle with square paper
  tiles, stepping on each tile as it is counted.

## Extension

"Draw a rectangle with 5 rows and 4 columns of squares. What is the
total? Now draw the same rectangle turned sideways — 4 rows and 5
columns. What changed, and what stayed the same?" (Both hold 20
squares: 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 20 and 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20. The rows and
columns trade places; the total stays 20.) Or: "Can you partition a
rectangle into rows of 3 and columns of 3 — a square array? Draw it."
(3 rows of 3: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9 squares.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) drawing squares of different sizes and
counting anyway — the same-size rule comes before the count, every
time; (2) counting the border squares twice (once for rows, once for
columns) — rows and columns are two separate counts of the same
squares, never added together; (3) leaving gaps or overlaps when
partitioning — the rectangle must be completely filled, edge to edge.
