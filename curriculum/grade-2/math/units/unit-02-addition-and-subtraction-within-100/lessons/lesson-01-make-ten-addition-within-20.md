# Lesson 01 — Make-Ten Addition within 20

**Student goal:** I can add within 20 by making a ten first, and I can say
why it works.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- 20 one-cubes (or counters) in two colors
- A ten-frame card (or draw one: two rows of five boxes)
- Number cards 1–9

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

Place-value retrieval from Unit 01: "Build 34 with rods and cubes. How many
tens? How many ones?" (Expected: 3 ten-rods, 4 one-cubes; 3 tens and 4 ones.)
If rods are shaky, rebuild 23 and 41 before starting — this whole unit stands
on knowing tens from ones.

## Teaching

We already know 10 + anything is easy: 10 + 4 is 14 because ten and four ones
make fourteen. **Make-ten** steals that easiness for harder sums.

Rule: take just enough from the second number to fill the first number up to
10. Then add what is left.

1. Look at the bigger number first. Ask: "How many more does it need to reach
   10?"
2. Split the other number into two parts: the part that fills the ten, and
   the leftover.
3. Add: 10 + leftover.

**Key vocabulary:** *make-ten* — filling one addend up to 10 first, then
adding the rest. A *ten-frame* shows 10 boxes; a full frame plus loose cubes
is the picture of make-ten.

## Worked example 1 — 8 + 5

The adult puts 8 cubes of one color in the ten-frame (2 boxes empty) and 5
cubes of the other color beside it.

> "8 needs **2** more to make 10. Take 2 from the 5: the 5 splits into 2 and
> 3. Slide the 2 into the frame — now the frame is full, that's 10. The
> leftover 3 sits outside: 10 + 3 = **13**. So 8 + 5 = 13."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** we didn't change the total — we just regrouped
the same 13 cubes as 10 + 3 instead of 8 + 5. Moving cubes around never
changes how many cubes there are. That is why the answer is trustworthy.

## Worked example 2 — 9 + 6

The adult fills the ten-frame with 9 cubes (1 box empty); 6 cubes wait beside
it.

> "9 needs **1** more to make 10. Take 1 from the 6: the 6 splits into 1 and
> 5. Fill the frame — 10. Leftover 5: 10 + 5 = **15**. So 9 + 6 = 15."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** 6 broke into 1 + 5 because 9 was one short of
10. The split always matches what the first number needs: 9 needs 1, 8 needs
2, 7 needs 3. Say the need before you split.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 5 short rounds with the cubes.

1. "Show 7 + 8. What does 7 need?" (Expected: 3; 8 splits 3 + 5; 10 + 5 = 15.)
2. "Show 8 + 6." (Expected: 8 needs 2; 6 splits 2 + 4; 10 + 4 = 14.)
3. "Show 9 + 7." (Expected: 9 needs 1; 7 splits 1 + 6; 10 + 6 = 16.)
4. Error analysis: the adult fills 9 and splits 5 into 2 + 3, making 9 + 2 +
   3 = 14. Ask: "I made an error — what is it?" (Expected: 9 needs 1, not 2;
   5 splits into 1 + 4; the true answer is 14 anyway — the split was wrong
   but the total was lucky.) Adult names the lesson: even a lucky answer
   doesn't excuse a wrong split.
5. "Show 6 + 8 two ways: start with 6, then start with 8." (Expected:
   6 needs 4 → 8 splits 4 + 4 → 14; 8 needs 2 → 6 splits 2 + 4 → 14. Same
   answer, different splits.)

## Independent practice

The learner works 5 sums with cubes, saying each split aloud — 9 + 4, 7 + 9,
8 + 8, 9 + 9, 5 + 8. Expected: 13, 16, 16, 18, 13. The adult only checks the
split logic, not speed.

## Application

"Two shelves hold toys: 8 on the top shelf, 7 on the bottom. How many toys?"
The learner draws or builds it: 8 needs 2, 7 splits 2 + 5, 10 + 5 = 15.
Expected answer: 15 toys, with the split named.

## Exit check

"Solve 9 + 5 with make-ten. Say the split first." Expected: 9 needs 1; 5
splits 1 + 4; 10 + 4 = 14.

## Supports

- **Seated-table variant:** ten-frame card on the table; slide cubes in.
- **Floor-play variant:** tape a giant ten-frame on the floor; the learner
  *is* a cube and steps in.
- **Language access:** point to the empty boxes while saying "needs two";
  the learner holds up that many fingers before touching any cube.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner points to the split on drawn circles
  instead of moving real cubes; adult scribes the equation.
- **Extension:** "If 9 + 6 = 15, what is 19 + 6?" (Make a *twenty*: 19 needs
  1, 6 splits 1 + 5, 20 + 5 = 25.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner splits the second number into any two parts
(e.g., 9 + 6 → 3 + 3) instead of matching the need. Always ask "how many does
it need to reach 10?" *before* any cube moves. This is the seed of the
place-value explanation work in Lesson 06 (2.NBT.B.9): regrouping into tens
is what makes the math safe. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
