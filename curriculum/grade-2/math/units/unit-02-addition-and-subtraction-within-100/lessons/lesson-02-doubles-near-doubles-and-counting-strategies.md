# Lesson 02 — Doubles, Near-Doubles, and Counting Strategies

**Student goal:** I can use doubles, near-doubles, count-on, and count-back
to solve within 20 quickly.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 5 of 16**

## Materials

- Two dice (or number cards 1–9)
- 20 one-cubes
- The 0–120 number-line strip

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

"Yesterday's strategy: solve 8 + 5 with make-ten." (Expected: 8 needs 2; 5
splits 2 + 3; 13.) If the split is forgotten, redo one round with the
ten-frame before starting — make-ten stays available all unit.

## Teaching

Today's toolbox has three more strategies. Choose the one that fits the
numbers — a good mathematician picks, not guesses.

1. **Doubles:** a number plus itself — 6 + 6, 7 + 7, 8 + 8, 9 + 9. These are
   worth memorizing because they unlock the next strategy. Say them rhythmically:
   5 + 5 = 10, 6 + 6 = 12, 7 + 7 = 14, 8 + 8 = 16, 9 + 9 = 18.
2. **Near-double:** a sum one away from a double — 6 + 7 is one more than
   6 + 6, so 12 + 1 = 13. Ask: "Which double is hiding inside?"
3. **Count on / count back:** for small steps, start at the bigger number and
   step forward or backward on the number line. 8 + 3: start at 8, say 9, 10,
   11. 12 − 3: start at 12, say 11, 10, 9. Never count from 1 — that is slow
   and error-prone.

**Key vocabulary:** *double*, *near-double*, *count on*, *count back*.

## Worked example 1 — 6 + 7 as a near-double

The adult lays out 6 cubes in one row and 7 in a second row, aligned.

> "Line them up: the 7-row has one extra cube sticking out. Cover that extra
> cube — what do you see? Two rows of 6: a double, 6 + 6 = 12. Uncover the
> extra one: 12 + 1 = **13**. So 6 + 7 = 13."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** 6 + 7 is *one more* than the double 6 + 6. We
borrowed a known fact and adjusted by 1. Adjustment is only safe because we
can see exactly where the extra 1 came from.

## Worked example 2 — 11 − 4 with count-back on the number line

The adult puts a finger on 11 on the number-line strip.

> "Start at 11, not at 1. Step back and say each landing number: 10 (one
> step), 9 (two steps), 8 (three steps), 7 (four steps). 11 − 4 = **7**. Check
> with think-addition: 7 + 4 = 11. It matches, so 7 is right."

**Reasoning to name aloud:** counting back works because subtraction means
"step down the line." Starting at the bigger number keeps the count short —
4 steps instead of 11. The think-addition check catches step-skips.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. "7 + 8 — which double hides inside?" (Expected: 7 + 7 = 14, one more →
   15.)
2. "8 + 9." (Expected: 8 + 8 = 16, +1 → 17.)
3. "9 + 10 with count-on." (Expected: start at 10, say 11…19 — ten steps →
   19. Point out: this is just 9 ones past 10.)
4. "14 − 4 with count-back." (Expected: 13, 12, 11, 10 → 10.)
5. Error analysis: "Tom says 7 + 8 = 14 because it's 'almost a double.'"
   Ask: "What did Tom forget?" (Expected: almost-a-double is one *more* than
   the double, not the double itself: 7 + 7 = 14, so 7 + 8 = 15.)

## Independent practice

5 quick solves; the learner names the strategy first, then solves — 4 + 4,
9 + 8, 11 − 2, 16 − 4, 5 + 6. Expected: 8 (double), 17 (near-double 8 + 8 =
16, +1), 9 (count back: 10, 9), 12 (count back: 15, 14, 13, 12), 11
(near-double 5 + 5 = 10, +1).

## Application

Roll two dice. "Double the first die, then add the second die's pips as a
near-double." If the dice show 5 and 6: "5 + 5 = 10, one more is 11." The
learner plays 3 rounds and records each as an equation.

## Exit check

"7 + 8. Name the strategy and solve." Expected: near-double; 7 + 7 = 14,
one more is 15.

## Supports

- **Seated-table variant:** mirror rows of cubes on the table.
- **Floor-play variant:** the learner jumps the count-on/count-back steps on
  a floor number line.
- **Language access:** gesture "same, same" for doubles; hold up one finger
  for the near-double extra.
- **Alternate response mode:** learner points to the double equation card,
  then to the "+1" card; adult writes.
- **Extension:** "I know 8 + 8 = 16. What is 18 + 8?" (Near-*twenty*: 16
  tens? No — 18 is 10 more than 8: 16 + 10 = 26.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: applying the double *without* the +1 (Tom's error
above), or adding 1 twice. Make the hidden double visible every time —
cover the extra cube, say the double, uncover, add 1. Count-on errors almost
always come from starting at 1 or skipping a landing number; insist on
saying each landing number aloud. Doubles worth memory this week: 5+5 through
9+9 (2.OA.B.2 names one-digit sums as the year-end memory goal).
