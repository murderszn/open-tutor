# Lesson 04 — Fairness, Equality, and Respect

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, crayons, two picture
cards (two children who want different games) or adult-drawn
equivalents, a small set of counters or blocks for sharing

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn what fairness,
equality, and respect mean, how to tell whether a rule is fair, and how
two people can want different things and both be worth hearing.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What does 'fair' mean to you?" Accept
any sensible answer ("everyone gets a turn," "the same for everyone").
If the learner is stuck, model: "Fair means everyone plays by the same
rules and everyone gets a fair chance."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "Three big words run a good community. **Fair** means everyone is
treated by the same rules and everyone gets a fair chance. **Equality**
means everyone counts the same — no one counts extra, and no one counts
less. **Respect** means treating people, rules, and authority with care,
even when you disagree. When we follow a rule we do not like, that is
respect for the rule. When we listen to a leader we disagree with,
without shouting, that is respect for authority — and authority earns
that respect by being fair."

"Here is the **fairness test**. Ask three questions about any rule.
One: does the same rule apply to everyone? Two: does everyone get a
fair chance? Three: does it keep people safe and help us get along? If
the answers are yes, yes, and yes, the rule is fair."

**Worked example 1 — the fairness test on a real rule:**

> Adult: "Rule: 'Everyone waits in line for the slide.' Question one:
> does the same rule apply to everyone? Yes — every child waits. Question
> two: does everyone get a fair chance? Yes — the line moves, and
> everyone gets a turn. Question three: does it keep people safe and
> help us get along? Yes — no pushing, no arguments. Three yeses: the
> rule is fair. Now try a bad rule: 'Only children wearing red get to
> use the slide.' Question one: same rule for everyone? No — it leaves
> some children out for no good reason. That rule fails the test. It is
> not fair."

**Worked example 2 — two points of view:**

> Adult: "This is a practice story. Sam wants to play tag. Priya wants
> to play hide-and-seek. Sam's **point of view** is: tag is fast and
> fun. Priya's point of view is: hide-and-seek is quiet and clever.
> Neither child is wrong. A point of view is what one person thinks or
> wants, and different people can see the same choice differently. So
> what do we do? We listen to both sides first — that is respect. Then
> we find a fair way to decide — take turns (tag today, hide-and-seek
> tomorrow), or vote (Lesson 05). The mistake would be to say 'Sam is
> right and Priya is wrong' before listening. Listening does not mean
> agreeing. It means: I heard you."

Key idea to say plainly: **A rule is fair when it applies to everyone,
gives everyone a fair chance, and keeps people safe and getting along.
Different points of view are both worth hearing; we listen first, then
decide fairly.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads rules and stories; the learner answers orally, by pointing,
or with counters. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "Rule: 'Everyone cleans up their own materials.' Fair or not fair?
   Use the test." *(fair — same rule for everyone, everyone gets the
   same job, keeps the room usable)*
2. "Rule: 'Only the tallest children get the new books first.' Fair or
   not fair? Which test question fails?" *(not fair — question one
   fails: the rule does not apply to everyone)*
3. "Sam wants tag, Priya wants hide-and-seek. What is Sam's point of
   view? What is Priya's?" *(Sam: tag is fast and fun. Priya:
   hide-and-seek is quiet and clever.)*
4. "You disagree with a rule. What does respect look like?" *(say so
   calmly and listen, follow the rule while it stands, ask for a fair
   way to change it — accept any answer that shows care without
   shouting or breaking the rule)*
5. "Share 6 counters between 2 children equally. How many each — and is
   that equality?" *(3 each; yes, because everyone counts the same)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws or writes (adult scribes) two rules: one fair rule and
one unfair rule, each with one sentence saying why ("This rule is fair
because ___" / "This rule is not fair because ___"). Then the learner
draws two speech bubbles — one for each side of a disagreement they
invent (two games, two books, two snacks) — with one sentence per side.

## Application

The learner judges a new classroom rule the adult proposes: "From now
on, only children who finish first get to choose the story." The
learner runs the fairness test out loud (same rule for everyone? fair
chance? safe and getting along?) and gives a verdict with a reason. Then
the learner proposes a fairer version ("everyone takes turns choosing
the story") and explains why it passes the test.

## Exit check

Prompt: "What are the three fairness-test questions?" Accept: does the
same rule apply to everyone? Does everyone get a fair chance? Does it
keep people safe and help us get along? (Any order; wording may vary.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only two test questions at first (same for everyone?
  fair chance?); add the third when two-question judging is solid. Use
  counters for every sharing task.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach "fair," "equal," and "respect"
  with actions first (sharing counters, listening with eyes on the
  speaker), word cards second; welcome home-language words next to
  English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may point to a thumbs-up or
  thumbs-down card for fair/not fair, draw the two points of view, or
  record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner explains why "equal" does not always mean
  "exactly the same" — for example, a child who cannot see well gets a
  large-print book, and that is still fair, because fairness means a
  fair chance, not identical treatment.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often think "fair" means "I get what
I want." Re-anchor: "Fair is not about getting your way. Fair is about
the same rules and a fair chance for everyone — even when the result is
not your favorite." A second common slip: thinking respect means
agreeing. Re-anchor: "You can respect a person or a rule and still
disagree. Respect is how you disagree — calmly, and by listening."
