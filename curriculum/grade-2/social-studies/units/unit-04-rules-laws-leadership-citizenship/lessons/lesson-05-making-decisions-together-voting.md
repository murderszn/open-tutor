# Lesson 05 — Making Decisions Together: Listening, Consensus, and Voting

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the printed
[Maple Lane class-vote illustration](../assets/maple-lane-class-vote.png)
(or the text-only alternative below), a small box or jar for the
practice ballot, two choice cards (a book picture, a blocks picture)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how a group makes
a fair decision: first listen, then try to agree, then vote — and you
will practice voting the fair way.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What are our three discussion rules?"
Accept listen, wait your turn, speak kindly (any order). If the learner
is stuck, model: "Listen when others speak. Wait your turn. Speak
kindly."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "When a group must decide, good citizens follow three steps. Step
one: **listen**. Step two: try for **consensus** — a decision everyone
agrees to, or can live with, after talking it over. Step three: if
consensus does not happen, **vote**. A vote is a way to decide in which
each person chooses once, and the choice with the most votes wins. One
person, one vote. No one votes twice. No one votes for someone else."

"And every discussion has rules — agreed-upon rules for discussions.
Here are ours: one, listen when others speak. Two, wait your turn. Three,
speak kindly, even when you disagree. Four, respond to the idea, not the
person — say 'I disagree with that idea because…' instead of 'You are
wrong.' When we follow these rules, everyone feels safe to speak, and
the group hears every point of view before it decides."

"Now look at this picture. The adult says: 'This is an AI-generated
practice picture, not a photograph of a real class.'"

**Text-only alternative (all the information needed for the questions):**
A teacher stands next to a choice chart with two pictures: an open
storybook on the left and three building blocks on the right. Five
children sit on a rug facing her. Two children have one hand raised high.
Three children sit with their hands down, listening. A small box with a
slot in the top — a ballot box — sits on a table at the side of the rug.
No words or numbers appear in the picture.

**Worked example 1 — reading the vote picture:**

> Adult: "What are the two choices on the chart? Story time — the book
> — and block time — the blocks. How do we know a vote is happening?
> Two children have one hand raised, and the others are listening: they
> are waiting for their turn, or they have already decided. What is the
> ballot box for? Some votes are secret: you put a paper with your
> choice in the box instead of raising your hand, so nobody feels pushed
> by what others chose. Both ways are fair, as long as the rule is one
> person, one vote."

**Worked example 2 — consensus, then vote:**

> Adult: "This is a practice story. The Maple Lane class must choose:
> story time or block time. First, they listen: Maya says stories help
> her imagine; Jon says blocks help him build. Then they try consensus:
> 'Can we do stories today and blocks tomorrow?' If everyone can live
> with that, the decision is made — no vote needed. But suppose half the
> class still wants blocks today. Then they vote. Each child chooses
> once. The choice with the most votes wins. And the children who did
> not win accept the result — they get their turn another day. That last
> part matters: losing a vote is not unfair. An unfair vote is one where
> someone votes twice, or someone is not allowed to vote at all."

Key idea to say plainly: **Decide together by listening first, trying
to agree, and voting when you cannot agree. A fair vote is one person,
one vote, and the choice with the most votes wins.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult uses the illustration and choice cards; the learner answers
orally, by pointing, or by placing cards. (Hints in parentheses are for
the adult.)

1. "Look at the picture. What are the two choices?" *(story time and
   block time)*
2. "How do you know a vote is happening in the picture?" *(children
   have one hand raised; there is a ballot box; the chart shows two
   choices)*
3. "What does one-person-one-vote mean?" *(each person chooses once; no
   one votes twice or for someone else)*
4. "Four children vote: 3 choose story time, 1 chooses block time.
   Which choice wins — and is that fair?" *(story time wins, most votes;
   yes, fair, because everyone got one vote)*
5. "Your choice loses the vote. What do you do?" *(accept the result,
   try the choice another day — losing is not unfair)*

## Independent practice

The learner runs a practice vote with the adult (and any family members
or stuffed-toy "voters" the adult plays, each with exactly one vote):
story time or block time, by raised hands or secret ballot in the box.
The learner counts the votes out loud, announces the winner, and
dictates one sentence for the adult to scribe: "We voted. ___ won with
___ votes. I accept the result because everyone got one vote." (If the
learner is alone with the adult, the adult votes once too — two voters,
one vote each.)

## Application

The learner gives a one-reason speech for a classroom choice: "We
should choose ___ because ___." The adult models first: "We should
choose story time because stories help us imagine new worlds." Then the
learner gives their own speech, the adult gives a different one, and the
pair votes fairly. The adult asks, "Did we follow the discussion rules?"
and the learner checks: listened, waited, spoke kindly.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Name the three steps for a group decision, in order." Accept:
listen, try to agree (consensus), vote. Then: "What makes a vote fair?"
Accept: one person, one vote; most votes wins.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only the raised-hands vote at first; add the secret
  ballot when hand voting is solid. Let the learner point to the
  picture's choices instead of naming them.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach "consensus" and "vote" with actions
  first (nodding together; raising one hand), word cards second; welcome
  home-language words next to English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may vote with a card placed
  in the box, point to a choice card, or record an audio answer the
  adult keeps. The text-only alternative replaces the picture for any
  learner who cannot see it.
- **Extension:** The learner explains why a secret ballot can be fairer
  than raised hands (nobody copies a friend's vote) and when raised
  hands are fine (small, low-stakes class choices).

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often think losing a vote means the
vote was unfair. Re-anchor: "Fair is about the process: one person, one
vote, everyone heard. Losing a fair vote is normal — your turn comes
another day." A second common slip: voting for what a friend votes for
instead of choosing yourself. Re-anchor: "Your vote is yours. Listen to
reasons, then choose what you think is best."
