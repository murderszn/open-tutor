# Lesson 03 — People in Authority and What Governments Do

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, crayons, role cards
(adult-made: teacher, principal, police officer, firefighter, mayor,
council member, crossing guard, librarian, bus driver, neighbor, child)
or adult-drawn equivalents

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn who people in
authority are, what their jobs are, what a government is, and why
everyone — not just leaders — matters in a community.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Who is in charge in our classroom?
Who is in charge in our school?" Accept "teacher" and "principal." If
the learner is stuck, model: "In our classroom, the teacher is in
charge. In the school, the principal is in charge."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "A person in **authority** is someone whose job gives them the
responsibility to lead, decide, or enforce rules in a place. In a
classroom, the teacher has authority: the teacher teaches, keeps the
class safe, and decides the class rules with the class. In a school,
the principal has authority: the principal leads the whole school,
helps teachers, and makes school-wide decisions."

"Now bigger. A town or city has a government. A **government** is the
people and offices that make and carry out rules for a community and
provide **services** everyone shares. The **mayor** leads a town or city
government. The **council** is a group of people chosen to help make
rules and decisions for the town. Police officers help keep people safe
and enforce laws. Firefighters put out fires and rescue people. These
are people in authority in a town — and the adult says: 'This is a
practice story, not a real town.' In our practice town of Maple Lane,
Mayor Ana leads the town, and the council helps her decide about the
park and the library. These are invented practice characters, not real
officeholders."

"What does a government actually do for everyone? It provides services:
schools where children learn, roads people drive on, parks where
families play, libraries where anyone can borrow books, and clean water
that comes out of the tap. You do not pay the librarian each time you
borrow a book — the town provides the library for everyone, because the
government collects money (called taxes, which grown-ups pay) and uses
it for shared things."

"Here is the most important part: **all people, not just official
leaders, play important roles in a community.** The crossing guard
helps children cross the street safely. The librarian helps everyone
find books. The bus driver gets children to school. Neighbors look out
for each other. And children play a role too: following rules, helping
classmates, and voting in class choices. A community works when
everyone does their part — that is how a community gets a common task
done, like cleaning a park: the council decides, the town workers bring
tools, and neighbors show up to help."

**Worked example 1 — matching jobs:**

> Adult: "Who teaches the class? The teacher. Who leads the whole
> school? The principal. Who helps keep drivers and walkers safe on the
> road? The police officer. Who leads the town? The mayor. Notice: each
> person in authority has a place — classroom, school, town — and a job
> in that place."

**Worked example 2 — a service, step by step:**

> Adult: "Think about the Maple Lane library. Who decided Maple Lane
> needed a library? The council, with the mayor. Who pays for the books
> and the building? The town, from money grown-ups pay in taxes. Who
> keeps the books in order and helps you find one? The librarian. Who
> uses the library? Everyone in town. One service — library — needed a
> decision, money, a worker, and users. That is a community working
> together."

Key idea to say plainly: **People in authority lead and decide in a
place. A government makes rules for a community and provides services
everyone shares. And everyone — leaders, workers, neighbors, children —
plays a part.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult shows role cards; the learner matches roles to jobs orally, by
pointing, or by placing cards. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "Who leads a town or city?" *(the mayor)*
2. "Who puts out fires and rescues people?" *(a firefighter)*
3. "Name one service the government provides for everyone." *(school,
   road, park, library, clean water — accept any shared service)*
4. "The crossing guard helps children cross the street. Is the crossing
   guard a leader or a helper — and why does the job matter?" *(a helper
   whose job matters because children get to school safely; accept
   "helper" with any sensible reason)*
5. "What is one role a child plays in a community?" *(following rules,
   helping classmates, voting in class choices, keeping shared places
   clean — accept any sensible role)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws or places three role cards in a row — one person in
authority, one helper, one child — and dictates one sentence per card
for the adult to scribe: "___ helps our community by ___." The adult
reads each sentence back for the learner to approve.

## Application

Role-play: "A day in Maple Lane." The adult narrates: "The Maple Lane
park has litter everywhere after a windy weekend. Who does what?" The
learner picks a role card and acts out that role's part (the mayor
announces a cleanup day, the council decides the date, town workers
bring bins, neighbors pick up litter, children sort recycling). Then the
learner names one other role that must help too, and explains why one
role alone cannot clean the whole park.

## Exit check

Prompt: "What is a government, and name one service it provides."
Accept: the people and offices that make rules for a community (or
"the group that runs the town") plus one shared service (school, road,
park, library, clean water).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only three roles at first (teacher, principal,
  mayor); add the rest when three-role matching is solid. Let the
  learner point instead of speaking.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach each role with a picture and an
  action first (a whistle for the crossing guard, a book for the
  librarian), word card second; welcome home-language role words next to
  English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may act out a role, arrange
  picture cards, or record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner explains what would happen if one service
  disappeared for a week (no library, no trash pickup) and who would
  notice first — and why.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often think only the "boss" matters
("the mayor does everything"). Re-anchor: "The mayor decides with the
council, workers do the work, and neighbors help. One person cannot run
a town alone." A second common slip: thinking government services are
free because "nobody pays at the door." Re-anchor simply: "Grown-ups pay
money called taxes, and the town uses it for things everyone shares.
You don't pay each time, but the town still pays."
