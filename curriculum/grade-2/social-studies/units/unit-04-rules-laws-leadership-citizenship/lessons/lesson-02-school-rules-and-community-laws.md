# Lesson 02 — School Rules and Community Laws

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, crayons, rule-or-law
cards (adult-made: "raise your hand in class," "use quiet voices in the
library," "walk around the pool," "stop at a red light," "do not take
what belongs to someone else," "put trash in the bin at the park")

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn what a law is, how
a law is different from a rule, and how rules work in public places.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Name one rule from yesterday and what
it is for." Accept any rule with a purpose. If the learner is stuck,
model one: "Walk in the hallway — so nobody gets hurt."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "Yesterday we learned rules. Today we meet a special kind of rule:
a **law**. A law is a rule made by a **government** that applies to
**everyone** in that place. Your classroom rule applies in your
classroom. Your family rule applies in your home. A law applies to a
whole town, city, state, or country — to everyone there."

"Rules in public places work the same way as rules at school, but they
belong to everybody who uses that place. In the library, we use quiet
voices so everyone can read. At the pool, we walk so nobody slips. At
the park, we put trash in the bin so the park stays nice for the next
family. These are rules for public places. They tell us how to share a
place with strangers, not just with our class."

"Now the difference. The adult says: 'This is a practice story, not a
real town's rule.' In our practice town of Maple Lane, drivers stop at a
red light. Is that a classroom rule? No — it is a law, because the town's
government made it, and it applies to every driver in town. If a driver
breaks it, the town can give a penalty — a fine, or a ticket — because
laws have government penalties behind them. A classroom rule broken
means a classroom consequence, like losing a turn. A law broken means a
government consequence. That is the test: who made it, and who must
follow it?"

**Worked example 1 — rule or law?**

> Adult: "'Raise your hand in class.' Who made it? The teacher. Who
> must follow it? The class. So it is a **rule**. Now: 'Stop at a red
> light.' Who made it? The government. Who must follow it? Every driver
> in town. So it is a **law**. Ask the two questions every time: who
> made it, and who must follow it?"

**Worked example 2 — a public-place rule:**

> Adult: "At the Maple Lane library, the rule is 'use quiet voices.'
> Why? So everyone can read and think. Who must follow it? Everyone in
> the library — children, grown-ups, visitors. That is how rules work in
> public settings: the rule belongs to the place, and everyone in the
> place follows it, even people you have never met. Try one: at the pool,
> why do we walk instead of run? Because the floor is wet and running
> can make someone slip and get hurt. Safety — the same purpose as at
> school, in a place shared with strangers."

Key idea to say plainly: **A rule is made for one place or group. A law
is a rule made by a government for everyone in that place. Public-place
rules help strangers share a place safely and fairly.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult shows rule-or-law cards; the learner sorts them and answers
orally, by pointing, or by placing cards. (Hints in parentheses are for
the adult.)

1. "'Raise your hand in class.' Rule or law?" *(rule — made by the
   teacher, for the class)*
2. "'Stop at a red light.' Rule or law?" *(law — made by the
   government, for every driver)*
3. "'Use quiet voices in the library.' Where does it live, and who must
   follow it?" *(a public place; everyone in the library)*
4. "'Do not take what belongs to someone else.' Rule or law — and why?"
   *(law — taking what is not yours is against the law everywhere in
   town, and the government can punish it; accept "law, because it is
   for everyone" as full credit)*
5. "Name one public-place rule and tell me what it is for." *(accept any
   real public-place rule with a sensible purpose, e.g., "walk at the
   pool, so nobody slips")*

## Independent practice

The learner draws or places four cards in two columns — RULE and LAW —
with one example in each column they choose themselves (adult scribes
labels), then dictates one sentence: "A rule is ___. A law is ___."
Accept: "A rule is for one place or group. A law is made by the
government for everyone."

## Application

The learner becomes the "rule maker" for a pretend place: the Maple Lane
park. The adult asks, "What two rules should the park have, and why?"
The learner names two rules (for example, "put trash in the bin" and
"take turns on the swings") with a purpose for each. Then the adult asks
the test question: "Should one of these be a law for the whole town
instead?" The learner picks one and explains why (for example, "the
trash rule, because a clean park matters to everyone in town").

## Exit check

Prompt: "Is 'stop at a red light' a rule or a law? How do you know?"
Accept: law, because the government made it and every driver must
follow it (or any answer that names both parts of the test).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only two cards at first (one clear rule, one clear
  law); add public-place rules when the two-card sort is solid. Let the
  learner point instead of speaking.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach "rule" and "law" with pictures
  first (a classroom picture, a traffic-light picture), word cards
  second; welcome home-language words next to English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may sort picture cards, act
  out the rule or law, or record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner explains what happens when a rule is broken
  versus when a law is broken, in their own words, and invents a fair
  consequence for each.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often call every strict rule a law
("my teacher's rule must be a law because it is serious"). Re-anchor
with the two questions: "Who made it? Who must follow it? If the answer
is 'the government' and 'everyone in town,' it is a law. Otherwise it is
a rule — even a very important one." A second common slip: thinking laws
only exist far away ("in the capital"). Re-anchor: "Laws live in your
town too — the red light on your street is a law."
