# Lesson 01 — Why We Have Rules

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, crayons, rule cards
(adult-made: "listen when others speak," "walk in the hallway," "take
turns," "put toys away," "raise your hand," "say please and thank you")
or adult-drawn equivalents

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn why we have rules,
and you will name rules from home, school, the playground, and public
places — and say what each rule is for.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What is one rule in our house or
classroom?" Accept any real rule ("no running inside," "brush teeth").
If the learner is stuck, model one: "In our classroom, we raise our hand
before we speak. That is a rule. Can you think of one more?"

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "A **rule** is an agreement about how to act in a place. Rules are
not there to spoil fun. Rules are there for three big purposes. First,
rules keep people **safe**. Second, rules make things **fair**, so
everyone gets a turn. Third, rules help everyone **get along and learn**
together."

"Think about a game with no rules. If we play tag and there are no
rules, what happens? Someone might tag too hard. Someone might cry.
Someone might quit. Nobody has fun. Rules are what make the game work."

**Worked example 1 — one rule, three purposes:**

> Adult: "Here is a rule: 'Walk in the hallway.' Why do we have it?
> Safety first: if everyone runs in the hallway, someone can bump into
> someone and get hurt. Fairness second: walking means nobody pushes past
> everyone else. Getting along third: a quiet, walking hallway lets
> other classes keep learning. One rule can do all three jobs at once."

**Worked example 2 — the same rule in different places:**

> Adult: "Listen to these three rules. At home: 'Put your toys away
> after you play.' In class: 'Put your materials away after the lesson.'
> At the park: 'Put trash in the bin.' Different places, same idea:
> taking care of a shared place so the next person can use it too. Now
> you try: is 'take turns on the swing' a home rule, a school rule, or a
> playground rule? It is a playground rule — and it is also fair, because
> everyone waits the same way."

Key idea to say plainly: **Rules are agreements that keep us safe, keep
things fair, and help us get along and learn. Every place has rules.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult shows rule cards or names rules; the learner answers orally, by
pointing, or by sorting cards. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "Where does this rule live: home, classroom, playground, or public
   place? 'Raise your hand before you speak.'" *(classroom)*
2. "'Take turns on the slide.' What is this rule for — safety, fairness,
   or getting along?" *(fairness — and safety too; accept either with a
   reason)*
3. "'Hold a grown-up's hand in a parking lot.' What is this rule for?"
   *(safety)*
4. "Which of these is NOT a rule: 'wash your hands,' 'eat ice cream
   every day,' 'listen when others speak'?" *(eat ice cream every day —
   that is a wish, not an agreement for a place)*
5. "Name a rule at home and tell me what it is for." *(accept any real
   rule with a sensible purpose)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws or places four pictures — one rule for home, one for
the classroom, one for the playground, one for a public place (such as a
library or store) — and dictates one sentence per picture for the adult
to scribe: "The rule is ___. It is for ___." The adult reads each
sentence back for the learner to approve.

## Application

The learner plays "no-rules game" for one minute: the adult and learner
try to play a simple card-sorting game while deliberately ignoring
turns. Then the adult stops the game and asks, "What went wrong? What
rule would fix it?" The learner names one rule that would make the game
work (for example, "take turns") and the pair replays the game with that
rule.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Tell me one rule, where it lives, and what it is for." Accept:
any real rule with its place and a sensible purpose (for example,
"Walk in the hallway, at school, so nobody gets hurt").

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only two places at first (home and classroom); add
  playground and public places when two-place sorting is solid. Let the
  learner point to a card instead of speaking.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach each rule with a picture first,
  word card second; welcome home-language rule words next to English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may act out a rule, arrange
  picture cards, or record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner invents a rule for a brand-new place (a
  treehouse club, a spaceship) and explains which purpose it serves —
  safety, fairness, or getting along.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often think rules exist "because
grown-ups said so" and cannot name a purpose. Re-anchor: "Every rule has
a job. Ask: does it keep someone safe? Does it make things fair? Does
it help us get along?" A second common slip: calling every wish or
preference a rule ("eat ice cream every day"). Re-anchor: "A rule is an
agreement for a place. A wish is something one person wants."
