# Lesson 04 — Kinds of Historical Sources: Photos, Objects, Interviews, Letters

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the adult-prepared practice
sources below (all fictional, read aloud by the adult), paper, crayons,
word cards: source, photograph, object, interview, fact, opinion

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn four kinds of
historical sources — photographs, objects, interviews, and letters — and
how to ask three questions about any source: who made it, when, and where?

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "If you wanted to know what school was
like long ago, what could you look at?" Accept any source-like answer (an
old photo, an old book, asking a grandparent). If the learner is stuck,
model one: "You could look at an old photograph of a classroom. That
photograph is a source — something that gives information about the past."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "A **historical source** is something that gives information about
the past. Historians — people who study the past — use sources the way
detectives use clues. Here are four kinds. Remember: every source in this
lesson is a **practice source** the adult made up for this lesson. None
of them is a real person's photograph, letter, or story."

**Practice Source 1 — a photograph (described aloud; no real photo is
shown):**

> "An old black-and-white photograph of a small schoolhouse with a bell
> tower. Children in old-fashioned clothes stand in front of it. The
> photograph shows what the school looked like — but it cannot tell us
> what the children were thinking, or what happened after the picture was
> taken."

**Practice Source 2 — an object:**

> "A metal lunch pail, dented, with a handle. A child long ago carried
> lunch to school in it. The object shows what children carried — but it
> cannot tell us what was inside it on any particular day."

**Practice Source 3 — an interview (read aloud as a short fictional
excerpt):**

> "Ms. Rosa, a fictional neighbor in our practice story, remembers: 'I
> walked to the little schoolhouse every morning. The bell rang, and we
> all hurried inside.' An **interview** is asking a person questions and
> listening to their answers. It tells us what one person remembers — but
> memories can be fuzzy, and one person's memory is not the whole story."

**Practice Source 4 — a letter (read aloud, heading first):**

> "Heading: 'From Ana, Maple Lane, June 12.' The letter says: 'Dear
> friend, our school is small. We need more room for books. Please come
> and see our reading corner.' A letter can tell us who wrote it, when,
> and where — right from its own heading. The **maker** is Ana. The
> **date** is June 12. The **place** is Maple Lane. We found all three
> from information *inside the source itself* — we did not have to guess."

Say: "Now the three questions for any source: **Who made it? When was it
made? Where was it made?** And one more check: is it a **fact** or an
**opinion**? A **fact** can be checked — 'The schoolhouse had a bell
tower' can be checked in the photograph. An **opinion** is what someone
thinks or feels — 'The little schoolhouse was the best school' cannot be
checked the way a fact can. Both can appear in sources; only facts can
prove a claim."

**Worked example 1 — what a source can and cannot show:**

> Adult: "The lunch pail shows us what a child carried lunch in. Can it
> show us the child's favorite subject? No — a pail cannot tell us that.
> Every source can show some things and cannot show others. Good history
> work says both: what the source shows, and what it cannot show."

**Worked example 2 — fact or opinion:**

> Adult: "'Ana's letter is dated June 12.' Fact or opinion? Fact — I can
> check it by reading the heading. 'Ana's school was wonderful.' Fact or
> opinion? Opinion — wonderful is what someone thinks, not something I
> can count or measure. A letter can hold both facts and opinions at the
> same time."

Key idea to say plainly: **Photographs, objects, interviews, and letters
are kinds of historical sources. For any source, ask: who made it, when,
where — and what can it show, and what can't it? Facts can be checked;
opinions cannot.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult re-reads or re-shows each practice source; the learner answers
orally, by pointing, or by sorting cards. (Hints in parentheses are for
the adult.)

1. "Is the schoolhouse picture a photograph, an object, or an interview?"
   *(a photograph)*
2. "Is the lunch pail a photograph, an object, or a letter?" *(an
   object)*
3. "Who made (wrote) the letter? When? Where?" *(Ana; June 12; Maple
   Lane — all from the heading)*
4. "Fact or opinion: 'The schoolhouse had a bell tower.'" *(fact — it can
   be checked in the photograph)*
5. "Fact or opinion: 'The little school was the best school ever.'"
   *(opinion — best is what someone thinks)*

## Independent practice

The learner sorts four cards (photo, pail, talking person for interview,
envelope for letter) into kind piles and says one thing each source can
show. Then the learner answers: "What can the interview tell us that the
photograph cannot?" (Accept: what a person remembers or felt; what
happened over time, not just one moment.)

## Application

The adult shows a new practice item: a described drawing of the Maple Lane
park with swings, signed "Drawn by Sam, Maple Lane." The learner names
its kind (a drawing — a source, like a photograph, that shows what a
place looked like), and finds its maker (Sam) and place (Maple Lane) from
the signature. The adult notes the date is missing: "This source does not
tell us when. That is a question we cannot answer from this source alone."

## Exit check

Prompt: "Name two kinds of historical sources, and tell me the maker,
date, and place of Ana's letter." Accept: any two of photograph, object,
interview, letter; Ana, June 12, Maple Lane.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Handle a real everyday object (a lunchbox) while discussing
  the pail; keep the four kinds on a picture word wall all week. Let the
  learner point instead of speaking.
- **Reading/language access:** Every source is read aloud; the letter
  heading is pointed to word by word; home-language labels welcomed.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may sort picture cards, draw
  each kind, or record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner invents a fifth practice source (e.g., a
  school song, a map) and says what kind it is, what it could show, and
  what it could not show.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often treat every old thing as
"proof" of whatever they already believe ("this old photo proves school
was better"). Re-anchor: "A source is a clue, not a verdict. One photo
shows one moment from one angle. We need more than one clue before we
decide." A second common slip: calling opinions "lies." Re-anchor:
"An opinion is not a lie. It is what someone thinks or feels. We just
cannot use it as proof the way we use a fact."
