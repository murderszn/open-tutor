# Lesson 05 — A Local Change: Questions, Two Accounts, and the Likelier Reason

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, crayons, the practice
story and evidence cards below (all fictional, read aloud by the adult),
word cards: account, reason, evidence, change

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will ask questions about a
change in our pretend neighborhood, compare two different accounts of
that change, and pick the reason that the evidence supports best.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What is one question you would ask about
how our school got its library?" Accept any genuine question (who built
it, when, why). If the learner is stuck, model one: "I would ask: who
decided we needed a library? Questions are how history starts."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "Remember: this is a **practice story** about fictional Maple Lane.
It is not real history. In our story, Maple Lane once had a tiny reading
room. Then the town built a bigger library. That is a **change** — when
something becomes different over time. Good history questions about a
change sound like this: Who asked for it? Who built it? Who used it? Why
did it happen?"

**The two accounts (read aloud; an account is one person's telling of
what happened):**

> **Account 1 — a neighbor remembers:** "So many people wanted books
> that the little reading room was crowded every day. The town built a
> bigger library so everyone would have room to read."
>
> **Account 2 — a short note:** "A big storm broke the old reading room's
> roof. The town had to build a new library."

Say: "Two accounts of the same change — and they give different reasons!
An account is not automatically true just because someone said it. We
check accounts against **evidence** — information from sources that
supports an answer or a reason."

**The evidence cards (read aloud):**

> **Evidence A — a photograph (described):** "A photograph of the little
> reading room, full of people. Every chair is taken; people are standing
> along the walls with books."
>
> **Evidence B — a letter (read aloud):** "From many neighbors, Maple
> Lane: 'Please build us a bigger library. We need more room and more
> books for our children.'"
>
> **Evidence C — what our practice set does NOT have:** "Our practice
> set has no storm photograph, no broken-roof picture, and no repair note.
> Nothing in our set shows a storm breaking the roof."

**Worked example 1 — weighing the reasons:**

> Adult: "Possible reason 1: people wanted more books and room. Evidence
> A shows a crowded room. Evidence B asks for more room and books. Two
> pieces of evidence support reason 1. Possible reason 2: a storm broke
> the roof. Evidence for reason 2 in our set? None — no storm photo, no
> repair note. So reason 1 is **likelier** — more likely than reason 2 —
> *with the evidence we have*. If a new source arrived showing storm
> damage, we would think again. That is honest history work: we decide
> with the evidence we have, and we stay open to new evidence."

**Worked example 2 — asking a question that evidence can answer:**

> Adult: "'Who built the library?' is a good question, but our practice
> set cannot answer it — no source names a builder. 'Did many people
> want a bigger library?' is also a good question, and Evidence A and B
> *can* answer it: yes. So we ask questions, then check: can our sources
> answer this one, or do we need another source?"

Key idea to say plainly: **When accounts disagree, compare them with
evidence. The reason with supporting evidence is likelier — and a new
source could change our minds.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult re-reads accounts and evidence as needed; the learner answers
orally, by pointing, or by sorting cards. (Hints in parentheses are for
the adult.)

1. "What changed in Maple Lane's story?" *(a tiny reading room became a
   bigger library)*
2. "Ask one question about the change." *(any genuine question: who
   asked, who built, when, why — accept all; note whether sources can
   answer it)*
3. "Account 1 says the library was built because…?" *(so many people
   wanted books and room)*
4. "Account 2 says the library was built because…?" *(a storm broke the
   roof)*
5. "Which reason has evidence in our set, and what is that evidence?"
   *(reason 1 — the crowded-room photograph and/or the neighbors'
   letter)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws or points to the evidence card that supports reason 1
(the crowded room or the letter) and dictates one sentence (adult
scribes): "The bigger library was built because people wanted more room
and books. I know because ___." The learner fills the blank with one
piece of evidence.

## Application

The adult offers a new pair of possible reasons for a different change
(the park swings, from Lesson 02): "Reason X: children asked for swings.
Reason Y: the swings fell from the sky." The learner picks the likelier
reason (X) and explains why in one sentence (children asking is an
ordinary, checkable kind of reason; swings do not fall from the sky).
The adult names that silly reasons can be ruled out quickly, but serious
reasons need evidence.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Two accounts disagree about why the library was built. Which
reason is likelier, and what is one piece of evidence for it?" Accept:
reason 1 (people wanted more books and room), with the crowded-room
photograph or the neighbors' letter as evidence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Keep the two accounts on separate cards with a picture
  each (crowd vs. storm cloud with a question mark); let the learner point
  to the supported account instead of explaining. Adult scribes all
  sentences.
- **Reading/language access:** Every account and evidence card is read
  aloud; the sentence frame "I know because ___" is on the table.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may sort evidence cards under
  "supports reason 1" / "supports reason 2" / "supports neither," or
  record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner writes (or dictates) a second question the
  practice set cannot answer, and names what kind of source could answer
  it (e.g., a builder's note, a town record — adult explains these are
  for later grades to study for real).

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often pick the more exciting account
(the storm!) rather than the better-evidenced one. Re-anchor: "Exciting
is not the same as supported. We follow the evidence, not the drama."
A second common slip: treating "likelier" as "proven forever." Re-anchor:
"Likelier means: with the evidence we have right now. New evidence could
change our answer, and changing your mind with new evidence is good
history work, not failure."
