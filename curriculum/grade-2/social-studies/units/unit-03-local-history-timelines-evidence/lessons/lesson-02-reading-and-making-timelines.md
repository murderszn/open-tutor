# Lesson 02 — Reading a Timeline and Making Your Own

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** a long paper strip, crayons, four
picture cards for the Maple Lane practice timeline (schoolhouse, library,
swings, today), word cards: timeline, event, first, last

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn what a timeline is,
how to read one, and how to make one — a line that shows events in the
order they happened.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What comes first: being a baby, or being
in grade 2?" Accept "being a baby." If the learner is stuck, model one:
"First you were a baby. Next you grew. Last — right now — you are in
grade 2. That is an order."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "A **timeline** is a line that shows events in the order they
happened. The earliest event — the one that happened first, longest ago —
goes on one end. The latest event — the one closest to now — goes on the
other end. You read a timeline the way you read a line of words: from one
end to the other, in order."

Lay out the Maple Lane practice strip (adult-drawn or card-arranged), left
to right:

> **Maple Lane practice timeline (fictional — a practice story, not real
> history):** (1) First, a small schoolhouse opens. (2) Next, a library
> opens. (3) Then, swings are added to the park. (4) Last, today — the new
> school building is open and children learn there now.

Say: "The arrow on a timeline points toward later time — toward today and
the future. If I want the first event, I look at the start of the line.
If I want the last event, I look at the end of the line."

**Worked example 1 — reading the strip:**

> Adult: "What happened first on Maple Lane's strip? I look at the start
> of the line: the schoolhouse opens. That is first. What happened last?
> I look at the end of the line: today, the new school building. What
> happened between the library and today? The swings were added to the
> park. I found it by reading left to right, in order — never jumping
> around."

**Worked example 2 — fixing a mixed-up strip:**

> Adult: "Here are the same four events, all mixed up: swings, today,
> schoolhouse, library. I ask: which had to happen first? The schoolhouse
> — the town's first school. Then the library. Then the swings. Last is
> today. Now the strip reads: schoolhouse, library, swings, today. I
> checked my order by asking, 'Does each event come after the one before
> it?' If an event is out of place, the story stops making sense — the
> swings cannot be added to a park before the town exists."

Key idea to say plainly: **A timeline puts events in order on a line.
Earlier events go at the start, later events go at the end, and you read
it from start to end.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult points to the practice strip; the learner answers orally, by
pointing, or by moving cards. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "Point to the first event on the strip." *(the schoolhouse opens)*
2. "Point to the last event." *(today — the new school building)*
3. "What happened next after the library opened?" *(swings were added to
   the park)*
4. "Did the library open before or after the schoolhouse?" *(after)*
5. Adult swaps two cards. "Is this strip in order now? How do you know?"
   *(no — the events are out of sequence; the learner names the swap)*

## Independent practice

The learner arranges the four Maple Lane cards on their own strip in order
and labels each with a number (1, 2, 3, 4) or a picture (adult scribes
words on request). Then the learner reads the strip aloud, start to end.

## Application

The learner builds a personal timeline strip with 3–4 events they choose
(a first day, a move, learning to ride, starting grade 2 — only events
the learner wants to share). The adult scribes labels. The strip stays in
private storage. The learner points to "now" on their strip and names the
event closest to now.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Here is a new strip: library, schoolhouse, today, swings. Is it
in order? Fix it." Accept: no, it is out of order; the fixed order is
schoolhouse, library, swings, today — with the learner pointing to each
event as they name it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Start with three events instead of four; number the
  positions on the strip (1, 2, 3) before placing cards. Let the learner
  point instead of speaking.
- **Reading/language access:** Picture cards first, words second; the
  adult reads every label aloud; home-language labels welcomed.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may build the timeline on the
  floor and walk it in order, or record an audio reading the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner adds a "future" end to the strip — one thing
  they hope will happen — and explains why it belongs after "today," not
  before it.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often read a timeline by picking the
picture they like best first, or by reading right to left. Re-anchor with
a finger walk: "Start here, at the earliest event. Walk your finger along
the line. Each step is later in time." A second common slip: thinking the
spaces between events must be equal — that a long gap means "nothing
happened." Re-anchor: "A timeline shows order, not equal time. Some gaps
are long, some are short; the order is what matters."
