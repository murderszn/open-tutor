# Lesson 03 — Reading a Community Map: Maple Lane

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the printed [Maple Lane
neighborhood map](../assets/maple-lane-map.png) *(AI-generated
illustration, inspected 2026-10-10 — the full text-only map description
below carries every question in this lesson)*, crayons, paper

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will read a real community map —
our pretend neighborhood, Maple Lane. You will find places on it and use
direction words to describe how to get from one place to another.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What are the two steps for finding
something on a map?" Accept: check the key (or read the labels) first, then
scan the map. If stuck, re-model Lesson 01's key habit.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "Reading a map means finding places and figuring out how they connect.
Good map readers always do the same two steps. Step one: find out how the
map explains itself — look for the **key**, the box that explains the
symbols. Step two: **scan** the map to find the place."

Introduce the map: "This is **Maple Lane**, a pretend neighborhood made for
practice — it is not a real place. Title first: the title says 'Maple
Lane.' Good — now I know what this map shows. This map is a little unusual:
it has no key box. Instead, every place is **labeled directly** — the words
are written right under each building. So step one here is: read the
labels. Then step two: scan."

Now read the map's layout together (adult reads slowly; learner follows
with a finger — on the picture if printed, or on the text description):

> **Maple Lane map — text description (the full map in words):** The top of
> the map is north; the compass rose in the top-right corner points north
> up. Two streets cross at the center: **Maple Street** runs east–west
> across the map; **Oak Avenue** runs north–south through the middle. The
> four blocks around the crossing hold these places: the **School** is in
> the northwest block — north of Maple Street and west of Oak Avenue. The
> **Fire Station** is in the northeast block — north of Maple Street and
> east of Oak Avenue. The **Library** is in the southwest block — south of
> Maple Street and west of Oak Avenue. The **Pond** is also in the
> southwest, just south of the library. The **Park** is in the southeast
> block — south of Maple Street and east of Oak Avenue. The **Grocery
> Store** is also in the southeast block, east of the park. **Houses** fill
> the remaining spaces. Every place is labeled directly with its name;
> there is no separate key box on this map.

Say: "To describe a **route** — the way from one place to another — use the
compass rose and direction words: 'Walk **south** on Oak Avenue, then turn
**east** on Maple Street.'"

**Worked example 1 — finding a place, modeled out loud:**

> Adult: "I need to find the Fire Station. Step one: how does this map
> explain itself? No key box — but every place is labeled, so I read the
> labels. Step two: scan the labels… School… Library… there! 'Fire Station'
> — in the northeast block. And I can describe where it is: the Fire
> Station is *north* of Maple Street and *east* of Oak Avenue. Two steps:
> read the labels, then scan."

**Worked example 2 — describing a route, modeled out loud:**

> Adult: "Now a route: from the School to the Library. The School is in the
> northwest block. The Library is in the southwest block — that is *south*
> of the school, on the same street, Oak Avenue. So my directions are:
> 'Start at the School. Walk **south** on Oak Avenue. Cross Maple Street.
> The Library is on your right, west of Oak Avenue.' Three steps, each with
> a direction word, in order. That is how you give map directions."

Key idea to say plainly: **To read a community map: first find how the map
explains itself (key or labels), then scan to find a place. To give
directions: name the starting place, then give each step in order using
north, south, east, or west.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each prompt. The learner reads the labels, then the map
(picture or the text description above).

1. "Find the Park. Is it north or south of Maple Street?" *(south;
   southeast block)*
2. "Point to the Fire Station. Is it east or west of Oak Avenue?" *(east;
   northeast block)*
3. "Is the Library east or west of Oak Avenue?" *(west)*
4. "Give me directions from the School to the Grocery Store." *(walk south
   on Oak Avenue, cross Maple Street, turn east — left — on Maple Street;
   the store is in the southeast block)*
5. "A visitor is at the Fire Station and wants to see the pond. Give two
   steps with direction words." *(walk south on Oak Avenue, cross Maple
   Street, then go west past the library — the pond is just south of it)*

## Independent practice

The learner traces (or describes, if using the text version) a route from
the Park to the Library with a crayon, then dictates the steps to the
adult: "Start at the Park. Walk ___ on ___. Then ___."

## Application

"Your friend is new to Maple Lane and is standing at the Fire Station.
They are hungry. Which place should they visit, and what directions would
you give?" (The Grocery Store: walk south on Oak Avenue, cross Maple
Street, turn east on Maple Street — the store is in the southeast block,
east of the park.)

## Exit check

Prompt: "Point to the Pond. Now tell me: what are the two steps good map
readers always do first?" Accept: look for the key — or read the labels, on
a map like this one — then scan; the pond is in the southwest, just south
of the library.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Number the steps on a strip ("1. key or labels, 2. scan");
  the learner touches each number as they do it. Reduce choices: "Is the
  park north or south of Maple Street?" (two options).
- **Reading/language access:** Read the map description aloud in short
  chunks; the learner repeats the place name and points (or taps the
  matching sentence).
- **Alternate response mode:** Pointing, tracing with a finger, or whispering
  answers for the adult to voice.
- **Extension:** The learner gives a *wrong* route on purpose ("Walk north
  from the School to the Library") and the adult has to catch the error —
  then they fix it together. Or: the learner adds one new place to the map
  (a bakery, a bus stop), draws its symbol, and writes its label.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often navigate by "near the picture of
the tree" instead of by streets and direction words — they match pictures,
not routes. Re-anchor: cover the labels with sticky notes and ask for the
route using *only* street names and direction words. A second slip: giving
directions backward (from the destination instead of the start). Model the
sentence frame every time: "Start at ___. Walk ___ on ___. Then ___."
**Map note:** the Maple Lane map is a fictional, AI-generated practice
illustration (see [assets/README.md](../assets/README.md)); never present
it as a real place. Note that this particular map labels places directly
rather than using a key box — name that difference out loud so the
key-first habit from Lesson 01 stays intact for maps that do have keys.
All questions in this lesson are answerable from the text-only description
above.
