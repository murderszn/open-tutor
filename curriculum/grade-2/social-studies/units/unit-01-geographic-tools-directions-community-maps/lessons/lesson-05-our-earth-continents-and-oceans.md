# Lesson 05 — Our Earth: Continents and Oceans

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** a globe (or inflatable globe),
blue and green crayons, paper, the unit's vocabulary word cards (globe,
continent, ocean)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will use a globe — a round model
of Earth — to find the seven **continents** (the very big pieces of land)
and the five **oceans** (the very big areas of water), and you will compare
two places you know.

**Readiness check:** Adult spins the globe and asks, "What colors do you
see most — blue or green?" Accept: blue. If stuck, model: "Most of Earth is
covered by water — that is why the globe looks so blue."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "A **continent** is one of Earth's seven very large pieces of land.
An **ocean** is one of Earth's five very large areas of water. There is
much more water than land — that is why our globe looks mostly blue."

Teach the seven continents with a chant, pointing to each on the globe as
you say it: "**North America, South America, Africa, Europe, Asia,
Australia, Antarctica.**" Say it twice. Have the learner echo.

Then the five oceans: "**Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic, Southern.**"
Point to each. "The Pacific is the biggest ocean. The Arctic Ocean is near
the North Pole — it is very cold. The Southern Ocean wraps around
Antarctica."

Name where we live: "We live on the continent of **North America**. The
**Pacific Ocean** touches North America on the west side, the **Atlantic
Ocean** touches it on the east side, and the **Arctic Ocean** is to the
north."

Now the comparison: "Places have two kinds of characteristics — things that
make them what they are. **Environmental** characteristics are about nature:
weather, land, water, plants, animals. **Cultural** characteristics are
about people: the languages they speak, the foods they eat, the holidays
they celebrate, the music they play."

**Worked example 1 — finding a continent and its oceans, modeled out
loud:**

> Adult: "Watch me find Australia. I spin the globe slowly… here is a
> medium-sized continent all by itself, south of Asia. That is **Australia**.
> Now the oceans around it: this big water on its east side is the
> **Pacific Ocean**, and this water on its west side is the **Indian
> Ocean**. I found the land first, then I named the water touching it."

**Worked example 2 — comparing two familiar places, modeled out loud:**

> Adult: "Let me compare two places I know: our town, and my cousin's town
> near the mountains. *Environmental:* our town is flat with a river; her
> town has tall mountains and snow in winter — the land and weather are
> different. *Cultural:* in our town many families speak Spanish at home and
> we have a big parade in spring; in her town most families speak English
> and they have a winter sledding festival — the people-parts are different
> too. Same Earth, different characteristics."

Key idea to say plainly: **Earth has seven continents and five oceans.
Every place has environmental characteristics (nature: weather, land, water)
and cultural characteristics (people: languages, foods, holidays).**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult spins the globe; the learner points and names.

1. "Point to North America. Which ocean is on its west side?" *(Pacific)*
2. "Point to Africa. Is it north or south of Europe?" *(south)*
3. "Find the biggest ocean. What is its name?" *(Pacific)*
4. "Point to Antarctica. Which ocean wraps around it?" *(Southern)*
5. "Name one environmental characteristic of a place you know." *(weather,
   land, water, plants, or animals — any accurate one)*
6. "Name one cultural characteristic of a place you know." *(language,
   food, holiday, music — any accurate one)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws a simple Earth: a blue circle (oceans) with green blobs
for at least **four** labeled continents (adult scribes labels on request).
The drawing does not need to be accurate in shape — naming and the
land/water idea are the goals.

## Application

"Two places: our town and a town a relative or friend lives in (the learner
chooses, spoken only — nothing is written into the project). Tell me one
way the *nature* is different and one way the *people-parts* are different."
The adult scribes the learner's two sentences if the learner wishes.

## Exit check

Prompt: "How many continents does Earth have? Name three. How many oceans?
Name two." Accept: seven continents (any three correct); five oceans (any
two correct).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Put small dot stickers on the seven continents before the
  lesson; the learner touches each sticker as they chant the names. Reduce
  the naming load: "Point to the continent we live on" before naming others.
- **Reading/language access:** Chant the lists to a clapping rhythm; pair
  each name with its globe location before any writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** Pointing, chanting along, or whispering names
  for the adult to confirm.
- **Extension:** The learner finds the continent that is *almost entirely*
  covered in ice (Antarctica) and explains why almost no people live there
  (environmental reason). Or: the learner names which two continents touch
  each other (North and South America; Europe and Asia; Africa and Asia).

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** "The ocean is the blue part *on* the map" —
learners sometimes think each blue patch is a different ocean, or that
oceans are small like the pond on the Maple Lane map. Re-anchor with the
globe: oceans are enormous and connected — "all the blue is one giant
connected water, and we give its big parts names." A second slip:
confusing *continent* with *country* ("Texas is a continent"). Re-anchor:
"Continents are the biggest land pieces — countries are smaller pieces
*inside* them. Our country is inside North America." Keep the two-places
comparison spoken and general; never record a learner's family details,
addresses, or personal stories in any repo file.
