# Lesson 04 — Maps, Globes, and Photographs

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** a globe, a flat map (the Maple Lane
map or a world map), one or two photographs of a familiar place (e.g., the
school or a park — adult-supplied prints, no learner photos), paper,
crayons

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will compare three geographic
tools — a map, a globe, and a photograph of the same place — and decide
what each one shows *best*.

**Readiness check:** Adult holds up the three tools and asks, "Which one of
these is round like a ball?" Accept: the globe. If stuck, let the learner
hold each one.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "Geographers — people who study places — use different tools for
different jobs. We will compare three."

Hold up the **map**: "A **map** is a flat drawing that shows where things
are. It is easy to carry and fold, and it can show tiny details of a small
place — every street in Maple Lane fits on one page. But a flat map cannot
show that Earth is round."

Hold up the **globe**: "A **globe** is a round model of Earth. It shows
Earth's true shape — round, like a ball — and it shows how the continents
and oceans really sit next to each other. But you can only see half of a
globe at once, and it cannot show tiny details like one street."

Hold up the **photograph**: "A **photograph** is a picture taken with a
camera. It shows what a place *really looks like* — the colors, the trees,
the people. But a photo only shows one view from one spot. It does not show
street names, and it does not show what is behind the camera."

**Worked example 1 — which tool shows Earth's shape best:**

> Adult: "Question: which tool shows best that Earth is round? Let me think
> out loud. The photograph shows one schoolyard — it cannot show the whole
> Earth. The flat map shows the whole world on one page, but it is flat —
> Earth is not flat. The globe is round, just like Earth. So the **globe**
> shows Earth's shape best, *because it has the same shape as Earth*."

**Worked example 2 — which tool shows the way to the library best:**

> Adult: "Question: a visitor wants to walk from the school to the library.
> Which tool helps most? The photograph shows what the school looks like,
> but no street names — the visitor would still be lost. The globe shows
> whole continents — far too big for one neighborhood. The **map** shows the
> streets, the labels, and the compass rose — exactly what the visitor needs.
> So the map wins this job, *because it shows streets and directions for a
> small place*."

Key idea to say plainly: **No tool is best at everything. The globe shows
Earth's true round shape. The map shows streets and directions for a small
place. The photograph shows what one spot really looks like.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each job. The learner picks **map**, **globe**, or
**photograph** and gives one reason.

1. "Show that Earth is round like a ball." *(globe — same shape as Earth)*
2. "Find the way from the park to the grocery store." *(map — streets and
   key)*
3. "Show what the school's front door really looks like." *(photograph —
   real colors and details)*
4. "Show all seven continents at once in their true shapes." *(globe —
   round like Earth; a flat map stretches them)*
5. "Show where the pond is in the neighborhood." *(map — small place, needs
   streets)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws the *same* small place twice on one page: once **as a
map** (top-down, with at least one symbol and a label) and once **as a
photograph** (what it looks like from the front, with colors and details).
The adult labels which is which if the learner dictates.

## Application

"Three visitors ask for help: one wants to see what the park looks like in
fall; one wants to walk to the fire station; one wants to know which ocean
is biggest. Match each visitor to the right tool and explain your match."
*(photo — real look of one spot; map — streets and route; globe — whole
Earth, oceans in true shape)*

## Exit check

Prompt: "Tell me one thing a map shows better than a photograph, and one
thing a photograph shows better than a map." Accept any accurate pair, e.g.:
a map shows street names and directions; a photo shows real colors and what
things look like.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Lay the three tools in a row and attach a job card to each
  ("find a street," "see Earth's shape," "see real colors"); the learner
  matches new job cards to the row.
- **Reading/language access:** Sentence frame: "The ___ shows ___ best
  because ___." The adult supplies the "because" starter if needed.
- **Alternate response mode:** Pointing to the chosen tool; drawing the
  answer.
- **Extension:** "What is one thing *none* of the three tools can show?"
  (e.g., what it *smells* like, what happened there yesterday, how the
  people feel). Discuss why. Or: the learner photographs — with the adult's
  camera — no; instead the learner *describes* a photo they would take to
  show a visitor the classroom.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** "The map is wrong because Earth is round" — or
the reverse, thinking the flat world map *is* Earth's shape. Re-anchor: the
map is not wrong; it is a *flattened picture*, useful for small places and
carrying around, and it stretches big shapes. Spin the globe and hold the
map side by side: "Same Earth, two pictures." A second slip: thinking a
photograph "shows everything." Cover half a photo with your hand: "What
can't we see now? Photos always leave something out — what is behind the
camera, what is around the corner."
