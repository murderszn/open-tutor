# Lesson 01 — The Parts of a Map

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, crayons, the unit's
vocabulary word cards (map, symbol, key, label, title), a printed sample map
**with a key box** (picture-book map or an adult-drawn one — the Maple Lane
map labels its places directly and has no key box, so save it for Lesson 03)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn the five parts every
good map needs — a title, symbols, a key, labels, and (starting next lesson)
a compass rose — and you will practice finding each part on a real map.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What is a picture that shows where things
are?" Accept anything close to "a map." If the learner is stuck, model one:
"A map is a drawing that shows where things are — like a drawing of our
classroom that shows the door and the desks."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Hold up a map — any map. Say: "A **map** is a drawing that shows where
things are. It is not a photograph. A photograph shows what a place *looks
like*; a map shows *where things are*."

Point to the top of the map. "The **title** tells what the map shows. This
title says 'Maple Lane.' Now I know: this map is about
our neighborhood, not about the whole world."

Point to a small picture on the map. "This little tree picture is a
**symbol**. A **symbol** is a small picture on a map that stands for a real
thing. The mapmaker cannot draw a whole real tree, so they draw a tiny tree
symbol instead."

Point to the key box. "The **key** shows each symbol and what it means.
This box is also called a *legend*. When I forget what a symbol means, I
look at the key."

Point to a word on the map. "These words are **labels**. A **label** names a
place or thing on the map. This label says 'library,' so I know this
building is the library."

**Worked example 1 — reading a map out loud, slowly:**

> Adult: "I want to find the pond on this map. First I look at the key.
> The key shows a blue wavy shape and says *pond*. A symbol stands for a
> real thing, and the key tells me what it means. Now I scan the map for
> that blue wavy shape… there! In the corner. That must be the pond. I did
> it in two steps: key first, then scan."

**Worked example 2 — what happens when a part is missing:**

> Adult: "Here is a map with no title. Hmm. It shows houses and a park and
> a store… but *whose* neighborhood is it? I cannot tell. That is the
> title's job — it tells what the map shows. A map without a title is like
> a book without a cover: you have to guess what it is about."

Key idea to say plainly: **A map needs five parts: a title that tells what
it shows, symbols that stand for real things, a key that explains the
symbols, labels that name places, and a compass rose that shows directions.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult points; the learner names the part and says what it does. (Hints in
parentheses are for the adult.)

1. Point to the title. "What does the title tell us?" *(what the map shows)*
2. Point to one symbol. "What is a symbol?" *(a small picture that stands
   for a real thing)*
3. Cover the key with your hand. "Why do I need to see the key?" *(it
   explains what the symbols mean)*
4. Point to a label. "What is this word doing on the map?" *(naming a place)*
5. Adult points to a symbol not yet named. "Use the key. What real thing
   does this symbol stand for?" *(learner reads the key and answers)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws **two** of their own map symbols on paper (for example, a
symbol for "my bed" and a symbol for "the front door") and builds a tiny
key box: symbol on the left, label on the right. (Adult scribes labels on
request.)

## Application

Look at any printed map in a picture book or on the Maple Lane map. The
learner finds the title and reads it aloud, then finds one symbol and uses
the key to say what it stands for.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Show me the key on this map. What does the key do, and what would
happen if the map had no key?" Accept: the key explains the symbols; without
it we would have to guess what each symbol means.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Pre-draw the key box with one symbol already in it; the
  learner adds a second. Let the learner point instead of speaking.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach each word with the object first ("this
  box is the key"), word card second; welcome home-language labels next to
  English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may answer every check by
  pointing, or record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner invents a symbol for something tricky (like
  "the noisy hallway") and explains to the adult why their symbol makes
  sense.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often think the symbol *is* the thing —
that the little tree on the map is a tiny tree, or that the map is as big
as the real place. Re-anchor: "The symbol is a *stand-in*. The map is much
smaller than the real neighborhood; everything is drawn tiny so it all
fits." A second common slip: reading symbols but never checking the key.
Insist on the key-first habit from day one — it is the routine Lessons 03
and the project depend on.
