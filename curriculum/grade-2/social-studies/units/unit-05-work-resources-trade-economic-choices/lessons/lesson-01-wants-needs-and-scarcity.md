# Lesson 01 — Wants, Needs, and Scarcity

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, crayons, picture cards
(water, food, a home, warm clothes, a toy car, a picture book, a bunch of
flowers, a pet puppy), two labeled piles or boxes: NEED and WANT

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn two words — **want**
and **need** — and one big idea: **scarcity**. Scarcity means there is not
enough of everything for everyone to have all they want, so we have to
choose.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What is one thing you need every day?
What is one thing you want but could live without?" Accept any true need
(water, food, sleep, a home) and any true want (a toy, a game). If the
learner is stuck, model one: "I need water to drink. I want a new hat,
but I can live without it."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "A **need** is something you must have to live and stay healthy and
safe. Water is a need. Food is a need. A home is a need. Warm clothes in
cold weather are a need. A **want** is something you would like to have,
but you can live without it. A toy car is a want. A bunch of flowers is a
want. A second picture book, when you already have books, is a want."

"Now the big idea. **Scarcity** means there is not enough of something
for everyone to have all they want. There are not enough toy cars in the
whole world for every child to have every toy car. There is not enough
time in one day to do everything we want to do. Because of scarcity, we
have to make a **choice** — we pick one thing when we cannot pick them
all."

**Worked example 1 — sort with a reason:**

> Adult: "Here are three cards: a glass of water, a toy car, a warm coat
> in winter. Water goes in NEED, because our bodies must have water to
> live. The toy car goes in WANT, because it is fun but we can live
> without it. The warm coat goes in NEED, because in cold weather we must
> stay warm to stay healthy. See how I gave a reason for each card? A
> reason tells *why* it is a need or a want."

**Worked example 2 — scarcity means choosing:**

> Adult: "Imagine the market has only one toy car left, and two children
> want it. There is not enough for both — that is scarcity. One child
> gets the toy car, and the other child must choose something else. Now
> imagine you have one afternoon. You want to play outside *and* read a
> book *and* help bake bread. There is not enough time for all three, so
> you must choose. Scarcity is not only about things — it is also about
> time. Whenever there is not enough, we choose."

Key idea to say plainly: **A need is something you must have to live and
stay healthy and safe. A want is something you would like to have. Because
of scarcity — not enough for everything — we have to choose.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult shows cards or names items; the learner answers orally, by pointing,
or by sorting cards into the NEED and WANT piles. (Hints in parentheses
are for the adult.)

1. "A drink of water on a hot day — want or need?" *(need — our bodies
   must have water)*
2. "A pet puppy — want or need?" *(want — lovely, but we can live
   without it; accept "need" only if the learner argues it is a service
   animal — then note the difference and move on)*
3. "Food for dinner — want or need?" *(need)*
4. "A bunch of flowers — want or need?" *(want)*
5. "Warm shoes in the snow — want or need? And why is there scarcity
   here?" *(need — feet must stay warm and dry; scarcity: the shop has
   only three pairs, and four children want them, so one child must
   choose something else)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws or picks two cards — one need and one want — and says
one sentence about each ("Water is a need because my body must have it";
"A toy car is a want because I can live without it"). The adult scribes
the sentences if the learner dictates them.

## Application

The learner looks around the room and names one real need they can see
(a water bottle, a coat) and one real want (a game, a decoration), and
says what would happen if there were not enough of the need for everyone
("We would have to share it, or take turns, or choose who gets it first").
The adult keeps the frame kind: scarcity is a problem to solve together,
not a reason to blame anyone.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Is a picture book a want or a need? And finish this sentence:
'Scarcity means ___.'" Accept: a picture book is a want (unless the
learner argues it is the only book for learning to read — then accept the
reasoned answer); scarcity means there is not enough of something for
everyone to have all they want, so we must choose.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only two cards at first (water = need, toy = want);
  add more cards when the two-card sort is solid. Let the learner point
  instead of speaking.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach each word with a picture first
  ("this glass of water is a need"), word card second; welcome
  home-language words for want and need next to English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may sort picture cards, act
  out needing water, or record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner finds one thing that can be *both* a want
  and a need (shoes: need in winter, want when they are a fancy second
  pair) and explains what changes the answer — the situation, not the
  thing itself.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often sort by "I like it a lot" (a
favorite toy becomes a "need") or by "I use it every day" (a favorite
game becomes a "need"). Re-anchor: "A need is about living and staying
healthy and safe, not about liking something a lot. You can love a toy
and still live without it." A second common slip: treating scarcity as
"there is none left at all." Re-anchor: "Scarcity means *not enough for
everyone to have all they want* — there may be some, just not enough for
everybody." Note for the adult: wants and needs are partly situational
(shoes in snow vs. shoes as fashion); accept reasoned answers and praise
the reason, not only the pile.
