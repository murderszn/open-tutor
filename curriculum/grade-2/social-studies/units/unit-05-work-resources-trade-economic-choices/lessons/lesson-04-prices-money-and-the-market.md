# Lesson 04 — Prices, Money, and the Market

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the printed market illustration,
10 play coins, blank price tags and a marker, the fixed price list on a
card (apple 2 · bread 3 · flowers 2 · book 4 · toy car 5 · haircut 3)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will read **prices** at a
market, learn what **money** does, and work out what it **costs** to make
something. You will use the Maple Lane market picture.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "How many play coins do we start with?
How many coins is an apple? How many coins is a loaf of bread?" Accept
the fixed numbers (10 coins; apple 2; bread 3). If the learner is stuck,
count the coins on the table together.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "A **market** is a place where buyers and sellers meet to trade.
Look at our market picture. A **price** is how many coins — or how much
money — something costs. The price is written on a **price tag** so
everyone can see it. **Money** — coins and paper bills — is what people
use to buy and sell things. Without money, people would have to trade
one thing for another thing every time — that is called barter, and it is
much harder: the baker would have to find someone who has exactly what
she wants and wants exactly her bread."

"Look at the picture with me. This is an AI-generated practice picture of
our pretend Maple Lane market — not a photograph of a real place."

**Text-only alternative (read this aloud if the picture is not used, or
if the learner cannot see it):** A wooden market stall under a striped
awning. On the table, from left to right: a basket of red apples with a
blank white price tag; a stack of brown bread loaves with a blank white
price tag; a bunch of pink, yellow, and purple flowers with a blank white
price tag; a glass jar labeled SAVE, half full of gold coins; and a stack
of three picture books with a blank white price tag. Behind the table
stands a smiling baker in a white apron and a white cap, holding out a
loaf of bread. In front of the table stands a child in a yellow shirt
holding a cloth bag. Green trees and a smiling sun are in the background.
Every question below can be answered from this description alone.

"Now the prices. In our pretend market the tags say: apple 2 coins,
bread 3 coins, flowers 2 coins, book 4 coins. (The toy car, 5 coins, and
the haircut, 3 coins, are sold at the next stall — they are on our price
list but not in this picture.)"

**Worked example 1 — reading prices and paying:**

> Adult: "You have 10 coins. You want a loaf of bread (3 coins) and a
> bunch of flowers (2 coins). 3 + 2 = 5 coins. You hand the baker 5
> coins. 10 − 5 = 5 coins left. The price tag told you the price before
> you bought, and the money let you pay without trading something else.
> That is what money does: it makes buying and selling easy and fair,
> because everyone can see the price."

**Worked example 2 — what it costs to make a loaf:**

> Adult: "The baker sells a loaf for 3 coins. But making it is not free.
> The flour costs 1 coin. The oven and the work time cost 1 coin. So the
> **cost of production** — what it costs to make the loaf — is 1 + 1 = 2
> coins. The baker sells the loaf for 3 coins. 3 − 2 = 1 coin left over.
> That 1 coin helps pay for the shop and lets the baker keep baking. If
> the loaf sold for only 1 coin, the baker could not keep making bread —
> the cost would be more than the price."

Key idea to say plainly: **A price tells you what something costs.
Money lets buyers and sellers trade easily. Making something costs
something too — flour, time, and tools — and the price has to cover
that cost.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult uses the illustration or its text alternative, the price list, and
10 play coins. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "From the picture (or the text alternative): name two goods for sale
   at the stall." *(Any two of: apples, bread, flowers, picture books.)*
2. "What does the SAVE jar hold, and what word is on its label?"
   *(Gold coins; the word SAVE.)*
3. "You buy an apple (2) and a book (4). How many coins do you spend?
   How many are left from 10?" *(2 + 4 = 6 spent; 10 − 6 = 4 left.)*
4. "The baker's loaf costs 2 coins to make and sells for 3 coins. How
   many coins are left over? What does that coin help pay for?"
   *(3 − 2 = 1 coin; it helps pay for the shop and lets the baker keep
   baking.)*
5. "Why is money easier than trading a loaf of bread for a haircut every
   time?" *(With money, the baker can sell bread for coins and then use
   the coins to buy a haircut later; with barter, she would have to find
   someone who wants bread and has a haircut to give right now.)*

## Independent practice

The learner writes (or the adult scribes) price tags for three goods from
the fixed list, places them on the table, and "sells" them to the adult,
counting the coins for one sale out loud ("The book is 4 coins. You gave
me 4 coins. Thank you."). Then the learner answers: "What did it cost
the baker to make the loaf — 2 coins — and what did she sell it for — 3
coins?"

## Application

The learner sets up a tiny market at home or in the classroom with three
real objects (a book, a toy, a snack) and blank price tags, and explains
to the adult why each price is what it is ("The book costs more than the
apple because it took more work to make"). Prices stay in play coins;
nothing real is bought or sold.

## Exit check

Prompt: "An apple costs 2 coins and a loaf of bread costs 3 coins. You
have 10 coins. You buy one of each. How many coins are left? And what is
the baker's cost to make one loaf?" Accept: 2 + 3 = 5 spent, 10 − 5 = 5
coins left; the baker's cost is 2 coins (flour 1 + oven and time 1).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only two prices at first (apple 2 + bread 3 = 5);
  add more when two-item adding is solid. Let the learner count real
  coins on the table for every sum and difference.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach price, money, and market with
  objects first (a real apple, real coins, a real tag), word cards
  second; welcome home-language words next to English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may point to the picture,
  move coins, or record an audio answer the adult keeps. The text
  alternative answers every image question.
- **Extension:** The learner explains what would happen if the baker
  raised the bread price to 5 coins ("fewer people might buy it, because
  5 coins is a lot") — a first, gentle step toward the idea that prices
  change what people choose. Do not teach supply/demand curves; stay
  with the concrete choice.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often think the price *is* the cost
of making something ("the bread costs 3 coins to make"). Re-anchor:
"The price is 3 coins. The cost of *making* it is 2 coins. The 1 coin
left over is not the same as the price." A second common slip: thinking
money itself is the goal ("the baker wants coins, not bread"). Re-anchor:
"Coins are a tool for trading. The baker wants income so she can buy
flour, pay for the shop, and buy what her family needs." A third slip:
reading the picture as a real place. Re-anchor every time: "This is a
pretend picture made by a computer program to help us practice. Our
prices are pretend too." Note for the adult: the illustration's price
tags are intentionally blank — the learner reads prices from the fixed
price list, never from the picture — so no garbled numbers can teach a
wrong price. All sums in this lesson are within 10 and were re-checked
against the answer key.
