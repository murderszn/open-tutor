# Lesson 03 — Work, Skills, and Income

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, crayons, picture cards
(baker, farmer, teacher, haircutter, bus driver, librarian, builder),
word cards: work, skill, tool, income, producer, consumer, good, service

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how people earn
money: they do **work**, and they are paid **income** for it. To do their
work well, people need **skills** — things they learned to do — and
**tools** — things that help them do the work.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Name one person who works in our
neighborhood. What do they do?" Accept any worker (teacher, baker,
driver, librarian). Then: "What do they need to know to do that job?"

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "**Work** is a job people do. A baker bakes bread. A farmer grows
apples. A teacher teaches. A haircutter cuts hair. People do work, and
for their work they are paid **income** — money they earn. Income is how
grown-ups get the money they use to buy food, pay for a home, and save."

"To do work well, a person needs a **skill** — something they learned to
do well — and a **tool** — a thing that helps them do the work. The baker
needs the skill of measuring flour and using the oven safely, and the
oven itself is the tool. The farmer needs the skill of knowing when
apples are ripe, and a ladder and baskets are tools."

"A **producer** is a person who makes a good or provides a service. The
baker is a producer of bread. The haircutter is a producer of haircuts.
A **consumer** is a person who buys or uses a good or a service. When
you buy an apple, you are the consumer. One person can be both: the
baker produces bread and consumes apples."

"A **good** is a thing you can touch — an apple, a loaf of bread, a book.
A **service** is work someone does for you — a haircut, fixing a bike,
teaching a lesson. Our town makes some goods and services here in Maple
Lane, and other goods come from far away. The bread is baked here. The
bananas come from far away — that is a story for Lesson 05."

**Worked example 1 — the baker:**

> Adult: "The baker's good is bread. Her skill is knowing how to measure
> flour, mix dough, and bake it just right — she learned that by
> practicing. Her tool is the oven. She is a producer. People who buy her
> bread are consumers. She earns income: people pay her 3 coins for each
> loaf, and that income is her pay for her work. (Teacher note: the
> oven is what economists call a *capital good* — a tool used to make
> other goods — and her baking know-how is *human capital*. The learner
> only needs the words 'tool' and 'skill'.)"

**Worked example 2 — goods and services in Maple Lane:**

> Adult: "Let's sort. Bread — a good, you can touch it and eat it. A
> haircut — a service, it is work done for you. A book — a good. A bus
> ride to school — a service, the driver does the driving for you. An
> apple — a good, grown by a farmer. Teaching — a service. Now, who is
> the producer of each? The baker makes the bread; the haircutter gives
> the haircut; the farmer grows the apple; the teacher gives the lesson.
> And who earns income? All of them — each is paid for their work."

Key idea to say plainly: **People do work. Work needs skills and tools.
People earn income — money — for their work. A producer makes a good or
provides a service; a consumer buys or uses it.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult shows worker cards; the learner answers orally, by pointing, or by
sorting. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "The farmer grows apples. Is the apple a good or a service? Is the
   farmer a producer or a consumer?" *(Good; producer.)*
2. "The haircutter cuts hair. Good or service? Producer or consumer?"
   *(Service; producer.)*
3. "Name one skill the teacher needs and one tool the teacher uses."
   *(Skill: knowing how to read and explain; tool: books, a board, or a
   pencil — accept any sensible pair.)*
4. "Who earns income here: the baker, or the person who buys the bread?
   Why?" *(The baker earns income — she is paid for her work. The buyer
   spends income.)*
5. "Name one good our town makes and one service our town provides."
   *(Any local pair, e.g., bread and haircuts; the library's story time
   is a service.)*

## Independent practice

The learner picks one worker card, draws the worker with one tool, and
dictates one sentence: "The ___ is a producer. Their skill is ___. Their
tool is ___." The adult scribes the sentence. Then the learner says
whether the worker's product is a good or a service.

## Application

The learner names one job they might like to do when they grow up, one
skill that job needs, and one tool it uses — and says who the consumers
would be. There is no wrong dream job; the adult checks only that the
skill, tool, and consumer fit the job.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Is a bus ride a good or a service? Who is the producer, and how
do they earn income?" Accept: a service; the bus driver is the producer;
they earn income because they are paid for their work.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only two workers at first (baker and farmer); add
  more when the good/service sort is solid. Let the learner point to
  picture cards instead of naming jobs from memory.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach each word with a picture first (an
  oven for tool, hands kneading dough for skill), word card second;
  welcome home-language job words next to English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may draw the worker and tool,
  act out the job, or record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner explains how one producer depends on
  another (the baker buys flour from the farmer; the farmer buys tools
  from the builder) — the start of the trade idea in Lesson 05.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often call every worker a "producer"
and every buyer a "consumer" without checking what is produced — or call
the buyer the producer because "they produce the money." Re-anchor:
"A producer makes the good or does the service. Money is what the
consumer *pays with*, not what the consumer produces." A second common
slip: "skill" and "tool" get mixed up. Re-anchor: "A skill lives in a
person — something they learned. A tool is a thing you can hold." A third
slip: thinking income is free money. Re-anchor: "Income is pay for work.
People earn it by doing a job." Note for the adult: D2.Eco.13
(capital goods and human capital) is teacher background only — the oven
is a capital good, the baker's know-how is human capital; do not teach
those terms to the learner. Keep all worker examples kind and ordinary;
never ask about a real family member's job or pay.
