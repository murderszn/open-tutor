# Lesson 05 — Saving, Banks, Trade, and Government Services

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the SAVE jar (or a clear jar
labeled SAVE), play coins, a small tray or box labeled BANK, picture
cards (banana, apple, bread, flowers, book, road, school, library, park,
fire truck), a simple world map or globe from Unit 01

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn why people
**save**, what a **bank** does, why people **trade** with far-away
places, and what **government services** are — things the town provides
for everyone, like roads, schools, libraries, and parks.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "How many coins is a picture book?
If you have 3 coins, can you buy it?" (4 coins; no — 3 is not enough.)
Then: "What could you do so that one day you *can* buy it?" Accept any
answer that points to waiting and keeping coins — that is saving.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Say: "**Saving** means keeping money — or coins — to use later instead
of spending it now. People save because some things cost more than they
have today, and because later they might need money for something
important. A **bank** is a safe place where people keep money. The bank
keeps a record of how much each person saved, and when people need their
money, they can take some out. In the United States, most banks are
insured by the government, which means the money people save there is
protected."

"Now meet Maya. Maya helps at the Maple Lane market stall, and she earns
**3 coins a week**. She wants a picture book that costs **4 coins**. After
one week she has 3 coins. 3 is less than 4 — not enough yet. So she saves
her coins in her SAVE jar and waits. After the second week she earns 3
more coins. 3 + 3 = 6 coins. Now she can buy the book for 4 coins, and
6 − 4 = 2 coins are left to save. Saving is waiting on purpose."

"**Trade** is giving one thing to get another thing, or buying and
selling between places. Why do people in one place trade with people in
another place? Because some things grow or are made in one place and not
in another. Bananas grow in warm countries far away — most of the bananas
sold in the United States grow in Guatemala, Ecuador, and Costa Rica.
That is a checked fact, not a pretend one. Maple Lane is too cold for
bananas, so we trade: the far-away farmers send us bananas, and we send
them coins — and things we make here, like bread, can travel to another
town's market. When you eat a banana, you are connected to a far-away
place where it grew. Goods travel by truck and by ship."

"Finally, **government services**. The town government provides goods
and services for everyone to use: roads to walk and drive on, schools to
learn in, the library from Unit 03, and the park with its swings. Everyone
can use them. The town pays for them with money collected from the people
who live there — grown-ups pay a small share of what they earn, and the
town uses it to build roads and run the library. That is why we take care
of them: they belong to all of us."

**Worked example 1 — Maya's saving:**

> Adult: "Maya earns 3 coins in week 1. She saves them: 3 coins in the
> jar. Week 2 she earns 3 more: 3 + 3 = 6 coins. The book costs 4 coins.
> 6 − 4 = 2 coins left. Maya buys the book *and* still has 2 coins saved.
> If she had spent her 3 coins in week 1 on flowers (2 coins), she would
> have had only 1 coin left, and after week 2 only 4 coins — just enough
> for the book and nothing saved. Waiting made her choice better."

**Worked example 2 — the banana's journey:**

> Adult: "Point to our town on the map. Now point far away, to where it
> is warm all year. Bananas grow there, in countries like Guatemala,
> Ecuador, and Costa Rica. A farmer picks them while they are still
> green. A truck carries them to a ship. The ship sails to our country.
> A truck carries them to our store. Then we buy them. That whole trip is
> trade: people in one place giving goods to people in another place,
> because each place has something the other place does not have. And
> Maple Lane's bread can travel the other way — to a market in another
> town. Trade goes both ways."

Key idea to say plainly: **People save so they can buy something later.
A bank keeps saved money safe and keeps a record. People trade because
different places make different things. The town government provides
roads, schools, libraries, and parks for everyone.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult uses the SAVE jar, the BANK tray, the globe/map, and the picture
cards. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "Maya has 3 coins after week 1. The book costs 4 coins. Can she buy
   it yet? What should she do?" *(No — 3 is less than 4. Save and wait
   for week 2.)*
2. "After week 2 Maya has 6 coins. She buys the book for 4. How many
   coins does she save?" *(6 − 4 = 2 coins.)*
3. "What does a bank do?" *(It keeps people's money safe and keeps a
   record of how much each person saved.)*
4. "Bananas come from far away. Apples can grow near our town. Which
   one travels farther to reach our store, and how does it travel?"
   *(Bananas travel farther — by truck and ship from warm countries far
   away.)*
5. "Name two things the town government provides for everyone."
   *(Any two of: roads, schools, libraries, parks; accept fire trucks /
   firefighters as a government service too.)*

## Independent practice

The learner puts 6 play coins in the SAVE jar, "buys" the book for 4
coins (takes 4 coins out and hands them to the adult), and counts the 2
coins left in the jar. Then the learner sorts four picture cards into
"from far away" (banana) and "made near here" (bread, flowers, apple)
and names one government service from the remaining cards.

## Application

The learner picks something they would like to save for (a book, a toy,
a game — never a real purchase) and, with the adult, works out a tiny
plan: "It costs ___ coins. I can save ___ coins a week. After ___ weeks
I will have enough." The plan stays pretend and in play coins.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Maya earns 3 coins a week. After two weeks, how many coins
does she have? She buys a book for 4 coins. How many does she save? And
name one thing the town government provides." Accept: 3 + 3 = 6 coins;
6 − 4 = 2 coins saved; one of roads, schools, libraries, parks (or
firefighters/police).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use only Maya's numbers (3, 3, 6, 4, 2) with real coins
  in the jar; add the trade sort only when the saving math is solid.
  Let the learner point to the map instead of naming countries.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach save, bank, trade, and government
  service with objects first (a jar, a tray, a banana, a picture of a
  library), word cards second; welcome home-language words next to
  English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may move coins, point to
  cards, or record an audio answer the adult keeps.
- **Extension:** The learner explains what would happen if nobody saved
  ("Maya could never buy the book") and what would happen if there were
  no trade ("we would have no bananas in winter") — consequences in
  plain words, not models.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often think saving means "never
spending" or that a bank "keeps your money forever and you never get it
back." Re-anchor: "Saving means spending *later*, not never. A bank
keeps your money safe *until you need it* — you can take it out."
A second common slip: thinking trade means "giving things away for
nothing." Re-anchor: "Trade is an exchange — both sides get something
they want. The banana farmer gets coins; we get bananas." A third slip:
thinking government services are free because no one pays at the door.
Re-anchor: "The library is free to *use*, but the town pays for it with
money collected from the people who live there. That is why we take care
of it." Note for the adult: the banana fact is the unit's one real-world
claim — most bananas sold in the United States grow in warm countries
far away, with Guatemala, Ecuador, and Costa Rica the largest suppliers
(USDA Specialty Crops Market News, checked 2026-11-05). Do not add other
origin statistics from memory. Keep all saving plans in play coins; never
ask about a real family income, bank account, or purchase. The FDIC
insurance note is adult background only; the learner meets "the bank
keeps money safe and keeps a record."
