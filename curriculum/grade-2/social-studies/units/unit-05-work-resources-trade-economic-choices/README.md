# Unit 05 — Work, Resources, Trade, and Economic Choices

**Grade:** 2 (ages 7–8) · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#17](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/17)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 19–22 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, 25–30 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

This unit teaches the economics tools the rest of the year reuses: telling a
**want** from a **need**; understanding **scarcity** — there is not enough
of everything for everyone to have all they want — so every choice has a
**benefit** (what you gain) and a **cost** (what you give up); naming
**goods** (things you can touch) and **services** (work someone does for
you); seeing who **produces** and who **consumes**; learning how people
earn **income** by doing **work** that needs **skills** and **tools**; reading
**prices** in a **market** and using **money** to trade; learning why people
**save** and what **banks** do; and seeing how **trade** moves goods from
far away to our town — and how **governments** provide goods and services
everyone can use, like roads, schools, libraries, and parks. The unit's
project — a "Maple Lane Market Day" stall plan — is a price list, a
save-and-spend plan, and a trade card, presented orally with drawings. The
play-store routine (10 play coins, one fixed price list) returns in Unit 08's
community project.

All of the unit's market examples use the fictional Maple Lane neighborhood
from Unit 01 and the fictional play coins and prices below. Maple Lane is
invented for practice. Nothing in this unit is a real place's economy, and
no real person's money or job is used. The adult says so out loud whenever
a practice price or coin is shown: "These are play coins, not real money."
The one real-world fact in the unit — that most bananas sold in the United
States grow in warm countries far away, such as Guatemala, Ecuador, and
Costa Rica — is labeled as a checked fact (USDA shipment data, 2026-11-05)
and is the adult's example, not a learner's claim.

## The unit's fixed play-store numbers (used identically everywhere)

Every lesson, practice set, quiz, and assessment in this unit uses the same
fictional numbers so the learner never has to re-learn them:

- The learner starts market play with **10 play coins**.
- **Price list:** apple **2 coins** · loaf of bread **3 coins** · bunch of
  flowers **2 coins** · picture book **4 coins** · toy car **5 coins** ·
  haircut (a service) **3 coins**.
- The baker's **cost of production** for one loaf: flour **1 coin** +
  oven and work time **1 coin** = **2 coins**; the loaf sells for **3
  coins**, so **1 coin** is left over to help pay for the shop.
- Maya's saving story: Maya earns **3 coins a week** helping at the
  market stall. A picture book costs **4 coins**. After week 1 she has
  3 coins (not enough); after week 2 she has 3 + 3 = **6 coins**; she
  buys the book for 4 and saves **2 coins**.

## Prerequisites (developmental expectations; re-taught here as needed)

- Sort objects and pictures into two groups (Unit 01 map symbols; Unit 02
  landform sorts).
- Retell a sequence of 2–3 familiar events in order (Unit 03 timelines).
- Follow agreed-upon rules for discussion and take turns (Unit 04, draft).
- Count to 10 and add and subtract small numbers within 10 (same-grade
  math routines, not taught here).
- Point, draw, and describe observations; dictate a sentence for an adult
  to scribe.

Diagnostic Weeks 1–2 probed wants and needs with a play-based task ("draw
one thing you want and one thing you need"). If the want/need sort is
shaky, re-teach Lesson 01 with the learner's own day (breakfast, shoes,
toys) before any market content.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain** scarcity — why people cannot have everything they want —
   and **sort** wants from needs, giving a reason for each sort
   (D2.Eco.1.K-2).
2. **Identify** the benefits and costs of a personal decision and **name**
   what is given up when a choice is made (D2.Eco.2.K-2).
3. **Describe** the skills and knowledge needed to produce certain goods
   and services; **explain** how people earn income; and **name** goods
   and services the local community produces and ones produced in other
   communities (D2.Eco.3.K-2, D2.Eco.4.K-2, D2.Eco.6.K-2).
4. **Identify** prices of products in a local market; **explain** the role
   of money in exchange; and **describe** simple examples of costs of
   production (D2.Eco.5.K-2, D2.Eco.7.K-2).
5. **Explain** why people save; **describe** the role of banks; and
   **describe** why people in one place trade goods and services with
   people in other places, naming a product made far away and sold here
   and a product made here that can travel far away (D2.Eco.9.K-2,
   D2.Eco.10.K-2, D2.Eco.14.K-2, D2.Eco.15.K-2, D2.Geo.7.K-2,
   D2.Geo.11.K-2).
6. **Describe** examples of the goods and services that governments
   provide (D2.Eco.12.K-2).

With adult support, the learner asks the unit's compelling question,
identifies facts and concepts for supporting questions, and determines
which sources will help answer them (D1.3.K-2, D1.5.K-2); the learner
presents a short explanation in correct sequence with reasons (D4.2.K-2).

## Essential question

*Why can't we have everything we want — and how do we choose well?*

## Vocabulary

**Want** — something you would like to have, but can live without. ·
**Need** — something you must have to live and stay healthy and safe, such
as food, water, a home, and clothes. · **Scarcity** — not having enough of
something for everyone to have all they want. · **Choice** — picking one
thing when you cannot pick them all. · **Benefit** — a good thing you get
from a choice. · **Cost** — what you give up when you make a choice. ·
**Good** — a thing you can touch that people make, grow, or buy, such as
an apple, a loaf of bread, or a book. · **Service** — work someone does
for you, such as a haircut or fixing a bike. · **Producer** — a person who
makes a good or provides a service. · **Consumer** — a person who buys or
uses a good or a service. · **Work** — a job people do. · **Skill** —
something a person has learned to do well. · **Tool** — a thing that helps
a person do work, such as an oven or a truck. · **Income** — the money
people earn for their work. · **Price** — how many coins (or how much
money) something costs. · **Money** — coins and paper bills people use to
buy and sell things. · **Market** — a place where buyers and sellers meet
to trade. · **Save** — to keep money (or coins) to use later instead of
spending it now. · **Bank** — a safe place where people keep money; the
bank keeps a record of how much each person saved. · **Trade** — giving
one thing to get another thing, or buying and selling between places. ·
**Government service** — a good or service the town or country provides
for everyone, such as roads, schools, libraries, and parks.

## Materials

Play coins (10 per learner — paper circles, bottle caps, or real coins
kept by the adult and counted back in); blank price tags and a marker;
the printed [Maple Lane market illustration](assets/maple-lane-market.png)
*(AI-generated illustration, inspected 2026-11-05 — every image-based task
ships with a full text-only alternative)*; picture cards for the want/need
sort, the goods/services sort, and the trade sort; a clear jar labeled
SAVE; a small tray or box for the "bank"; paper, crayons or colored
pencils. All activities are adult-supervised; play coins are counted out
and counted back in at the end of every session so none is lost or taken
home as real money. No real money is used, and no learner's family money
or job is discussed or collected.

## Differentiation and accessibility

- Oral, pointing, drawing, and manipulative response modes for every check;
  the adult scribes dictated writing; short labeled products reviewed the
  same day.
- Large-print price tags and picture cards; real objects (an apple, a
  loaf, a book) on the table beside their cards for low-vision learners;
  coins in a tray with raised edges so they do not roll away.
- Vocabulary taught with objects and pictures first, word second; visual
  word wall; home-language labels welcomed alongside English.
- Short sessions with movement breaks; every lesson has a seated-table
  and a floor-play variant (market stall on the floor, walked through in
  order).
- Every illustration ships with a text-only alternative; color is never the
  only cue (labels always accompany color; prices are numbers, not colors).
- Hearing support: face the learner during discussions; visual turn-taking
  cues; written/drawn option for every spoken check.
- Speaking tasks accept audio recording in place of live presentation; the
  adult may record on the learner's behalf.

## Safety

Adult supervises every activity and previews every procedure, read-aloud,
and practice item. Play coins are counted out and counted back in every
session; no real money is used and no learner's family money, income, or
job is discussed or collected. Practice prices and the Maple Lane market
are fictional; the adult says so. No real addresses, learner photos,
family stories, or personal information appear anywhere in the learner's
work. Market play stays on familiar, safe ground with the adult. No sharp
tools.
