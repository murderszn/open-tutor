# Lesson 03 — People Change Places

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the printed [Riverside Valley
illustration](../assets/riverside-valley-illustration.png) *(AI-generated
illustration of a fictional community — inspected 2026-10-17; a full
text-only description follows below)*, vocabulary word cards (farm, road,
building, environment), paper and crayons

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will see how people change the
land around them — with farms, roads, and buildings — and you will find
those changes in a picture of a valley community.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Name one landform from Lesson 01."
Accept any of the six. "Today we ask a new question: when people move to
a landform, do they leave it exactly the same? Let's look."

## The Riverside Valley illustration — text-only description

*(Read this aloud whenever the learner cannot see the image. Every
question about the illustration is answerable from this description.)*

"A storybook drawing of a make-believe place called Riverside Valley —
not a real town. In the back, green hills rise up, with one tall
snow-capped peak. A wide blue river winds through the middle. On one side
of the river is flat land with neat rows of farm fields, where tiny
people are planting and carrying baskets. Three small houses with red
roofs stand near the fields, and a brown dirt road runs past them. A
small wooden bridge crosses the river. On the other side of the river is
a calm blue lake, where two tiny people are fishing from the shore. Trees
are scattered around, and a smiling sun shines in a blue sky."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

"People do not just live *on* the land — they **change** it. Look at the
Riverside Valley picture. Long ago, this valley had only hills, a river,
trees, and grass. Then people came. What did they change?"

Point to each change in the picture (or in the description):

1. "**Farms.** People cleared flat land and planted neat rows of crops.
   The plain was just grass before; now it grows food. A farm is land
   where people grow crops or raise animals."
2. "**Roads.** People packed down a dirt road so they could walk and
   carry things from the houses to the fields. The road was not there
   before — people made it."
3. "**Buildings.** People built houses with red roofs to live in. They
   also built a wooden **bridge** so they could cross the river."

Key idea to say plainly: **People change places. They clear land for
farms, build roads to travel on, and put up houses and bridges. Every
change helps people live — and every change changes the land.**

**Worked example 1 — before and after:**

> Adult: "Picture this valley *before* people came: hills, a river,
> trees, grass. Now look at the picture *after* people came: farm fields,
> a road, houses, a bridge. I will name one change and say how it helps:
> 'People built a bridge over the river. Before the bridge, crossing the
> river was hard and wet. After the bridge, people walk across dry.' That
> is the pattern: **before → people changed something → after, and life
> is different.**"

**Worked example 2 — one change, two questions:**

> Adult: "Look at the farm fields. Question one: what did people change?
> They changed flat grassy land into planted rows — a farm. Question two:
> how does the change help them? It grows food they can eat and share.
> Now you try with the road: what did people change, and how does it help?
> *(They made a dirt path where there was only grass; it helps them walk
> and carry things between houses and fields.)*"

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

The learner studies the illustration (or hears the description) and
answers. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "Point to one farm. What did people change to make it?" *(cleared flat
   land; planted rows of crops)*
2. "Point to the road. What was there before the road?" *(grass and
   open land — people made the path)*
3. "Point to a building. Why did people build houses here, near the
   fields?" *(to live close to their farm work)*
4. "Point to the bridge. What problem does the bridge solve?" *(crossing
   the river without getting wet or stuck)*
5. "Name one thing in the picture people did **not** change." *(the
   hills, the river itself, the sun — accept any natural feature)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws "before and after": on the left, the valley with only
hills, river, trees, and grass; on the right, the same valley with one
people-made change (a farm, a road, or a house). The adult scribes the
dictated caption: "Before: ___. People changed ___. After: ___."

## Application

Think-aloud walk: the adult names a familiar, safe place (the school
yard, a nearby park — no addresses) and the learner names one way people
changed it: "People built the school building"; "People planted the
trees in rows"; "People made the sidewalk." If no safe example comes to
mind, reuse the Riverside Valley picture.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Name two ways people change a place, and tell me how one of
them helps people." Accept: farms, roads, buildings/bridge (any two),
plus one helping reason ("farms grow food"; "roads help people travel";
"houses give shelter"; "bridges help cross water").

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Work with one change at a time — farms only, then roads,
  then buildings. Let the learner point instead of speaking.
- **Reading/language access:** Teach "farm," "road," and "building" with
  the picture first, word card second; welcome home-language labels.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may place sticky-note arrows on
  the printed illustration to mark each people-made change.
- **Extension:** "Can a change help *and* hurt?" (A road helps travel but
  covers grass where animals lived; a farm grows food but the river water
  is shared.) The learner draws one good and one tricky result of the
  bridge.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often think farms, roads, and houses
"were always there." Re-anchor with the before/after frame every time:
"Close your eyes. Erase the houses, the road, the fields. What is left?
Hills, river, trees, grass. *People* added the rest." A second common
slip: calling the river or hills people-made — sort each named item into
"people made it" versus "nature made it" until the sort is quick.
