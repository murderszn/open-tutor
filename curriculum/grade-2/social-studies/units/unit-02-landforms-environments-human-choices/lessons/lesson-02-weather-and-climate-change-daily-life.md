# Lesson 02 — Weather and Climate Change Daily Life

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** clothing picture cards (coat,
rain boots, sandals, sun hat, umbrella, sweater), vocabulary word cards
(weather, climate, environment), paper and crayons

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn the difference
between weather and climate, and you will explain how each one changes
what people wear, eat, build, and do.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Look out the window (or tell me from
this morning): what is the sky doing today?" Accept anything observed:
sunny, cloudy, rainy, windy, hot, cold. "That is today's **weather**.
Now — what is the weather *usually* like here in winter? That 'usually'
is something new. Let's learn it."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

"**Weather** is what the sky is doing *today* — rain, sun, wind, hot, or
cold. Weather can change by tomorrow. **Climate** is what the weather is
*usually* like in a place, year after year. Weather is today; climate is
usually."

Hold up the clothing cards one at a time. "Weather changes what we wear
**today**. If it rains today, we grab rain boots and an umbrella. If it
is hot today, we wear sandals."

"Climate changes what people **always keep ready**. In a place where the
climate is cold — where winter is long and snowy every year — every
family owns a heavy coat, and houses have strong heaters. In a place
where the climate is hot and dry, houses have thick walls that stay cool,
and people grow food that likes heat. Weather picks today's clothes;
climate shapes the whole way of life."

"Weather and climate also change **food, shelter, and activities**. Rainy
weather means indoor play today. A hot climate means cold drinks and
shade are part of everyday life; a cold climate means warm soups and
indoor games are normal in winter. Farmers watch both: today's weather
tells them when to water; the climate tells them which crops will grow."

Key idea to say plainly: **Weather is what the sky does today. Climate
is what the weather is usually like in a place. Both change how people
dress, eat, build homes, and spend their days.**

**Worked example 1 — weather or climate?**

> Adult: "I will read two sentences. You tell me: weather or climate?
> One: 'It is raining this afternoon, so we will play inside.' Hmm —
> 'this afternoon' means *today*. That is **weather**. Two: 'In this town
> it snows every winter, so every house has a heater.' 'Every winter' —
> that happens year after year. That is **climate**. The clue words are
> 'today/this afternoon' for weather and 'every year/usually' for
> climate."

**Worked example 2 — climate shapes choices:**

> Adult: "Two children pack for a trip. Maya is visiting her cousins
> where the climate is hot and dry — almost no rain all year. What should
> she pack? Light clothes, a sun hat, and a water bottle. Now Leo is
> visiting grandparents where the climate is cold — snow every winter.
> Heavy coat, boots, mittens. Neither child checked *today's* weather
> yet. They packed for the **climate** — what is *usually* true there.
> Tomorrow's weather might still surprise them, and then they adjust."

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

Adult reads each item; the learner answers weather or climate, then names
one choice it changes. (Hints in parentheses are for the adult.)

1. "It is windy today, so we hold our hats." *(weather — clothing/activity
   today)*
2. "It rains almost every afternoon here, so houses have slanted roofs."
   *(climate — shelter built for usual rain)*
3. "The sky is cloudy this morning." *(weather — what we see today)*
4. "Summers are long and hot here, so children play outside in the
   evening." *(climate — activity shaped by usual heat)*
5. Adult holds up the umbrella card. "When do we grab this — for weather
   or for climate?" *(weather — rain today; accept "both" with a reason,
   since a rainy climate means owning one)*

## Independent practice

The learner draws **two** pictures side by side: "a rainy day" (weather)
and "a place where it is usually snowy" (climate). Under each, the adult
scribes the learner's dictated sentence about what people wear or do
there. Then the learner circles which picture shows "today" and which
shows "usually."

## Application

Dress-the-character: the adult names three places — (a) "here, today"
(adult states today's actual weather), (b) "a hot, dry place," (c) "a
cold, snowy place" — and the learner picks clothing cards for a paper
figure visiting each, saying why: "Boots, because it is rainy today"
(weather); "Sun hat, because it is usually hot there" (climate).

## Exit check

Prompt: "Tell me in your own words: what is weather, and what is
climate? Give one example of each." Accept: weather is what the sky is
doing today (example: "it is sunny today"); climate is what the weather
is usually like (example: "it is usually cold in winter"). One clear
example of each earns full credit.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Start with weather only; add climate after the learner can
  sort three "today" sentences. Use the window or a weather picture every
  session to anchor "today."
- **Reading/language access:** Pair each new word with a gesture — point
  at the sky for weather, sweep an arm in a big circle ("year after
  year") for climate.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may sort picture cards into
  "today" and "usually" trays instead of speaking.
- **Extension:** "How could the climate of a place change what people
  eat?" (Crops that like heat versus crops that like cold; fishing near
  warm water versus storing food for long winters.) The learner draws a
  meal from a hot place and a meal from a cold place.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often use the two words for the same
thing ("climate means weather"). Re-anchor with the time clue every
time: "**Today** = weather. **Usually, year after year** = climate."
A second common slip: thinking climate never matters for today — correct
gently: "Climate tells us what to *own and build*; weather tells us what
to *grab this morning*." Keep examples tied to clothing, food, shelter,
or activities the learner has lived.
