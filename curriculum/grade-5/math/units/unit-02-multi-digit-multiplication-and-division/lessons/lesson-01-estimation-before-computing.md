# Lesson 01 — Estimation Before Computing

**My goal:** I can estimate products and quotients by rounding and by choosing
compatible numbers, and I can use the estimate to decide whether an actual
answer is reasonable.

**Time:** about 40 minutes (5 readiness · 12 teaching · 8 guided · 12
independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper and pencil, a hundreds chart if wanted. No calculator —
the point is that your brain checks the work before the pencil does.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.B.5, 5.NBT.B.6 (support) · **Unit objectives:** UO1, UO2, UO5

## Explicit teaching

### Why estimate first?

Multiplying and dividing big numbers is detail work, and detail work invites
one slipped digit. An **estimate** is a fast, rough answer you make *before*
the exact one. Then you compare: if the exact answer is nowhere near the
estimate, something slipped — check it, don't trust it.

Two tools make estimates fast:

1. **Rounding:** replace each number with a nearby round number, then compute.
   23 × 45 → 20 × 50 = 1,000. The real answer (1,035) is close. Good.
2. **Compatible numbers:** pick numbers near yours that divide or multiply
   cleanly. 936 ÷ 24 → 960 ÷ 24 = 40 (because 24 × 40 = 960 is easy). The real
   answer (39) is close. Good.

**Rules of the road:**
- An estimate is a *range check*, not a second exact answer. Don't "fix" it
  to look exact — its job is to say "about 1,000," nothing more.
- Rounding **both** numbers up gives a *high* estimate; rounding both down
  gives a *low* one. Watch the direction so you know which side the truth is on.

### Worked example 1 — estimate a product

Estimate **14 × 126**.

Round each factor to its greatest place: 14 → 10, 126 → 100.
Estimate: 10 × 100 = **1,000**.

The actual answer is 1,764. Is 1,764 "near" 1,000? Eh — close-ish, but this
estimate is weak because 14 is nearly halfway between 10 and 20. Try a better
rounding: 14 → 15, 126 → 130. Estimate: 15 × 130 = 1,950. Now 1,764 sits
comfortably near 1,950. **Lesson:** rounding one or both numbers *toward* their
true value (15 is closer to 14 than 10 is) gives a tighter estimate.

### Worked example 2 — compatible numbers for division

Estimate **1,344 ÷ 32**.

Compatible-number move: find a multiple of 32 near 1,344. 32 × 40 = 1,280.
Estimate: about **40**.
The actual answer is 42. 42 is near 40 — the estimate did its job.

### Worked example 3 — range thinking

Estimate **775 ÷ 18**, then say what range the answer must fall in.

Round up: 800 ÷ 20 = 40. Round down-friendly: 720 ÷ 18 = 40.
Estimate: about **40**. The actual answer is 43 R1 — inside a sensible range
(40-ish, definitely not 4 and definitely not 400). A quick range check also
rules out place-value slips: 18 × 4 = 72 (way too small), 18 × 400 = 7,200
(way too big), so the quotient must be a two-digit number in the 40s.

### Worked example 4 — an estimate that warns you

**18 × 57.** Round both up: 20 × 60 = **1,200**.
Actual: 1,026. The estimate is noticeably high — and that is *information*:
both factors were rounded up, so the real answer *must* be lower. If your
pencil later says 1,926, you know to re-check (1,926 > 1,200 can't be right).

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work these with your adult or partner. Hints are given — cover the answer space
and try first.

**G1.** Estimate **25 × 38**. Then check: is the actual answer (950) reasonable?
*Hint: round 25 → 25 (already tidy) and 38 → 40. Which direction did you
round?*

**G2.** Estimate **775 ÷ 18** using 800 ÷ 20.
*Hint: 800 ÷ 20 = 80 ÷ 2. What two-digit estimate do you get?*

**G3.** Maya estimates 29 × 41 as 30 × 40 = 1,200, then writes "≈ 1,190" as her
estimate because she wants it to look closer to the real answer. What is wrong
with this?
*Hint: what is the job of an estimate?*

**G4.** Estimate **4,806 ÷ 9** with compatible numbers.
*Hint: find a multiple of 9 near 4,806 — try 4,500 ÷ 9.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Estimate each product, noting whether your estimate is high, low, or
close: (a) 287 × 42 (b) 3,742 × 6.
**I2.** Estimate each quotient with compatible numbers: (a) 7,248 ÷ 6
(b) 2,916 ÷ 7.
**I3.** A learner estimates 96 × 24 as 100 × 20 = 2,000, computes exactly, and
gets 2,304. Is the exact answer reasonable? Explain in one sentence.
**I4.** **Explain your reasoning:** A classmate says, "Estimating is pointless
because it never gives the right answer." Write two sentences defending
estimation, using one example from this lesson.

## Applied task

You are checking a grocery receipt. The receipt says 18 packs of juice at $3
each cost $540. Without multiplying exactly, decide whether the receipt is
right. Then multiply to confirm.

## Exit check

Estimate **3,108 ÷ 29**. Write your estimate, then use it to judge whether an
exact answer of 107 is reasonable. One or two sentences.

## Supports, alternate response modes, extensions

- **Reading/language access:** "Estimate" = *a fast guess before the real
  work*. Keep a word wall: estimate, compatible, reasonable.
- **Alternate response mode:** say estimates aloud; use a number line to show
  the range the answer must fall in instead of writing sentences.
- **Extension:** for 14 × 126, find three different roundings and rank them by
  closeness to 1,764. When is rounding *one* factor better than rounding both?

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconceptions:** treating the estimate as a promise ("my answer
  must equal the estimate"); rounding both factors away from truth and then
  trusting the weak estimate; estimating *after* computing and "adjusting" the
  estimate to match (see G3) — that destroys the check.
