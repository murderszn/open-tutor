# Lesson 04 — Two-Digit Divisors and Remainder Stories

**My goal:** I can divide with two-digit divisors using partial quotients and
area models, and I can explain what a remainder means in a real story.

**Time:** about 40 minutes (5 readiness · 14 teaching · 8 guided · 10
independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper and pencil, grid paper, the
[packing-workshop illustration](../assets/packing-workshop-illustration.png)
printed or on screen.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.B.6 · **Unit objectives:** UO2, UO3, UO5

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. From Lesson 03's warm-up: what is 24 × 40? What is 32 × 40?
2. Estimate 936 ÷ 24 with compatible numbers.
3. 775 ÷ 18 — before computing, guess: will the quotient be closer to 4, 40,
   or 400? Why?

## Retrieval warm-up (3 minutes, from Unit 01)

- 1,280 ÷ 10 = 128 · 45.6 × 10 = 456 — digit shifts in both directions.
- Say the remainder rule from Lesson 03 out loud: "the remainder must be
  smaller than the divisor."

## Explicit teaching

### Two-digit divisors are just bigger chunks

Nothing new about the method — your friendly chunks just get bigger. For a
divisor like 24, your friends are 24 × 10 = 240, 24 × 30 = 720, 24 × 40 = 960.

### Worked example 1 — 936 ÷ 24

| Step | Chunk | Remaining |
|---|---|---|
| 24 × 30 = 720 | 30 | 936 − 720 = 216 |
| 24 × 9 = 216 | 9 | 216 − 216 = 0 |

Quotient: 30 + 9 = **39**.
Check: 39 × 24 = 936. (40 × 24 = 960, minus one 24 = 936.) ✓
Estimate check (Lesson 01): 960 ÷ 24 = 40. 39 is close. ✓

### Worked example 2 — 1,344 ÷ 32

| Step | Chunk | Remaining |
|---|---|---|
| 32 × 40 = 1,280 | 40 | 1,344 − 1,280 = 64 |
| 32 × 2 = 64 | 2 | 64 − 64 = 0 |

Quotient: 40 + 2 = **42**.
Check: 42 × 32 = 1,344. (40 × 32 = 1,280; 2 × 32 = 64.) ✓

### Worked example 3 — division on an area model

**936 ÷ 24** as a rectangle 24 units wide. Peel off area in chunks:

- A strip of length 30 has area 24 × 30 = 720. Remaining: 936 − 720 = 216.
- A strip of length 9 has area 24 × 9 = 216. Remaining: 0.
- Total length: 30 + 9 = **39**.

Same answer as the table — different picture, same chunks. Use whichever
picture your brain likes.

### Worked example 4 — the remainder is not the answer (yet)

**775 ÷ 18** — the button workshop.

| Step | Chunk | Remaining |
|---|---|---|
| 18 × 40 = 720 | 40 | 775 − 720 = 55 |
| 18 × 3 = 54 | 3 | 55 − 54 = 1 |

Quotient: 40 + 3 = **43 R1**. Check: 43 × 18 = 774; 774 + 1 = 775. ✓

Now the **story** decides what 43 R1 means. The workshop packs 775 buttons into
boxes that hold 18 each.

- Full boxes filled: **43**.
- Buttons left over: **1**.
- Boxes needed to pack *every* button: **44** (the leftover button needs a box
  too).

Same numbers, three different answers — the question you ask picks the
meaning. "43 R1" alone answers none of them.

### The illustration activity

Look at the [packing-workshop illustration](../assets/packing-workshop-illustration.png):
a workbench with stacked wooden crates, glass jars of red, blue, yellow, and
green buttons, and one open crate that is only partly full of buttons, plus a
blank order sheet.

**Question:** The workshop packed 775 buttons into boxes of 18. How many full
boxes did they fill? What does the partly-full crate in the picture represent?

**Text-only alternative (answer without the picture):** The exact number of
buttons cannot be counted from the illustration — it is a story scene, not a
photograph of the count. The story numbers are: 775 buttons total, 18 buttons
per box. 775 ÷ 18 = 43 R1, so the workshop filled **43 full boxes** with
**1 button left over**. The partly-full open crate in the picture represents
that leftover — the remainder that needs its own box if every button must be
packed (44 boxes in all).

people, text, or numerals; button counts in the art are decorative and must
not be counted.*

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Divide **1,152 ÷ 24** by partial quotients. Check by multiplication.
*Hint: 24 × 40 = 960 is a friendly first chunk.*

**G2.** Divide **2,860 ÷ 26**. Check by multiplication.
*Hint: 26 × 100 = 2,600.*

**G3.** 1,000 eggs are packed into cartons of 12. How many full cartons? How
many eggs are left over? How many cartons are needed to pack all the eggs?
*Hint: three questions, three answers — like Worked Example 4.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Divide by partial quotients or area model; check by multiplication:
(a) 1,960 ÷ 28 (b) 4,200 ÷ 35.
**I2.** Divide 3,108 ÷ 29. Write the quotient, remainder, and the full check.
**I3.** **Explain your reasoning:** Ava computes 775 ÷ 18 = 43 R1 and writes,
"You need 43 boxes." What is her error? What should she write instead?
**I4.** Draw an area model for 1,152 ÷ 24 (peel off two chunks) and label each
strip's area.

## Applied task

The band room has 1,530 chairs to stack on carts that hold 34 chairs each.
(a) How many full carts? (b) How many chairs are left over? (c) How many carts
are needed to move every chair? Show the division and write each answer in a
complete sentence.

## Exit check

Divide **1,530 ÷ 34** by partial quotients. Then answer: to move all 1,530
chairs, how many carts are needed? (One number, one sentence of reasoning.)

## Supports, alternate response modes, extensions

- **Reading/language access:** "full groups," "left over," "in all" are the
  three question types — sort story questions into these buckets before
  computing.
- **Alternate response mode:** act out the remainder story with real objects
  (buttons, beans, paper clips) and small boxes; describe the leftover aloud
  instead of writing.
- **Extension:** 9,240 ÷ 42 = 220. Estimate first (about 200), then find two
  different chunk paths to 220. Which path uses fewer steps, and why?

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconceptions:** treating "43 R1" as the final answer without
  interpreting (Ava's error); assuming the remainder always rounds the answer
  *up* (it depends on the question — "full boxes" rounds *down*); choosing
  chunks too small for two-digit divisors; counting buttons in the illustration
  instead of using the story numbers.
- **Answer-key reference:** all items solved in `answer-key.md` (Lesson 04).
