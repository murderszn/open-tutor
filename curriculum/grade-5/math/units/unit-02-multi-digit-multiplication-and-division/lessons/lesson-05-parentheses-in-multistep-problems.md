# Lesson 05 — Parentheses in Multistep Problems

**My goal:** I can write a multistep word problem as a numerical expression
using parentheses (and brackets or braces), and I can evaluate expressions
with these symbols.

**Time:** about 40 minutes (5 readiness · 12 teaching · 8 guided · 12
independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** paper and pencil. The legacy budget numbers from Session 04
(14 lamps × $126, 18 microphones × $57, 25 boxes × $38, $70 shipping) — keep
them handy.

**Standards:** 5.OA.A.1 · **Unit objectives:** UO4, UO1

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Evaluate: 3 × (126 + 57). What did you do first?
2. Evaluate: (3 × 126) + 57. Is the answer the same as #1? Should it be?
3. In your own words: what do parentheses tell a reader to do?

## Retrieval warm-up (3 minutes, from Unit 01)

- (10 × 456) + (3.4 × 100) = 4,560 + 340 = 4,900.
- Grouping marks are promises: "do this part first."

## Explicit teaching

### Parentheses are instructions, not decoration

A **numerical expression** uses numbers and operation symbols (+, −, ×, ÷) to
describe a calculation. **Parentheses ( )** — and later **brackets [ ]** and
**braces { }** — tell the reader which part to evaluate *first*. Inside any
pair, work left to right; nested pairs evaluate innermost first.

Why does this matter? Because without them, the same string of numbers can
mean two different things:

- 3 × (126 + 57): add first → 3 × 183 = **549**.
- (3 × 126) + 57: multiply first → 378 + 57 = **435**.

549 ≠ 435. The parentheses aren't style — they change the answer. When you
write the workshop's order total, parentheses are how you make sure the next
person reads *your* problem.

### Worked example 1 — the full order as one expression

The production budget: 14 lamps at $126 each, 18 microphones at $57 each,
25 storage boxes at $38 each, plus $70 shipping.

Expression: **(14 × 126) + (18 × 57) + (25 × 38) + 70**

Evaluate stepwise:
1. 14 × 126 = 1,764
2. 18 × 57 = 1,026
3. 25 × 38 = 950
4. Add: 1,764 + 1,026 + 950 + 70 = **3,810**

Each parenthesized piece is one line of the order; the final + 70 is the
shipping line. The expression *is* the order sheet, written in math.

### Worked example 2 — the same numbers, different meaning

A school buys 3 bundles. Each bundle has 126 pencils and 57 erasers.

- Expression A: **3 × (126 + 57)** → one bundle = 183 items; 3 bundles =
  3 × 183 = **549** items.
- Expression B: **(3 × 126) + 57** → 378 pencils + 57 erasers = **435** items.

Expression A answers "how many items in 3 bundles?" Expression B answers
something else entirely (3 bundles of pencils, plus 57 loose erasers). Write
the expression that matches *your* story — then evaluate it.

### Worked example 3 — the remainder as an expression

From Lesson 04: 775 buttons, boxes of 18, 43 full boxes, 1 left over.

**775 − (43 × 18) = 775 − 774 = 1**

The parentheses force the multiplication first; what remains is exactly the
remainder. This is the check from Lesson 04, written as one line:
**dividend − (quotient × divisor) = remainder**.

### Worked example 4 — nested grouping marks

**[24 + (3 × 6)] − 10**

1. Innermost pair first: (3 × 6) = 18.
2. Brackets next: [24 + 18] = 42.
3. Finally: 42 − 10 = **32**.

Innermost → outermost. The brackets are just bigger parentheses so the eye can
tell the layers apart.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Evaluate: 2 × (5 + 5) and (2 × 5) + 5. Which matches "2 packs, each
with 5 red and 5 blue marbles"?
*Hint: do the parentheses first in each.*

**G2.** Evaluate: 100 − (25 + 25) and (100 − 25) + 25.
*Hint: the first says "spend 50 of 100"; the second says "spend 25, then get
25 back."*

**G3.** Write the buttons remainder as an expression for 1,000 eggs in cartons
of 12 (83 full cartons, 4 left over), following Worked Example 3.
*Hint: 1,000 − ( ___ × ___ ) = ___.*

**G4.** Evaluate: { [ (4 + 4) × 2 ] − 6 } ÷ 2.
*Hint: innermost first — then the division last.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Evaluate: (a) 4 × (36 + 24) (b) (4 × 36) + 24. For each, write the
story it could match in one sentence.
**I2.** Write an expression for this story and evaluate it: "14 boxes at $126
each, plus $70 shipping." (One expression, one answer.)
**I3.** Write an expression for this story and evaluate it: "Buy 3 items at $45
each, then take $20 off the total."
**I4.** **Explain your reasoning:** Noah evaluates 20 − (8 + 4) as
20 − 8 + 4 = 16. What did he do wrong? Show the correct evaluation.

## Applied task

The workshop's shipping rule: orders get a $70 shipping line added *after*
the items are totaled. Write the full order expression for 25 boxes at $38
each plus the shipping line, using parentheses. Evaluate it, then write one
sentence saying what would go wrong if the parentheses were dropped.

## Exit check

Evaluate: **50 − (3 × (4 + 2))**. Show each step.

## Supports, alternate response modes, extensions

- **Reading/language access:** "evaluate" = *find the value of*. Color-code
  nested pairs (innermost one color) to make the order visible.
- **Alternate response mode:** build expressions with physical cards for
  numbers, operators, and grouping marks; rearrange the cards to show how
  meaning changes.
- **Extension:** write two different expressions for "4 packs of 12, give away
  10" that give different answers, and explain which matches which story.
  Then try brackets: 100 − [ (12 + 8) × 2 ] = ?

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconceptions:** evaluating left-to-right through parentheses
  (Noah's error); adding grouping marks around everything "to be safe" (which
  changes nothing but hides structure); writing an expression that doesn't
  match the story (Expression A vs. B confusion).
