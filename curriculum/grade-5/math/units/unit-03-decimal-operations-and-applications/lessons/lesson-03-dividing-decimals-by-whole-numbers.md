# Lesson 03 — Dividing Decimals by Whole Numbers

**My goal:** I can divide a decimal by a whole number, and divide a whole
number to get a decimal quotient, by sharing tenths and hundredths fairly.

**Time:** about 40 minutes (5 readiness · 12 teaching · 8 guided ·
12 independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** play money (dollars, dimes, pennies) or base-ten pieces,
place-value mat.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.B.7 · **Unit objectives:** UO3

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. Share $4.80 equally among 6 friends. How much does each friend get?
   (Think in dimes first.)
2. What is 48 ÷ 6? What is 4.8 ÷ 6? How are the answers related?
3. You cut a 3-metre rope into 4 equal pieces. Is each piece longer or
   shorter than 1 metre? Why?

## Explicit teaching

### Share the tenths, then the hundredths

Division by a whole number is sharing. The trick is to share the *biggest*
place first and trade down when a place will not share evenly — exactly
like sharing dollars, then dimes, then pennies.

The decimal point in the quotient goes **straight above** the decimal
point in the dividend. That is not a magic rule: it keeps each digit of the
answer in the same place as the digits it came from. Tenths shared give a
tenths digit in the answer.

### Worked example 1 — a decimal divided by a whole number

**4.8 ÷ 6**

Think in tenths: 4.8 = 48 tenths. 48 tenths ÷ 6 = 8 tenths = **0.8**.

Written method: 6 goes into 4 ones zero times, so write 0 and the decimal
point, then share the 48 tenths: 6 × 8 tenths = 48 tenths, remainder 0.

Check by multiplying back: 0.8 × 6 = 4.8. Division and multiplication undo
each other — always check this way.

### Worked example 2 — a whole number that gives a decimal answer

**3 ÷ 4**

3 ones will not share into 4 equal whole parts. Trade down: 3 ones =
30 tenths. 30 tenths ÷ 4 = 7 tenths, remainder 2 tenths. Trade again:
2 tenths = 20 hundredths. 20 hundredths ÷ 4 = 5 hundredths.

**Answer: 0.75.** Each share is less than 1 — which makes sense, because
we shared 3 among 4 people and nobody can get a whole one.

Another check: 0.75 × 4 = 3.00. And 3/4 as a fraction is 0.75 — the same
fact in two costumes.

### Worked example 3 — a larger quotient

**9.6 ÷ 8**

96 tenths ÷ 8 = 12 tenths = 1.2. The answer has a whole-number part (1)
and a tenths part (0.2). In the written method, 8 goes into 9 ones once
(remainder 1 one = 10 tenths), then 16 tenths ÷ 8 = 2 tenths.
**Answer: 1.2.** Check: 1.2 × 8 = 9.6.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** 7.2 ÷ 9. (Hint: how many tenths is 7.2?)

**G2.** 5.4 ÷ 6. (Hint: 54 tenths shared among 6.)

**G3.** 2 ÷ 5. (Hint: trade the 2 ones for tenths, then for hundredths if
needed. Is the answer more or less than 1?)

**G4. Error analysis.** Tomas computed 6.3 ÷ 7 = 9. His digits are right
but his answer is wrong. What is missing, and what is the correct answer?

## Supports, alternate modes, extensions

- **Support:** act out the sharing with play money — dollars, then dimes,
  then pennies — before writing anything.
- **Alternate mode:** explain the sharing steps orally while the adult
  writes the long-division steps.
- **Extension:** 4.8 ÷ 0.6 — dividing *by* a decimal. (Hint: 4.8 ÷ 0.6 is
  the same question as 48 ÷ 6, because both numbers were multiplied by 10.
  Answer: 8. This is enrichment only; it is not required in this unit.)

## Teacher note

Two errors dominate: the missing decimal point (6.3 ÷ 7 = 9 instead of
0.9) and stopping at a remainder instead of trading down (3 ÷ 4 = "0 R3"
instead of 0.75). Both are fixed by the same question: "What *place* is
this digit in, and does your answer make sense as an amount?" Guidance:
