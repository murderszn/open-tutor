# Lesson 01 — Digit-Value Relationships

**My goal:** I can explain that a digit in one place is worth 10 times as much
as the same digit one place to its right, and 1/10 as much as the same digit
one place to its left — in whole numbers up to 1,000,000 and in decimals.

**Time:** about 40 minutes (5 readiness · 12 teaching · 8 guided · 12
independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** digit cards or paper strips, a place-value mat
(hundred-thousands through thousandths — draw one if needed), pencil and paper.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.A.1 · **Unit objectives:** UO1

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Answer before we start — this tells us what is already solid.

1. Read this number aloud: **246,810**.
2. In 246,810, what is the value of the digit 4? Name the place value it sits in.
3. In 7,777, are all four 7s worth the same amount? Write one sentence with
   your answer.

*(If reading large numbers or naming place values feels shaky, that is exactly
what today fixes — stay with us.)*

## Explicit teaching

### The one rule that runs the whole number system

Every place in our number system is connected to its neighbors by the number
**10**. That is the entire secret of "base ten":

- Moving **one place to the left** makes a digit worth **10 times as much**.
- Moving **one place to the right** makes a digit worth **1/10 as much**
  (divide by 10).

It works for whole numbers *and* for decimals, because the decimal places are
just the pattern continuing to the right of the ones place.

### Worked example 1 — whole numbers

Look at the number **55,000**.

- The left 5 sits in the **ten-thousands** place: its value is 50,000.
- The right 5 sits in the **thousands** place: its value is 5,000.

Check the rule: is 50,000 equal to 10 × 5,000? Yes — 10 × 5,000 = 50,000.
So the left 5 is worth **10 times** the right 5.

Read the rule the other way: 5,000 = 50,000 ÷ 10, so the right 5 is worth
**1/10** of the left 5.

**The rule only connects neighboring places.** In 606, the left 6 (600) is not
10 times the right 6 (6) — they are two places apart, so it is 10 × 10 = 100
times. Keep your eyes on *adjacent* places.

### Worked example 2 — the rule crosses the decimal point

Look at **0.66**.

- The first 6 sits in the **tenths** place: its value is 6/10 (0.6).
- The second 6 sits in the **hundredths** place: its value is 6/100 (0.06).

Check the rule: is 6/10 equal to 10 × 6/100? 10 × 6/100 = 60/100 = 6/10. Yes.
The tenths digit is worth **10 times** the hundredths digit — even though both
are "after the decimal point."

One more step right: 0.666. The hundredths 6 is 10 times the thousandths 6
(6/100 = 10 × 6/1000). The pattern never breaks.

### Why this matters

If a digit is 10 times its right neighbor everywhere, then multiplying by 10
shifts every digit one place left, dividing by 10 shifts every digit one place
right — and "reading decimals" is just naming each digit's value. The rest of
this unit (powers of ten, expanded form, comparing, rounding) is this one rule
used over and over.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work these with your adult or partner. Hints are given — cover the answer space
and try first.

**G1.** In **222,000**, compare the value of the leftmost 2 with the value of
the middle 2.
*Hint: write each digit's value (place × digit) before comparing.*

**G2.** In **3.3**, how does the 3 in the ones place compare to the 3 in the
tenths place? Use "10 times" or "1/10" in your answer.
*Hint: 3 ones = 3; 3 tenths = 3/10. Which is bigger, and by what factor?*

**G3.** Fill in the blanks: "A digit in the hundreds place is worth **___**
times the same digit in the tens place. A digit in the ones place is worth
**___** of the same digit in the tens place."
*Hint: hundreds and tens are neighbors; so are ones and tens — but watch the
direction.*

**G4 — Error analysis.** Lena says: "In **606**, the left 6 is 10 times the
right 6, because it is to the left." Explain her mistake and give the correct
relationship.
*Hint: name the place of each 6, write each value, then divide the larger by
the smaller.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** In **9,999**, how does the value of the leftmost 9 compare to the
value of the 9 right next to it?

**I2.** In **121,210**, the digit 1 appears in the hundred-thousands place and
in the thousands place. How many times greater is the hundred-thousands 1 than
the thousands 1? Show the division you used.

**I3.** Write your own 5- or 6-digit number in which one digit appears twice
and the left copy is worth exactly 10 times the right copy. Explain how you
know the copies are one place apart.

**I4.** In **7.77**, the 7 in the tenths place is worth how many times the 7
in the hundredths place? Explain using the adjacent-place rule.

**I5 — True or false, with explanation.** "In **505,050**, the 5 in the
hundred-thousands place is 100 times the 5 in the thousands place."

## Application task

**Digit detective.** Write a 6-digit number that has at least one digit
appearing twice. Trade with a partner (or your adult). For your partner's
number: name both places, write each digit's value, and state the exact
relationship ("10 times", "1/10", "100 times", …) with a multiplication or
division check. Then check each other's work.

## Exit check

In **0.55**, explain in one or two sentences how the value of the first 5
(the tenths digit) compares to the value of the second 5 (the hundredths
digit). Use the words "10 times" or "1/10".

## Reading / language-access support

- Keep a place-value word wall visible: hundred-thousands … ones · tenths,
  hundredths, thousandths — with a sample number labeled.
- Teach vocabulary with the mat first ("point to the tenths place"), the word
  second.
- Home-language place-value terms may be written beside the English terms.

## Alternate response mode

- Learners may **point and speak** instead of writing: point to each digit on
  the mat and say its value aloud.
- The adult may **scribe** written explanations while the learner dictates the
  reasoning.
- Digit cards can be physically moved one place left/right to "act out" the
  10× and 1/10 shifts.

## Extension

A county fair reports attendance as **1,111,111** over seven days (above our
1,000,000 band — treat it as a challenge number). Pick any two adjacent 1s,
state each value, and verify the 10× relationship with multiplication. Then
explain why the leftmost 1 is 1,000,000 times the rightmost 1 (how many
10× steps is that?).

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:**
- Applying the 10× rule to non-adjacent places (as in G4) — always ask
  "how many place-steps apart?" and multiply the 10s.
- Thinking the rule stops at the ones place — the decimal examples exist to
  break this; insist on naming the tenths/hundredths places.
- Confusing "10 times greater" with "10 more" — require the multiplication
  check (e.g., 10 × 5,000 = 50,000, not 5,010).
