# Lesson 03 — Powers of Ten

**My goal:** I can explain what happens to digits when I multiply or divide by
powers of 10, and I can use exponents like 10³ to write powers of 10.

**Time:** about 40 minutes (5 readiness · 12 teaching · 8 guided · 12
independent · 3 exit).

**Materials:** place-value mat, digit strips (for the slider game in Session
06), pencil and paper.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.A.2 · **Unit objectives:** UO3

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

1. What is 10 × 10? What is 10 × 10 × 10? Write both answers.
2. In 36 × 100, predict the product before computing — what pattern do you
   expect? (Any prediction is fine; we test it today.)
3. Write 1,000 as a multiplication of tens.

## Explicit teaching

### Multiplying by powers of 10 moves digits left

Take **36 × 100**. First compute: 36 × 100 = 3,600. Now watch the *digits*,
not the zeros:

- The 3 moved from the **tens** place to the **thousands** place.
- The 6 moved from the **ones** place to the **hundreds** place.
- Each digit shifted **two places left** — and every digit is now worth
  100 times what it was. That is exactly what "multiply by 100" means.

It looks like "two zeros got added," but the real story is the **digit
shift**: 100 = 10 × 10, so multiplying by 100 applies the Lesson 01
"one place left = 10 times" rule twice.

### The same shift works for decimals

Take **4.2 × 100**. Compute: 4.2 × 100 = 420. Watch the digits:

- The 4 moved from **ones** to **hundreds**.
- The 2 moved from **tenths** to **tens**.
- The decimal point didn't "move" — the *digits* moved two places left, and
  the point stays put marking the ones place. We say the point "moves right
  two places" as shorthand, but digits shifting is the true picture.

So 4.2 × 100 = 420 because every digit became 100 times its old value:
4 ones → 4 hundreds (400), 2 tenths → 2 tens (20); 400 + 20 = 420.

### Dividing by powers of 10 moves digits right

**345 ÷ 100 = 3.45.** Each digit shifts two places right: 3 hundreds → 3
ones, 4 tens → 4 tenths, 5 ones → 5 hundredths. Dividing by 100 makes every
digit 1/100 of its old value.

**0.7 ÷ 10 = 0.07.** The 7 shifts one place right: tenths → hundredths.

### Worked example 1 — multiply a decimal

**6.3 × 10³.** First, what is 10³? The exponent 3 counts the zeros:
10³ = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000. So 6.3 × 10³ = 6.3 × 1,000.
Shift each digit three places left: 6 ones → 6 thousands (6,000);
3 tenths → 3 hundreds (300). Total: **6,300**.
Check: 6.3 × 1,000 = 6,300. ✓

### Worked example 2 — divide a whole number

**52,000 ÷ 10³.** 10³ = 1,000. Shift each digit three places right:
5 ten-thousands → 5 tens (50); 2 thousands → 2 ones (2). Total: **52**.
Check: 52 × 1,000 = 52,000. ✓

### Exponents: a shorthand for powers of 10

An **exponent** tells how many times the **base** (here, 10) is multiplied by
itself:

- 10¹ = 10 (one zero)
- 10² = 10 × 10 = 100 (two zeros)
- 10³ = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000 (three zeros)
- 10⁴ = 10,000 · 10⁵ = 100,000 · 10⁶ = 1,000,000

The exponent counts the zeros. In student work this unit we use **positive
exponents only** (10¹–10⁶).

> **Teacher note — 10⁰:** If a learner asks, 10⁰ = 1. Explain carefully with
> the pattern: 10³ = 1,000, 10² = 100, 10¹ = 10 — each step divides by 10 —
> so the next step down is 10 ÷ 10 = 1. Do not require 10⁰ in student work.

## Guided practice (with hints)

**G1.** Evaluate **7 × 1,000**. Explain how each digit's value changed.
*Hint: which places did the 7 move through?*

**G2.** Evaluate **2.5 × 10²**. First write 10² as a whole number.
*Hint: shift each digit two places left.*

**G3.** Evaluate **89 ÷ 10³**. First write 10³ as a whole number.
*Hint: shift each digit three places right; you will need a decimal point.*

**G4 — Error analysis.** Maya says 4.6 ÷ 100 = 46. "I moved the point the
wrong way," she admits, "but I don't know which way is right." Explain the
correct shift with digit values, and give the right answer.
*Hint: dividing makes each digit smaller — should the answer be bigger or
smaller than 4.6?*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Evaluate **12 × 10⁴**. Show the exponent-to-whole-number step.

**I2.** Evaluate **0.35 × 10³**.

**I3 — Missing power.** Fill in the blank with a power of 10 written with an
exponent: 6.8 × ___ = 680. Explain how you found it.

**I4.** Evaluate **40,000 ÷ 10³**. Check your answer by multiplying back.

**I5 — Explain your reasoning.** Why does multiplying by 10³ move the decimal
point three places to the right? Answer with digit shifts, not just "that's
the rule."

## Reading / language-access support

- "Exponent" is new — anchor it physically: write 10³, point at the small 3,
  and say "three tens multiplied."
- Let learners describe shifts in their own words first ("the numbers slid
  left"), then attach the formal vocabulary.
- Home-language terms for "power" and "multiply/divide" are welcome on the
  word wall.

## Alternate response mode

- **Slider strips:** physically slide digit cards on the place-value mat
  instead of writing the shifted number.
- **Oral explain:** describe the digit shift aloud while the adult records it.
- Draw arrows on the mat showing each digit's move.

## Extension

**Exponent puzzles.** (a) Write 250,000 as a digit times a power of ten with
an exponent (two ways). (b) Is 3 × 10² + 4 × 10¹ + 5 × 10⁰ the same as 345?
Explain carefully — and say what 10⁰ means here. (c) Order these from least to
greatest without fully multiplying: 4.9 × 10³, 49 × 10², 490 × 10¹.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:**
- "Adding zeros" as a mindless rule — it breaks on decimals (4.2 × 100 ≠
  4.200) and on division; always demand the digit-shift explanation.
- Moving the decimal point the wrong way when dividing (G4) — anchor with
  "dividing makes digits smaller, so they slide right."
- Reading 10³ as "10 times 3" — the exponent counts *factors*, not a
  multiplier; drill with expanded 10 × 10 × 10.
- Confusing 10⁰ — only address if asked, with the divide-by-10 pattern.
