# Lesson 01 — A Fraction Times a Whole Number

**Grade 5 · Unit 05, Lesson 01 · ~40 minutes · Objective UO1**

**Goal (in one sentence):** I can find (a/b) × q by splitting q into b
equal parts and taking a of them — and I can tell a story for it.

**Materials:** fraction strips or paper bars, a multiplication list
(2s through 12s).

**Readiness check (answer before the lesson):**

1. What is 2 × 4? What is 4 ÷ 2?
2. Write 8/3 as a mixed number.
3. If 4 friends share 12 apples equally, how many apples does each
   friend get? Write the division.

## Teaching

In Grade 4 you learned that (2/3) × 4 can mean "2 groups of 4
thirds." This lesson gives that product a second, stronger meaning:
**a partition**. To find (2/3) × 4, split 4 into **3 equal parts** and
take **2** of those parts.

> (2/3) × 4 means: cut 4 into 3 equal parts, then take 2 parts.

Each part is 4 ÷ 3 = 4/3. Two parts are 2 × (4/3) = 8/3 = 2 2/3.
That is the same as the shortcut you may already know:

> (a/b) × q = (a × q) ÷ b

For (2/3) × 4: (2 × 4) ÷ 3 = 8 ÷ 3 = 8/3. The partition tells you
*why* the shortcut works — the numerator counts the parts you take,
the denominator counts how many parts the whole was split into.

**The rule to remember:** the fraction tells you how much of the
whole number you keep. (3/4) × 8 is three-fourths *of* 8, which is
less than 8. (5/4) × 8 is five-fourths of 8, which is more than 8 —
you will prove that in Lesson 03.

### Worked Example 1 — (2/3) × 4 = 8/3

Split 4 into 3 equal parts: each part is 4 ÷ 3 = 4/3.

Take 2 parts: 2 × (4/3) = 8/3.

As a mixed number: 8 ÷ 3 = 2 remainder 2, so 8/3 = 2 2/3.

Check with the shortcut: (2 × 4) ÷ 3 = 8 ÷ 3 = 8/3. ✓

**Story context:** Four friends bake 4 pans of granola bars. They eat
2/3 of all the bars. How many pans' worth did they eat? (2/3) × 4 =
8/3 = 2 2/3 pans.

### Worked Example 2 — (3/4) × 6 = 4 1/2

Split 6 into 4 equal parts: each part is 6 ÷ 4 = 6/4 = 3/2.

Take 3 parts: 3 × (3/2) = 9/2 = 4 1/2.

Check with the shortcut: (3 × 6) ÷ 4 = 18 ÷ 4 = 18/4 = 9/2 = 4 1/2. ✓

**Story context:** A recipe needs 6 cups of flour. You are making 3/4
of the recipe. How much flour do you need? (3/4) × 6 = 4 1/2 cups.

## Guided practice

**G1.** Find (1/2) × 10. (Split 10 into 2 parts; take 1.)

**G2.** Find (2/5) × 15. (Split 15 into 5 parts; each part is 3; take 2.)

**G3. Error analysis.** Sam says (3/4) × 8 = 3/32 "because I
multiplied the bottom number by 8 and left the top number alone." Is
he right? Explain the mistake, and give the correct answer.

## Application

A cook makes 5 batches of soup. Each batch needs 2/3 cup of cream.
How many cups of cream does the cook need in all? Show the partition
(5 split into 3 parts) and write the answer as a mixed number.

## Exit check

1. Find (3/4) × 8. Show the split-and-take step.
2. Write a story for (1/2) × 6 = 3.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** lay out 4 paper bars, cut each into 3 equal pieces,
  and physically take 2 pieces from each bar before writing numbers.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may point to the parts they
  took and dictate the shortcut step to the adult.
- **Extension:** find (2/3) × 4 × (1/2) — first (2/3) × 4 = 8/3,
  then half of that is 4/3 — and explain each step as a partition.

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** multiplying the denominator by the whole
  number (G3: (3/4) × 8 = 3/32). Fix: the denominator counts the
  parts; it does not change when you take parts of a whole number.
  Use the bar: 3/4 of 8 bars is 6 bars, not 3/32 of a bar.
- **Likely misconception:** reading (a/b) × q as "a divided by b,
  times q" with no meaning. Keep asking: "Split into how many parts?
  Take how many?"
