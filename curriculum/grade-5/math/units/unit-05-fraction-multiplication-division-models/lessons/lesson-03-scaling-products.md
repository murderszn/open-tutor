# Lesson 03 — Scaling: Bigger, Smaller, or the Same?

**Grade 5 · Unit 05, Lesson 03 · ~40 minutes · Objective UO3**

**Goal (in one sentence):** I can tell whether a product is greater
than, less than, or equal to a factor — without multiplying — by
looking at the size of the other factor.

**Materials:** the scaling card (">1 grows · <1 shrinks · =1 stays"),
fraction strips.

**Readiness check (answer before the lesson):**

1. Is 3/4 greater than or less than 1? Is 5/4 greater than or less
   than 1?
2. Find (3/4) × 8 and (5/4) × 8.
3. From Unit 03: is 0.4 × 0.7 greater than or less than 0.4? Why?

## Teaching

Multiplication does not always make things bigger. When you multiply
by a fraction, the *size of the fraction* decides what happens to the
other number. This is called **scaling** (resizing):

> - Multiply by a fraction **greater than 1** → the product is
>   **greater** than the other factor. (You took more than all of it.)
> - Multiply by a fraction **less than 1** → the product is **less**
>   than the other factor. (You took only part of it.)
> - Multiply by a fraction **equal to 1** (like 5/5 or 3/3) → the
>   product **equals** the other factor. (You took all of it, exactly
>   once.)

You can compare **without multiplying**. Ask only: is the fraction
I am multiplying by bigger than 1, smaller than 1, or equal to 1?

Why does this work? Remember Lesson 01: (a/b) × q takes *a* parts out
of *b* parts of q. If a < b, you take fewer parts than there are, so
you end up with less than q. If a > b, you take more parts than there
are in one whole q — so you end up with more than q. And multiplying
by 1 is the same as multiplying by a/b where a = b: the fraction
equivalence rule a/b = (n × a)/(n × b) says multiplying a/b by 1 does
not change it.

**The rule to remember:** never say "multiplication always makes it
bigger." Say: "bigger than 1 grows it, smaller than 1 shrinks it,
exactly 1 keeps it."

### Worked Example 1 — Compare (3/4) × 8 and (5/4) × 8 with 8

- 3/4 < 1, so (3/4) × 8 < 8. (Check: (3/4) × 8 = 6, and 6 < 8. ✓)
- 5/4 > 1, so (5/4) × 8 > 8. (Check: (5/4) × 8 = 10, and 10 > 8. ✓)

No multiplication was needed to make the comparison — only the size
of 3/4 and 5/4 next to 1.

### Worked Example 2 — (2/3) × 9, (7/6) × 12, and (5/6) × 1

- (2/3) × 9: 2/3 < 1, so the product is less than 9.
  (Check: (2/3) × 9 = 6 < 9. ✓)
- (7/6) × 12: 7/6 > 1, so the product is greater than 12.
  (Check: (7/6) × 12 = 14 > 12. ✓)
- (5/6) × 1: multiplying any number by 1 leaves it unchanged — but
  note the other direction too: 1 × (5/6) = 5/6, and (5/6) × 1 = 5/6.
  Multiplying a fraction by 1 does not change the fraction, which is
  the same fact as 5/6 = (1 × 5)/(1 × 6).

**Decimal reprise (Unit 03):** 0.4 × 0.7 = 0.28, which is less than
0.4 and less than 0.7 — because 0.7 < 1 and 0.4 < 1. The scaling idea
is the same for decimals and fractions.

## Guided practice

**G1.** Without multiplying, is (4/5) × 7 greater than or less than 7?
Explain using the size of 4/5.

**G2.** Without multiplying, is (9/8) × 6 greater than or less than 6?
Explain.

**G3. Error analysis.** Leo says "Multiplying always makes a number
bigger, so (1/2) × 8 must be bigger than 8." Is he right? Explain,
and give the correct product.

## Independent practice

**I1.** Is (1/3) × 12 greater than, less than, or equal to 12? Then
compute to check.

**I2.** Is (3/2) × 10 greater than, less than, or equal to 10? Then
compute to check.

**I3.** Is (5/5) × 9 greater than, less than, or equal to 9? Then
compute to check.

**I4.** Is (7/10) × 20 greater than, less than, or equal to 20? Then
compute to check.

**I5.** Is (11/10) × 5 greater than, less than, or equal to 5? Then
compute to check.

**I6.** Explain in words why (2/3) × 18 is less than 18 but
(4/3) × 18 is greater than 18. Use the partition meaning from
Lesson 01: how many parts of 18 do you take in each case?

## Application

A juice recipe calls for 4 cups of apple juice. Maya makes 2/5 of the
recipe; Noah makes 7/5 of the recipe.

1. Without multiplying, who makes more than 4 cups and who makes less?
2. Compute both amounts to check your predictions.

## Exit check

1. Is (3/4) × 16 greater than or less than 16? Explain without
   multiplying.
2. Is (5/4) × 16 greater than or less than 16? Explain without
   multiplying.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** hold the scaling card while answering; place the
  fraction on a number line next to 1 before deciding.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may sort product cards into
  "grows / shrinks / stays" piles and dictate the reason for each.
- **Extension:** prove that (3/2) × (2/3) = 1 using the area model,
  and explain in scaling words why the product equals 1 even though
  one factor is greater than 1 and the other is less than 1.

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** "multiplication always makes bigger"
  (G3) — the whole-number habit. Fix with the partition: taking 1/2
  of 8 keeps only half of it.
- **Likely misconception:** comparing the product to the *fraction*
  instead of to the other factor. Always ask: "Bigger or smaller than
  *which* number?"
