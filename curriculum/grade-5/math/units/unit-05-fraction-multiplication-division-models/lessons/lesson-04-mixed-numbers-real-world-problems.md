# Lesson 04 — Mixed Numbers and Real-World Problems

**Grade 5 · Unit 05, Lesson 04 · ~40 minutes · Objective UO4**

**Goal (in one sentence):** I can solve real-world problems by
multiplying fractions and mixed numbers — converting to improper
fractions first, then using a model or an equation.

**Materials:** the Fraction Feast recipe card (from the legacy
Fraction Feast & Recipe Scaling Studio), measuring cups, fraction strips.

**Readiness check (answer before the lesson):**

1. Write 2 1/2 as an improper fraction. Write 1 1/3 as an improper
   fraction.
2. Find (5/2) × 2.
3. Find (3/4) × 2.

## Teaching

Real recipes, gardens, and workshops rarely use proper fractions
alone — they use **mixed numbers**. The rule for multiplying them is
simple, and it is the only new step in this lesson:

> **Convert each mixed number to an improper fraction first, then
> multiply across.** Never multiply the whole-number parts and the
> fraction parts separately.

Why? A mixed number like 2 1/2 is one number (5/2), not two numbers
glued together. Multiplying 2 1/2 × 2 means "two groups of five
halves" — 10 halves, which is 5. If you multiplied the parts
separately (2 × 2 = 4, then 1/2 × 2 = 1, total 5) you would get
lucky here — but 1 1/2 × 2 would come out as 2 1/2 instead of 3, so
the separate-parts shortcut is wrong in general. Convert first, every
time.

After converting, everything is Lesson 01 and Lesson 02 again:
multiply across, cancel where you can, simplify, and check the answer
against a model or a scaling prediction (Lesson 03).

### Worked Example 1 — Doubling a recipe: 2 1/2 × 2 and 3/4 × 2

One batch of flatbread needs 2 1/2 cups of flour and 3/4 cup of
water. You are making 2 batches.

- Flour: 2 1/2 = 5/2. (5/2) × 2 = 10/2 = **5 cups**.
- Water: (3/4) × 2 = 6/4 = **1 1/2 cups**.

Scaling check: 2 batches is more than 1 batch, so each amount grows
— 5 > 2 1/2 and 1 1/2 > 3/4. ✓ (These are the legacy studio's
doubling tasks, re-solved here.)

### Worked Example 2 — A fraction of a mixed number: 1 1/3 × 3/4

You have 1 1/3 cups of batter and use 3/4 of it. How much do you use?

- Convert: 1 1/3 = 4/3.
- Multiply: (4/3) × (3/4) = 12/12 = **1 cup**.
- Cancel first to see it faster: the 4s and the 3s cancel,
  (1/1) × (1/1) = 1.
- Scaling check: 3/4 < 1, so the answer must be less than 1 1/3 —
  and 1 < 1 1/3. ✓

**Story in words:** taking 3/4 of 1 1/3 cups leaves exactly 1 cup.

## Application

A trail-mix recipe makes 3/4 pound per bag. A scout group packs
6 bags.

1. How many pounds of trail mix is that in all?
2. The group then eats 2/3 of what they packed. How many pounds did
   they eat? (Two multiplications — show both.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** write the mixed number, draw its improper-fraction
  bar (for 2 1/2, five halves), then multiply — never skip the
  conversion line.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may build the product with
  fraction strips and dictate the conversion to the adult.
- **Extension:** triple the flatbread recipe (2 1/2 cups flour × 3 =
  7 1/2 cups; 3/4 cup water × 3 = 2 1/4 cups) and check each amount
  with scaling.

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** multiplying whole parts and fraction
  parts separately (G3). Fix: convert first, every time — the mixed
  number is one number.
- **Likely misconception:** forgetting to simplify, or leaving an
  improper fraction when the problem asks for a mixed number. The
  answer key states the expected form for every item; accept an
  equivalent unsimplified answer only as a first draft, then ask for
  the simplified form.
