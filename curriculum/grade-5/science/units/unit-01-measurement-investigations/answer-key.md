# Answer Key — Unit 01

**Grade 5 Science · Unit 01 — Models, Measurement, and Controlled Investigations**
**For the guiding adult only.** Never place beside student questions. All
solutions were worked independently and reconciled with the questions on
2026-10-11. Datasets marked *fictional practice data* are invented for
practice, not real observations.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**
1. a. Testable — measure each tree's height (m) and compare. b. Not testable
   ("nicest" is opinion). Testable reword: "Which season has the most
   daylight hours?" — measure hours of daylight. c. Testable — measure fall
   time (seconds) for each ball. d. Not testable — clouds don't dream; not a
   science question.
2. Observations (any two direct sense notes): "It feels bumpy/rough,"
   "It is about as long as my hand," "It is light." Inference: "I think it
   is a pinecone." Test: smell it, shake and listen, feel for scales.
3. Observation: "The sidewalk is wet" / "the sky is cloudy." Inferences:
   "It rained" / "someone washed the sidewalk." Rule out washing by checking
   whether nearby sidewalks and the road are also wet — if only this one is
   wet, washing is more likely.

**Independent practice**
1. Testable — measure the volume of water the cup holds, in milliliters
   (fill it, pour into a graduated cylinder).
2. Not testable ("better" is opinion). Reword (any testable version):
   "Which meal keeps me full for more hours?" — measure time until hungry.
3. Testable — measure the time until each cube fully melts (minutes).
4. Not testable ("beautiful" is opinion). Reword: "What colors appear in
   the sky at sunset?" — observe and record colors.
5. Observation. 6. Inference. 7. Observation. 8. Inference ("because…" explains).

**Exit check** — 1. A testable question can be answered by observing,
measuring, or testing — you can name what you'd measure or observe.
2. Sample: Observation — "The light is on and the table is clear."
Inference — "Someone cleaned up before we started."

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice**
1. Station results vary by object — check: estimate recorded *before*
   measuring; tool zeroed; units written (g, mL, °C); leaf drawing labeled
   with two observations.
2. a. 80 mm ÷ 10 = **8 cm**. b. 3 m × 100 = **300 cm**. c. 1,500 g ÷ 1,000 =
   **1.5 kg**. d. 0.5 L × 1,000 = **500 mL**.

**Independent practice**
1. Ruler or tape measure, centimeters (meters for a room). 2. Balance or
   kitchen scale, grams. 3. Graduated cylinder or measuring cup, milliliters.
   4. Thermometer, degrees Celsius.
5. 45 ÷ 10 = **4.5 cm**. 6. 6 × 1,000 = **6,000 m**. 7. 2,000 ÷ 1,000 =
   **2 L**; check: 2 × 1,000 = 2,000 ✓.
8. 500 g → **0.5 kg** (check: 0.5 × 1,000 = 500 ✓); 2 kg → **2,000 g**
   (check: 2,000 ÷ 1,000 = 2 ✓); 1,250 g → **1.25 kg** (check: 1.25 × 1,000
   = 1,250 ✓).

**Exit check** — 1. The book started at the 2-cm mark, so subtract 2:
27 − 2 = **25 cm**. 2. 350 ÷ 100 = **3.5 m** (meters are bigger → fewer →
divide).

**Extension** — 1.5 km → 1,500 m → **150,000 cm**. Multiplying by 100 twice
(×100 ×100) is the same as multiplying by 10,000 in one step.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**
1. a. Fair — only the design changes. b. Unfair — light/dark also changed
   (two variables: soil and light). c. Fair — only string length changes.
2. Variable: surface type (the two surfaces). Controls (any 3): same ball,
   same ramp height, same release mark, same floor area, same measurer.
   Measured: roll distance in cm. Trials: 3 per surface.
3. Check the learner's table: columns labeled (e.g., "Trial 1 (cm)"), units
   present, one distance recorded.

**Independent practice**
1. Variable: amount of water. 2. Controls (any two): same plant type and
   size, same soil, same pot size, same sunlight, same time period.
3. Unfair — sunlight changed too, so you can't tell whether the fertilizer
   or the sunlight caused any difference.
4. One trial could be a fluke or a mistake; repeated trials reveal the
   pattern and build trust in the result.
5. Measured: amount of water absorbed, in milliliters (grams also
   acceptable with a scale).
6. Missing: column labels (Trial 1, Trial 2, Trial 3), units in the
   headings, and row labels explaining what A and B are.
7. Light-ball average: (90 + 93 + 91) ÷ 3 = 274 ÷ 3 ≈ **91.3 cm**.
   Heavy-ball average: (88 + 92 + 90) ÷ 3 = 270 ÷ 3 = **90 cm**. Pattern:
   the averages differ by about 1 cm — essentially no difference. Honest
   claim: "In this test the light and heavy balls rolled about the same
   distance, so ball mass made little or no difference here."
8. Problems: (1) only one trial — it could be a fluke; (2) "always"
   overclaims — one test on one floor can't prove what happens everywhere.

**Exit check** — 1. The variable is the one thing *you* change; controls are
everything you keep the same on purpose. 2. The first try could be a
mistake or a lucky fluke — trials show the pattern.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**
1. Averages: low = (82 + 85 + 84) ÷ 3 = 251 ÷ 3 ≈ **83.7 → ~84 cm**; high =
   (141 + 138 + 143) ÷ 3 = 422 ÷ 3 ≈ **140.7 → ~141 cm**. Graph check:
   title, x-axis "Ramp height," y-axis "Distance rolled (cm)," even scale
   (e.g., 0–160 by 20s), two bars at ~84 and ~141.
2. Points: (0, 48), (5, 39), (10, 27), (15, 14), (20, 2). Drops: 9, 12, 13,
   12 g — biggest drop **10→15 minutes (13 g)**.
3. Sample model drawing with labels (ramp, books, ball, floor, arrow) and a
   caption stating the learner's current explanation; plus one named
   observation that would force a revision (e.g., "if the low ramp won a
   trial, my height explanation would need rethinking").

**Independent practice**
1. Sun average: (12 + 13 + 12) ÷ 3 = 37 ÷ 3 ≈ **12.3 cm**. Shade average:
   (7 + 8 + 7) ÷ 3 = 22 ÷ 3 ≈ **7.3 cm**.
2. Graph described/drawn: title, x-axis "Light condition," y-axis "Average
   height (cm)," even scale, bars at ~12.3 and ~7.3.
3. (a) is supported. (b) overclaims — "always" and "every plant" go beyond
   two plants in one test.
4. Ordered pairs: (0, 60), (5, 52), (10, 46), (15, 42).
5. Trend: the temperature fell over time. Fastest drop: **0→5 min (8 °C)**;
   then 6 °C, then 4 °C — cooling slowed down.
6. The scale is uneven (0, 5, 20, 21, 100) — it visually exaggerates some
   differences and hides others; the graph lies about the data.
7. Sample revision: "Both plants got exactly 50 mL of water daily, so water
   can't explain the difference. My new model: the sunlight made the
   difference — the sun plant likely used the light to grow taller."
8. Sample: A wrong model isn't failure — it means the evidence taught you
   something. The revised model is more accurate than the first, and that
   replacement is exactly how science moves forward.

**Exit check** — 1. Bar graph: compare groups/categories. Line graph: show
change over time. 2. Because new evidence can improve the explanation —
defending the first model anyway means ignoring evidence.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**
1. Sample: Criteria — "the soil is still damp on Monday morning" (testable);
   "no plant shows wilted leaves Monday." Constraints — "uses only materials
   already in the classroom"; "setup takes under 15 minutes Friday."
2. Check: question is testable; variable named; controls list complete
   (same ball, ramp, floor, release mark, measurer); measurement with units
   (cm) and trial count (3) stated.
3. Project sheet read; safety check confirmed; ramp set up for Session 14.

**Independent practice**
1. (a) is a **criterion** — "hold 20 marbles" is testable. (b) is a
   **constraint** — "only 10 straws" is a materials limit.
2. Sample: Criterion — "500 mL of muddy water comes out with no visible dirt
   in under 5 minutes." Constraint — "uses only the materials in the kit."
3. In order: question, plan, data, graph, claim and evidence, revised model.
4. The table holds the exact values; the graph makes the pattern and
   comparison visible at a glance — partners, not rivals.
5. Missing: a comparison — what did the plant *without* fertilizer do? One
   measurement alone proves nothing.
6. Sample: The learner expected mass to matter a lot; the evidence showed
   only ~1 cm difference across three trials each, so mass barely mattered
   on that ramp. The revised thinking: ramp height may matter more — the
   next test should change the height.

**Exit check** — 1. Criteria: what a successful solution must do (testable).
Constraints: limits on materials, time, or cost. Example criterion: "the
package keeps the cookie whole after a day in the backpack." Example
constraint: "uses only craft-box materials." 2. One format lets anyone find
the question, data, and claim fast; it makes investigations comparable; and
it builds the year-long record-keeping habit.

## Practice

**Set A** — 1. 9 cm. 2. 400 cm. 3. 2.5 kg. 4. 3,000 mL. 5. 0.68 m.
6. 750 g. 7. 1,200 m. 8. 0.45 L. 9. 2 m 30 cm = 200 + 30 = **230 cm**.
10. 1.5 L = 1,500 mL; 1,500 ÷ 250 = **6 cups**.

**Set B** — 1. a. Balance/scale, grams. b. Graduated cylinder/measuring cup,
milliliters. c. Tape measure/meter stick, meters. d. Thermometer, °C.
2. 215 − 15 = **200 g**. Rule broken: zero the tool before measuring.
3. The cylinder is narrow so small volume changes are readable, and it has a
marked scale; a wide bowl has no markings and the water level barely moves.

**Set C** — 1. Make the same: seed type, amount of water, soil amount and
cup size, start time (temperature/light are the variable being tested — one
cup in light, one in dark).
2. Variable: light vs. dark. Controls (any three): same seed type, same
water amount, same soil, same cup size. Measured: days until sprouting (or
sprout height in cm after 7 days). Trials: 3 cups per condition.
3. One trial could be a fluke or contain a mistake; with no repeats there's
no pattern to trust.
4. Sponge A: (48 + 51 + 50) ÷ 3 = 149 ÷ 3 ≈ **49.7 mL**. Sponge B:
   (62 + 60 + 63) ÷ 3 = 185 ÷ 3 ≈ **61.7 mL**. Honest claim: "In this test
   Sponge B absorbed more water on average (~62 mL vs ~50 mL)." NOT
   supported: "Sponge B is the best sponge there is" / "Sponge B always
   absorbs more."
5. Flaw: two things changed at once (ramp height AND ball), so the cause of
   the longer roll is unknown. Fix: use the same ball and change only the
   ramp height.

**Set D** — 1. Bar graph — the months are categories to compare. x-axis:
"Month"; y-axis: "Books read (number)."
2. Pairs: (1, 4), (2, 7), (3, 11), (4, 12). Trend: the plant grew taller
   each week; growth slowed between weeks 3 and 4.
3. Problems: the y-axis has no units; the scale is uneven (0, 10, 15, 100),
   which distorts the visual comparison.
4. Sample revision: "The towel-wrapped cube melted much more slowly in the
   same warm room, so room warmth alone doesn't explain the melting. My new
   model: the towel slowed heat reaching the ice — insulation matters, not
   just the air temperature."
5. Sample: A wrong model replaced by a better one is progress — the evidence
   taught you something real. Science advances by replacing weaker
   explanations, not by defending first guesses.

**Set E** — 1. a. Testable — measure the juice volume in mL. b. Not testable
("coziest" is opinion). 2. a. Observation. b. Inference. 3. 1,750 ÷ 1,000 =
**1.75 L**; check: 1.75 × 1,000 = 1,750 ✓. 4. Criteria: what success must do
(e.g., "keeps food cold for 4 hours" — testable). Constraints: limits (e.g.,
"must fit inside the backpack"). 5. In own words: question, plan, data,
graph, claim with evidence, revised model.

## Unit assessment

   is opinion and can't be measured.
2. Measure how long each crayon lasts — e.g., days of coloring use, or
   minutes of coloring time (accept any measurable definition with a unit).
3. Observation (any one): "There are dark clouds" / "the playground is
   empty." Inference (any one): "It is about to rain" / "everyone went
   inside because of the weather."
4. a → (i) balance. b → (ii) graduated cylinder. c → (iii) thermometer.
   d → (iv) hand lens.

   ball, same release method/height, same measurer, same tape.
   b. Smooth: (210 + 205 + 208) ÷ 3 = 623 ÷ 3 ≈ **207.7 → ~208 cm**.
   Carpet: (95 + 102 + 98) ÷ 3 = 295 ÷ 3 ≈ **98.3 → ~98 cm**.
   c. Sample: "My evidence is that the smooth-floor average (~208 cm) was
   more than double the carpet average (~98 cm) across three trials each, so
   in this test the ball rolled farther on the smooth floor."

## Project scoring guidance

Use the rubric in [project.md](project.md). Notes for the adult:

- **Fair-test plan:** the variable must be ramp height *only*. If the
  learner also changed the ball or the push, score 1 and re-teach Lesson 03
  before accepting the report.
- **Data quality:** expect low-ramp averages roughly 60–120 cm and high-ramp
  averages clearly larger on the same floor; exact values vary by setup —
  score the *recording*, not the numbers. Spoiled trials noted and re-run
  earn full credit.
- **Graph:** the two bars must reflect the computed averages; an even scale
  is required. If the learner graphed raw trials instead of averages, score
  2 and show the averaged version.
- **Claim and evidence:** the claim must stay inside this test ("in this
  test…", "on this floor…"). "Always" language caps the score at 2.
- **Report and model:** the revision must explain *what the evidence
  changed*, not just redraw the setup. "The ramps were the same length so
  length wasn't the cause; starting higher made it faster" is a strong
  revision.
