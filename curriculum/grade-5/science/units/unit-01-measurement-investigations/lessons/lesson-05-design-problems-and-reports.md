# Lesson 05 — Design Problems and Investigation Reports

**Unit:** 01 — Models, Measurement, and Controlled Investigations · **Grade 5**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 13 of 16

## Goal

You will **define a simple design problem** with **criteria** and
**constraints**, and you will learn the unit's **investigation-report
format** — then launch the project.

## Materials

- Notebook, pencil; the project sheet ([project.md](../project.md))
- Ramp materials for the project launch (board, books, ball, tape, ruler)

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

The adult asks: "Our classroom plants keep tipping over when the window is
open. If we asked an engineer to fix it, what would 'fixed' look like? What
limits would they have?" Expected: "fixed" = the pots stay upright in a
breeze (a **criterion**); limits = can't spend much, can't block the window,
must be safe ( **constraints**). This is engineering thinking — today's
lesson.

## Teaching

### Part A — Criteria and constraints

A **design problem** states a need, what a good solution **must do**
(**criteria**), and the **limits** on the solution (**constraints** — usually
materials, time, or cost). This is 3-5-ETS1-1, and you will use it again in
Unit 08's engineering capstone.

> Problem: "Design a container that keeps a lunch apple from bruising in a
> backpack."
> Criteria: the apple has no new bruises after a normal school day; the
> container opens and closes easily.
> Constraints: uses only materials from the craft box; build time under
> 30 minutes; must fit inside the backpack.

Notice: criteria are *testable* (you can check for bruises), and constraints
are *limits* (not wishes). "It should be pretty" is neither — unless
prettiness is a stated criterion.

**Worked example 1 — spot the missing piece.**
"Build the tallest tower you can from paper and tape!" *Thinking:* there is a
goal (tallest) but no criteria for what counts as a tower (must it stand for
10 seconds? hold a weight?) and no constraints (how much paper? how much
time?). Without criteria, "tallest" can't be judged; without constraints,
the problem has no shape. Fixed version: "Build the tallest free-standing
paper tower that stands for 10 seconds, using at most 5 sheets of paper and
30 cm of tape, in 20 minutes."

**Worked example 2 — criteria vs. constraints.**
Problem: "Design a ramp setup that rolls a ball exactly 100 cm."
- Criterion: the ball stops between 95 and 105 cm from the ramp base.
- Criterion: the setup is repeatable (3 trials land in the same zone).
- Constraint: only the classroom ramp kit may be used.
- Constraint: testing time is 15 minutes.
*Thinking:* criteria describe success (measurable!); constraints describe
limits. If a teammate says "it must be fun," ask: is that a criterion we can
test, or just a wish?

### Part B — The investigation-report format

Every investigation you run this year — starting with the project — is
written up in the same **six-part report**. Learn it now; reuse it all year.

1. **Question** — the testable question.
2. **Plan** — variable, controls (list them), what is measured (with units),
   number of trials. Include the labeled model drawing of the setup.
3. **Data** — the labeled data table, every trial recorded.
4. **Graph** — bar or line graph with title, labeled axes with units, even
   scale.
5. **Claim and evidence** — one honest claim the data support, written as
   "My evidence is ___"; one claim the data do *not* support.
6. **Revised model** — the setup drawing again, with a revised caption
   explaining what the evidence changed about your thinking.

**Worked example 3 — a mini-report, annotated.**
*Fictional practice data.*
1. Question: "Does a heavier ball roll farther than a lighter ball on the
   same ramp?"
2. Plan: variable = ball mass (light vs. heavy); controls = same ramp, same
   height, same floor, same release mark, same measurer; measured = roll
   distance in cm; trials = 3. (Setup drawing with labels.)
3. Data: table — Light: 90, 93, 91 cm. Heavy: 88, 92, 90 cm.
4. Graph: bar graph of averages — Light ≈ 91 cm, Heavy ≈ 90 cm.
5. Claim: "My evidence is that the averages differ by about 1 cm across
   three trials each, so in this test ball mass made little or no
   difference." Not supported: "Heavy balls always roll shorter."
6. Revised model: "I thought heavier would roll much farther. The evidence
   changed my model: on this ramp, mass barely mattered — maybe the ramp
   height matters more. Next test: change the height."

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Write criteria and constraints together** for: "Design a way to keep
   our bean seeds' soil moist over the weekend." (Two testable criteria, two
   real constraints.)
2. **Report walk-through.** Using the Lesson 03 ramp demo data from your
   notebook, draft report parts 1–3 together. The adult checks that the
   controls list is complete before you continue.
3. **Project launch.** Read [project.md](../project.md) together. The adult
   confirms the safety check, and you set up the ramp for Session 14's
   trials.

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (6 tasks).

1. Which is a criterion and which is a constraint? a) "The bridge must hold
   20 marbles." b) "You may use only 10 straws." Explain each choice.
2. Fix this problem statement: "Make something that cleans water." Add one
   testable criterion and one constraint.
3. List the six parts of the investigation report in order.
4. Why does the report require *both* a table and a graph? (One sentence.)
5. A learner's claim: "My evidence is the plant grew 5 cm, so fertilizer
   works." What is missing? *(No comparison — what did the plant without
   fertilizer do? One measurement proves nothing alone.)*
6. Look at worked example 3's revised model. In your own words, what did the
   evidence change about the learner's thinking?

## Application — launch the Ramp-Roll Fair Test

Begin the unit project ([project.md](../project.md)): write the question and
the plan (parts 1–2 of the report), draw and label the setup model, and run
one practice trial at each ramp height to check that your measuring routine
works. Real trials run in Session 14.

## Exit check

1. Define **criteria** and **constraints** in your own words, with one
   example of each.
2. Why does every investigation this year use the same report format?

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** the six report parts are on a wall card; the
  adult reads the project sheet aloud on request.
- **Alternate response mode:** dictate the report; draw the model instead of
  describing it.
- **Extension:** write criteria and constraints for a *real* household
  problem (a wobbly table, a dark hallway) — bring it to Unit 08, where you
  will run the full design cycle.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Confusing criteria with constraints — drill
with "can I test it? (criterion) or is it a limit? (constraint)." (2)
Treating the report as decoration after the "real" fun — the report *is*
the science; the test without the record doesn't count. **Answer key:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-05). **Next:** project trials
(Session 14), review week, formative quiz, unit assessment.
