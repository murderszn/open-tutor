# Lesson 04 — Showing Data and Building Models

**Unit:** 01 — Models, Measurement, and Controlled Investigations · **Grade 5**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 10 of 16

## Goal

You will display data in **bar graphs** and **line graphs**, and you will
build and **revise a scientific model** after new evidence.

## Materials

- Notebook, pencil, ruler; graph paper or the unit's graph template
- The Lesson 03 ramp data (or the fictional practice table below)

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

The adult shows the Lesson 03 practice table (low ramp: 82, 85, 84 cm; high
ramp: 141, 138, 143 cm) and asks: "Without doing any math, which ramp won?"
Expected: the high ramp — you can *see* every high number beats every low
number. "A graph makes that 'seeing' even faster. Today you build one."

## Teaching

### Part A — Bar graphs: comparing groups

A **bar graph** compares amounts across categories. It needs: a title, a
labeled x-axis (the categories), a labeled y-axis (the numbers **with
units**), an even scale, and bars drawn to the right heights.

When you have repeated trials, graph the **average** (mean) of each group —
it represents the whole set of trials in one number.

**Worked example 1 — average, then graph.**
*Fictional practice data from Lesson 03.*
Low ramp trials: 82, 85, 84 cm. Average = (82 + 85 + 84) ÷ 3 = 251 ÷ 3 ≈
83.7 cm → about **84 cm**.
High ramp trials: 141, 138, 143 cm. Average = (141 + 138 + 143) ÷ 3 =
422 ÷ 3 ≈ 140.7 cm → about **141 cm**.
*Thinking:* the bar graph has two bars — "Low ramp" at ~84 cm and "High
ramp" at ~141 cm, y-axis labeled "Distance rolled (cm)" with an even scale
(0, 20, 40, … 160). The picture shouts what the table whispered: the high
ramp's bar is much taller.

**Worked example 2 — read a graph, don't overclaim.**
Look at the bar graph you just imagined. Which statements does it support?
a. "The high ramp's average distance was greater." — Supported.
b. "The high ramp will always win on any floor." — Not supported. The graph
   shows *this test*, not every floor.
c. "Each high-ramp trial beat each low-ramp trial." — Supported by the
   *table*, not the graph of averages. Know which display your claim comes
   from.

### Part B — Line graphs: watching change over time

A **line graph** shows how something changes step by step — usually over
time. Points are plotted in order and connected; a rising line means
increase, a flat line means no change.

**Worked example 3 — a melting line.**
*Fictional practice data.* An ice cube's mass is measured every 5 minutes as
it melts on the counter: 0 min → 48 g; 5 min → 39 g; 10 min → 27 g;
15 min → 14 g; 20 min → 2 g.
*Thinking:* x-axis = "Time (minutes)", y-axis = "Mass (g)". Plot (0, 48),
(5, 39), (10, 27), (15, 14), (20, 2) and connect. The line falls steeply —
the **trend** is decreasing mass. Between 10 and 15 minutes the drop is
13 g, the steepest part. A line graph lets you see *when* change is fastest,
which a bar graph can't show.

### Part C — Models explain; evidence revises

A **model** is a drawing, object, or description that represents something
real and helps *explain* it. Your ramp drawing with labels (ramp, books,
ball, floor, arrow showing the roll) is a model. Models are not decorations —
they carry your current explanation, and **good scientists revise their
models when new evidence arrives**.

**Worked example 4 — revise the model.**
First model (before testing): a drawing of the ramp with the caption "I
think the ball rolls farther because the ramp is longer."
New evidence: the high ramp and the low ramp used the *same* board — same
length — but the ball still rolled farther from the high one.
Revised model: redraw with the caption "The ramps are the same length, so
length isn't the cause. I think the ball rolls farther because it *starts
higher* — it has farther to fall, so it moves faster at the bottom."
*Thinking:* the evidence ruled out one explanation (length) and suggested a
better one (starting height → speed). The model changed because the evidence
changed. That is how science moves.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Graph the ramp data.** Compute the two averages (check: low ≈ 84 cm,
   high ≈ 141 cm) and draw the bar graph together: title, labeled axes with
   units, even scale. The adult checks the scale before bars are drawn.
2. **Plot the melting line.** Using the ice-cube data above, plot the five
   points and connect them. Answer: during which 5-minute interval did the
   mass drop the most? (10→15 min, 13 g.)
3. **Model revision talk.** Draw your current model of *why* the ball rolls
   farther from the high ramp (labeled diagram + one caption sentence).
   Then the adult asks: "What evidence would prove your model wrong?" Name
   one observation that would force a revision.

## Independent practice

Write, draw, or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. *Fictional practice data.* Plant heights after 2 weeks: Sun plant: 12,
   13, 12 cm. Shade plant: 7, 8, 7 cm. Compute both averages.
2. Sketch the bar graph for #1 (title, labeled axes with units, even scale).
   You may describe it in words instead of drawing.
3. Which claim does your graph support? a) "Sun plants averaged taller."
   b) "Sunlight always makes every plant taller." Explain your choice.
4. *Fictional practice data.* Water temperature cooling: 0 min → 60 °C;
   5 min → 52 °C; 10 min → 46 °C; 15 min → 42 °C. Plot the line graph
   points (list the four ordered pairs).
5. Describe the trend in #4 in one sentence. During which interval did the
   temperature drop fastest?
6. A bar graph's y-axis goes 0, 5, 20, 21, 100. What is wrong, and why does
   it matter? *(Uneven scale — it visually lies about the differences.)*
7. **Model revision.** First model: "The plant in sun grew taller because it
   got more water." New evidence: both plants got exactly 50 mL of water
   daily. Revise the model in one captioned drawing or two sentences.
8. **Error analysis.** "My model was wrong, so my whole investigation was a
   failure." Do you agree? Explain what a revised model actually shows.

## Application — graph your own data

Take the fair-test plan you wrote in Lesson 03's application (or the ramp
project data from Session 14) and turn its table into a bar graph: title,
labeled axes with units, even scale, averaged bars. Write one honest claim
the graph supports and one claim it does *not* support. Tape the graph into
your notebook next to the table — table and graph are partners, not rivals.

## Exit check

1. When do you choose a bar graph, and when do you choose a line graph?
2. In one sentence: why do scientists revise models instead of defending the
   first one?

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** graph vocabulary on the wall (title, axis,
  scale, trend); the adult reads data aloud on request.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the bar graph with stacked blocks or
  strips of paper instead of drawing; dictate captions.
- **Extension:** convert the ramp averages to meters (84 cm → 0.84 m;
  141 cm → 1.41 m) and re-label the y-axis — does the comparison change?
  (No — converting units rescales the picture, never the conclusion.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Graphing every trial as its own bar instead
of averaging — allow it once, then show how averages make the comparison
readable. (2) Uneven scales chosen to "make my bar look bigger" — treat as a
teachable moment about honest displays. (3) "Revising a model = admitting I
was dumb" — celebrate revision explicitly; it is the unit's core habit.
