# Lesson 01 — Questions Science Can Answer

**Unit:** 01 — Models, Measurement, and Controlled Investigations · **Grade 5**
**Time:** ~40 minutes · **Session:** 1 of 16

## Goal

You will sort questions into **testable** and **not testable**, and tell an
**observation** apart from an **inference**.

## Materials

- Notebook and pencil; the unit vocabulary cards (or a fresh vocabulary page)
- Four "station cards" for Session 2 (the adult sets these up later): a
  mystery object in a bag (touch only), a sealed jar of water, a plant or
  leaf, an ice cube in a cup

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

The adult reads three questions aloud. You answer: "testable" or "not
testable — yet."

1. "How many legs does our dog have?" → *testable* (you can look and count)
2. "Is blue the prettiest color?" → *not testable* (opinions can't be
   measured)
3. "Which lunch keeps you full longest?" → *testable* (you could observe and
   record)

No writing yet — just talk. If #3 was tricky, good: the lesson explains why.

## Teaching

### Part A — Testable questions

Science answers questions you can investigate by **observing, measuring, or
testing**. A question is **testable** when you can name what you would
*observe or measure* to answer it.

> **Testable:** "Does a plant grow taller in sunlight or in shade?"
> You could grow two of the same plant, put one in sun and one in shade, and
> **measure** their heights after two weeks.

> **Not testable:** "Do plants like sunlight better?"
> "Like" is a feeling word — you can't measure a feeling. But notice: you can
> *reword* it into something testable. "Does a plant grow taller in sunlight
> or shade?" is the testable version of the same curiosity.

Three clues a question is testable:

1. It asks about something you can **see, count, measure, or time**.
2. You can name the **measurement or observation** that would answer it.
3. Someone else could repeat your test and get a comparable answer.

**Worked example 1 — sort and reword.**
Question: "What is the fastest animal in the world?"
*Thinking:* "Fastest" can be measured — speed in kilometers per hour. This is
testable: look up or measure recorded top speeds and compare. The
measurement: speed (km/h).
Question: "Are cheetahs the bravest animals?"
*Thinking:* "Bravest" is an opinion word — no tool measures bravery. Not
testable. A testable reword: "Do cheetahs run faster than lions?" — measure
each animal's top speed and compare.

**Worked example 2 — name the measurement.**
Question: "Does warm water dissolve sugar faster than cold water?"
*Thinking:* Testable. What would I measure? The **time** until the sugar
disappears, in seconds, for warm water and for cold water. Then I compare the
two times. Naming the measurement is the most important step — if you can't
name it, the question isn't ready.

### Part B — Observation vs. inference

An **observation** is something you notice with your senses or a tool — it
describes what is there. An **inference** is an idea about what the
observation *means* — it goes one step beyond what you directly saw.

> You see footprints in wet sand. "There are footprints in the sand" is an
> **observation**. "A person walked here this morning" is an **inference** —
> it might be true, but you didn't see the person.

Scientists need both, but they must never confuse them. Inferences are
starting ideas, not conclusions; you test an inference by making more
observations.

**Worked example 3 — the cold cup.**
Maya holds a cold cup of water. She notices drops on the outside of the cup.
- Observation: "There are small drops of water on the outside of the cup."
- Observation: "The drops are cold."
- Inference: "The drops came from the water inside leaking through the cup."
  (An inference! She didn't see a leak. In Lesson 04's spirit, another
  possible inference is that water from the air gathered on the cold cup —
  and a test could decide between them.)

**Worked example 4 — the empty bird feeder.**
- Observation: "The bird feeder is empty at noon."
- Inference: "Birds ate all the seeds this morning." (Maybe — but the wind
  could have spilled them, or a squirrel visited. An inference needs testing.)

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Do these together, talking through each one.

1. Sort each question. Say "testable" or "not testable," and for testable
   ones name the measurement:
   a. "How tall is the tallest tree on our street?"
   b. "Which season is the nicest?"
   c. "Do heavier balls fall faster than lighter balls?"
   d. "What do clouds dream about?"
2. The adult shows you the mystery bag (touch only, no peeking). Say two
   observations ("It feels bumpy," "It is about as long as my hand"). Then
   say one inference ("I think it is a pinecone") and one more observation
   you could make to test that inference ("I could smell it" / "I could
   shake it and listen").
3. Read: "The sidewalk is wet, and the sky is cloudy." Give one observation
   (the wet sidewalk / the cloudy sky) and two different inferences (it
   rained; someone washed the sidewalk). Which inference could a test rule
   out? (Check whether other sidewalks nearby are wet too.)

## Independent practice

Write or dictate your answers (8 tasks).

1. Testable or not? "How many milliliters of water fit in my favorite cup?"
   If testable, name the measurement.
2. Testable or not? "Is pizza better than spaghetti?" If not, reword it into
   a testable question.
3. Testable or not? "Does the ice cube melt faster on the counter or in the
   fridge?" Name the measurement.
4. Testable or not? "Why is the sky beautiful?" Reword it into a testable
   question.
5. Observation or inference? "The leaf has tiny hairs along its edge."
6. Observation or inference? "The plant is thirsty."
7. Observation or inference? "The thermometer reads 24 °C."
8. Observation or inference? "The water is warm because it sat in the sun."

## Application — the question board

Start the unit's **question board** (a notebook page or wall space you will
keep all year). Write **three testable questions you genuinely wonder about**,
and for each one, write what you would measure or observe to answer it.
Circle the one you most want to test this year. The adult dates the page.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each:

1. In your own words, what makes a question testable?
2. Give one observation and one inference about this room right now.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** vocabulary is introduced with examples first,
  word second; the adult reads questions aloud on request; home-language
  labels welcome on the question board.
- **Alternate response mode:** all practice may be answered orally or by
  drawing; the adult scribes dictated answers.
- **Extension:** find the flaw — "This plant grew 3 cm, so sunlight makes
  plants grow" — what is missing? (A comparison: how much did a plant
  *without* the extra sunlight grow? One measurement proves nothing without
  something to compare it to — the seed of Lesson 03's fair tests.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners often call any science-y question
testable — insist on *naming the measurement*. (2) "I think the plant is
thirsty" feels like an observation to many learners; press for what was
*directly* noticed (droopy leaves) vs. the idea about it. (3) Some learners
treat inferences as wrong answers — affirm that inferences are useful
starting ideas; the error is only *stopping* at the inference. **Answer key:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-01). **Next:** Lesson 02 (measuring
tools and metric conversions).
