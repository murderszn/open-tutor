# Lesson 01 — The Four Spheres, Two at a Time

**Unit 05 · Session 1 (Week 19) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will name Earth's four spheres — geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, biosphere — sort Earth features into the right sphere, and draw your first two-sphere interaction model: two spheres, arrows, and one sentence that says what each sphere does to the other.

## Materials

Science notebook, four sphere word cards (geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, biosphere), a ball or globe if you have one, pencil and crayons. No investigation materials today — this lesson is sorting, naming, and modeling.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

In Unit 02 you learned that a **model** explains what you observe — and that a good model gets revised when new evidence arrives. Today's question: *Earth is one planet, but scientists talk about it as four connected parts. What four parts would you guess — land, water, air, and what else?* Say your guess. The fourth part is the one that is alive.

## Teaching

### Part A — Four spheres, one planet

Earth is not one single thing. Scientists describe it as four big systems, called **spheres** — each one is a system, and each one is part of the whole Earth system:

- **Geosphere** — "geo" means earth. All the solid ground: rock, soil, mountains, sand, and the land under the ocean. If you can stand on it or dig in it, it is probably geosphere.
- **Hydrosphere** — "hydro" means water. All the water on Earth: oceans, rivers, lakes, rain, snow, ice, glaciers, and water underground. Ice counts as hydrosphere, because it is frozen water.
- **Atmosphere** — "atmo" means air. All the gases around Earth: the air you breathe, wind, and clouds. Weather happens in the atmosphere.
- **Biosphere** — "bio" means life. All living things: plants, animals, fungi, tiny living things you cannot see, and people. If it is alive, or was alive, it belongs in the biosphere.

A **system** is a group of parts that work together. Each sphere is a system — and the four spheres work together as one bigger system, Earth. The spheres are always interacting: water shapes rock, air moves water, living things change soil, and so on. That is what this whole unit is about.

**Worked example 1 — sorting.** Look at this list: *mountain, river, cloud, oak tree, ocean, soil, wind, fish, glacier, rain, mushroom, sand.*

- Geosphere: mountain, soil, sand.
- Hydrosphere: river, ocean, glacier, rain.
- Atmosphere: cloud, wind. (A cloud is tiny drops of water floating in the air — the water in it is hydrosphere, but the cloud itself, as moving air and weather, is sorted as atmosphere in this unit. When a part could fit two spheres, say so and pick the one that matters for your question.)
- Biosphere: oak tree, fish, mushroom.

A common error is to put a **glacier** in the geosphere because it is solid and sits on land. But a glacier is frozen water — hydrosphere. Solid does not mean rock.

**Worked example 2 — observation vs. inference.** Science starts with what you actually see, and then with what you think it means. Those are two different things.

- **Observation:** what you notice with your senses. "The grass is wet." You can see it and feel it.
- **Inference:** a conclusion you draw from what you observed. "It must have rained last night." You did not see the rain — you guessed it from the wet grass. It might be right (rain) or wrong (a sprinkler ran, or morning dew formed).

In this unit you will write observations first and inferences second, and you will never call an inference an observation. (This check is revised from the legacy Earth-spheres check-in quiz, whose Scratch questions are optional enrichment.)

### Part B — The two-at-a-time rule

Here is the rule that keeps every model in this unit honest: **describe two spheres at a time.** Four spheres all interact at once in real life — but when you model, you pick two and explain exactly what happens between them. Two spheres, one arrow each way if both change each other, and one sentence.

Look at a puddle on a warm afternoon. The puddle is hydrosphere; the warm air above it is atmosphere. The air warms the water, and the water slowly leaves the puddle and joins the air. That is a **hydrosphere ↔ atmosphere** interaction. Say it in one sentence: *"Warm air heats the puddle water, and the water rises into the air, so the puddle shrinks."*

Now try one yourself: rain falls from a cloud onto soil, and a plant drinks the water. That is three spheres at once (atmosphere, geosphere, biosphere) — too many for one model. Split it: first model **atmosphere → geosphere** (rain falls on soil), then model **geosphere → biosphere** (the plant takes water from the soil). Two models, each two spheres, each honest.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult.

**L1-G1.** Sort these 6 features into spheres: *snow, beach sand, eagle, fog, river stone, seaweed.* Write one word — the sphere — beside each. (Fog is a cloud near the ground: atmosphere.)
**L1-G2.** For each pair, write the two spheres and one sentence about the interaction: (a) a tree's roots hold soil in place during heavy rain; (b) wind blows sand into a new dune shape.
**L1-G3.** "The sidewalk is wet, so it rained." Is that an observation, an inference, or one of each? Split the sentence into the observation part and the inference part.

## Independent practice

**L1-I1.** Draw four boxes labeled with the four spheres. Put these 8 features in the right boxes: *lake, mountain, butterfly, thunderstorm, coral reef, clay, iceberg, grass.* (A coral reef is built by living coral animals, but the reef rock itself is geosphere — put the living coral in biosphere and note the rock in geosphere. Either answer with that note is correct.)
**L1-I2.** Pick one pair from your boxes and draw a two-sphere model: two circles, one arrow, and one sentence explaining the interaction. Label the spheres.
**L1-I3 (error analysis).** A learner says, "A snowball is geosphere because it is a hard ball like a rock." Explain the mistake and give the right sphere, with a reason.

## Application

Tonight, look out a window for two minutes and list 4 things you can see, one from each sphere. For each thing, write one observation sentence (what you see) and one inference sentence (what you think it means) — and label which is which.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *Name the four spheres, and explain in your own words why this unit models two spheres at a time instead of all four at once.*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the feature lists aloud; the learner may sort by pointing to sphere cards instead of writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the two-sphere sentences while the adult writes them, or draw the features instead of listing them.
- **Extension:** find one thing in your home that belongs to two spheres at once (for example, a potted plant: biosphere plant + geosphere soil) and explain both parts.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "a glacier is rock because it is solid" and "a cloud is water, so it is hydrosphere" (clouds are covered in Lesson 03; for now, atmosphere is the working answer, with the note that the water inside is hydrosphere). The two-at-a-time rule is the unit's load-bearing idea — enforce it in every later model. See the teacher guide for reteach steps. Answer-key reference: Lesson 01 section of `answer-key.md`.
