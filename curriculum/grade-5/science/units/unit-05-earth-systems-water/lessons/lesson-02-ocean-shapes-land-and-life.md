# Lesson 02 — The Ocean Shapes Land and Life

**Unit 05 · Session 3 (Week 19) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will explain three ways the ocean changes the world around it: it supports ecosystems, shapes landforms, and influences climate — and you will model each one as a two-sphere interaction.

## Materials

Science notebook, pencil, a shallow tray or baking pan, sand or soil, a cup of water, a spoon. The adult supervises the tray model.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Yesterday you sorted Earth's features into four spheres. Today's question: *the ocean is the biggest part of the hydrosphere. If the ocean disappeared overnight, what would change first — the beach, the fish, or the weather near the coast?* Say your guess and one reason. There is no single right answer yet — the ocean touches all three.

## Teaching

### Part A — The ocean supports ecosystems and shapes land

The ocean is home to more living things than any other place on Earth — fish, whales, coral, seaweed, tiny plankton. That is **hydrosphere → biosphere**: the ocean's water gives living things a place to live, food, and the water they need. A coral reef is the clearest example: living coral animals build a reef in warm, shallow ocean water, and thousands of other living things move in. No ocean, no reef.

The ocean also shapes the land. Waves hit a beach again and again, carrying sand away from one place and dropping it in another. Over time, waves can wear down a rocky cliff into a smooth shore, or build up a sandy beach where there was rock. That is **hydrosphere → geosphere**: water changes rock and soil.

**Worked example 1 — beach tray model.** The adult pours a spoonful of water at one end of a sand tray, tipped slightly, three times. Each time, watch where the sand moves. The water carries sand downhill and drops it at the low end. Claim: *moving water moves land.* Evidence: the sand pile at the low end grows with each pour; the high end loses sand. The model is small, but the rule is the same at a real beach: moving water carries sand and soil from one place to another. A common error is to say the water "destroyed" the sand. It did not — the sand moved. The sand is still there, in a new place.

**Worked example 2 — rainwater and limestone.** Rainwater is slightly sour — not sour like a lemon you can taste, but just enough to slowly dissolve a certain kind of rock called limestone. Over thousands of years, rainwater dripping through cracks in limestone can hollow out a cave underground. That is **hydrosphere → geosphere**, very slow and very strong. The cave is the evidence: the rock did not vanish; it dissolved and was carried away by the water, a little at a time, for a very long time. (This example is revised from the legacy sphere-interactions quiz; the Scratch questions in that quiz are optional enrichment.)

### Part B — The ocean influences climate

**Climate** is the usual weather of a place over a long time — not today's weather, but what a place is usually like. The ocean changes climate. Water warms up slowly and cools down slowly, much slower than land. So a town next to the ocean has milder weather: cooler summers and warmer winters than a town far inland at the same latitude. The ocean stores the sun's warmth in summer and gives some of it back in winter. That is **hydrosphere → atmosphere**: the ocean changes the air above it, and the air sets the climate for the land and living things nearby.

**Worked example 3 — two coastal towns.** Town A sits on the coast. Town B sits 500 miles inland. Both are at the same latitude. In January, Town A is 5 °C and Town B is −5 °C. In July, Town A is 22 °C and Town B is 30 °C. Which town has milder weather, and why? Town A: its summers are cooler and its winters are warmer. The reason is the ocean next to it — the water warms and cools slowly, so the air above it stays closer to the middle all year. The inland town has no big water nearby to steady its air, so it swings hot and cold.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult. Use the two-sphere rule: name the pair, then the sentence.

**L2-G1.** A sea turtle lays eggs on a sandy beach, and the hatchlings run to the water. Which two spheres interact when the hatchlings enter the ocean, and what does each sphere do?
**L2-G2.** Waves wear away a rocky cliff a little every year. Name the sphere pair, and explain where the rock goes — is it destroyed?
**L2-G3.** A coastal town and an inland town have the same latitude. The coastal town's coldest month is warmer. Name the sphere pair behind that difference and explain it in one sentence.

## Independent practice

**L2-I1.** A coral reef supports 500 kinds of fish. Name the sphere pair, and write one sentence explaining why the reef could not exist without the ocean.
**L2-I2.** Rainwater drips through limestone for a thousand years and a cave forms. Name the sphere pair. Then explain why this is slow evidence, not fast evidence — what would you need to see to believe it?
**L2-I3 (error analysis).** A learner says, "The ocean makes the coast warmer in winter and cooler in summer, so the ocean controls the weather everywhere." Explain the mistake: what is right about the claim, and what is too big about it? (Hint: "everywhere.")

## Application

Find a coastline on a map or globe (a printed map is fine). Pick one coastal city and one inland city at about the same latitude. Ask the adult to help you look up their usual January and July temperatures, and write one sentence comparing them. Does the coastal city look milder? If the numbers surprise you, write what you would check next.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *Name two different things the ocean does to the world around it, and name the sphere pair for each one.*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the town temperatures aloud; the learner may answer by pointing to the tray model instead of writing sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the two-sphere sentences while the adult writes them.
- **Extension:** research one more ocean example — ocean currents carrying warm water north, or a mangrove forest holding a shoreline together — and model it as a two-sphere interaction.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "the ocean controls weather everywhere" (it steadies coastal climate; inland weather has other drivers) and "water destroys rock" (it moves or dissolves it; the material goes somewhere). The limestone-cave example is slow-change evidence — use it to revisit the Unit 03 idea that some evidence takes a very long time to form. See the teacher guide for reteach steps.
