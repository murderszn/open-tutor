# Lesson 04 — Where Earth's Water Is

**Unit 05 · Session 7 (Week 20) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will describe and graph where Earth's water is, using the 100-cups model: out of 100 parts of all Earth's water, about 97 parts are salty ocean water and about 3 parts are fresh water.

## Materials

Science notebook, pencil, 100 small cups or 100 counters (buttons, beans, or paper squares), two labeled jars or boxes ("ocean" and "fresh water"), a 1-liter measuring jug, a teaspoon, blue food coloring (optional; adult handles it). The adult supervises the water demonstration.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

You have modeled how the spheres interact. Today's question is about amounts: *if you could gather all the water on Earth — every ocean, river, lake, glacier, and drop underground — into 100 cups, how many cups do you think would be ocean water, and how many would be fresh water you could drink?* Say your guess and one reason. Most people guess far more fresh water than there really is.

## Teaching

### Part A — The 100-cups model

A **reservoir** is a place where water is stored — the ocean is a reservoir, a lake is a reservoir, a glacier is a reservoir, and water underground is a reservoir. The USGS (the United States Geological Survey, the scientists who measure America's water) counted all of Earth's water. Here is what they found, rounded to whole parts out of 100 so a grade-5 learner can graph it:

- **All Earth's water:** about **97 parts out of 100 are salty ocean water**, and about **3 parts out of 100 are fresh water**. The exact USGS table says oceans hold 96.54 percent of all water; the unit rounds to 97 and says so.
- **How much water is that?** All Earth's water together is about 332.5 million cubic miles — a number too big to picture. The 100-cups model is the picture: 100 cups, 97 salty, 3 fresh.
- **About 71 percent of Earth's surface is covered by water.** But almost all of that water is the ocean — salty, and not drinkable.

Try it with the counters: count out 100 counters into one pile. Move 97 into the "ocean" jar and 3 into the "fresh water" jar. Look at the two jars. The ocean jar is nearly full; the fresh-water jar holds almost nothing. That picture is the point of this lesson.

**Worked example 1 — reading the model.** A learner counts 100 cups: 97 ocean, 3 fresh. She says, "Most of Earth's water is ocean water." Her evidence: 97 is bigger than 3 — in fact, 97 out of 100 is nearly all of it. Her claim is supported because the numbers, not her feelings, say so. A common error is to say "3 cups is plenty." Three cups out of 100 is a small share — and Lesson 05 will show that most of those 3 cups are frozen or underground.

**Worked example 2 — the 1,000 mL demonstration.** The USGS made a classroom demonstration with 1,000 mL (one liter) of water standing for all Earth's water. The numbers are labeled practice data from the USGS Water Use poster:

- **972 mL** is ocean water (salt water).
- **28 mL** is fresh water.
- Check: 972 + 28 = 1,000. The two parts add up to the whole — always check that first.

Of the 28 mL of fresh water, **23 mL is frozen in ice caps and glaciers**, **4 mL is ground water** underground, and only **a few drops** are left for lakes, rivers, and water in the air and soil. The adult can show this with a measuring jug and a teaspoon: pour 972 mL of blue water into a big jug (ocean), 28 mL into a small cup (fresh), then split the small cup into 23 mL, 4 mL, and a few drops. The drops are the water most people actually drink from.

### Part B — Graphing the amounts

A claim about amounts is stronger when you can see it. Draw a bar graph:

- Title: "Where Earth's Water Is (parts out of 100)."
- Two bars: **Ocean (salt water) — 97** and **Fresh water — 3**.
- Label each bar with its number. The ocean bar should be about 32 times as long as the fresh-water bar — if the fresh bar is 1 square tall, the ocean bar is 97 squares tall, which will not fit on one page. That is why the unit also uses the 1,000 mL demonstration: same numbers, easier to pour than to draw.

**Rule for every graph in this unit:** label the bars, write the numbers on them, and say where the numbers came from (USGS, rounded). A graph without numbers is a picture; a graph with numbers is evidence. The atmosphere's tiny share of water is **not** on your graph — the framework's boundary for this standard excludes the atmosphere from the reservoirs you graph, even though the USGS table lists a small atmospheric amount.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult. All tables below are labeled practice data.

**L4-G1.** Count 100 counters into "ocean" (97) and "fresh" (3). Write the claim "Nearly all Earth's water is ocean water" and the two numbers that support it.
**L4-G2.** In the 1,000 mL demonstration, 972 mL is ocean and 28 mL is fresh. Show the addition that proves the parts make the whole. Then say in one sentence what 28 mL out of 1,000 mL means.
**L4-G3.** Draw the two-bar graph for 97 and 3. Your fresh-water bar is 3 squares tall. How tall should the ocean bar be? If it does not fit on the page, what is one honest way to draw it anyway? (Hint: break the bar and write the number on it — but say that you broke it.)

## Independent practice

**L4-I1.** A learner says, "Earth is the blue planet, so most of its water must be fresh and drinkable." Use the 97 / 3 numbers to explain the mistake in two sentences.
**L4-I2.** The USGS says about 71 percent of Earth's surface is water-covered. Does that mean 71 percent of Earth's water is fresh? Explain why surface cover and fresh water are two different questions.
**L4-I3 (error analysis).** A learner graphs the ocean bar as 97 squares and the fresh-water bar as 30 squares. What is wrong, and what should the fresh-water bar be? What claim would the wrong graph wrongly support?

## Application

Tonight, ask the adult where your home's water comes from — a lake, a river, a well (ground water), or a city supply. Write one sentence naming the reservoir, and one sentence saying whether that reservoir is part of the 97 or the 3.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *Out of 100 parts of all Earth's water, how many parts are ocean water and how many are fresh water — and where did those numbers come from?*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult counts the counters aloud with the learner; the learner may point to the jars instead of writing numbers.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build the graph with stacked counters or blocks instead of drawing bars.
- **Extension:** look up your town or county's water source and find which reservoir it draws from; add it to your graph as a labeled note, not a bar (your town's share is far too small to see on this graph).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "the blue planet means mostly fresh water" (nearly all of it is salty ocean) and "71 percent water-covered means 71 percent fresh" (coverage is not the same as fresh share). The 97 / 3 rounding must be stated every time the numbers appear — the exact USGS figure is 96.54 percent oceans. Do not let the fresh-water bar be drawn at 30 by accident (that is Lesson 05's ground-water number, not this lesson's). See the teacher guide for reteach steps.
