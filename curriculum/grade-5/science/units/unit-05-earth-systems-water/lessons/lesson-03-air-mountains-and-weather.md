# Lesson 03 — Air, Mountains, and Weather

**Unit 05 · Session 5 (Week 20) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will explain how the atmosphere changes land and living things through weather and climate, and how mountain ranges change winds and clouds — and you will model both as two-sphere interactions.

## Materials

Science notebook, pencil, a book or a stack of books, a spray bottle with water, a towel. The adult supervises the spray-bottle model.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Yesterday the ocean shaped the land and steadied the climate. Today's question: *stand in front of a mountain range. The wind blows toward the mountains, full of wet air from the ocean. What do you think happens to that air when it hits the mountains — does it go over, around, or through?* Say your guess. You will test it with a book and a spray bottle.

## Teaching

### Part A — The atmosphere shapes land and living things

**Weather** is what the air is doing right now — rain, wind, sun, snow. **Climate** is the usual weather of a place over a long time. The atmosphere changes the land through weather: rain washes soil downhill, wind blows sand into new shapes, and freezing water in cracks can split rock apart over many winters. That is **atmosphere → geosphere**. And the atmosphere changes living things through weather and climate: a dry, hot summer means plants grow slowly and animals must find water; a rainy season means plants grow fast. That is **atmosphere → biosphere**.

**Worked example 1 — wind and sand.** Wind blows across a dry, sandy place day after day. The sand grains hop and roll, and slowly a dune grows where the wind drops them. The wind did not create the sand — it moved it, the same way ocean waves move beach sand (Lesson 02). Claim: *moving air moves land.* Evidence: dunes grow on the side the wind blows toward, and shrink on the side the wind blows away from. A common error is to say the wind "made" the dune out of nothing. It did not — it moved sand that was already there.

**Worked example 2 — rain and soil.** After a heavy rain, a garden bed has a little gully where the soil washed away. The rain (atmosphere) moved the soil (geosphere). The soil is not gone — it is at the bottom of the garden, or in the gutter, or in the stream. Where did the soil go? Follow the water, and you will find the soil.

### Part B — Mountains change winds and clouds

A mountain range is a wall of geosphere standing in the path of the atmosphere. When moist air blows toward mountains, the air is forced to climb. As it climbs, it cools, and the water in it gathers into clouds — and often falls as rain or snow on the side the wind comes from. That side gets wet and green. Then the air crosses the top and sinks down the far side, now dry. That far side gets little rain and stays dry. This is the framework's own example: **the influence of mountain ranges on winds and clouds in the atmosphere** — a geosphere ↔ atmosphere interaction.

**Worked example 3 — the book-and-spray model.** The adult stands a book upright on the table (the mountain). The learner sprays a fine mist from one side, low, toward the book (the moist wind). Watch: the mist piles up and wets the table on the spray side, and almost no mist reaches the far side. Claim: *the mountain blocks the wet air, so the near side gets the water and the far side stays dry.* Evidence: the table is wet on the spray side and dry on the far side. The model is not the real thing — real mountains are far bigger and the air climbs over them — but the rule it shows is real: mountains make one side wetter and the other side drier. A common error is to say the far side gets *no* water at all. It gets less water, not none — some clouds do cross, and rivers can carry water around the mountains.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult.

**L3-G1.** A mountain range runs north to south. Wet wind blows in from the west. Which side (west or east) gets more rain? Name the sphere pair and explain in one sentence.
**L3-G2.** A farmer plants a garden on the dry side of a mountain range and the plants grow poorly. Name the sphere pair behind the problem, and name one thing the farmer could do that does not change the mountain.
**L3-G3.** Wind blows sand off a beach and piles it against a fence. Name the sphere pair, and say where the sand came from and where it went — was any sand created or destroyed?

## Independent practice

**L3-I1.** A town at the foot of a mountain gets 200 cm of rain a year; a town 50 km past the mountain gets 30 cm a year. Which town is on the wet side? Name the sphere pair and explain the difference in one sentence.
**L3-I2.** Freezing rain fills a crack in a rock, freezes overnight, and the crack gets wider. Name the sphere pair. Explain why the rock cracks — what does the freezing water do?
**L3-I3 (error analysis).** A learner says, "Mountains make rain, so if we removed the mountains it would never rain." Explain the mistake: what do mountains actually do to wet air, and where else does rain come from?

## Application

Look at a map of the United States (a printed map is fine). Find a mountain range — the Rockies in the west, or the Appalachians in the east. Pick a city on each side of the range and ask the adult to help you look up how much rain each city gets in a year. Write one sentence: does the wetter city sit on the side the wet wind comes from? What would you check if the numbers do not match the model?

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *A mountain range stands in the path of wet wind. What happens to the clouds and the rain, and which side stays drier? Name the sphere pair.*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the rainfall numbers aloud; the learner may answer by pointing to the book-and-spray model instead of writing sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the two-sphere sentences while the adult writes them, or draw the mountain, the clouds, and the dry side instead of writing.
- **Extension:** find a real place shaped by this rule — for example, the dry land east of a coastal mountain range — and write a two-sphere model for it with a source named.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "the far side gets no water at all" (it gets less, not none) and "mountains make rain out of nothing" (mountains squeeze water out of air that is already wet; they do not create the water). The book-and-spray model is a model, not the real thing — say so out loud, and name one way it differs from real mountains (real air climbs over the top; the spray mostly stops at the book).
