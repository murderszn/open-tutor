# Practice — Unit 05: Earth Systems, Spheres, and Water Distribution

Work with the adult where an investigation is involved. All tables in this file are labeled practice data unless a task tells you to measure yourself. Solutions are in `answer-key.md` (teacher file — do not read it before finishing).

## Set P1 — The four spheres (Lesson 01)

**P1-1.** Sort these 12 features into spheres. Write the sphere beside each: *volcano, rain, eagle, fog, river stone, seaweed, glacier, thunderstorm, coral, clay, iceberg, grass.*

**P1-2.** Observation or inference? Label each sentence, then explain the difference in your own words: (a) "The grass is wet." (b) "It must have rained last night." (c) "The soil is dark and damp." (d) "The garden was watered this morning." (Revised from the legacy Earth-spheres check-in quiz; its Scratch questions are optional enrichment.)

**P1-3.** For each pair, name the two spheres and write one interaction sentence: (a) a beaver builds a dam and a pond forms; (b) rain falls on a dusty road and the dust settles; (c) a tree drops leaves that rot into soil.

**P1-4.** A learner sorts a snowball into the geosphere "because it is a hard ball like a rock." Correct the sort and give the reason in one sentence.

**P1-5.** Draw a two-sphere model for this event: *wind shakes apples off a tree.* Label both spheres, draw the arrow, and write the sentence. (Which two spheres? The wind is atmosphere; the tree is biosphere.)

**P1-6 (error analysis).** "The biosphere is the biggest sphere because living things are everywhere." Give one reason the claim is wrong — and name which sphere is actually biggest by how much of Earth it covers. (Hint: think about Lesson 04's ocean number.)

## Set P2 — The ocean shapes land and life (Lesson 02)

**P2-1.** Match each effect to its sphere pair. Effects: (a) waves build a sandy beach; (b) a coral reef shelters young fish; (c) a coastal town has mild winters. Pairs: hydrosphere→geosphere, hydrosphere→biosphere, hydrosphere→atmosphere.

**P2-2.** Rainwater drips through limestone for a thousand years and a cave forms. (a) Name the sphere pair. (b) Is the rock destroyed or moved? Explain with the cave as evidence.

**P2-3.** Two towns sit at the same latitude. Coastal Town A: January 6 °C, July 21 °C. Inland Town B: January −4 °C, July 29 °C. (a) Which town has milder weather? (b) Which sphere explains the difference, and how?

**P2-4.** A learner says, "The ocean makes fish, so without the ocean there would be no living things at all." What is right about the claim, and what is too big about it? Name one living thing that does not live in the ocean.

**P2-5.** Waves carry sand from the north end of a beach to the south end every winter. In spring the north end is thinner and the south end is thicker. (a) Name the sphere pair. (b) Where did the sand go — was any sand created or destroyed?

## Set P3 — Air, mountains, and weather (Lesson 03)

**P3-1.** A mountain range runs north to south. Wet wind blows in from the west. (a) Which side gets more rain — west or east? (b) Name the sphere pair. (c) What happens to the air after it crosses the mountains?

**P3-2.** Town W (west of the mountains) gets 180 cm of rain a year; Town E (east of the mountains) gets 25 cm a year. (a) How many more centimeters does Town W get? Show the subtraction. (b) Name the sphere pair behind the difference.

**P3-3.** Wind blows sand off a beach and piles it against a fence. (a) Name the sphere pair. (b) Where did the sand come from, and where did it go? Was any sand created or destroyed?

**P3-4.** Freezing rain fills a crack in a rock, freezes overnight, and the crack gets wider. (a) Name the sphere pair. (b) Explain why the crack widens — what does the freezing water do?

**P3-5.** A learner says, "If we removed the mountains, it would never rain." Explain the mistake in two sentences: what do mountains actually do to wet air, and where else does rain come from?

**P3-6 (error analysis).** "Weather and climate are the same thing — both mean rain." Explain the difference with one example of each. (Hint: today vs. usually.)

## Set P4 — Water numbers (Lessons 04–05)

All rows are practice data from the rounded USGS figures. Rounding is stated wherever it appears.

**P4-1.** Out of 100 parts of all Earth's water, 97 parts are ocean and 3 parts are fresh. (a) Show that the parts add up to the whole. (b) Write the claim "Nearly all Earth's water is ocean water" with both numbers as evidence.

**P4-2.** The USGS 1,000 mL demonstration: 972 mL ocean, 28 mL fresh. (a) Show the addition that proves the parts make the whole. (b) Of the 28 mL fresh, 23 mL is frozen and 4 mL is ground water. How many mL are left for lakes, rivers, and water in the air and soil? Show the subtraction.

**P4-3.** Why does the 100-cups model say 3 cups of fresh water while the 1,000 mL demonstration says 28 mL? (Hint: 28 mL out of 1,000 mL is 2.8 out of 100 — and 2.8 rounds to 3 when you count whole cups.)

**P4-4.** Fresh water splits into 69 parts frozen, 30 parts underground, 1 part in lakes and rivers. (a) Show that the parts add up to 100. (b) Which share is biggest, and by how many parts is it bigger than the smallest share?

**P4-5.** Use both models together: the fresh-water slice is 3 cups out of 100 total, and 69 of every 100 fresh parts are frozen. About how many cups out of the 100 total are frozen fresh water? (Work it out: 69 out of 100, of 3 cups, is about 2 cups. Show your thinking.) What about ground water — about how many cups out of the 100 total? (30 out of 100, of 3 cups, is about 1 cup.)

**P4-6 (error analysis).** A learner draws the all-water graph with the ocean bar 97 squares tall and the fresh-water bar 30 squares tall. (a) What is wrong? (b) What should the fresh-water bar be? (c) What claim would the wrong graph wrongly support?

## Set P5 — Mixed review (all lessons)
