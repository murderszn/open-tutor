## Lesson 01 — The four spheres, two at a time

**L1-G1.** Snow — hydrosphere. Beach sand — geosphere. Eagle — biosphere. Fog — atmosphere. River stone — geosphere. Seaweed — biosphere.

**L1-G2.** (a) Tree roots hold soil: **biosphere → geosphere**. Sentence: "The tree's roots hold the soil in place, so the rain cannot wash it away." (b) Wind blows sand into a dune: **atmosphere → geosphere**. Sentence: "The wind moves sand and piles it into a new dune shape."

**L1-G3.** One of each. Observation: "The sidewalk is wet." Inference: "It rained." (The wet sidewalk was seen; the rain was guessed — a sprinkler or dew could also explain it.)

**L1-I1.** Lake — hydrosphere. Mountain — geosphere. Butterfly — biosphere. Thunderstorm — atmosphere. Coral reef — living coral: biosphere; reef rock: geosphere (either answer with that note is correct). Clay — geosphere. Iceberg — hydrosphere. Grass — biosphere.

**L1-I2.** Any valid pair with two labeled circles, one arrow, and one sentence. Sample: river (hydrosphere) → soil (geosphere): "The river carries soil downstream and drops it where the water slows."

**L1-I3.** The snowball is frozen water, so it belongs in the **hydrosphere**. Hardness does not decide the sphere — ice is hard and water is soft, but both are water.

**Exit check.** The four spheres are geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, biosphere. Two at a time because four spheres interact all at once in real life, and a model that tries to show everything at once cannot say exactly what each sphere does to the other; two spheres, one arrow, one sentence stays honest and checkable.

## Lesson 02 — The ocean shapes land and life

**L2-G1.** Hatchlings entering the ocean: **biosphere → hydrosphere** (accept hydrosphere ↔ biosphere). Sentence: "The hatchlings leave the land and enter the ocean, where the water gives them a home and food."

**L2-G2.** Waves wear a cliff: **hydrosphere → geosphere**. The rock is not destroyed — it is broken into smaller pieces and carried away as sand and sediment; the beach or the seafloor is where it ends up.

**L2-G3.** Coastal town milder: **hydrosphere → atmosphere**. Sentence: "The ocean warms and cools slowly, so the air above it stays closer to the middle all year."

**L2-I1.** Coral reef: **hydrosphere → biosphere**. Sentence: "The reef needs warm, shallow ocean water; without the ocean, the coral animals could not live and the reef could not exist."

**L2-I2.** Limestone cave: **hydrosphere → geosphere**. Slow evidence: it takes thousands of years. To believe it you would need to see dripping water, rock that is dissolving, or a cave with water still dripping inside it.

**L2-I3.** Right: the ocean steadies coastal climate. Too big: "everywhere" — places far inland are far from the ocean, and their weather is shaped by other things (land, mountains, latitude). The ocean's steadying reaches the coast, not the whole planet.

**Exit check.** Any two of: the ocean supports ecosystems (hydrosphere→biosphere), shapes landforms (hydrosphere→geosphere), influences climate (hydrosphere→atmosphere) — each with its sphere pair named.

## Lesson 03 — Air, mountains, and weather

**L3-G1.** The **west** side gets more rain. Pair: **geosphere → atmosphere**. Sentence: "The mountains force the wet air upward, where it cools and makes clouds and rain on the west side."

**L3-G2.** The problem is **geosphere → atmosphere**: the mountains block the wet air, so the dry side gets little rain. What the farmer can do without changing the mountain: water the garden (irrigation), plant crops that need little water, or collect and store rainwater.

**L3-G3.** Wind blows sand: **atmosphere → geosphere**. The sand came from the beach and went to the fence. No sand was created or destroyed — it moved.

**L3-I1.** The town with 200 cm is on the wet side. Pair: **geosphere → atmosphere**. Sentence: "The mountains push the wet air up, it cools and rains on that side, and the far side gets the dry air left over."

**L3-I2.** Freezing rain in a crack: **hydrosphere → geosphere** (accept atmosphere → geosphere if the learner explains it as rain). The crack widens because water expands when it freezes, wedging the rock apart.

**L3-I3.** Mountains do not make rain out of nothing. They squeeze water out of air that is already wet. Rain also comes from storms, clouds over flat land, and evaporation over the ocean — none of which need a mountain.

**Exit check.** The mountains force the moist air upward; it cools, clouds form, and rain falls on the windward side; the far side stays drier. Pair: geosphere → atmosphere.

## Lesson 04 — Where Earth's water is

**L4-G1.** 97 counters in "ocean," 3 in "fresh." Claim: "Nearly all Earth's water is ocean water — 97 parts out of 100 are ocean, and only 3 parts out of 100 are fresh."

**L4-G2.** 972 + 28 = 1,000. The parts make the whole. 28 mL out of 1,000 mL means fresh water is a small share — less than 3 parts out of 100.

**L4-G3.** The ocean bar is 97 squares tall. Honest way to draw it when it does not fit: break the bar with a zigzag, write "97" on it, and say in a note that the bar is broken.

**L4-I1.** The mistake: blue means water, not fresh water. Nearly all of that blue is salty ocean — 97 parts out of 100 — and only 3 parts out of 100 are fresh.

**L4-I2.** No. 71 percent is the share of Earth's *surface* covered by water. The fresh share is a different question: 3 parts out of 100 of the water itself are fresh. Coverage counts the salty ocean too.

**L4-I3.** The fresh-water bar should be **3** squares tall, not 30. The wrong graph would wrongly claim fresh water is about a third of all water (30 of 100 parts).

**Exit check.** 97 parts ocean (salt water), 3 parts fresh water, out of 100 parts of all Earth's water. The numbers come from the USGS (rounded from 96.54 percent oceans).

## Lesson 05 — Fresh water is rare

**L5-G1.** 69 counters "frozen," 30 "underground," 1 "lakes and rivers." Claim: "Most fresh water is frozen — 69 parts out of 100 are in glaciers and ice caps, 30 parts are underground, and only 1 part is in lakes and rivers."

**L5-G2.** Any two interactions other than river-shapes-land, with pairs. Samples: deer drinks river water — **biosphere ↔ hydrosphere**; clouds form over the mountains — **geosphere ↔ atmosphere**; trees grow beside the river — **biosphere ↔ hydrosphere**.

**L5-G3.** Corrected: "69 parts **of the fresh water** are frozen." The error: 69 is out of 100 *fresh* parts, not 100 total parts of all Earth's water.

**L5-I1.** Three bars: Frozen 69, Underground 30, Lakes and rivers 1 — every bar labeled with its number, source written under the graph ("USGS Water Science School, rounded").

**L5-I2.** Any fifth interaction with a two-sphere model sentence, plus one way the model could be wrong. Sample: tree roots hold the riverbank soil — **biosphere → geosphere**; the model could be wrong if the bank is rock, not soil — evidence that would change it: seeing the bank erode even where trees grow.

**L5-I3.** Lakes and rivers hold about **1 part in 100** of the fresh water, not most of it. The mistake is easy because drinking water comes from lakes and rivers — the water people use most is the water there is least of.

**Exit check.** 69 parts frozen, 30 parts underground, 1 part in lakes and rivers (of 100 fresh parts). Most fresh water is hard to use because it is frozen far away at the poles or locked underground, and only about 1 part in 100 sits in lakes and rivers people can reach.
