# Unit 05 — Earth Systems: Spheres and Water Distribution

**Grade:** 5 · **Subject:** Science · **Track issue:** [#27](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/27)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 19–22 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, about 40 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

Everything in this unit answers one question: **how do Earth's four big systems work together — and where is all the water?** The learner first meets the four spheres — geosphere (land and rock), hydrosphere (water and ice), atmosphere (air), and biosphere (living things) — and learns the unit's golden rule: describe **two spheres at a time**. The ocean shapes beaches, feeds coastal life, and softens coastal weather. Mountains push moist air upward, making clouds and rain on one side and dry land on the other. Then the unit turns to the water itself: out of 100 parts of all Earth's water, about **97 parts are salty ocean water and only about 3 parts are fresh**. And of those 3 fresh parts, about 2 parts are frozen in glaciers and ice caps, about 1 part sits underground, and only a few drops are in lakes and rivers. The learner graphs those numbers twice — once for all water, once for fresh water — and ends the unit able to say, in one sentence, why most of Earth's fresh water is hard for people to use.

Boundary (held throughout): interactions are modeled **two spheres at a time**, per the 5-ESS2-1 assessment boundary. Water amounts are graphed only for the reservoirs the framework names — oceans, lakes, rivers, glaciers, ground water, and polar ice caps; the atmosphere is **excluded** from the learner's water graphs, per the 5-ESS2-2 boundary, even though the USGS table lists a tiny atmospheric share. Percentages are "parts out of 100" language (the scope-and-sequence previews percent; percents are formally a grade-6 topic), and every number is rounded from the USGS table with the rounding stated. No molecular explanations, no causes of seasons, no star or planet content — those belong to other units.

## Prerequisites (re-taught here as needed)

- Unit 01 habits: ask a testable question, run a fair test (change one thing, repeat trials), record in labeled tables, graph quantities, and revise a claim when new evidence arrives. Unit 05 opens with the model-revision habit from Unit 02.
- Unit 02 ideas: models explain what you observe; a model gets revised when new evidence arrives. Every sphere-interaction model in this unit is drawn, checked against an observation, and revised.
- Unit 04 ideas: matter moves among living things and the environment; the biosphere in this unit is the same living world the learner modeled in Unit 04.
- Measuring and graphing quantities in tables and bar graphs (Units 01–04); reading a bar graph to compare amounts.
- Science safety practices: the adult supervises every investigation and every outdoor observation; hands washed after soil and outdoor work.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Name the four spheres** — geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, biosphere — and sort at least 10 of 12 Earth features into the correct sphere (5-ESS2-1).
2. **Develop a model using one example** to describe how two spheres interact, with labeled arrows and a sentence explaining what each sphere does to the other — two spheres at a time, no more (5-ESS2-1).
3. **Explain with examples** how the ocean influences ecosystems, landform shape, and climate; how the atmosphere influences landforms and ecosystems through weather and climate; and how mountain ranges influence winds and clouds (5-ESS2-1).
4. **Describe and graph the amounts and percentages of water** in Earth's reservoirs — oceans, glaciers and polar ice caps, ground water, lakes, and rivers — using the rounded USGS figures: about 97 of 100 parts ocean (salt water) and about 3 of 100 parts fresh water (5-ESS2-2).
5. **Describe and graph the fresh-water split** — about 69 of 100 parts frozen in glaciers and ice caps, about 30 of 100 parts underground as ground water, about 1 of 100 parts in lakes, rivers, and other surface water — and explain in one sentence why most fresh water is hard for people to use (5-ESS2-2).

## Essential question

*How do Earth's land, water, air, and living things change each other — and where is Earth's water, really?*

## Standards and source notes (verified 2026-11-06)

- **NGSS 5-ESS2-1** — "Develop a model using an example to describe ways the geosphere, biosphere, hydrosphere, and/or atmosphere interact." Clarification: examples could include the influence of the ocean on ecosystems, landform shape, and climate; the influence of the atmosphere on landforms and ecosystems through weather and climate; and the influence of mountain ranges on winds and clouds in the atmosphere. The geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere are each a system. Assessment boundary: interactions of two systems at a time. (Source: NSTA 5-ESS2 topic page, opened 2026-11-06; wording matches the track audit crosswalk, scope-and-sequence §4.)
- **NGSS 5-ESS2-2** — "Describe and graph the amounts and percentages of water and fresh water in various reservoirs to provide evidence about the distribution of water on Earth." Assessment boundary: reservoirs limited to oceans, lakes, rivers, glaciers, ground water, and polar ice caps; the atmosphere is excluded. (Source: NSTA 5-ESS2 topic page, opened 2026-11-06; wording matches the track audit crosswalk.)
- **DCI ESS2.A** (teacher background): "Earth's major systems are the geosphere (solid and molten rock, soil, and sediments), the hydrosphere (water and ice), the atmosphere (air), and the biosphere (living things, including humans). These systems interact in multiple ways to affect Earth's surface materials and processes. The ocean supports a variety of ecosystems and organisms, shapes landforms, and influences climate. Winds and clouds in the atmosphere interact with the landforms to determine patterns of weather." (NSTA 5-ESS2 page, opened 2026-11-06.)
- **DCI ESS2.C** (teacher background): "Nearly all of Earth's available water is in the ocean. Most fresh water is in glaciers or underground; only a tiny fraction is in streams, lakes, wetlands, and the atmosphere." (NSTA 5-ESS2 page, opened 2026-11-06.)
- **USGS Water Science School, "How much water is there on, in, and above the Earth?"** (opened 2026-11-06): all Earth's water is about 332.5 million cubic miles (1,386 million cubic kilometers); oceans hold about 96.5 percent of all Earth's water; about 71 percent of Earth's surface is water-covered; "of the total freshwater, over 68 percent is locked up in ice and glaciers. Another 30 percent of freshwater is in the ground." The USGS table (Shiklomanov 1993, via Gleick) gives: oceans 96.54% of total water; ice caps, glaciers, and permanent snow 68.7% of fresh water (1.74% of total); fresh ground water 30.1% of fresh water (0.76% of total); fresh lakes 0.26% of fresh water; rivers 0.006% of fresh water. The unit rounds these to 97 / 3 and 69 / 30 / 1 ("parts out of 100") and states the rounding wherever the numbers appear. The USGS table's atmosphere share (0.04% of fresh water) is **not** graphed, per the 5-ESS2-2 boundary.
- **USGS Water Use poster, middle-school page** (search-verified 2026-11-06): the 1,000 mL demonstration — 972 mL oceans, 28 mL fresh water; of the fresh water, 23 mL ice caps and glaciers, 4 mL ground water, a few drops surface water, a drop in air and soil. Lesson 04 uses this demonstration as labeled practice data.

## Safety and materials

All investigations use household and outdoor-observation materials (clear jar with lid, small pebbles, potting soil, moss or small seedlings, water, spray bottle, science notebook, bar-graph templates, printed water-data tables). The **adult supervises every outdoor observation and every jar step**, prepares a clean unchipped container and fresh potting mix, and washes hands with the learner after soil and outdoor work. Use only plants the adult has identified as suitable; **do not taste plants or soil**. Keep the jar in bright indirect light, never in hot direct sun. If mold appears, the adult removes the affected material; discard the setup if mold persists. If a learner cannot do the outdoor safari, the teacher guide gives observation alternatives (adult-supplied photos, the generated river-valley image, or the jar model) that teach the same evidence.
