# Lesson 02 — Particle Models of Solids, Liquids, and Gases

**Unit 02 · Session 3 (Week 7) · ~40 minutes · Modeling lesson**

## Goal

You will draw **particle models** — labeled drawings that explain what you
observe — for a solid, a liquid, and a gas, and you will use each model to
**predict** how that state of matter behaves.

## Materials

Science notebook, pencil, the particle-model reference card below (an
AI-generated illustration — a *model*, not a photograph), three familiar
objects: an ice cube or rock (solid), a cup of water (liquid), an inflated
balloon (gas).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Yesterday's claim: *matter is made of particles too small to be seen.* Point to
each object and say which state of matter it is. Then answer: *if all three are
made of tiny particles, why does the rock keep its shape, the water pour, and
the air fill the whole balloon?* Today's drawings will answer that.

## Teaching

### Part A — What a particle model shows

A model does not try to draw every real particle — there are far too many, and
they are too small to see. Instead it shows the **pattern**: how the particles
are *arranged* and how much *space* is between them. Three patterns explain
almost everything you observe:

- **Solid:** particles packed **tightly in an orderly arrangement**. They
  cannot move past each other — so the solid keeps its shape.
- **Liquid:** particles **close together but jumbled**, able to slide past
  each other — so the liquid pours and takes the shape of its container, but
  still takes up about the same amount of space.
- **Gas:** particles **spread far apart** with lots of empty space — so the gas
  spreads out to fill its whole container, and can be squeezed (Lesson 01!).

**Worked example 1 — the ice cube.** Observation: the ice cube keeps its shape
when you move it from the tray to the table. Model: draw the particles packed
tightly in neat rows. Prediction from the model: because the particles cannot
slide past each other, the cube will *not* pour or spread — which matches what
you see. The model explains the observation.

**Worked example 2 — pouring water.** Observation: water poured from a cup
into a bowl takes the bowl's shape. Model: draw the particles close together
but jumbled, sliding past each other. Prediction: the particles can rearrange,
so the water *will* take a new shape but keep about the same volume. Test it:
pour 100 mL from the cup to the bowl — the volume stays 100 mL. The model is
supported.

### Part B — Draw first, then compare

Now **draw your own** particle models *before* looking at the reference card.
In your notebook, make three labeled boxes — SOLID, LIQUID, GAS — and draw
your best guess for each, showing arrangement and spacing. This is your first
draft; Unit 01 taught you that first drafts get revised.

Then study the reference card:

**Text-only alternative:** Panel 1 (solid): dozens of small circles packed in

Panel 3 (gas): the same circles inside a closed jar, far apart with large

**Worked example 3 — comparing and revising.** A learner drew gas particles
*clustered in the bottom of the jar*. Comparing with the reference card, the
cluster is wrong: the card shows gas particles spread through the *whole*
jar. Revision: redraw the gas particles spread out, and write why — *because
air fills the whole balloon, the particles must be spread out, not sitting at
the bottom.* That sentence is the evidence-based revision (Unit 01's habit,
reused).

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Model check.** For each of your three drawings, the adult asks: *what does
   this arrangement predict?* Solid → keeps shape; liquid → pours, keeps
   volume; gas → fills container, squeezes.
2. **Fix the model.** The adult describes a flawed drawing: "particles of a
   solid drawn far apart like a gas." You explain what observation that model
   would wrongly predict (the solid would spread out / pour — but rocks don't
   pour), then fix the drawing.

## Independent practice

1. Draw particle models for: (a) the water in your cup, (b) the air in the
   balloon, (c) the rock. Label each and write one prediction under each
   drawing.
2. **Which model is it?** Read each observation and name the state + the
   arrangement it implies:
   - a. "The juice kept the same amount when I poured it into a new cup."
   - b. "The smell of baking bread reached every corner of the kitchen."
   - c. "The wooden block did not change shape when I picked it up."
   (Answer-key reference: AK-2.2.)

## Application — the mystery state

The adult shows a sealed clear bag half-filled with water and half with air
(**do not open**). Draw a particle model of *the whole bag* showing both
states, and write one sentence explaining how the drawing shows where each
state's particles are. (Answer-key reference: AK-2.3.)

## Exit check

Look at your solid drawing. Explain in one sentence **why the tight, orderly
arrangement explains the observation that solids keep their shape.**

## Supports

- **Reading access:** arrangement words (*tightly, orderly, jumbled, spread*)
  are acted out first: learners huddle shoulder-to-shoulder (solid), mill
  around bumping gently (liquid), then spread across the room (gas).
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may build the three arrangements with
  dried beans or beads on a tray instead of drawing; the adult photographs the
  tray for the notebook.
- **Extension:** the Lesson 01 extension asked about squeezing *water* in the
  syringe. Draw the particle model that explains why water barely squeezes
  (particles already close together — little space to remove) versus air
  (much space between particles).

## Teacher note

Watch for the classic error: drawing gas particles *only at the bottom* of the
container, or drawing liquid particles in neat rows like a solid. Both come
from everyday experience (liquids look flat and still; "heavy air sinks").
Counter with the balloon and smell evidence. Do not let learners label
particles "atoms" — the unit keeps particles qualitative. Answer key:
`answer-key.md` §AK-2.
