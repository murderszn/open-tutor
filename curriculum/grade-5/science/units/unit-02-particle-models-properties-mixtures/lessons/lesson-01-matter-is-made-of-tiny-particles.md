# Lesson 01 — Matter Is Made of Particles Too Small to See

**Unit 02 · Session 1 (Week 7) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will gather **evidence** that matter is made of tiny **particles** — pieces so
small you cannot see them, even with a hand lens — and you will state the claim
in your own words: *matter is made of particles too small to see.*

## Materials

Balloon (uninflated), plastic oral/medicine syringe **with no needle**, clear
cup of water, hand lens, science notebook. The adult checks the syringe first
and supervises all squeezing.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

In Unit 01 you learned that a **model** is a drawing or diagram that explains
what you observed — and that you **revise** a model when new evidence arrives.
Today's question: *Can something be real even if you cannot see it?* Say one
example (wind, smells, heat from a stove) and what told you it was there.

## Teaching

### Part A — Air is matter, and matter is made of particles

Blow up the balloon halfway and tie it. Look at it, feel it.

> **What you observe:** the balloon got bigger, rounder, and firmer. It pushes
> back when you squeeze it. Something is inside it — **air**.

Now the key question: *is air "nothing," or is it matter?* Matter is anything
that takes up space and can push or be pushed. The inflated balloon takes up
more space than the flat one, and the air inside pushes back against your
hands. So **air is matter**.

But here is the puzzle: you cannot see the air inside. If air is matter, and
you cannot see it, then matter must be made of pieces **too small to see**.
Scientists call these tiny pieces **particles**.

**Worked example 1 — the balloon as evidence.** Claim: *air is made of tiny
particles.* Evidence: (1) the balloon gets bigger when air goes in — the air
takes up space; (2) the balloon feels firm and pushes back — the air pushes.
Reasoning: something that takes up space and pushes must be made of *something*,
but we cannot see any pieces. Therefore the pieces are too small to see. That
is exactly what "matter is made of particles too small to be seen" means.

### Part B — Squeezing air proves the particles can be pushed together

Put your finger firmly over the tip of the syringe (the small end). Pull the
plunger out to the 10 mL mark to trap air inside, then push the plunger in
slowly while your finger still covers the tip.

> **What you observe:** the plunger moves in — the trapped air takes up *less*
> space. When you let go, the plunger pushes back out.

**Worked example 2 — the syringe as evidence.** Claim: *the air is made of
particles with space between them.* Evidence: the same amount of air fits into
a smaller space when you push, and it pushes back when you release.
Reasoning: if air were one solid, unbroken thing, it could not be squeezed
into less space. Because it *can* be squeezed, it must be made of tiny pieces
with empty space between them — like a crowd of people who can huddle closer
together. The particles themselves are still too small to see.

Notice what this rules out: the idea that air is "nothing." Nothing cannot
push back.

### The claim, in your words

Scientists' model is: **all matter — solids, liquids, and gases — is made of
particles too small to be seen.** Today you found evidence for gases (air).
This week you will build particle drawings for solids and liquids too.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Evidence or not?** The adult reads each statement; you say whether it is
   *evidence* for tiny particles or not, and why.
   - a. "The balloon got firmer when I blew more air in." *(evidence — the air
     takes up space and pushes)*
   - b. "Balloons are fun at parties." *(not evidence — an opinion)*
   - c. "The syringe plunger pushed back when I let go." *(evidence — the
     trapped air pushes)*
   - d. "I cannot see the air." *(evidence only together with a and c — not
     seeing it is the puzzle, the pushing is the evidence)*
2. **Say the reasoning chain** for the syringe in your own words: *what you
   did → what you observed → what it tells you about particles.* The adult
   listens for all three links.

## Independent practice

In your notebook, make a two-column table:

| What I did | What I observed |
|---|---|

Fill one row for the balloon and one row for the syringe. Then write one
sentence under the table: the **claim** these observations support. (Answer-key
reference: AK-1.1.)

## Application — the dissolving preview

Stir one spoonful of sugar into a clear cup of water until you cannot see the
sugar anymore. **Do not taste.** Draw what the cup looks like now, and write
one question: *where did the sugar go?* Do not answer it yet — that question
drives Lessons 04 and 05. (This is a question science can answer, from Unit 01.)

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: **Why does the syringe investigation count as
evidence that air is made of tiny particles, instead of just being "nothing"?**

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the worked examples aloud while the
  learner follows; key words (*particle, evidence, matter*) are already defined
  in the unit vocabulary list.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the reasoning chain and
  the exit check to the adult, who scribes.
- **Extension:** predict what happens if you trap *water* in the syringe and
  push (adult supervises; seal the tip tightly). Compare: water barely squeezes
  at all. What does that suggest about the space between water's particles
  versus air's? (You will model this in Lesson 02.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "the air disappears when the balloon pops, so it wasn't
real." Counter with the push-back evidence: real things push. Another: learners
may say the syringe got "lighter" or "emptier" — redirect to *space taken up*,
not weight. Keep the word *particle* qualitative and visual ("tiny pieces");
do not introduce atoms or molecules. Answer key: `answer-key.md` §AK-1.
