# Lesson 05 — Dissolving: The Particles Are Still There

**Unit 02 · Session 9 (Week 9) · ~40 minutes · Investigation + model-revision lesson**

## Goal

You will test the prediction your particle model made — *dissolved sugar's
particles are still in the water* — with **weighing** and **evaporation**, and
you will **revise** your Week-7 particle drawing with the new evidence.

## Materials

Balance or kitchen scale (grams), two clear cups, water, sugar, spoon, shallow
dish, science notebook, your Lesson 01/02 drawings. Adult supervises; **no
tasting**. (Evaporation takes hours: start the dish early in the day or the
day before so crystals are visible today.)

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Open your notebook to the Lesson 01 application drawing — the cup where the
sugar "vanished." Read your question again: *where did the sugar go?* Your
Lesson 04 model predicted an answer: *its particles spread out among the
water's particles — still there, too small to see.* Today you test that
prediction like a scientist: with evidence.

## Teaching

### Part A — Weighing: the sugar did not vanish

Weigh the cup of water. Write it down: ______ g. Weigh one spoonful of sugar.
Write it down: ______ g. Stir the sugar into the water until it dissolves.
Weigh the cup again.

> **What you observe:** the cup with the dissolved sugar weighs about the same
> as the water + sugar weighed separately. (Small spills or a drop left on the
> spoon explain tiny differences — note them honestly, Unit 01 style.)

**Worked example 1 — weighing as evidence.** Claim: *the sugar's particles are
still in the cup.* Evidence: the dissolved mixture weighs the same as water +
sugar did before mixing. Reasoning: if the sugar had truly vanished, the
weight would have dropped back to the water's weight alone. It did not — so
the sugar is still there, in pieces too small to see. This is the same logic
as the balloon: *what you cannot see can still be real, if it pushes or
weighs.*

### Part B — Evaporation: bringing the particles back into view

Yesterday (or this morning), the adult poured some sugar-water into a shallow
dish and left it undisturbed. Look at it now.

> **What you observe:** the water is gone from the dish, and tiny crystals sit
> at the bottom. They look like sugar. Test properties: they are white,
> gritty, crystalline — the same properties as the sugar you started with.

**Worked example 2 — evaporation as evidence.** Claim: *the crystals are the
same sugar, returned.* Evidence: (1) the crystals have sugar's properties
(white, gritty, crystalline — two-property rule from Lesson 03); (2) the only
material added to the dish was the sugar-water. Reasoning: the water's
particles left into the air (too small to see — like Lesson 01's air), and the
sugar's particles were left behind, close enough together to see again. The
particle model *predicted* this: spread-out particles can come back together.

Notice the boundary you are holding: you explained *what happened* (water
particles left, sugar particles stayed) without explaining the invisible
*machinery* of how. That is exactly the grade-5 line, and it is enough.

### Part C — Revise your model (the Unit 01 habit, on new ground)

Take out your Week-7 first-draft particle drawing of the sugar-water cup.
Revise it now, in a new color:

1. Show sugar particles spread among water particles (not "gone").
2. Add a label: *"Weighing showed the sugar is still there."*
3. Add a label: *"Evaporation brought the crystals back."*
4. Write one sentence under the drawing: *what changed in my model, and what
   evidence forced the change?*

**Worked example 3 — a revision sentence.** Before: "I drew the cup with only
water particles because the sugar disappeared." After: "I added small sugar
particles spread through the water because the mixture weighed the same as
water + sugar, and evaporation left sugar crystals behind." The sentence names
the old model, the new evidence, and the new model — the full revision habit.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Evidence chain.** The adult asks for the full chain, aloud: *prediction
   from the model → test → observation → conclusion.* You give it for the
   weighing test, then for the evaporation test.
2. **Revision check.** The adult reads your revision sentence and checks for
   all three parts (old model, new evidence, new model). Fix what's missing.

## Independent practice

1. **Salt water.** The adult dissolves salt in water (a new material, same
   question). Predict: will the mixture weigh water + salt, or just water?
   After weighing, write whether the prediction was supported and what it
   tells you about salt's particles. (Answer-key reference: AK-5.1.)
2. **Explain it to a 3rd grader.** Write three sentences explaining where
   dissolved sugar "goes," using the word *particles* and one piece of
   evidence. No tasting, no vanishing — honest words only.

## Application — the unit's story so far

Make a one-page "evidence board": three boxes labeled BALLOON/SYRINGE,
POWDER TESTS, SUGAR WATER. In each box, draw the key observation and write the
claim it supports. This board becomes your study sheet for the Week-10 review
and the project.

## Exit check

Answer honestly: **which piece of evidence convinced *you* most that dissolved
sugar is still there — the weighing or the evaporation? Why?** (There is no
single right answer; the reasoning must cite the test.)

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the revision sentence frame is posted: "Before I drew
  ___. The evidence ___ showed ___. Now I draw ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the evidence chain and
  revision sentence; the adult scribes, and the learner draws the revised
  model.
- **Extension:** design a fair test (Unit 01 routine) for the question: *does
  warmer water dissolve sugar faster?* Write the plan only — variable,
  controls, trials, and what you would measure. (Do not run it; it is a
  planning exercise.)

## Teacher note

If the weighing shows a suspiciously *large* difference, treat it as data,
not failure: find the spill, the wet spoon, the un-dissolved grains — honest
error analysis beats a perfect number. If evaporation produced no visible
crystals (too little sugar, too short a wait), use the adult's backup dish and
discuss *why* the first attempt failed — that discussion is science, not a
wasted session. This lesson deliberately stops short of conservation language
("mass is conserved"); Unit 03 will name and extend it. Answer key:
`answer-key.md` §AK-5.
