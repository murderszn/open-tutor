# Lesson 04 — Mixtures

**Unit 02 · Session 7 (Week 8) · ~40 minutes · Investigation + modeling lesson**

## Goal

You will explain **mixtures** with particle models: when materials mix, each
one **keeps its own properties**, and you will predict which mixtures can be
**separated** — and how.

## Materials

Clear cups, water, sand, sugar, spoon, coffee filter or paper towel + funnel,
science notebook. Adult supervises; **no tasting**.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Yesterday's rule: *identify with two properties, never one.* Today's question:
*what happens to properties when you mix things together?* Stir a spoonful of
sand into a cup of water. Observe for one minute. Does the sand still look
like sand? Does the water still look like water? Your model must explain what
you see.

## Teaching

### Part A — Two mixtures, two stories

**Mixture 1: sand + water.** Stir, then stop and watch. The sand sinks. The
water above it is clear again. Run the property tests: the settled stuff is
still gritty (hardness), still tan (color) — it is still sand. The water still
pours like water. **In this mixture, each material kept its properties.**

**Mixture 2: sugar + water.** Stir until you cannot see the sugar. Now test
properties: is there anything gritty? No. Can you see sugar grains? No. But is
the water still just water? In Lesson 05 you will prove it is not — the sugar
is still there, as particles too small to see. Even so, **no new material was
made**: nothing fizzed, changed color, or got hot (contrast this with Unit 03's
baking-soda-and-vinegar preview — *that* makes something new).

The unit's rule: **a mixture combines materials without changing what they
are.** Each keeps its properties. (A mixture that *does* make a new substance
is not a mixture at all — it is a chemical change, Unit 03's topic.)

**Worked example 1 — particle model of sand + water.** Draw the cup. Draw sand
particles as large, heavy dots sitting on the bottom; draw water particles as
small dots filling the spaces around and above them. The model explains the
observation: the sand particles are still *sand* particles (same size, same
grittiness — same properties), just sitting among water particles. Nothing
about either particle changed.

**Worked example 2 — particle model of sugar + water.** Draw the cup. Draw
water particles close and jumbled — and now draw sugar particles as small dots
**spread among** the water particles, too small and spread out to see. The
model explains the puzzle from Lesson 01's application: the sugar *seems* to
vanish because its particles spread out among the water's particles — but the
model says they are still there. Lesson 05 tests that prediction.

### Part B — Can you un-mix it?

If nothing changed about the materials, you should be able to **separate** a
mixture back into its parts. Test both mixtures:

- Pour the sand + water mixture through the filter. Result: the sand stays in
  the filter; clear water drips through. **Separated** — the filter's holes
  are smaller than sand grains but bigger than water's particles.
- Pour the sugar + water mixture through a fresh filter. Result: the liquid
  that drips through is *still sweet-looking* — clear, but the sugar is not
  caught. **Not separated** — sugar's particles are too small and spread out
  for the filter.

Draw the filter as a row of holes in each particle model and show which
particles get caught. This is the payoff of modeling: the drawing *predicts*
the separation result before you pour.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Predict, then pour.** New mixture: dry rice + dry sand, shaken together.
   Draw the particle model first, then predict: can a kitchen sieve separate
   them? Why? Then test. (Answer-key reference: AK-4.1.)
2. **Mixture or not?** The adult names pairs; you say *mixture* or *not a
   mixture* and give the property evidence:
   - trail mix (nuts + raisins) — ?
   - a rusty nail (iron + rust) — ?
   - salt stirred into soup — ?

## Independent practice

1. Draw particle models for (a) sand + water before filtering, (b) the same
   mixture after filtering (two cups: filter with sand, cup with water). Label
   which particles are where.
2. **Separation plan.** You spill a mixture of paper clips and sand on the
   table. Write a two-step separation plan using only a magnet and a sieve,
   and explain each step with properties. (Answer-key reference: AK-4.2.)

## Application — the kitchen survey

With the adult, find **three mixtures** in the kitchen (examples: cereal with
milk, salad, a spice blend). For each, name the parts, state one property each
part keeps in the mixture, and say how you *could* separate it (filter, magnet,
picking apart, evaporation). Record in a table.

## Exit check

One sentence: **why does the filter separate sand from water but not sugar
from water?** Answer with particles, not just "because."

## Supports

- **Reading access:** *mixture* and *separate* are acted out first (mix two
  colors of beads, then separate them with a sieve) before any reading.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build mixture models with two
  sizes of beads in a clear cup instead of drawing; the adult photographs each
  stage.
- **Extension:** research question — ocean water is salt + water. People get
  drinking water from it by *evaporation* (leaving the salt). Draw the particle
  model of what happens when ocean water sits in a shallow pan in the sun for
  days. (This previews Lesson 05's evaporation evidence and Unit 06's water
  story.)

## Teacher note

The big misconception: "dissolved sugar is *gone*." Do not let it stand, but
do not fully resolve it either — Lesson 05 is built to resolve it with
evidence. Another: "filtering *changed* the sand back" — no, the sand never
changed; the filter only sorted by size. Keep the mixture/chemical-change line
bright: fizzing, color change, or heat means Unit 03 territory. Answer key:
`answer-key.md` §AK-4.
