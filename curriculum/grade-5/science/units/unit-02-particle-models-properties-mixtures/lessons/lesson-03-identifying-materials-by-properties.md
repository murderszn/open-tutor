# Lesson 03 — Identifying Materials by Their Properties

**Unit 02 · Session 5 (Week 8) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will **identify unknown materials** by observing and measuring their
**properties** — color, hardness, reflectivity, response to a magnet, and
solubility — and you will learn the detective's rule: **never name a material
from one property alone.**

## Materials

Four small labeled cups (A, B, C, D) each holding a spoonful of a white powder
— prepared by the adult from **baking soda, salt, sugar, flour** (adult's
secret list); bar magnet; hand lens; 4 clear cups of water + spoons for
solubility tests; penny (for hardness/scratch comparison); science notebook.
Adult previews all powders for eye/skin safety; **no tasting**.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Yesterday's particle models explained *behavior* (pouring, shape). Today's
question is different: *how do you tell materials apart?* Look at the four
powders. They all look white. Observation: **color alone cannot identify
them.** What other properties could you test? List ideas before reading on.

## Teaching

### Part A — The property toolkit

Scientists identify materials by **properties** — characteristics you can
observe or measure. For this unit's toolkit:

- **Color** (white, gray, sparkly?) — useful, but never enough alone.
- **Hardness** — does it scratch, or get scratched? (Compare against the
  penny: a material the penny scratches is *softer* than the penny.)
- **Reflectivity** — does it shine or glitter in the light? (Use the hand
  lens: salt crystals sparkle; flour looks dull.)
- **Response to magnetic forces** — does the magnet attract it? (Most powders:
  no. Keep this test — it matters for metals in the practice set.)
- **Solubility** — does it **dissolve** (seem to vanish) when stirred into
  water?

**Worked example 1 — the magnet test.** A nail and a gray bolt look alike.
Hold the magnet near each: the iron nail jumps to the magnet; the aluminum
bolt does not. Conclusion: *response to magnetic forces* separates them, even
though color and hardness looked the same. One property was not enough —
*color* said "same," *magnetism* said "different."

**Worked example 2 — solubility.** Two white powders, salt and sand (from the
scope-and-sequence's example pair). Stir a spoonful of each into water. The
salt seems to vanish (dissolves); the sand sinks and sits on the bottom (does
not dissolve). Conclusion: *solubility* identifies which is which. Note the
careful word: the salt did not *disappear* — Lesson 05 will prove its
particles are still there.

### Part B — The powder investigation (fair-test habits on)

Run the full toolkit on cups A–D, **one powder at a time**, recording in a
table. Keep tests fair: same spoonful size, same amount of water, same
stirring time for each powder (Unit 01's fair-test routine, reused).

| Powder | Color (hand lens) | Hardness vs. penny | Reflectivity | Magnet? | Solubility in water |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | | | | | |
| B | | | | | |
| C | | | | | |
| D | | | | | |

After the table is complete, identify each powder from the adult's secret list
(baking soda, salt, sugar, flour). The rule: **each identification must cite at
least two properties.** Typical honest results: salt sparkles (reflectivity)
and dissolves; sugar dissolves and its grains look glassy; flour does not
dissolve — it clouds the water; baking soda fizzes is *not* tested here (that
needs vinegar — a Unit 03 investigation).

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Two-property rule drill.** The adult covers the labels and asks: "Powder B
   — prove it." You must name two properties from the table and the conclusion
   they force. (E.g., "dissolves completely and sparkles under the lens — that
   matches salt, not flour.")
2. **What would fool you?** The adult asks: "If you only tested color, what
   mistake could you make?" (All four are white — color alone identifies
   nothing. This is the point of the lesson.)

## Independent practice

1. **Mystery liquid.** The adult shows two clear liquids in unlabeled cups:
   water and cooking oil. You may observe but not taste. Plan **three property
   tests** (ideas: how it pours, whether a drop of food coloring mixes in, how
   it feels between fingers — adult supervises) and identify each liquid,
   citing two properties. (Answer-key reference: AK-3.1.)
2. **Property riddles.** Identify the material from the property clues; name
   the two decisive properties:
   - a. "Gray, shiny, hard, jumps to a magnet." → ?
   - b. "White powder, dull, does NOT dissolve, clouds the water." → ?
   - c. "Clear liquid, pours easily, a pinch of salt vanishes in it." → ?
   (Answer-key reference: AK-3.2.)

## Application — the hardware drawer

With the adult, open a drawer of fasteners or a toolbox. Find **two metal
objects that look similar** and run the toolkit on them (magnet test,
scratch/hardness comparison, reflectivity). Write a two-sentence "detective
report": what you tested, and whether they are the same material or different.

## Exit check

In one or two sentences: **why is it a mistake to identify a material from a
single property?** Use today's powders as your example.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the property words are on a word wall with a sketch for
  each (magnet, sparkle, spoon-in-water). The adult reads the table headings
  aloud before testing begins.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may point to and order picture
  cards (magnet / no-magnet, dissolves / sinks) to build the table, with the
  adult scribing words.
- **Extension:** design a test that would separate **sugar from salt** — both
  white, both dissolve, both sparkle. (Honest answer at grade 5: *very hard*
  with this toolkit — crystal shape under strong magnification differs, and
  that is exactly why scientists keep inventing better tests. Naming the limit
  is good science.)

## Teacher note

Common error: learners "identify" from the adult's reactions or from one
striking property ("it dissolved, so it's sugar!" — salt dissolves too).
Enforce the two-property rule every time, including in the project. Safety:
flour dust irritates — keep faces away from cups, no blowing on powders;
solubility cups are single-use per powder to keep tests fair. Do not let the
vinegar-fizz test sneak in — it makes a *new substance* and belongs to Unit 03.
