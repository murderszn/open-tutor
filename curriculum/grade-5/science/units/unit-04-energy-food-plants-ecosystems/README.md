# Unit 04 — Energy in Food, Plants, and Ecosystems

**Grade:** 5 · **Subject:** Science · **Track issue:** [#27](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/27)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 15–18 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)); Week 18 is the midyear review.
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, about 40 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

Everything in this unit answers one question: **where do plants get the stuff they grow from, and where does the energy in food come from — and where does it all go next?** The learner starts with a 400-year-old puzzle: a scientist grew a willow tree for five years, and the tree gained 164 pounds while its soil lost only about 2 ounces. The soil plainly did not supply the tree. Plants get the materials they need for growth **chiefly from air and water** — the sun supplies the energy, not the building material. Then the unit follows that energy: sunlight captured by plants becomes plant matter, plant matter becomes animal food, and the energy in that food powers body repair, growth, motion, and warmth. Finally it follows the **matter**: when plants and animals die, decomposers break their remains down, and the matter returns to the soil, air, and water, where new plants can use it again. The unit's one big contrast: **energy flows one way through an ecosystem; matter cycles around and around.**

Boundary (held throughout): explanations stay **observable and macroscopic**. The unit does not teach molecular photosynthesis, does not name molecules, and does not distinguish mass from weight — the scale reading is called **weight**, per the grade-5 boundaries carried from Units 01–03. "Plants make food" means plants form plant matter from air and water using sunlight energy; the learner argues from growth and weighing evidence, not chemistry.

## Prerequisites (re-taught here as needed)

- Unit 01 habits: ask a testable question, run a fair test (change one thing, repeat trials), record in labeled tables, graph quantities, and revise a claim when new evidence arrives. The midyear review sessions reuse the Unit 01 report format.
- Unit 02 ideas: matter is made of particles too small to see; a model explains what you observe. Lesson 03 reuses particle-model language ("tiny pieces too small to see") for matter moving between organisms and the environment.
- Unit 03 ideas: the total weight of matter is conserved; gas can leave a system and change a measured weight. Lesson 03 uses the "left the system" idea when leaf-litter matter moves into soil, air, and decomposers' bodies.
- Science safety practices: the adult supervises every investigation, and the learner never tastes investigation materials, never eats wild mushrooms or plants, and washes hands after handling soil or leaf litter.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Support an argument with evidence** that plants get the materials they need for growth chiefly from air and water, not from the soil — using the van Helmont weighing evidence and a new weighing case, and naming what the evidence cannot prove (5-LS1-1).
2. **Use models (diagrams and flow charts)** to describe that the energy in animals' food was once energy from the sun, and to name its four body uses — body repair, growth, motion, and maintaining body warmth (5-PS3-1).
3. **Develop a model** to describe the movement of matter among plants, animals, decomposers, and the environment, including the idea that matter that is not food (air, water, decomposed materials in soil) is changed by plants into matter that is food (5-LS2-1).
4. **Explain the energy/matter contrast** in the learner's own model: energy flows one way from the sun through producers and consumers and does not cycle back to the sun, while matter cycles among organisms and the environment (5-PS3-1, 5-LS2-1, crosscutting concept: Energy and Matter).
5. **Revise a midyear model** using evidence from Units 01–04: state one claim from each earlier unit, cite its evidence, and correct one earlier misconception in writing or drawing (practices: constructing explanations; engaging in argument from evidence).

## Standards and source notes (verified 2026-10-31)

- **NGSS 5-PS3-1** — "Use models to describe that energy in animals' food (used for body repair, growth, motion, and to maintain body warmth) was once energy from the sun." Clarification: examples of models could include diagrams and flow charts. Disciplinary core idea PS3.D: the energy released from food was once energy from the sun that was captured by plants in the chemical process that forms plant matter (from air and water). (Source: NGSS grade-5 performance expectations; wording re-confirmed 2026-10-31 against the California Department of Education NGSS listing for 5-PS3-1 and the track audit crosswalk, scope-and-sequence §7.)
- **NGSS 5-LS1-1** — "Support an argument that plants get the materials they need for growth chiefly from air and water." Clarification: emphasis is on the idea that plant matter comes mostly from air and water, not from the soil. Assessment boundary: no molecular explanations of photosynthesis. (Source: NGSS grade-5 performance expectations; wording re-confirmed 2026-10-31 against the OKScience Framework 5-LS1-1 page, which states the same clarification and boundary, and the track audit crosswalk.)
- **NGSS 5-LS2-1** — "Develop a model to describe the movement of matter among plants, animals, decomposers, and the environment." Clarification: emphasis is on the idea that matter that is not food (air, water, decomposed materials in soil) is changed by plants into matter that is food. Assessment boundary: no molecular explanations. (Source: NGSS grade-5 performance expectations; wording matches the track audit crosswalk, scope-and-sequence §7.)
- **Van Helmont's willow experiment (teacher background, retold for learners in Lesson 01):** in the early 1600s Jan Baptista van Helmont planted a 5-pound willow shoot in 200 pounds of oven-dried soil, watered it with rain or distilled water only for five years, and re-weighed: the tree weighed 169 pounds and about 3 ounces (a gain of 164 pounds), while the dried soil weighed 200 pounds less about 2 ounces. He concluded the tree's growth came from water alone — a conclusion modern science corrects: the growth material came chiefly from **air and water**, and the experiment's real achievement is ruling out the soil as the main source. (Source: van Helmont's own account as reproduced in the College of Saint Benedict / Saint John's University Biology 106 lecture notes and summarized by IFLScience, both checked 2026-10-31. The lesson states van Helmont's limits honestly: he did not track fallen leaves, and he did not test air's role.)
- **Practices:** developing and using models; engaging in argument from evidence; analyzing and interpreting data (weighing tables, leaf-litter mass graph); constructing explanations from evidence.

## Safety and materials

All investigations use household or outdoor-observation materials (potted plant or seedling, kitchen scale, notebook, clear lidded container, leaf litter, hand lens, gloves). The **adult supervises every investigation**, and the learner **never tastes** investigation materials, **never eats wild mushrooms, berries, or plants**, and **washes hands** after handling soil or leaf litter. Wild animals are observed, never approached or handled. If outdoor observation is impossible, the teacher guide gives observation alternatives (adult-collected leaf litter in a tray, video, or the generated-image activity) that teach the same evidence.
