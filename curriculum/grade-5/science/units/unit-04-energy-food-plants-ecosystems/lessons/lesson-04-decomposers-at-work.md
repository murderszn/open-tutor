# Lesson 04 — Decomposers at Work

**Unit 04 · Session 7 (Week 16) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson (observation begins; check-ins continue in Sessions 8 and 10)**

## Goal

You will set up a safe leaf-litter observation, predict what decomposers will do, and use a practice dataset to graph how leaf matter changes over time — without claiming that matter vanishes.

## Materials

Adult-supervised: a clear lidded container or tray, freshly fallen leaves, a small amount of garden soil, a spray bottle of water, gloves, a hand lens, science notebook, pencil. The adult collects the materials and checks the setup. You will observe, not taste or handle anything with bare hands after the setup, and you will wash your hands when you finish.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

In Lesson 03 you drew decomposers breaking down a fallen leaf. Today you watch it happen for real — slowly. Before you look at any results: what changes do you expect to see in the leaves over two weeks? Say or write two predictions (for example, "the leaves will get softer" or "the soil under them will get darker").

## Teaching

### Part A — Setting up the observation (adult leads)

The adult places a layer of soil in the container, adds a layer of fallen leaves, sprinkles a little water so the leaves are damp but not soggy, and closes the lid loosely so air can still enter. The container stays at room temperature, out of direct sunlight. You record the starting observation: leaf color, stiffness, smell (sniff from a distance only — never press your nose to the container), and any tiny animals visible (pill bugs, worms, and other small soil animals are welcome guests; they are decomposers and helpers, and they stay in the container).

**Safety rules, every time:** the adult handles the container setup; you wear gloves for handling leaves and soil; nothing from the container goes in your mouth; wild mushrooms are never tasted; hands are washed after every check-in; animals are observed, never squeezed or removed from their habitat.

### Part B — Reading a decay dataset

Real leaf-litter decay takes weeks, so today you also practice with a labeled practice dataset from a learner who weighed a mesh bag of leaves left outdoors. The bag lets tiny decomposers and water in and out but keeps the leaf pieces together for weighing. (These are practice numbers, not your measurements.)

| Week | Leaf-bag weight | What the learner observed |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 40 grams | Dry, stiff, whole leaves |
| 2 | 34 grams | Leaves softer, darker at the edges |
| 4 | 27 grams | Leaves in pieces, dark soil forming underneath |

**Worked example 1 — graphing and explaining the drop.** Draw a bar graph: three bars at 40, 34, and 27 grams. The bars shrink — but the matter did not vanish. Remember Unit 03: when a measured weight drops, ask what **left the system**. Here, leaf matter left the bag in three ways: into decomposers' bodies as they ate and grew, into the soil underneath as dark crumbly material, and into the air as gas (the same "gas escapes" idea from the open-cup investigation in Unit 03). Total change: 40 − 27 = **13 grams** left the bag over four weeks. A common error is to write "the leaves disappeared." The graph answers that: the leaves broke down and their matter moved — the evidence is the darker soil and the decomposers that grew.

**Worked example 2 — predictions versus results.** Suppose a learner predicted "the leaves will stay the same because nothing eats dead leaves." The observations contradict that prediction: the leaves softened, darkened, broke into pieces, and lost 13 grams. In science, a wrong prediction is useful: it tells you the old idea ("dead leaves just sit there") was wrong and the new idea ("decomposers break dead matter down") fits the evidence better. You will revise your own predictions at the Session 8 check-in.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult. Use the practice dataset above.

**L4-G1.** Compute the weight change from Week 0 to Week 2, and from Week 2 to Week 4. Which two-week period lost more?
**L4-G2.** Name three places the 13 grams of lost leaf matter could have gone, using the decomposer loop from Lesson 03.
**L4-G3.** Write one sentence revising this prediction: "Dead leaves never change." Use one observation from the table as evidence.

## Independent practice

**L4-I1.** A second leaf bag starts at 50 grams and weighs 38 grams after four weeks. How many grams left the bag? Name two places that matter could have gone.
**L4-I2.** A learner says, "Decomposers are disgusting, so they don't matter for the ecosystem." Explain, using the matter loop, what would happen to a meadow if decomposers stopped working (dead matter piles up; matter stops returning to soil, air, and water; new plants run short of material).
**L4-I3 (error analysis).** A learner weighs a leaf bag before and after drying it in the sun and claims the weight loss proves decomposers ate the leaves. Explain why this test is unfair: drying removes water, which is a different change from decomposition. How would you redo the test fairly? (Keep the bag's water conditions the same, or compare two bags kept the same way, one with decomposers present and one kept clean — a fair test from Unit 01.)

## Application

At the Session 8 check-in (and again in Session 10), observe your real leaf-litter container and record: leaf color and stiffness, soil color underneath, and any tiny animals or fungi you see. Compare each check-in with your predictions from the readiness check. Do not open the container without the adult, and do not remove anything from it.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *A leaf bag loses 13 grams in four weeks. Did the matter vanish? Where did it go?*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the dataset table aloud; the learner may answer by pointing to the shrinking bars on the graph instead of writing sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate observations while the adult writes them, or draw the leaf changes instead of writing.
- **Extension:** if your container shows tiny animals, draw one, label it as a decomposer or helper, and write one sentence about what it does in the loop. (Do not name a species you are unsure of — "small soil animal" is a fine label.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "decay means disappearance" and "decomposers are unimportant." Both are addressed by the graph and the loop. The mold-safety note matters: this unit deliberately observes outdoor leaf litter rather than growing mold indoors. See the teacher guide for reteach steps and for the no-outdoor-access alternative.
