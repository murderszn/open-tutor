# Lesson 01 — Plants Grow Chiefly from Air and Water

**Unit 04 · Session 1 (Week 15) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will use a 400-year-old weighing experiment to argue, with numbers, that plants get the materials they need for growth chiefly from air and water — not from the soil.

## Materials

Science notebook, pencil, the two weighing tables below. No investigation materials are needed today: this lesson argues from a historical experiment's recorded numbers, and your own weighing case comes in guided practice.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Think about a tree near your home. It grew from a small seedling into something far heavier. Where did all that extra tree-stuff come from? Most people guess "from the soil." Say your guess and one reason. Keep it in mind — today's numbers will test it.

## Teaching

### Part A — The willow-tree puzzle

In the early 1600s, a scientist named Jan Baptista van Helmont wanted to answer exactly your readiness question. He did something careful that most people never do: he **weighed everything first**.

- He took an earthen pot and filled it with soil that he had dried in an oven. The dry soil weighed **200 pounds**.
- He planted a willow shoot (a young willow stem) that weighed **5 pounds**.
- For **five years** he watered the pot with rainwater or distilled water only, whenever it was needed. A pierced lid kept dust and dirt from blowing into the pot.
- After five years he dried the soil again and weighed everything a second time.

Here is what he found:

| What was weighed | Before (start) | After (5 years later) |
|---|---|---|
| Willow tree | 5 pounds | 169 pounds and about 3 ounces |
| Soil (dried) | 200 pounds | 200 pounds less about 2 ounces |

Now do the subtraction the way van Helmont did:

- Tree gain: 169 pounds − 5 pounds = **164 pounds** of new tree — wood, bark, and roots.
- Soil loss: about **2 ounces**. There are 16 ounces in a pound, so 2 ounces is only **1/8 of a pound**.

**Worked example 1 — the argument in numbers.** Claim: *the tree's new growth did not come chiefly from the soil.* Evidence: the tree gained 164 pounds, but the soil lost only about 2 ounces — far too little to supply 164 pounds of tree. Reasoning: if the tree had been "eating" the soil, the soil would have to lose about as much weight as the tree gained. It lost almost nothing, so the growth material came from somewhere else: the water he added, and — as scientists later showed — the air around the tree. A common error is to say "the soil lost 2 ounces, so 2 ounces of the tree came from soil and the rest from water." That is closer, but still misses the air: modern science says plant growth material comes **chiefly from air and water together**, and the soil supplies only small amounts of other materials the plant needs.

**Worked example 2 — what the experiment cannot prove.** Van Helmont himself concluded that the tree grew from water alone. Was he right? Not completely. His experiment rules out the soil as the main source, but it does **not** separate water from air — the tree sat in open air for five years, and air was never weighed. He also did not keep track of the leaves that fell off in four autumns, so his tree weight is slightly low. Good scientists say what their evidence rules out *and* what it leaves open. Our claim today is therefore careful: plants get growth materials **chiefly from air and water**, not from the soil — and we say so because the soil-loss number is far too small, while water was added and air was always present.

### Part B — The words that matter

- **Matter** is the stuff things are made of. The tree's new wood is new matter — it had to come from somewhere.
- **Soil** holds a plant up and supplies small amounts of materials, but it is not the plant's main food source. Saying "plants eat soil" is the misconception this lesson retires.
- **Air and water** are the chief sources. In later science you will learn the details of how plants use sunlight energy to form plant matter from air and water; in grade 5 the argument from weighing evidence is enough, and no molecular explanation is required.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult. The bean-plant case below is a labeled practice dataset, not your measurements.

A learner plants a bean seedling (2 grams) in a pot of dry soil (500 grams). She waters it for six weeks and adds nothing else. At the end, the plant weighs 62 grams and the dried soil weighs 498 grams.

**L1-G1.** How many grams did the plant gain? Show the subtraction.
**L1-G2.** How many grams did the soil lose? Show the subtraction.
**L1-G3.** State the claim in one sentence: did the plant's new growth come chiefly from the soil? Use both numbers as evidence.

## Independent practice

**L1-I1.** A sunflower seedling weighs 3 grams. After a summer it weighs 400 grams. Its pot of soil lost 5 grams. Write the claim and the evidence (two numbers).
**L1-I2 (error analysis).** A learner says, "The willow tree gained 164 pounds, so the soil must have lost 164 pounds and van Helmont weighed it wrong." Use the actual soil number (about 2 ounces lost) to explain why this is wrong, and what the small soil loss really shows.
**L1-I3.** Van Helmont did not weigh the fallen leaves. Did that make the tree's measured gain too high or too low? Explain in one or two sentences.

## Application

Find a plant at home or outside (adult supervises; do not pick or eat anything). Sketch it and write one sentence predicting what a five-year weighing experiment like van Helmont's would show for its soil: would the soil lose about as much as the plant gains, or far less? Give your reason.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *A tree gains 164 pounds while its soil loses about 2 ounces. What does that prove about where the tree's growth came from, and what does it leave open?*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the weighing table aloud; the learner may answer by pointing to the two numbers in the table instead of writing sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the claim while the adult writes it, or draw the before/after pot and tree with the numbers labeled.
- **Extension:** research with the adult what later scientists added to van Helmont's story — the role of air — and write two sentences explaining why "water alone" was an incomplete conclusion. (See the resource pack; the adult previews any page first.)
