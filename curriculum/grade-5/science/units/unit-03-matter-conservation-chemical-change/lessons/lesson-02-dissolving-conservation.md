# Lesson 02 — Dissolving: the Sugar Is Still There

**Unit 03 · Session 3 (Week 11) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will weigh water and sugar separately, stir the sugar into the water until it dissolves, weigh the mixture — and explain why "the sugar vanished" is the wrong conclusion even though you can no longer see it.

## Materials

Kitchen scale (grams), clear cup, water, sugar, spoon, science notebook. The adult supervises pouring and enforces no tasting during the investigation.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Yesterday the scale showed that melted ice weighs the same as frozen ice. Today's question: *when sugar stirs into water and you cannot see it anymore, did the sugar stop existing?* Say your guess. Then remember Unit 02: sugar dissolved in water was already evidence that matter is made of particles too small to see. Today the scale gets the final word.

## Teaching

### Part A — Weigh the parts, then weigh the whole

Weigh the cup of water: **180 g**. Weigh the sugar separately: **20 g**. Pour the sugar in, stir until you cannot see grains anymore, and weigh the cup again: **200 g**.

> **What you observe:** 180 g + 20 g = 200 g, and the mixture weighs 200 g. The sugar's grains are gone from sight, but its weight is still in the cup.

This is the same conservation claim as Lesson 01, in a new change: **dissolving**. Dissolving spreads the sugar's particles through the water (Unit 02's particle model). Spreading out is not the same as disappearing. If the sugar had ceased to exist, the mixture would weigh 180 g — just the water. It weighs 200 g.

### Part B — "Seems to vanish" is the framework's own warning

The grade-5 framework puts it this way: the amount (weight) of matter is conserved when it changes form, **even in transitions in which it seems to vanish**. "Seems" is doing important work. Your eyes report "gone." The scale reports "200 g." When eyes and scale disagree, trust the measurement — and then explain the disagreement with the particle model: the sugar particles are still there, mixed among the water particles, too small to see.

**Worked example 1 — the missing 20 g test.** A learner stirs 20 g of sugar into 180 g of water and predicts the mixture will weigh 180 g "because the sugar is gone." The scale reads 200 g. Her prediction was wrong, and that is useful: the 20 g difference is exactly the sugar's weight, still present. Claim: *dissolving conserved the weight.* Evidence: 180 g + 20 g = 200 g before mixing; 200 g after. Reasoning: nothing left the cup, so all the matter is still there.

**Worked example 2 — the evaporation trap.** A learner leaves a sugar-water cup open for a week. The water level drops and the weight drops too. Did dissolving destroy matter? No — this is a different change. Water **evaporated**: it left the cup as invisible water vapor and joined the air. The sugar stays behind (you can see grains again when all the water is gone). The lesson: always name what left the system before claiming matter vanished. Lesson 05 makes this the whole point with fizzing gas.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult. All rows are labeled practice data.

| Trial | Water | Sugar added | Mixture weight | Sugar still there? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 150 g | 10 g | 160 g | ? |
| B | 150 g | 30 g | 180 g | ? |
| C | 150 g | 10 g | 150 g (cup spilled during stirring) | ? |

**L2-G1.** For trials A and B, show the addition and state the claim.
**L2-G2.** For trial C, explain why the 150 g result does not break the conservation claim.
**L2-G3.** A friend says, "Dissolving is like magic — the sugar disappears." Give a two-sentence reply using the 200 g result from Part A.

## Independent practice

**L2-I1.** Salt water: 120 g of water + 15 g of salt = ? Predict the mixture weight, then explain what a scale reading of 135 g would prove.
**L2-I2.** Explain in your own words why you cannot see the sugar anymore but can still weigh it. Use the word **particles**.
**L2-I3 (error analysis).** A learner stirs sugar into water, weighs 200 g, then pours half the mixture into another cup and weighs the first cup: 100 g. She claims half the matter vanished. What is wrong with her claim? (Hint: where is the other half?)

## Application

Design a test for a new dissolving question: does warm water dissolve sugar faster than cold water — and does the weight still conserve in both? Write the question, the before weights, and the prediction. (You will run the weighing part in the project; the speed question is a fair-test extension from Unit 01.)

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *You stir 25 g of sugar into 175 g of water. The mixture weighs 200 g. Is the sugar gone? Use the numbers to defend your answer.*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the table aloud; "conserve" is defined on the board as "keep the same total."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may show the answer with a bar drawing (water bar + sugar bar = mixture bar) instead of a written addition sentence.
- **Extension:** predict what happens to the weight if the sugar water is left open until all the water evaporates (adult supervises over days). The remaining solid should weigh about the sugar's original weight — the water left, the sugar stayed.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "dissolved = gone" and "evaporation = destruction." Both are answered by naming what left the system. See the teacher guide for the evaporation follow-up. Answer-key reference: Lesson 02 section of `answer-key.md`.
