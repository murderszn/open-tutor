# Lesson 03 — Baking Soda and Vinegar: a Gas Means a New Substance

**Unit 03 · Session 5 (Week 12) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will mix baking soda and vinegar, observe the fizz, and decide — with evidence — whether mixing them made a **new substance** or just a mixture of the same two substances.

## Materials

Kitchen scale (grams), clear cup, baking soda, vinegar, spoon, safety glasses or goggles, towel, science notebook. **The adult pours all vinegar, checks eye protection before mixing, and never seals a fizzing mixture in a rigid container.** This lesson uses an open cup on purpose; the sealed version comes in Lesson 05.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Lessons 01–02 showed that melting and dissolving keep the same matter. Today mixing may do something different. Question: *when two things are mixed, is the result always just the two things together?* Think of sand and water (Unit 02): mixed, but still sand and still water. Could mixing ever make something that was not there before? Keep that question open while you watch the fizz.

## Teaching

### Part A — The fizz investigation

The adult puts a spoonful of baking soda in the cup. You watch as the adult pours in vinegar. Bubbles rush up, the mixture foams, and the cup may feel slightly cooler.

> **What you observe:** a gas forms — bubbles that rise and pop. Before mixing there was a white powder and a clear liquid. During mixing there is foam and fizz that neither starting substance showed on its own.

Now the key question: is the gas a **new substance**? A new substance is matter with properties the starting substances did not have. The gas was not in the cup before mixing. You cannot get it back by filtering, the way you can filter sand out of water. And when the fizz stops, the liquid left behind does not behave like plain vinegar anymore. So the evidence points one way: **mixing baking soda and vinegar results in new substances, including a gas.**

### Part B — The evidence rules for "new substance"

From today on, use these four evidence clues (any one strong clue is enough to claim a new substance, but you must name it):

1. **A gas forms** (bubbles/fizz that was not there before).
2. **A new solid forms** (a solid appears that was not there before — Lesson 04's curds).
3. **The temperature changes** noticeably (warmer or cooler) without heating or cooling from outside.
4. **A color change that separating cannot undo** (stirring or filtering does not bring the old colors back).

Caution: bubbles alone are not always a new substance — shaking soapy water makes bubbles too, but that is just air mixed in, and the soap and water are unchanged. That is why the unit asks for the whole story: does the gas keep coming from the mixture itself, and can you recover the starting substances unchanged? With baking soda and vinegar, you cannot.

**Worked example 1 — the gas verdict.** Claim: *baking soda + vinegar makes a new substance.* Evidence: (1) bubbles of gas form during mixing; the gas was not present before; (2) after the fizz stops, the leftover liquid is not the same as the starting vinegar — the fizz cannot be undone by filtering. Reasoning: a substance with new properties (a gas) appeared, so a new substance formed. This matches the framework's own example pair: baking soda and vinegar is listed as a combination that **does** form new substances.

**Worked example 2 — the soapy-water contrast.** Claim: *shaking soapy water does NOT make a new substance.* Evidence: bubbles appear, but they stop as soon as shaking stops; the water is still soapy water, and nothing new keeps forming. Reasoning: the bubbles are trapped air, not a gas produced by the mixture. One clue (bubbles) is not enough on its own — you need the gas to be produced by the mixing and the starting substances to be changed.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult. Decide "new substance" or "no new substance" and name the evidence.

**L3-G1.** Baking soda + vinegar: fizzing gas, leftover liquid changed. Verdict and evidence?
**L3-G2.** Shaken soapy water: bubbles while shaking, soapy water afterward. Verdict and evidence?
**L3-G3.** A learner says, "The gas proves the weight must have gone up, because gas was added." Explain the error: was gas added from outside, or produced from the substances already in the cup?

## Independent practice

**L3-I1.** Vinegar is poured on plain sand. Nothing fizzes, nothing changes color, the sand can be rinsed and recovered. Verdict and evidence?
**L3-I2.** Two cups: cup A has baking soda + water (no fizz); cup B has baking soda + vinegar (fizz). What does the comparison prove about which pair makes a new substance? (This is a fair test: one thing changed — water vs. vinegar.)
**L3-I3 (explain your reasoning).** A learner claims the fizzing gas is "just air that was hiding in the powder." Give two reasons this is wrong: (a) the gas keeps forming after mixing starts, and (b) the starting substances are changed afterward. Write both reasons in your own words.

## Application

Find one fizzing change at home or in a video the adult previews (bread rising, a fizzy drink opened — adult supervises; no tasting during investigation time). Name the gas evidence you observe and one thing you would check to rule out "just trapped air."

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *Baking soda and vinegar fizz and make a gas. Did mixing make a new substance? Name the evidence.*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the four evidence clues are posted as a picture-word card (gas cloud, solid lump, thermometer, color drop).
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort evidence cards into "new substance" / "no new substance" piles and explain one card orally instead of writing all answers.
- **Extension:** research question (adult previews sources): the gas made here is carbon dioxide, the same gas that makes bread rise and fizzy drinks fizz. Why is naming the gas *not* required to make the grade-5 claim? (Answer: the claim rests on observed new properties — gas forming — not on naming the chemical.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "bubbles always mean a new substance" (soapy water corrects this) and "the gas came from outside." The fair-test comparison in L3-I2 is the key reteach. Safety: eye protection is non-negotiable for fizzing mixtures; the adult pours vinegar. Answer-key reference: Lesson 03 section of `answer-key.md`.
