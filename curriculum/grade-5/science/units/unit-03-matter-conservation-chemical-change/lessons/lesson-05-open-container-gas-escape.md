# Lesson 05 — The Sealed Bag and the Open Cup

**Unit 03 · Session 9 (Week 13) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson with image activity**

## Goal

You will explain the unit's honest puzzle: why a fizzing mixture in an open cup weighs *less* afterward, while the same mixture in a sealed bag weighs the same — and you will defend the conservation claim for both cases.

## Materials

Kitchen scale (grams), sealable plastic bag, clear cup, baking soda, vinegar, safety glasses or goggles, science notebook, the unit image `../assets/sealed-bag-conservation.png`. The adult pours all vinegar, checks the bag seal, and enforces eye protection. **Never seal a fizzing mixture in a rigid container; a flexible bag is used here with a small amount only, and the adult stops the test if the bag strains.**

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Lessons 01–02 said weight stays the same. Lesson 03 said mixing can make a gas. Put them together and predict: *if the gas leaves the cup, what happens to the weight you can measure?* Say your prediction before looking at the image below.

### Part B — The open cup: same reaction, different measurement

Now run the same mixing in an open cup (adult pours the vinegar). Weigh before: cup + baking soda + vinegar = **210 g**. Let it fizz fully, then weigh again: **198 g**. The scale lost 12 g.

Did conservation fail? No — and here is why. Conservation is a claim about **all** the matter in the system. In the sealed bag, the gas stayed in the system, so the scale still saw it: 250 g before, 250 g after. In the open cup, the gas **escaped into the air**. The 12 g of gas is still matter — it is just no longer on the scale. Measured weight dropped because matter **left the system**, exactly like the spilled water in Lesson 01's unfair comparison and the evaporated water in Lesson 02.

> **The honest rule:** *if matter leaves the system, the measured weight drops, and that does not break conservation. If nothing leaves the system, the measured weight stays the same — even when a gas forms.*

**Worked example 1 — defending conservation in the open cup.** Claim: *the baking-soda-and-vinegar reaction conserved matter even though the open cup lost 12 g.* Evidence: (1) the sealed-bag version of the same reaction stayed at 250 g; (2) the only difference between the two runs is that the open cup let gas escape. Reasoning: the missing 12 g is the escaped gas, counted in the sealed run and uncounted in the open run. Same reaction, same matter — different system boundary.

**Worked example 2 — the wrong conclusion to avoid.** A learner says, "The open cup proves reactions destroy matter." The error: she treated "what is on the scale" as "all the matter there is." The sealed bag shows the missing piece. Whenever a measurement drops, the first question is always: *did something leave the system?* — not *was something destroyed?*

## Guided practice

Work with the adult. All rows are labeled practice data.

| Run | Before | After | System | What happened to the weight? Why? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (sealed bag) | 250 g | 250 g | Nothing left | ? |
| B (open cup) | 210 g | 198 g | Gas escaped | ? |
| C (open cup, no fizz) | 180 g | 180 g | Nothing left | ? |

**L5-G1.** For each row, state whether the result supports conservation and why.
**L5-G2.** In run B, how many grams of gas escaped? Show the subtraction.
**L5-G3.** A learner proposes sealing every fizzing test in a glass jar with a tight lid. Why is that unsafe, and what should be used instead? (Hint: gas pressure; see Materials.)

## Independent practice

**L5-I1.** A sealed bag of baking soda and vinegar weighs 300 g before mixing and 300 g after. A friend says, "Nothing happened — the weight didn't change, so no new substance formed." Use the puffed-up bag to correct her: what changed, and what stayed the same?
**L5-I2.** An open cup weighs 160 g before fizzing and 149 g after. How much gas escaped? If the same reaction ran sealed, what would the after weight be? Explain.
**L5-I3 (explain your reasoning).** A fizzy drink is opened: gas escapes with a hiss, and the drink later tastes flat. Did the drink lose matter when it was opened? Defend your answer with the sealed/open rule.

## Application

Write the unit's two-sentence summary for a younger learner: one sentence for "weight stays the same when nothing leaves," one sentence for "measured weight drops when gas escapes." Test it: read it aloud to the adult — can they tell which investigation each sentence describes?

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *A sealed bag stays at 250 g after fizzing, but an open cup drops from 210 g to 198 g. Does this break conservation? Why or why not?*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the image's text alternative is read aloud; "system" is defined as "everything you are weighing together."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may answer the image questions by pointing to the matching panel and reading the scale numbers aloud.
- **Extension:** predict the open-cup result for the milk-and-vinegar test (no gas forms, so the weight should stay the same even open — and check it, adult supervising). This tests whether the learner can apply the escape rule only where a gas actually forms.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "gas makes things lighter" and "a weight drop disproves conservation." Both are answered by the sealed/open pair. Safety note: never let a learner seal a rigid container around a fizzing reaction. Answer-key reference: Lesson 05 section of `answer-key.md`.
