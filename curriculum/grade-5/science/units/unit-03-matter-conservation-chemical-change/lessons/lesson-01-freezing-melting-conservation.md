# Lesson 01 — Freeze and Melt: the Weight Stays the Same

**Unit 03 · Session 1 (Week 11) · ~40 minutes · Investigation lesson**

## Goal

You will weigh water before it freezes, while it is ice, and after it melts — and you will state the unit's first claim in your own words: *when matter changes form, its total weight stays the same.*

## Materials

Kitchen scale (grams), sealable plastic bag, ice cubes or a cup of water, towel, science notebook, bar-graph template (draw three bars). The adult handles the freezer steps; this lesson uses ice already frozen, so no waiting is needed.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

In Unit 02 you learned that matter is made of particles too small to see, and that a **model** explains what you observe. Today's question: *if ice melts into water, is there less stuff, more stuff, or the same amount of stuff?* Say your guess and one reason. There is no wrong guess yet — the scale will decide.

## Teaching

### Part A — Weigh first, then change, then weigh again

Put the sealable bag with ice cubes on the scale. Write the number down. That is the **before** weight. Seal the bag, lay it on a towel, and wait while you do Part B. When the ice has melted, weigh the sealed bag again. That is the **after** weight.

The rule for every investigation in this unit is the same: **weigh before, change, weigh after, compare.** If you skip the before weight, you have nothing to compare with, and no claim is possible.

> **What you observe (example run):** the sealed bag of ice weighs **120 g**. After the ice melts, the sealed bag of water weighs **120 g**. The ice changed form — solid to liquid — but the scale number did not change.

### Part B — Why the sealed bag matters

Suppose you weighed loose ice cubes on a plate, then melted them, and some water dripped off the plate. The after weight would be smaller — but not because matter vanished. Matter **left the plate**. A sealed bag keeps all the matter in one **system** — everything you are weighing — so the comparison is fair. This is the same fair-test idea from Unit 01: change one thing (solid to liquid) and keep everything else the same, including keeping all the matter in the bag.

**Worked example 1 — reading a before/after table.** A learner weighs a sealed bag of ice: 120 g. She freezes nothing new and adds nothing. After melting, the bag weighs 120 g. Claim: *the weight stayed the same.* Evidence: 120 g before, 120 g after. Reasoning: nothing was added and nothing left the sealed bag, so the same matter is there in a different form. A common error is to say "the ice disappeared because it melted." The graph answers that: the melted bar is exactly as tall as the ice bar.

**Worked example 2 — spotting an unfair comparison.** A learner weighs ice cubes on an open plate: 90 g. They melt, some water runs off the plate, and the plate now weighs 78 g. Can she claim that melting destroys matter? No. The comparison is unfair because matter left the system — the spilled water was never weighed. The fix is a sealed bag or a deep bowl, so all the water stays in the weighing. This is the exact trap Lesson 05 returns to with fizzing mixtures.

## Guided practice

Work with the adult. Use the practice-dataset table below (labeled practice data, not your measurements).

| Trial | Before (ice) | After (melted water) | Same or different? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 85 g | 85 g | ? |
| B | 140 g | 140 g | ? |
| C | 60 g | 52 g (water spilled) | ? |

**L1-G1.** For trials A and B, state the claim in one sentence each.
**L1-G2.** For trial C, explain why the 52 g result does NOT prove that melting destroys matter.
**L1-G3.** Draw the bar graph for trial A: two bars, labeled "ice" and "melted water," both reaching 85 g. What does the equal height show?

## Independent practice

**L1-I1.** A sealed bag holds 200 g of ice. After melting it holds 200 g of water. Write the claim and the evidence (two numbers).
**L1-I2.** A learner says, "Ice is heavier than water because ice is hard." Use the 120 g example from Part A to explain why hardness does not decide weight here.
**L1-I3 (error analysis).** A learner weighs an open cup of ice (150 g), melts it, wipes up a spill, and weighs 144 g. She claims melting lost 6 g of matter. Is she right? Explain what really happened and how to redo the test fairly.

## Application

Tonight, find one more freeze/melt change at home (a frozen food item thawing, an ice cube in a drink — adult supervises). Predict the before/after weights before weighing, then check. Record your prediction, both weights, and whether the result matched your prediction.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences: *A sealed bag of ice weighs 110 g. After it melts, what should the bag weigh, and why?*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the table aloud; the learner may answer by pointing to the equal bars on the graph instead of writing sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the claim while the adult writes it, or draw the before/after bags instead of writing.
- **Extension:** predict and test a second phase change — freeze a sealed bag of liquid water overnight (adult handles the freezer) and compare all three weights: liquid, frozen, melted. All three should match.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "melting makes matter vanish" and "hard things weigh more." Both are addressed by the equal bars. See the teacher guide for reteach steps. Answer-key reference: Lesson 01 section of `answer-key.md`.
