# Unit 04 — Founding Documents, Constitution, and Citizenship

**Grade 5 Social Studies · Weeks 15–18 of 36 · 16 sessions (~40 minutes each)**

**Status:** validated draft — delivered 2026-10-31; not yet educator-reviewed
or merged.

This unit answers the question Unit 03 left open: after winning
independence, how did Americans build a government that could last — and
whose rights did that government protect? Learners read the Declaration of
Independence's ideas in plain language, follow the long summer of debate
that produced the Constitution, learn the three branches and the checks
that keep any one branch from taking over, study the Bill of Rights
amendment by amendment, and then practice citizenship themselves: making
fair rules by deliberation, comparing ways groups make decisions, and
proposing a policy for a real problem.

**Essential question:** Who gets to make the rules, what stops the
rule-makers from going too far, and how can ordinary people change the
rules when they are unfair?

**Sensitive-content note (for the guiding adult):** This unit tells the
truth plainly at a grade-5 level: the Declaration said "all men are
created equal" while enslavement continued, women could not vote, and
Indigenous nations were not treated as part of the new government. The
Constitution protected slavery in compromises the unit names honestly
(the Three-Fifths Compromise and the ban on ending the slave trade before
1808), and it took a war and later amendments to begin undoing those
wrongs. No graphic detail is used; the focus stays on ideas, systems, and
the people who pushed for wider rights. All outside sources are
adult-selected and previewed. See the [teacher guide](teacher-guide.md)
for session-level guidance.

## Lessons

1. [Lesson 01 — The Declaration's Ideas: Rights Nobody Can Take Away](lessons/lesson-01-declaration-ideas-natural-rights.md)
   — Unalienable rights, consent of the governed, and the honest question
   of who was included — and who was not — in 1776 (Week 15).
2. [Lesson 02 — A New Plan: The Constitution's Three Branches and Checks](lessons/lesson-02-constitution-structure-branches-checks.md)
   — Why the first plan failed, the 1787 Convention and its compromises,
   the Preamble's six purposes, three branches, checks and balances, and
   federalism (Week 16).
3. [Lesson 03 — The Bill of Rights: Rules That Protect Freedoms](lessons/lesson-03-bill-of-rights-rules-protect-freedoms.md)
   — The first ten amendments in plain language, later voting-rights
   amendments, and how a bill becomes a law and how the Constitution can
   be amended (Week 17).
4. [Lesson 04 — Citizenship: Participation, Decisions, and Changing Laws](lessons/lesson-04-participation-changing-laws-decisions.md)
   — What citizens do, how groups make decisions (vote, consensus, leader
   decides), deliberative process, and proposing a policy for a real
   problem (Week 18).
5. [Lesson 05 — Convention Scene Investigation: What a Picture Can and Cannot Prove](lessons/lesson-05-convention-scene-investigation.md)
   — Observation of the generated Constitutional Convention illustration
   (labeled AI reconstruction, never evidence); the deterministic
   branches-and-checks diagram; what illustrations show and what only
   documents and sources can prove (Week 16, S3).

## Prerequisites (from Units 01–03 and grade-4 end-of-year objectives)

- Sequence events across centuries; explain probable causes and effects.
- Name a perspective and its reasons; explain why people in the same
  period saw the same event differently (Unit 02, Lesson 04; Unit 03).
- Compare two accounts of the same event; infer audience and purpose;
  separate fact from opinion (Unit 03, Lesson 03).
- Explain purposes of rules; describe roles of people in authority;
  explain how groups make rules that protect freedoms (grade-4 entry
  objectives, re-taught in Lesson 03).
- Develop a claim supported by evidence from more than one source
  (Unit 03, Lesson 04).

Unit 04 re-teaches the purposes of rules and laws before the Constitution,
rather than assuming that grade-4 objective is secure, and turns Unit 03's
claim-with-evidence routine toward a new question: did the Revolution
fulfill the Declaration's promises?

## Measurable objectives

By the end of this unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Explain the Declaration's ideas** — state what unalienable rights and
   consent of the governed mean in plain language, and explain who was
   included and who was not in 1776, with reasons why that exclusion
   matters. (C3 D2.Civ.3, 8, 10.3-5; D2.His.4, 5.3-5)
2. **Explain the Constitution's structure** — name the Preamble's six
   purposes, the three branches and what each does, at least three checks
   one branch has on another, and how power is shared between the federal
   government and the states. (C3 D2.Civ.1, 3, 5.3-5)
3. **Explain how laws are made and changed** — order the steps of a bill
   becoming a law (including veto and veto override) and the two-step
   amendment path (proposal and ratification), and explain why the
   amendment process exists. (C3 D2.Civ.12.3-5)
4. **Apply the Bill of Rights** — match at least eight of the first ten
   amendments to plain-language protections, explain the later
   voting-rights amendments (15th, 19th, 26th) in one sentence each, and
   decide rights scenarios with reasons. (C3 D2.Civ.3, 4, 8.3-5)
5. **Practice deliberation and participation** — compare decision
   procedures (vote, consensus, leader decides), use a deliberative
   process to make a fair rule with reasons, and propose a policy for a
   real classroom or school problem with steps for making it happen.
   (C3 D2.Civ.2, 7, 9, 11, 13.3-5; D4.8.3-5)
6. **Develop a claim from evidence** — write a claim about whether the
   Revolution fulfilled the Declaration's promises, supported by at least
   two pieces of evidence from different sources, with reasons linking
   each piece to the claim. (C3 D2.His.16.3-5; D3.4.3-5; D4.2.3-5)

## Vocabulary

| Term | Kid-friendly definition |
|---|---|
| unalienable right | A right that belongs to a person simply for being human and cannot rightly be taken away — in the Declaration: life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. |
| consent of the governed | The idea that a government is legitimate only because the people being governed agree to it. |
| constitution | A written plan that sets up a government, says what it may and may not do, and protects people's rights. |
| Articles of Confederation | The first American plan of government (1781–1789): a loose league of states with a weak central government that could not tax or enforce laws. |
| Constitutional Convention | The 1787 Philadelphia meeting where delegates wrote the Constitution. |
| compromise | An agreement in which each side gives up something to reach a shared plan. |
| Preamble | The Constitution's opening paragraph, beginning "We the People," which states the plan's six purposes. |
| legislative branch | The branch that makes laws: Congress, the House of Representatives plus the Senate. |
| executive branch | The branch that carries out laws: the President, Vice President, and departments. |
| judicial branch | The branch that interprets laws and decides whether they follow the Constitution: the courts, headed by the Supreme Court. |
| checks and balances | The system in which each branch can limit the power of the other two, so no one branch takes over. |
| federalism | Power shared between the federal (national) government and the state governments. |
| veto | The President's refusal to sign a bill; Congress can still pass it with a two-thirds vote in both houses (a veto override). |
| amendment | A formal change or addition to the Constitution. |
| Bill of Rights | The first ten amendments (ratified 1791), which list protections for people's freedoms. |
| ratify | To formally approve — a constitutional amendment needs approval by three-fourths of the states. |
| citizen | A member of a country, with rights (such as voting, for adult citizens) and responsibilities (such as obeying laws and serving on juries when called). |
| deliberation | Talking through a decision carefully — hearing reasons, weighing them, and then deciding together. |
| petition | A written request, signed by many people, asking a government to act. |
| due process | Fair treatment by the law: the government must follow fair rules before it can punish someone or take their property. |

## Standards and source notes

Framework: the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social
Studies State Standards (National Council for the Social Studies), grades
3–5 band — the same framework verified for this track's scope-and-sequence
(2026-10-04; codes and descriptions carried from that verified crosswalk).
**No state adoption, accreditation, or alignment certification is claimed.**
The 3–5 band spans three grades; this unit teaches its indicators at the
grade-5 upper-band level, as the scope-and-sequence specifies.

Key facts in this unit were checked 2026-10-31 against: the National
Archives transcriptions of the Constitution and the Bill of Rights
(Preamble wording; Articles I–III; veto and override in Article I,
Section 7; amendment proposal and ratification in Article V; the Bill of
Rights proposed September 25, 1789, and ratified December 15, 1791, with
the note that a proposed article later became the 27th Amendment in
1992); the track's scope-and-sequence audit (which verified the kept
Declaration quiz: Jefferson, age 33, Philadelphia, "Life, Liberty, and
the pursuit of Happiness," July 4, 1776); and the Resource Pack's verified
references (Ben's Guide, Ducksters amendment pages, Crash Course U.S.
History #8). Where a figure is an estimate or a convention count — for
example, the 55 Convention delegates — the unit says so.

**Original passages in this unit are classroom models, not quotations.**
Short phrases from the Declaration and Constitution are public-domain
texts and are marked as quotations; longer learner-facing passages are
original paraphrases in grade-5 language, labeled as paraphrases, never
presented as the founders' own words. Lesson 05's illustration is an
AI-generated reconstruction, labeled as such, never historical evidence.
