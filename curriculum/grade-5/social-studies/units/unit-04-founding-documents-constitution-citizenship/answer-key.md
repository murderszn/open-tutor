# Answer Key — Unit 04

For the guiding adult only. Every lesson task, practice item, quiz
question, and assessment item is solved here. Open responses accept any
wording that carries the stated reasoning; the samples show the bar, not
the only correct text.

## Lesson 01 (A1–A9)

- **A1.** An unalienable right is a right you have just because you are
  a person, which cannot rightly be taken away. Example: life, liberty,
  or the pursuit of happiness.
- **A2.** Consent means the people being governed agree to the
  government. No — a rule imposed without discussion or any chance to
  object lacks consent, however well meant.
- **A3.** Any two, each with a fact: enslaved people (remained enslaved
  after 1776); women (could not vote); Indigenous nations (not treated
  as part of the new government); men without property (could not vote
  in most states at first).
- **A4.** Paraphrase (sample): "Everyone is born with equal rights; no
  one is born with a natural right to rule over others." Claimed:
  equality of rights. Left out in practice: enslaved people, women,
  Indigenous nations, and propertyless men were not treated as equals.
- **A5.** Acceptable reasoning: Wheatley wrote about liberty while
  herself enslaved, so the gap between the Declaration's words and
  American practice was not abstract — a real person lived it, praised
  in print for poems about freedom while denied freedom herself.
- **A6.** False. The Declaration declared independence and principles;
  it freed nobody. Slavery continued and was ended by the 13th
  Amendment in 1865.
- **A7.** Error 1: The Declaration granted no voting rights — it
  declared independence and principles. Error 2: Not "everyone" could
  vote — voting was limited (mostly property-holding men at first);
  wider voting came later through state changes and the 15th, 19th,
  and 26th Amendments.
- **A8.** Sample chart — FOR: the colonies won independence and built
  a government based on consent (elections, a written Constitution).
  AGAINST: slavery continued and women could not vote. Each piece
  needs its "for"/"against" label and one linking reason.
- **A9.** Consent of the governed means a government is legitimate only
  because the people agree to it. Britain lost it, in the colonists'
  argument, by taxing people who had no representatives in Parliament
  — taxation without representation.

## Lesson 02 (B1–B9)

- **B1.** Legislative — makes laws. Executive — carries out laws.
  Judicial — interprets laws.
- **B2.** (a) Legislative. (b) Executive. (c) Judicial.
- **B3.** Congress can override the veto with a two-thirds vote in both
  houses (House and Senate).
- **B4.** Any two, each with its problem: Congress could not tax → it
  could not pay debts or support an army; no president → nobody carried
  out the laws; no national courts → disputes between states had no
  judge; changing the plan needed all 13 states → one "no" blocked
  every fix.
- **B5.** Small states: they feared a few big states would outvote them
  every time. Large states: they wanted representation matching their
  larger populations.
- **B6.** It counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a person
  for representation and taxation. No — it freed nobody; it gave
  slaveholding states more seats and political power.
- **B7.** Federal only: (a) declare war, (d) print money. State only:
  (b) run public schools, (e) issue driver's licenses. Shared:
  (c) collect taxes, (f) build roads.
- **B8.** Step 1 — Legislative: Congress passes the bill. Step 2 —
  Executive: the President vetoes it. Step 3 — Legislative: Congress
  overrides with two-thirds in both houses, so the bill becomes law.
  Step 4 — Judicial: a court rules the law unconstitutional, so the law
  is struck down. (Order matters: a veto override makes it law first;
  judicial review tests it after.)
- **B9.** The Preamble is the Constitution's opening paragraph, stating
  the plan's purposes. Any two of: form a more perfect Union; establish
  Justice; insure domestic Tranquility; provide for the common defence;
  promote the general Welfare; secure the Blessings of Liberty.

## Lesson 03 (C1–C9)

- **C1.** Religion, speech, press, peaceful assembly, petition.
- **C2.** (a) 4th. (b) 6th. (c) 1st.
- **C3.** Any two: they feared a strong national government would abuse
  power as Britain had (soldiers in homes, banned speech, unfair
  trials); they wanted written protections before ratifying; they
  believed rights needed explicit limits on government power.
- **C4.** 2nd → (v) keep and bear arms. 3rd → (iii) no soldiers housed
  in private homes in peacetime without consent. 5th → (iv) fair
  process (no double jeopardy, no forced self-incrimination, due
  process, just payment). 8th → (ii) no cruel and unusual punishments.
  10th → (i) powers reserved to the states or the people.
- **C5.** Order: (c) introduce → (b) committee → (d) both houses pass
  → (a) President signs or vetoes → (e) override with two-thirds in
  both houses.
- **C6.** Proposal: two-thirds of both houses of Congress (or a
  convention called by two-thirds of the states). Ratification:
  three-fourths of the states. The bars are high so the plan stays
  stable yet can still grow — change requires broad agreement, not a
  narrow majority.
- **C7.** 15th (1870): the vote cannot be denied because of race. 19th
  (1920): the vote cannot be denied because of sex. 26th (1971):
  citizens 18 and older cannot be denied the vote because of age.
- **C8.** (i) 1st Amendment, violated — peaceful assembly (and speech):
  a protest may not be banned because officials dislike its message.
  (ii) 1st Amendment, violated — speech: a respectful opinion about a
  rule is protected; schools may regulate disruption, not ban opinions.
  (iii) 5th Amendment, violated — just compensation: the government
  must pay a fair price when it takes private property for public use.
- **C9.** Sample: The Bill of Rights lists things the government may
  not do — limits on power — because the Declaration says people
  already have these rights. A gift can be taken back by the giver; a
  protection says the government never had the power to take the right
  in the first place.

## Lesson 04 (D1–D9)

- **D1.** Any four beyond voting: obey laws, pay taxes, serve on a jury,
  serve in office or on a board, speak up, petition, write to
  representatives, join a peaceful protest, stay informed, volunteer,
  help the community. At least one must be child-doable (speak up,
  volunteer, learn the issues).
- **D2.** (a) Vote — everyone's enjoyment matters and the decision is
  quick. (b) Leader decides — safety and expertise matter most.
  (c) Consensus — everyone must accept the rules for the game to work.
- **D3.** Order: state the problem → hear all views → weigh reasons →
  decide and state the reason → review after trying the rule.
- **D4.** Any reasonable pair. Sample FOR: a quieter, safer park for
  small children. Sample AGAINST: dog owners lose a place to exercise
  animals that are part of their families.
- **D5.** Accept any real problem with one policy and two steps.
  Sample: Problem — lunchroom trash on the floor. Policy — a sorting
  station by the door. Steps — (1) ask the principal for bins;
  (2) assign rotating student monitors.
- **D6.** No. Majority rule decides, but minority rights mean the 5
  students' interests still count; a fair process looks for compromise
  (for example, the game allowed at certain times or places) instead of
  simple majority imposition.
- **D7.** Error 1: Voting is only one form of citizenship — obeying
  laws, jury duty, speaking up, petitioning, volunteering, and staying
  informed all count. Error 2: Children can participate too (speak up,
  volunteer, learn issues, practice deliberation); citizenship is not
  only adult voting.
- **D8.** Sample: "Walk in the halls, except when running to get help
  in an emergency." Decided by vote — everyone is affected, and a vote
  is quick and fair. Exception: running to get help. Accept reasonable
  variants with rule + procedure + reason + exception.
- **D9.** Any two lawful ways: vote for representatives who share their
  views; petition or write to representatives; speak publicly and
  peacefully; run for office or serve on a board; support an amendment
  through Congress and the states.

## Lesson 05 (E1–E8)

- **E1.** Any five visible observations: men in 18th-century coats and
  wigs seated at long tables; one man standing and gesturing as if
  speaking; papers, quill pens, and inkwells on the tables; black
  three-cornered hats on chairs and tables; a stone fireplace with a
  fire on the right; tall multi-paned windows on the left and center.
- **E2.** (c) Nothing about 1787 — it is a modern AI illustration. It
  can suggest what a meeting might have looked like, but it proves no
  historical fact: no one in 1787 posed for it, and its details are
  invented.
- **E3.** Any two groups with Lesson 01 connections: women (could not
  be delegates or vote); enslaved people (labored and served yet were
  denied the rights being debated); children; Indigenous nations (not
  treated as part of the new government). The absence matters because
  it shows whose voices the Convention did not include.
- **E4.** Diagram shows what the picture cannot: structure and checks
  (for example, veto and override relationships between branches).
  Picture suggests what the diagram cannot: the human scene — debate,
  note-taking, the room itself.
- **E5.** Madison's convention notes — a primary source made at the
  time by someone present. The illustration proves nothing (modern
  imagining). The Constitution's text states the final plan, not what
  anyone argued on a given day.
- **E6.** (a) Standing, gesturing, speaking to the room. (b) Any three:
  sheets of paper, quill pens, inkwells, a rolled document, black
  three-cornered hats. (c) A stone fireplace with a burning fire.
- **E7.** Sample: "We the students of this class, in order to learn in
  peace and treat everyone fairly, do establish this constitution for
  our classroom." Accept variants with two stated purposes.
- **E8.** Sample: Because it was generated in 2026, not made in 1787 —
  it imagines a scene rather than recording one, so it can suggest but
  never prove.

## Practice banks (P1–P24)

- **P1.** A right you have just because you are a person, which cannot
  rightly be taken away. Example: life, liberty, pursuit of happiness.
- **P2.** No — a rule made without asking the people, with no way to
  object, lacks their consent.
- **P3.** True — most of the Declaration is a list of grievances
  against the king; the ideas sit in the second paragraph.
- **P4.** Thomas Jefferson; approved July 4, 1776.
- **P5.** Any group + fact (see A3).
- **P6.** Sample: Promise — all people have unalienable rights.
  Shortfall — enslaved people remained enslaved. Why it matters: the
  promise became the standard later generations used to demand change,
  and the founding itself was incomplete.
- **P7.** (a) Legislative (the Senate confirms). (b) Executive.
  (c) Judicial.
- **P8.** Two-thirds vote in both houses (House and Senate) — a veto
  override.
- **P9.** House of Representatives — seats by population. Senate — two
  senators per state.
- **P10.** Schools: state. Print money: federal. Collect taxes: shared.
- **P11.** House: 2 years. Senate: 6 years. President: 4 years, at most
  two terms (22nd Amendment).
- **P12.** The Senate; the legislative branch.
- **P13.** Five freedoms. Any three: religion, speech, press, assembly,
  petition.
- **P14.** (a) → (ii) no unreasonable searches; warrant needs probable
  cause. (b) → (iii) speedy public trial, impartial jury, lawyer.
  (c) → (i) no cruel and unusual punishments.
- **P15.** 1791; the Bill of Rights.
- **P16.** 27 amendments. The 27th Amendment (congressional pay),
  ratified 1992.
- **P17.** 1st Amendment (press and speech); violated — the government
  may not ban criticism of itself.
- **P18.** 6th Amendment; it requires a speedy and public trial, an
  impartial jury, knowledge of the charge, confronting witnesses, and
  assistance of counsel (a lawyer).
- **P19.** (a) Vote. (b) Leader decides. (c) Consensus.
- **P20.** State the problem → hear all views → weigh reasons → decide
  and state the reason → review after trying the rule.
- **P21.** Accept any real problem + policy + one step. Sample:
  Problem — books left on the library floor. Policy — a return bin by
  the door. Step — ask the librarian for the bin and assign weekly
  tidiers.
- **P22.** Respect (listening, then answering with a reason). Another
  virtue + example: honesty (telling the truth even when it is hard);
  responsibility (doing your assigned job); courage (speaking up
  fairly); cooperation (working together on a shared task).
- **P23.** Any two lawful ways (see D9).
- **P24.** No. Majority rule does not license singling out one student;
  minority rights protect individuals from majority exclusion. Fair
  process seeks a compromise — for example, rotating games so everyone
  plays sometimes.

## Project exemplar (20-point sketch)

- **Preamble:** "We the students of this class, in order to learn in
  peace, treat everyone fairly, and solve problems together, do
  establish this constitution for our classroom."
- **Articles:** Article I — the Rule Council (five rotating members)
  proposes rules. Article II — the Day Leaders (two rotating students)
  carry out the rules and keep the schedule. Article III — the
  Fairness Panel (three rotating members) settles disagreements about
  what a rule means.
- **Bill of Rights (five):** speak respectfully without being laughed
  at; a fair hearing before any consequence; ask for help without
  embarrassment; use shared materials fairly; propose a rule change at
  any time.
- **Amendment process:** any student may propose a change; proposal
  needs a majority of the class; ratification needs three-fourths.
  Ratification vote recorded (for example, 18 for, 3 against, 1
  abstaining). Reflection names one proud rule (the fair hearing) and
  one compromise (giving up an unlimited free-talk proposal so the
  class could agree).
