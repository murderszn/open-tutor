# Lesson 02 — A New Plan: The Constitution's Three Branches and Checks

**Goal:** I can explain why the first American plan failed, what the
Constitution set up instead, and how the three branches check one
another.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 16, S1–S2). **Materials:** Unit
vocabulary list, the branches-and-checks diagram in
[assets](../assets/branches-checks-diagram.svg) (for task B8).
**Readiness check:** Ask the learner to name one Declaration idea from
Lesson 01 (unalienable rights or consent of the governed). If it is
missing, re-read Lesson 01's one-paragraph paraphrase together.

## Teaching

### The first plan did not work

Winning independence (Unit 03) left a new problem: thirteen states that
needed one government. The first plan, the Articles of Confederation
(1781–1789), was a loose league of states with a weak center. It failed
for reasons a fifth grader can test:

- **Congress could not tax.** It could only ask states for money, and
  states often refused — so the national government could not pay its
  debts or support an army.
- **There was no president** to carry out the laws, and **no national
  courts** to settle disputes between states.
- **Changing the plan needed all 13 states to agree** — one "no" blocked
  every fix.

In 1786–1787, Shays' Rebellion — indebted Massachusetts farmers, led by
Daniel Shays, protesting foreclosures and shutting down courts — showed
that the weak center could not even keep order. Leaders agreed: the plan
had to be replaced, not patched.

### The summer of 1787

In the summer of 1787, 55 delegates from 12 states met in Philadelphia
(Rhode Island sent nobody). George Washington presided. James Madison
took detailed notes and is often called the "Father of the Constitution."
The meetings were secret — windows shut in summer heat — so delegates
could argue honestly and change their minds. On September 17, 1787, they
signed the Constitution. It took effect only after 9 of the 13 states
ratified it; that happened in 1788, and the new government began in
1789.

Two hard compromises made agreement possible, and both must be taught
honestly:

- **The Great Compromise.** Large states wanted representation by
  population; small states wanted equal votes. The deal: a two-part
  Congress — a House of Representatives by population, and a Senate with
  two senators per state.
- **The Three-Fifths Compromise.** Southern states wanted enslaved
  people counted for representation (to gain seats) but not for taxes;
  Northern states objected. The deal counted each enslaved person as
  three-fifths of a person for representation and taxation. Say plainly
  what it was: a compromise that gave slaveholding states more political
  power and protected slavery. It freed nobody. The Constitution also
  barred Congress from ending the slave trade before 1808. These
  compromises are why Lesson 01's promise and practice did not match —
  and why later amendments (Lesson 03) were needed.

Ratification was a real fight. **Federalists** (Hamilton, Madison, Jay —
authors of 85 essays called the *Federalist Papers*) argued for a strong
national government. **Anti-Federalists** feared it would become a new
tyranny and demanded a bill of rights. The promise of a Bill of Rights
won the last states over.

### The Preamble: six purposes

The Constitution opens with one sentence, the Preamble, beginning "We
the People." It states six purposes — the job description for the whole
plan:

1. Form a more perfect Union (hold the states together better than the
   Articles did).
2. Establish Justice (fair laws and courts).
3. Insure domestic Tranquility (peace at home, order without tyranny).
4. Provide for the common defence (protect the country).
5. Promote the general Welfare (help the people's well-being).
6. Secure the Blessings of Liberty to ourselves and our Posterity
   (protect freedom for us and for future generations).

### Three branches, checks, and shared power

| Branch | Who | Main job | Term basics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legislative | Congress: House of Representatives (by population) + Senate (2 per state) | Makes laws | House: 2 years; Senate: 6 years |
| Executive | President, Vice President, departments (the Cabinet) | Carries out laws; leads the armed forces; deals with other countries | President: 4 years, at most two terms (22nd Amendment, 1951) |
| Judicial | Supreme Court (9 justices) and lower federal courts | Interprets laws; decides whether laws follow the Constitution | Justices serve for life during good behavior |

**Checks and balances** means each branch can limit the others:

- The President can **veto** a bill; Congress can **override** the veto
  with a two-thirds vote in both houses.
- The Senate **confirms** presidential appointments and treaties.
- Courts can **declare a law unconstitutional** (judicial review, first
  claimed in *Marbury v. Madison*, 1803 — enrichment detail).
- Congress can **impeach and remove** a president or other official.
- The President **nominates** federal judges.

**Federalism** shares power between the national government and the
states: the federal government prints money, declares war, and regulates
trade between states and with other countries; states run schools, issue
licenses, and handle most local police powers; both collect taxes, build
roads, and run courts.

### Worked example 1 — Sorting a news story by branch

*Story:* "Congress passed a law funding new bridges. The President
signed it. A court later ruled that one part of the law violated the
Constitution."

*Modeled reasoning:* Three branches, three jobs, in order. Congress
passing the law is **legislative** (making laws). The President signing
it is **executive** (carrying out — a signed bill becomes a law to
enforce). The court ruling is **judicial** (interpreting — testing the
law against the Constitution). The trick is the verb: *passes*,
*signs*, *rules* each point to a different branch.

### Worked example 2 — Why the Great Compromise has two houses

*Question:* Why not just one house, by population?

*Modeled reasoning:* One house by population would let a few big states
outvote all the small ones every time — small states would never have
agreed. One house with equal votes would let a few small states block
the big ones — big states would never have agreed. Two houses give each
side a chamber where its principle rules, and a bill must pass both.
The compromise is not elegant; it is the price of agreement.

## Guided practice

**B1.** Name the three branches and one job each, in your own words.

**B2.** Which branch is acting in each case? (a) Congress votes on a law
to build a road. (b) The President signs that law. (c) The Supreme Court
decides the law violates the Constitution.

**B3.** Explain one check: how can Congress still pass a bill after the
President vetoes it? Name the vote needed, in both houses.

## Independent practice

**B4.** Why did the Articles of Confederation fail? Name two weaknesses
and, for each, the problem it caused.

**B5.** The Great Compromise: in one sentence, why did small states want
equal Senate seats? In one sentence, why did large states want House
seats by population?

**B6.** The Three-Fifths Compromise: in plain language, what did it
count, and did it free anyone? Answer both parts, in two or three
sentences.

**B7.** Federalism sort. Put each power in the right column — federal
only, state only, or shared: (a) declare war, (b) run public schools,
(c) collect taxes, (d) print money, (e) issue driver's licenses,
(f) build roads.

## Applied task

**B8.** Using the branches-and-checks diagram, trace this path in four
steps, naming the branch at each step: Congress passes a bill → the
President vetoes it → Congress overrides the veto → a court later rules
the law unconstitutional. For each step, write what happened and which
branch acted.

## Exit check

**B9.** In one sentence, what is the Preamble? Then name two of its six
purposes, in your own words.

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** The Preamble's six purposes may be matched to
  plain-language cards before writing; the adult reads the 1787 phrases
  aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** B7 may be done as a three-column card
  sort; B8 may be drawn as a four-box flow with arrows.
- **Extension:** Read (adult-selected) one short *Federalist Papers*
  argument summary — why Hamilton thought a strong national government
  would protect liberty rather than threaten it — and write one sentence
  agreeing or disagreeing, with a reason.
- **Teacher note — likely misconceptions:** (1) "The President makes
  the laws" — correct to "Congress makes them; the President carries
  them out and can veto." (2) The Three-Fifths Compromise is often
  misread as freeing people or as a statement about human worth; correct
  to "a political counting rule that increased slaveholding states'
  power." (3) "Checks" are often confused with "branches" — a branch is
  a part of government; a check is something one branch does to limit
  another. Solutions: see [answer key](../answer-key.md) (B1–B9).
