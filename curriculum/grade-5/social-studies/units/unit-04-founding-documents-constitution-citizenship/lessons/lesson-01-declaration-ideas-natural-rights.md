# Lesson 01 — The Declaration's Ideas: Rights Nobody Can Take Away

**Goal:** I can explain unalienable rights and consent of the governed in
my own words, and I can explain who was — and was not — treated as equal
in 1776.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 15, S1–S2). **Materials:** Unit
vocabulary list, evidence-chart paper (for task A8), the short
Declaration phrases in this lesson. **Readiness check:** Ask the learner
to retell, in two sentences, why the colonies declared independence
(Unit 03: taxes without representation; the king would not listen). If
the retelling stalls, re-read the Unit 03 Lesson 02 summary together
before starting.

## Teaching

### The promise in one paragraph

On July 4, 1776, in Philadelphia, representatives of the thirteen colonies
approved the Declaration of Independence. Most of it is a long list of
complaints against King George III. But its second paragraph states an
idea that changed the world — and it is the part this unit studies. In
grade-5 paraphrase (not the founders' exact words), the idea runs like
this:

> All people are created equal. Every person has certain rights that
> cannot rightly be taken away — among them life, liberty, and the
> pursuit of happiness. Governments exist to protect those rights, and a
> government is legitimate only because the people being governed agree
> to it. When a government keeps destroying those rights instead of
> protecting them, the people may change it or replace it.

The exact short phrases the Declaration uses are public-domain text, and
the adult may read them aloud: "all men are created equal," "endowed by
their Creator with certain unalienable Rights," "Life, Liberty and the
pursuit of Happiness," and governments "deriving their just powers from
the consent of the governed."

Three words carry the whole lesson:

- **Unalienable** means "cannot be taken away or given away." A right is
  unalienable when you have it simply because you are a person — not
  because a king or a law gave it to you, so no king or law can rightly
  remove it.
- **Consent of the governed** means the people being governed agree to
  the government. A government that rules people who never agreed — and
  who have no say — has lost its legitimacy, in the Declaration's
  argument.
- **Equal** here means equal in rights, not equal in everything. The
  Declaration does not claim that all people are equally strong, rich,
  or talented. It claims that no person is born with a natural right to
  rule over others.

### Worked example 1 — Reading "unalienable" the way a historian does

*Question:* The Declaration says people have a right to "life." Does
that mean nobody is ever allowed to be punished?

*Modeled reasoning:* No. A right to life means the government may not
take your life unjustly — it may not kill people it dislikes, or let
soldiers harm people without a fair trial. It does not mean a person who
harms others faces no consequences. Rights set limits on power; they do
not remove all rules. Notice the move: we read the word, asked what job
it does in the sentence, and checked the answer against the idea of
limits on power, not against a dictionary alone.

### Worked example 2 — Consent, tested with a classroom case

*Question:* A teacher announces a new rule — no talking at all during
lunch — without asking the class, and the class never gets to discuss
it. Does that rule have the class's consent?

*Modeled reasoning:* No. Consent means the governed had a real chance to
agree or object — a discussion, a vote, or representatives who speak for
them. An announced rule with no discussion and no way to object is the
opposite of consent, even if the rule itself is well meant. This is the
colonists' complaint in miniature: Parliament taxed them, and they had
no representatives in Parliament to consent or object.

### The honest question: who was included in 1776?

The Declaration's words are universal — "all men." In practice in 1776,
the new government did not treat everyone as equal:

- **Enslaved people** remained enslaved. The Declaration's author,
  Thomas Jefferson, himself enslaved people. The new nation kept slavery
  legal, and the Constitution (Lesson 02) protected it in compromises.
  Ending slavery took a war and the 13th Amendment in 1865 — a story
  Units 06–07 will tell.
- **Women** could not vote and had almost no political rights, even
  though the Declaration spoke of equality. Voting regardless of sex
  came with the 19th Amendment in 1920 (Lesson 03).
- **Indigenous nations** were not treated as part of the new government.
  The Declaration itself called Indigenous peoples "merciless Indian
  Savages" in its list of complaints — language the adult names as
  wrong and hurtful, and a reminder that the document's authors did not
  live up to their own principle.
- **Men without property** could not vote in most states at first;
  voting rights widened state by state over the following decades.

Phillis Wheatley — an enslaved poet in Boston whose poems supported
liberty, carried over from Unit 03 — makes the question sharp: a woman
who was enslaved could write about freedom while the country that
praised freedom kept her enslaved. The promise and the practice did not
match, and that gap is the engine of the rest of American history in
this track.

## Guided practice

**A1.** In your own words, what is an unalienable right? Give one example
from the Declaration's list of three (life, liberty, pursuit of
happiness).

**A2.** The Declaration says governments get "their just powers from
the consent of the governed." Explain consent in one sentence. Then
answer: does a rule imposed on a class that never got to discuss it have
that class's consent? Give one reason.

**A3.** Name two groups who were NOT treated as equals under the new
government in 1776, and give one fact showing it for each group.

## Independent practice

**A4.** Paraphrase the sentence "all men are created equal" in your own
words. Then write one sentence about what the sentence claimed, and one
sentence about what it left out in practice in 1776.

**A5.** Phillis Wheatley was an enslaved poet in Boston who wrote poems
supporting liberty. In two or three sentences, explain why her story
makes the "who was included" question sharper. (There is no single
correct wording; the key gives acceptable reasoning.)

**A6.** True or false, with evidence: "The Declaration of Independence
ended slavery in America." Explain your answer in two sentences.

**A7.** Error analysis: A classmate says, "The Declaration gave everyone
the vote in 1776." Find two errors in that sentence and correct each
one.

## Applied task

**A8.** Start the unit evidence chart (kept through Lesson 04 and used
in the assessment). Write the claim at the top: *"The Revolution
fulfilled the Declaration's promises."* Under it, write one piece of
evidence FOR the claim and one piece AGAINST it, using only what this
lesson taught. Label each piece "for" or "against" and add one reason
linking it to the claim.

## Exit check

**A9.** In one or two sentences: what does "consent of the governed"
mean, and why did the colonists say Britain had lost it?

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** The adult reads the Declaration phrases aloud and
  the learner repeats the paraphrase in their own words before writing.
  Sentence starters are allowed: "An unalienable right is…" and
  "Consent means…".
- **Alternate response mode:** A5 and A8 may be answered orally or as a
  labeled drawing (two columns, "promise" and "practice") with the adult
  scribing the learner's words.
- **Extension:** Find one more founding-era voice the adult selects
  (for example, Abigail Adams's 1776 "remember the ladies" letter, in
  excerpt) and add a third row to the evidence chart: whose rights was
  she asking about?
- **Teacher note — likely misconceptions:** (1) Learners often hear
  "equal" as "the same in every way"; correct to "equal in rights."
  (2) Learners often assume the Declaration freed enslaved people or
  gave women the vote; A6 and A7 exist to break that assumption early.
  (3) "Consent" is often confused with "obeying"; consent is agreement
  before the rule, not obedience after it. Solutions: see
  [answer key](../answer-key.md) (A1–A9).
