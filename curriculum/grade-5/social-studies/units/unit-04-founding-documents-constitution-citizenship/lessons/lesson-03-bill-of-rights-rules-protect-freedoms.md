# Lesson 03 — The Bill of Rights: Rules That Protect Freedoms

**Goal:** I can explain the first ten amendments in plain language, name
the later voting-rights amendments, and order the steps that make a bill
a law and amend the Constitution.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 17, S1–S2). **Materials:** Unit
vocabulary list, amendment scenario cards (task C8).
**Readiness check:** Ask the learner to name the three branches and one
check (Lesson 02). If the check is missing, re-read Lesson 02's checks
list together.

## Teaching

### Why a Bill of Rights had to be added

The Constitution as signed in 1787 listed the government's powers but
said little about the people's rights. Anti-Federalists refused to
ratify without protections: they remembered British soldiers in homes,
banned pamphlets, and unfair trials, and they feared a strong national
government would do the same. The deal that won ratification was a
promise: a bill of rights would be added at once.

Congress proposed 12 amendments on September 25, 1789. Ten were ratified
by three-fourths of the states on December 15, 1791 — the Bill of
Rights. (One of the two unratified proposals, about when changes to
Congress's pay take effect, was finally ratified in 1992 as the 27th
Amendment — proof that the amendment process still works, slowly.)

### The ten amendments in plain language

| Amendment | Plain-language protection |
|---|---|
| 1st | Five freedoms: religion, speech, press, peaceful assembly, petition (asking the government to fix a problem). |
| 2nd | The right to keep and bear arms. |
| 3rd | No soldiers quartered (housed) in private homes in peacetime without the owner's consent. |
| 4th | No unreasonable searches or seizures; police need a warrant based on probable cause — a good reason, sworn before a judge, naming the place and things. |
| 5th | Fair process in criminal cases: grand jury, no double jeopardy (being tried twice for the same crime), no forced self-incrimination, no loss of life, liberty, or property without due process, and just payment if private property is taken for public use. |
| 6th | Fair trial rights: speedy and public trial, impartial jury, knowing the charge, confronting witnesses, and a lawyer. |
| 7th | Jury trial in civil (non-criminal) cases over $20, as written in 1791. |
| 8th | No excessive bail or fines, and no cruel and unusual punishments. |
| 9th | Listing some rights does not mean people have no other rights. |
| 10th | Powers not given to the federal government, and not forbidden to the states, belong to the states or the people. |

Notice the pattern: most of the Bill of Rights is a list of things the
government may **not** do. Rights here are limits on power, not favors
the government hands out — the same idea as Lesson 01's unalienable
rights.

### Later amendments that widened who counts

The Bill of Rights did not finish the job. Later amendments changed who
could vote and who was protected:

- **13th (1865):** Slavery is abolished (except as punishment for a
  crime) — the promise of Lesson 01 begins to reach enslaved people.
- **14th (1868):** Anyone born or naturalized in the United States is a
  citizen; states may not deny any person equal protection of the laws.
- **15th (1870):** The right to vote cannot be denied because of race.
- **19th (1920):** The right to vote cannot be denied because of sex.
- **26th (1971):** Citizens 18 and older cannot be denied the vote
  because of age.
- **22nd (1951):** A president may serve at most two terms.

There are 27 amendments in all. Units 06–07 will tell how the 13th,
14th, and 15th were won — and then undermined, and then defended again.

### How a bill becomes a law (simplified)

1. A member of Congress **introduces** a bill (money bills must start in
   the House).
2. A **committee** studies it, changes it, and votes on whether to send
   it on.
3. The **House and the Senate both pass** the same version.
4. The **President signs it** — it becomes law — or **vetoes** it.
5. If vetoed, Congress can **override** with a two-thirds vote in both
   houses. If the President neither signs nor returns it within ten days
   (Sundays excepted) while Congress is in session, it becomes law
   anyway.

### How the Constitution is amended

1. **Proposal:** two-thirds of both houses of Congress propose an
   amendment (or two-thirds of the states call a convention to propose
   one).
2. **Ratification:** three-fourths of the states approve it (through
   their legislatures or special conventions).

Two high bars, on purpose: the plan must be hard enough to change that
it stays stable, and possible enough to change that it can grow — as it
did with the Bill of Rights, the end of slavery, and the vote.

### Worked example 1 — The First Amendment in a school story

*Story:* A student writes a respectful letter to the town newspaper
saying the school lunch policy is unfair. The principal says, "No one
may criticize school rules in public."

*Modeled reasoning:* The letter is **speech** and **press** — two First
Amendment freedoms. A respectful opinion about a policy is exactly what
the First Amendment protects: the government (and a public school acts
as government here) may not punish opinions it dislikes. The adult adds
the honest limit: schools may set rules about disruption — a protest
that blocks classes is different from a letter — but "you may not
criticize us" is not a disruption rule; it is a ban on opinion, and the
First Amendment forbids that.

### Worked example 2 — The Fourth Amendment in a search story

*Story:* Police want to search a family's home for a stolen bicycle.
They have no warrant and no good reason to think the bicycle is there.

*Modeled reasoning:* The Fourth Amendment says no unreasonable searches,
and a home search normally needs a **warrant** — a judge's permission
based on **probable cause**, a good sworn reason naming the place and
the things. "We would like to look" is not probable cause. So the search
may not go ahead on that basis. The move to copy: name the amendment,
name its test (warrant + probable cause), apply the test to the facts.

## Guided practice

**C1.** Name the five freedoms of the First Amendment, in your own
words.

**C2.** Which amendment is at stake in each case? (a) Police search a
home without a warrant or good reason. (b) A person accused of a crime
asks for a lawyer. (c) A newspaper prints criticism of the mayor.

**C3.** Why did the Anti-Federalists demand a Bill of Rights before they
would ratify the Constitution? Give two reasons, in your own words.

## Independent practice

**C4.** Match each amendment to its plain-language protection: 2nd,
3rd, 5th, 8th, 10th — with: (i) powers not given to the federal
government belong to the states or the people; (ii) no cruel and
unusual punishments; (iii) no soldiers housed in private homes in
peacetime without consent; (iv) fair process — no double jeopardy, no
forced self-incrimination, due process, just payment for taken
property; (v) the right to keep and bear arms.

**C5.** Put these bill-to-law steps in order: (a) the President signs
or vetoes; (b) a committee studies and votes; (c) a member introduces
the bill; (d) the House and Senate both pass the same version; (e) if
vetoed, Congress may override with two-thirds in both houses.

**C6.** The amendment path has two steps. Name both steps with their
fractions (proposal, ratification). Then explain in one sentence why
the bars are set so high.

**C7.** In one sentence each, state what the 15th, 19th, and 26th
Amendments changed about voting.

## Applied task

**C8.** Rights scenario cards. For each scenario, name the amendment,
say whether the right is protected or violated, and give one reason:
(i) A town bans a peaceful protest march because officials dislike its
message. (ii) A student is punished for writing a respectful letter
saying a school rule is unfair. (iii) The government takes a family's
car for public use and pays nothing.

## Exit check

**C9.** In two sentences: why is the Bill of Rights better described as
a list of protections (limits on government) than as a list of gifts
from the government? Connect your answer to Lesson 01's unalienable
rights.

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** Amendment numbers may be matched to
  plain-language cards before writing; the adult reads scenario cards
  aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** C4 and C5 may be done as card sorts;
  C8 may be answered orally with the adult scribing.
- **Extension:** Research (adult-selected) one more amendment — the 13th
  or 14th — and write two sentences on what changed for whom. Preview
  only; Units 06–07 teach these in full.
- **Teacher note — likely misconceptions:** (1) "The Bill of Rights
  gives us rights" — correct to "it lists protections for rights the
  Declaration says we already have." (2) Learners often think any
  government action they dislike violates the Constitution; C8 trains
  the test: name the amendment, apply its rule to the facts.
  (3) School-speech limits are genuinely subtle; keep C8(ii) at the
  opinion-vs.-disruption distinction and do not overclaim. Solutions:
  see [answer key](../answer-key.md) (C1–C9).
