# Lesson 04 — Citizenship: Participation, Decisions, and Changing Laws

**Goal:** I can name ways citizens participate, compare decision
procedures, use deliberation to make a fair rule, and propose a policy
for a real problem.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 18, S1–S2). **Materials:** Unit
vocabulary list, the learner's evidence chart from Lesson 01 (for the
assessment preview). **Readiness check:** Ask the learner to name one
Bill of Rights protection and one way a law can be changed (Lesson 03).
If either is missing, re-read the Lesson 03 plain-language table and
amendment path together.

## Teaching

### What citizens do

A citizen is a member of a country. Adult citizens have rights — voting
is the most famous — and responsibilities. But citizenship is not only
voting, and it is not only for adults. Participation takes many forms:

- **Vote** (citizens 18 and older) and learn about candidates and issues
  before voting.
- **Obey laws** and **pay taxes** that fund roads, schools, and courts.
- **Serve on a jury** when called, and **serve in office** or on a
  school or town board.
- **Speak up:** discuss issues respectfully, write to representatives,
  **petition** for change, join a peaceful protest.
- **Stay informed:** read news from more than one source, check facts
  before repeating claims.
- **Help the community:** volunteer, clean a park, help a neighbor,
  take part in a school or town project.

Children participate too: they can speak up, volunteer, learn the issues,
and practice deliberation in class — which is what this unit's project
does. Democracy, in the C3 Framework's words, relies on people's
responsible participation: if nobody takes part, the system runs on
whoever shouts loudest.

### Civic virtues and democratic principles

Two short lists guide the whole lesson:

- **Civic virtues** (how good citizens act): honesty, respect for
  others, responsibility, courage to speak up fairly, and cooperation.
- **Democratic principles** (how good governments act): liberty
  (freedom), equality under the law, justice (fair treatment), the rule
  of law (even leaders must obey the law), and majority rule with
  minority rights — the majority decides, but the minority's rights and
  interests still count.

### How groups make decisions

| Procedure | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vote | Everyone votes; the most votes win | Fair and fast; everyone had a say | Losers may feel ignored |
| Consensus | Discussion continues until everyone can accept the decision | Everyone owns the result | Slow; one holdout can block it |
| Leader decides | A teacher, principal, or official decides | Fast; good for safety and expertise | People may feel unheard |

No procedure is right for every decision. A fire-drill route is a
leader-decides choice (safety and expertise matter most). A class
celebration is a vote or consensus choice (everyone's enjoyment
matters). The skill is matching the procedure to the decision — and
using **deliberation** either way.

### Deliberation: the five-step routine

1. **State the problem** clearly, in one sentence.
2. **Hear all views,** especially views you disagree with.
3. **Weigh reasons:** which reasons are strong, which are weak, and what
   evidence supports each?
4. **Decide** by the chosen procedure (vote, consensus, or leader
   decision), and **state the reason** for the decision in public.
5. **Review:** after trying the rule, check whether it worked and change
   it if it did not.

### How people change laws

Ordinary people change laws through the system, not around it: vote for
representatives who share their views; petition and write to
representatives; speak publicly and peacefully; run for office or serve
on boards; and support amendments through Congress and the states
(Lesson 03's two-step path). The civil-rights movements of Units 06–07
used every one of these tools.

### Worked example 1 — Matching the procedure to the decision

*Question:* A class must choose a field-trip destination. Three
procedures are proposed: the teacher picks, the class votes, or the
class talks until everyone agrees. Which fits?

*Modeled reasoning:* The teacher picking is fast but leaves students
feeling ignored about their own trip. Consensus gives everyone ownership
but could take all period on a low-stakes choice. A vote fits: everyone
had a say, the decision is quick, and the losers accepted the procedure
in advance. The reasoning move to copy: judge a procedure by fairness,
speed, and who is affected — not by which outcome you personally want.

### Worked example 2 — Deliberating a hallway rule

*Question:* "No running in the hall." Deliberate it.

*Modeled reasoning:* (1) Problem: students get hurt in crowded halls.
(2) Views: some say running is sometimes needed (late for class, helping
someone hurt); others say walking rules are ignored. (3) Reasons:
safety evidence is strong; "I like running" is weak against injuries.
(4) Decision by vote: keep the rule, with a stated exception — running
to get help in an emergency is allowed. (5) Review after two weeks: did
injuries fall? The rule is fairer than a flat ban because it heard the
objection and answered it with an exception, not a dismissal.

## Guided practice

**D1.** Name four ways citizens participate, beyond voting. (One must be
something a child can do.)

**D2.** Match each situation to its best decision procedure — vote,
consensus, or leader decides — and give one reason: (a) the class picks
a movie for a celebration; (b) the principal sets the fire-drill route;
(c) three friends agree on rules for a game everyone will play.

**D3.** Put the five deliberation steps in order: weigh reasons; decide
and state the reason; state the problem; review after trying the rule;
hear all views.

## Independent practice

**D4.** A town proposes a new rule: no dogs in the park. Give one reason
FOR the rule and one reason AGAINST it. (Deliberation requires hearing
both sides before deciding.)

**D5.** Policy proposal: pick one real classroom or school problem.
State it in one sentence, propose one policy to address it, and list
two steps for making it happen (who acts, and what happens first).

**D6.** A class votes 20–5 to ban a game that 5 students love. The
majority says "we voted, so it's fair." Is it? Explain majority rule
with minority rights in two or three sentences, and suggest one
compromise.

**D7.** Error analysis: "Citizenship means only voting once every few
years." Find two errors in that sentence and correct each one.

## Applied task

**D8.** Write a fair classroom rule using deliberation: state the rule,
give its reason, say how it was (or would be) decided — vote, consensus,
or leader decides, with why that procedure fits — and name one
exception. (This draft may feed the class constitution project.)

## Exit check

**D9.** In one or two sentences: how can ordinary people change a law?
Name at least two lawful ways.

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading access:** Decision-procedure cards (vote / consensus /
  leader decides) may be sorted before writing; sentence starters are
  allowed: "One reason for is…; one reason against is…".
- **Alternate response mode:** D4–D6 may be answered orally or as a
  two-column chart; D8 may be presented as a poster.
- **Extension:** Interview an adult (at home or school, adult-arranged)
  about one time they took part in a community decision, and add their
  one-sentence account to the evidence chart with the adult's permission
  — no names are published.
- **Teacher note — likely misconceptions:** (1) "Democracy means the
  majority always gets its way" — correct with minority rights and the
  compromise habit (D6). (2) "Participation is only for adults" —
  correct with child-appropriate participation (D1, D8). (3) Deliberation
  is often confused with "arguing until someone wins"; correct to
  "hearing reasons and deciding together." Solutions: see
  [answer key](../answer-key.md) (D1–D9).
