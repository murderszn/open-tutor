# U01 Practice Banks

**Unit:** [U01 — North American Geography, Indigenous Histories, and Sources](README.md)

These banks feed the S2 skills-practice sessions (Weeks 3–6). Each bank has a
guided set (done with the adult, thinking aloud) and an independent set
(10–14 written tasks, reviewed by the adult the same day). **Solutions are
in the [answer key](answer-key.md) only — never in this file.**

Content adapts the legacy quizzes marked "Revise → U01" in the track audit
(Midwest, Southwest, West & Pacific, Indigenous nations), reconciled to this
unit's 4-region model and with answer keys separated per the audit. All
questions are re-solved independently in the answer key.

## Bank 1 — Map reading (Week 3 S2)

*Use a North America political map with title, key, compass rose, scale bar,
and grid. Cite a map tool for every claim.*

**Guided (with the adult):**

1. Read the title. What question does this map answer? What question does
   it not answer?
2. Using the compass rose, name the country north of the United States and
   the country south of it.
3. Find the scale bar. If one segment = 500 miles and two cities are 3
   segments apart, about how far apart are they? Show the multiplication.
4. Find the grid square for Mexico City. Explain how the grid helps someone
   else find it.

**Independent (12 tasks):**

1. List the six map tools and what each tells you.
2. What does the key's star symbol mean on your map? How do you know?
3. Name the ocean west of North America and the ocean east of it. Which
   tool proved east from west?
4. Find the Mississippi River with the key's river symbol. Name one state
   it borders.
5. Estimate the distance between Chicago and New Orleans with the scale
   bar. Show your work.
6. A friend says, "Alaska is south of Texas." Agree or disagree with map
   evidence.
7. Name the five Great Lakes shown on the map.
8. Write an evidence claim about the location of the Rocky Mountains,
   citing a symbol and the compass rose.
9. Your map's scale bar says 500 miles per segment; a state map's says 50.
   Which is the large-scale map, and what does it show in more detail?
10. A thematic map uses bigger dots for bigger cities. Why doesn't the
    biggest dot have to be a capital? Use the key in your answer.
11. Find Ottawa, Canada's grid square. Write it.
12. Make one evidence claim about any place on the map, citing one
    specific map feature.

## Bank 2 — Region sorting (Week 4 S2)

*Use `resources/us_states.csv` (`subregion` column) and a relief map. Our
official model is the 4-region one: United States Northeast / Midwest /
South / West.*

**Guided (with the adult):**

1. Read the `subregion` value for Oklahoma. Which region? Find Oklahoma on
   the relief map — does its landscape fit?
2. Texas is "Southwest" in the 5-region model but `United States South` in
   our CSV. Explain why both can be reasonable, and which model this unit
   uses.
3. Name one landform, one climate fact, and one water feature of the
   Midwest. How do they explain the "Breadbasket" nickname?
4. Predict: New York or Wyoming — which has more people per square mile?
   Check `population_density_per_sq_mi` in the CSV. Were you right?

**Independent (12 tasks):**

1. Define **region** and **climate** in your own words.
2. Sort into our 4 regions (CSV `subregion` column): Maine, Minnesota,
   Florida, Oregon.
3. Sort: Pennsylvania, Missouri, Alabama, Idaho. For each, add one map
   observation that fits its region (mountain, river, coast, or plain).
4. Which region touches the most Great Lakes? Name the lakes (HOMES) and
   explain one way the lakes shape that region.
5. The Rio Grande forms the Texas–Mexico border. Which CSV region is Texas
   in? Name two things a border river gives nearby places.
6. Four Corners (Arizona, New Mexico, Utah, Colorado) is the only point
   where four states meet. Which of our 4 regions contains all four? What
   does that suggest about how western borders were drawn?
7. Santa Fe, New Mexico (founded 1610) is the oldest state capital in the
   U.S. Which region is New Mexico in? Why would a capital be founded in
   desert country — what did its founders need there?
8. Explain why the Southwest is dry and the Southeast is rainy, using
   landforms and distance from water.
9. A textbook's 5-region model puts Arizona in the "Southwest"; our CSV
   puts it in the West. Explain to a friend why both are reasonable and
   which one this unit uses.
10. Write an evidence claim: "The Midwest's environment made it a farming
    region." Cite one landform, one climate fact, and one water feature.
11. Which two states meet at Four Corners' northern point pair — that is,
    which two of the four share the northern border of the monument point?
    (Hint: think about which states are north of the point.)
12. Name the capital of each of these states from the CSV `capital` column:
    Illinois, Texas, California. Which region is each state in?

## Bank 3 — Culture areas and living nations (Week 5 S2)

**Guided (with the adult):**

1. Point to each of the 8 culture areas on an environment map. For the
   Arctic and the Southwest, name the key environmental challenge.
2. Explain the Three Sisters: the three crops and how each helps the
   others.
3. Why did Plains nations build portable teepees while Eastern Woodlands
   nations built permanent longhouses? Give the environment reason.
4. What does "living nations" mean? Name one nation and one present-day
   fact about it.

**Independent (12 tasks):**

1. List the 8 culture areas, each with a short phrase describing its
   environment.
2. Arctic: name two hunted animals and two different uses people found for
   each.
3. Northwest Coast: what is a totem pole, what do its figures represent,
   and what is it NOT?
4. Great Plains: how did the bison shape food, shelter, and one more part
   of life?
5. Eastern Woodlands: describe a longhouse (materials, who lived there,
   why its size fit farming villages).
6. Southwest: name two ways people modified the desert to farm and one way
   they adapted their homes to heat.
7. California: what is a *tomol*? What does it tell you about Chumash
   travel and trade?
8. Southeast: how did the long growing season shape daily life? Contrast
   with the Arctic in two sentences.
9. Plateau: why were rivers the "highways" of this area? Name two things
   rivers provided.
10. Define **nation**, **sovereign**, and **homeland**.
11. Write the "some historians argue" paragraph about the Haudenosaunee
    Confederacy and American democracy: one piece of evidence for the
    claim, one reason historians disagree. Do not state it as settled fact.
12. A museum label reads, "These peoples vanished in the 1800s." Explain
    two things wrong with it, using this unit's framing.

## Bank 4 — Source kinds (Week 6 S2)

**Guided (with the adult):**

1. Make a two-column chart: for each of the 4 source kinds, one thing it
   CAN tell us and one thing it CANNOT.
2. Fact or opinion? "Beans add nitrogen to the soil." / "The Three Sisters
   is the smartest way anyone ever farmed." Explain each.
3. The adult shows one approved source. Together: maker? audience (with
   in-source clue)? purpose (with in-source clue)?
4. A 1900 photograph shows a Pueblo cornfield. What does it prove, and
   what can't it tell you?

**Independent (12 tasks):**

1. Define **source**, **audience**, and **purpose**.
2. Name the 4 source kinds; one sentence on what each is.
3. Oral accounts: two strengths, one limit.
4. Artifacts: a clay pot is found in a desert village — two things it can
   tell us, two things it cannot. (Stick to what the object itself proves.)
5. Maps: why ask "whose map is this?" Give one example of what a European
   explorer's map might leave out.
6. Photographs: explain with "before and after" why a photo is not the
   whole truth.
7. Label fact or opinion and explain: (a) "Corn, beans, and squash are
   called the Three Sisters." (b) "The Grand Canyon is the most beautiful
   place on Earth." (c) "The Grand Canyon was carved by the Colorado
   River."
8. A website shouts: "BEST totem-pole video EVER!!" Fact or opinion? Should
   the excitement make you trust it more or less? Why?
9. Two sources disagree about why a village moved (elder's account: the
   river changed course; old map: village never moved). Give two possible
   explanations where neither source is lying.
10. Why do historians use multiple sources instead of trusting one?
    Use the words "origin, structure, and context."
11. Read this label (original, composed for this lesson): *"Step closer.
    This basket held acorns gathered each fall for hundreds of years. Run
    your eyes along the weave — can you see how tight it is? Imagine the
    hands that made it."* Infer the audience and purpose from two in-source
    clues.
12. The CSV lists Texas density as 108.5 people per square mile. Show a
    rough division check (`population_approx ÷ area_sq_mi`) and say whether
    the figure is plausible.
