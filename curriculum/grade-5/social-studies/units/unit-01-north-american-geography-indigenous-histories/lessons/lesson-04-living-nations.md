# Lesson 04 — Living Nations

**Unit:** [U01 — North American Geography, Indigenous Histories, and Sources](../README.md) · **Week 5 (S3–S4)**
**Time:** two ~40-minute sessions (S3 concept, S4 review + project work).
**Student goal:** I can explain that Indigenous nations are living, sovereign
communities with homelands today — and compare life in the past to life now,
explaining why people in the same period saw things differently.

**Materials:** [Resource Pack](../resource-pack.md) (adult pre-selects 2–3
Indigenous-authored, tribal, or museum sources); project sheet
([project.md](../project.md)).

**A note before we begin:** Everything in [Lesson 03](lesson-03-culture-areas.md)
was about how the land shaped daily life long ago. This lesson is about
*now*. The nations you studied did not vanish. They are here — governing
themselves, speaking their languages, teaching their children, and shaping
American life today.

## Readiness check (S3 opening, ~5 minutes)

1. Name two culture areas and one nation from each.
2. What does **sovereign** mean? (Having the right to make your own laws and
   govern your own community.)
3. A learner says, "Indigenous peoples lived long ago." In one sentence,
   explain what is wrong — and right — about that sentence. (Right: their
   histories stretch back thousands of years. Wrong: "lived" in the past
   tense, as if they are gone.)

## The big idea: nations, not artifacts

A **nation** is a group of people with a shared identity, history, and —
for tribal nations — the right to govern themselves. The United States
recognizes **hundreds of federally recognized tribal nations**, and each
one is a **sovereign** government: it makes its own laws, runs its own
schools and courts and health programs, and manages its own land. Treaties
— formal agreements between nations — are the legal foundation of that
relationship. That is why we say **nations** and **peoples**, and why we
say **homelands**: these are not "tribes" from a storybook; they are
governments and communities with a past, a present, and a future.

**Present-day connections** — the same nations from Lesson 03, today:

- The **Haudenosaunee Confederacy** still governs through its Grand Council,
  which meets at Onondaga — the same council fire that has burned for
  centuries. Haudenosaunee artists, writers, athletes, and leaders shape
  modern American life.
- The **Diné (Navajo) Nation** — *Diné* means "the people" in their own
  language; it is their preferred name — is the largest tribal nation in
  the United States, with its own government, schools, and a living
  language spoken by well over a hundred thousand people.
- **Pueblo peoples** still live in villages their ancestors built centuries
  ago — Taos Pueblo has been continuously inhabited for roughly a thousand
  years — farming, holding ceremonies, and welcoming respectful visitors.
- **Pacific Northwest nations** still fish the salmon runs their ancestors
  fished, carve totem poles for new generations, and teach their languages
  in tribal schools.

Comparing **life then and now** (C3 D2.His.2.3-5): a Haudenosaunee child
long ago might have lived in a bark longhouse and learned the Three
Sisters planting from family; a Haudenosaunee child today might live in a
modern house, go to school, play lacrosse — a game with Haudenosaunee
roots — and still learn the language and ceremonies of her nation. Some
things changed enormously; the nation itself continued.

**Why honest history matters here:** the story also includes forced removal
from homelands, broken treaties, and children taken to boarding schools —
named plainly, at an age-appropriate level, never graphic. Nations survived
these things. When we study Indigenous histories, we study *survivors and
their descendants*, not a vanished world. Every encounter in this track is
examined from at least two perspectives — for example, a treaty looks very
different from the side that wrote it and the side that was promised land
"forever" and then watched the promise break (C3 D2.His.4, 5.3-5).

### Worked example 1: Then and now (Diné)

**Question:** How is Diné life today both different from and connected to
Diné life 200 years ago?

**Reasoning:** *Different:* 200 years ago most Diné lived by herding sheep
and farming in the high desert, traveling by foot and horse; today many
Diné live in towns, drive cars, work as teachers, nurses, engineers, and
artists, and use the internet. *Connected:* the Diné language is still
spoken; weaving and silversmithing traditions continue; the Nation governs
itself through its own elected government; ceremonies and clan
relationships still organize family life.

**Claim:** "Diné life changed enormously in 200 years — new homes, jobs,
and technology — but the Nation continues: its government, language, and
traditions connect today's Diné to their ancestors." **Evidence:**
Diné-led sources (tribal government pages, museum exhibits) describe both
the modern Nation's services and its living traditions; the preferred name
*Diné* itself ("the people") is used by the Nation today.

### Worked example 2: A debated claim — the Haudenosaunee and American democracy

**Question:** Did the Haudenosaunee Confederacy influence the United States
Constitution? This is where historians say: *it's debated — and here's the
evidence.*

**The evidence some historians point to:** The Haudenosaunee Confederacy —
five nations (Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca; the Tuscarora
joined later, making six) united under the Great Law of Peace, governed by
a Grand Council where each nation had a voice — was a working democracy
long before 1776. In 1751, Benjamin Franklin wrote that the colonies
should form a union *like* the Iroquois Confederacy, which had held
together for ages. In 1988, the U.S. Congress passed a resolution
acknowledging the Iroquois Confederacy's democratic example. **Some
historians argue** this shows the Confederacy influenced early American
democratic thinkers.

**The disagreement:** Other historians say the evidence is thin — that
Franklin admired the Confederacy but the Constitution's framers drew mostly
on European ideas, and that a Congressional resolution is a political
statement, not proof. They point out that admiration is not the same as
influence.

**What a careful student does:** We do NOT state it as settled fact. We
say: "**Some historians argue** the Haudenosaunee Confederacy influenced
American democratic thinkers, pointing to Franklin's 1751 letter and the
1988 Congressional resolution; other historians disagree, saying the
evidence shows admiration more than direct influence." That sentence is
honest, and honesty is the whole skill: when experts disagree, you report
the disagreement and the evidence — you don't pick the answer that sounds
best.

## Guided practice (S3)

Do these together:

1. Why do we say "living nations" instead of "ancient peoples"? Give two
   reasons.
2. What is a treaty? Why does the word "treaty" prove that tribal nations
   are governments, not just groups of people?
3. Practice the debated-claim sentence: in your own words, explain the
   Haudenosaunee-influence debate and name one piece of evidence each side
   uses.
4. Name one present-day connection for any nation from Lesson 03. Where did
   you learn it — and is that source Indigenous-authored, museum, or
   tribal? (Why does that matter?)

## Independent practice (S3–S4 — 10 written tasks; adult reviews the same day)

1. Define **nation**, **sovereign**, and **homeland** in your own words.
2. Name three tribal nations that exist today and one fact about each one's
   present-day life (government, language, art, land — your choice).
3. Explain why the word "tribes" alone can be misleading, and why this unit
   says "nations/peoples."
4. The Diné call themselves Diné. Why does using a nation's *own* name for
   itself matter? (Think: who gets to name you?)
5. Then-and-now: choose one nation. Write two sentences about life 200
   years ago and two sentences about life today. End with one sentence
   about what *continued*.
6. A museum label says: "These peoples vanished in the 1800s." Using this
   lesson, explain two things wrong with that label.
7. Treaties: in two sentences, explain what a treaty is and why broken
   treaties matter to the nations involved.
8. Write the debated-claim paragraph: "Some historians argue…" (Haudenosaunee
   influence). Include one piece of evidence for the claim and one reason
   historians disagree. Do not state it as settled fact.
9. Two perspectives: a new railroad is proposed across a nation's homeland
   in the 1800s. Write two sentences from a settler's perspective (why they
   want it) and two from a nation's member's perspective (why they might
   oppose it). Neither side is a villain — each has reasons.
10. Present-day connection: with your adult's approved source, find one
    living tradition, art form, or public role of any nation you studied.
    Write 3 sentences and name the source.

1. Complete the sentence: "Indigenous nations are ______ communities with
   ______." (living, sovereign / homelands)
2. Why do we say "some historians argue" about the Haudenosaunee and the
   Constitution?

## Supports and extension

- **Supports:** Sentence starters for the debated-claim paragraph ("Some
  historians argue ___ because ___. Other historians disagree because
  ___."); the adult reads approved sources aloud; present-day connection
  may be drawn or told orally.
- **Extension:** Compare two nations' present-day governments (e.g., the
  Diné Nation and the Haudenosaunee Confederacy): how are leaders chosen in
  each? Write a paragraph citing the tribal or museum source.

## Teacher note — common misconceptions and sensitive content

- The past-tense habit ("they lived, they hunted") is the deepest
  misconception. Correct to present tense for living nations every time:
  "the Diné *are*…", "Haudenosaunee artists *create*…".
- Learners may repeat the debated claim as fact after hearing the evidence.
  The exit check exists to lock in the "some historians argue" framing.
- **Sensitive content:** forced removal, broken treaties, and boarding
  schools are named plainly and briefly, at an age-appropriate level, with
  no graphic detail. If the learner asks for more detail than is
  age-appropriate, the adult acknowledges the question honestly ("Yes, that
  happened, and it was wrong") and returns to the lesson's focus: survival,
  sovereignty, and living nations. All sources are adult-selected.
