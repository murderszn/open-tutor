# Lesson 05 — Working with Sources

**Unit:** [U01 — North American Geography, Indigenous Histories, and Sources](../README.md) · **Week 6**
**Time:** three ~40-minute sessions (S1 launch, S2 practice, S3 dataset
task + quiz) + Week 6 S4 unit review and assessment.
**Student goal:** I can name four kinds of historical sources, explain what
each can and cannot tell me, infer a source's audience and purpose from
clues inside it, tell fact from opinion, and use data as evidence for a
claim.

**Materials:** adult pre-selects 2–3 sources about an Indigenous nation
(Indigenous-authored, tribal, or museum sources preferred); printed
`resources/us_states.csv` excerpt (Texas and Rhode Island rows, or the adult
reads them aloud).

**A note before we begin:** Everything you learned in Lessons 03–04 came
from *somewhere* — from stories people told, objects people made, maps
people drew, photographs people took. This lesson asks: how do we know what
we know? And how do we judge whether a source deserves our trust?

## Readiness check (S1 opening, ~5 minutes)

1. Name one thing you learned in Lesson 03. Where do you think that
   information came from — who told it, wrote it, or made it?
2. If someone tells you "my grandmother taught me this story," is that
   history? (Yes — if we treat it carefully. Stories passed down are
   sources too.)
3. What is the difference between a **fact** (can be checked and proven)
   and an **opinion** (what someone feels or believes)?

## The big idea: every source has a maker, an audience, and a purpose

A **source** is anything that gives us information about the past. The four
kinds you will use in this track:

**1. Oral accounts** — history and knowledge passed down by spoken
storytelling, often by elders. *Can tell us:* a nation's own stories,
values, explanations of events, and knowledge (farming, navigation,
medicine) in the nation's own words. *Cannot tell us:* exact dates the way
a written record can; and like all retelling, details can shift over many
generations — which is why historians compare oral accounts with other
sources instead of using one alone.

**2. Artifacts** — objects people made or used: pottery, tools, clothing,
buildings, toys. *Can tell us:* what materials people had, what skills
they mastered, what daily life looked and felt like. *Cannot tell us:*
what people *thought* or *why* they did things — an artifact doesn't come
with its owner's diary. We infer carefully, and we say when we're guessing.

**3. Maps** — drawings of places made by someone, at some time, for some
reason. *Can tell us:* where things were, what the maker thought was
important enough to draw, and how people saw their world. *Cannot tell us:*
what the maker left out — and every map leaves things out. A European
explorer's map might show rivers and forts but not the villages that had
been there for centuries. Ask: *whose map is this, and what did they choose
to show?*

**4. Photographs** — moments frozen by a camera. *Can tell us:* what
something looked like at one instant — faces, clothing, buildings.
*Cannot tell us:* what happened before or after the photo, or why the
photographer chose *that* moment. A photograph of a ceremony doesn't tell
you what the ceremony meant to the people in it.

**Audience and purpose — read from inside the source.** Every source was
made *for someone* (its **audience**) *for a reason* (its **purpose**): to
teach, to convince, to record, to remember, to celebrate, to sell. You can
often infer both from clues *within the source itself* — its words,
pictures, tone, and what it emphasizes (C3 D2.His.11.3-5). A poster that
shouts "COME SEE!" in giant letters was made for the public, to persuade.
A quiet label in a museum was made for visitors, to teach.

**Fact vs. opinion** (C3 D3.2.3-5): "Salmon return to Northwest rivers
every year" is a fact — it can be checked. "Salmon are the most delicious
fish in the world" is an opinion — it can't be proven, only felt. Opinions
aren't lies, but they can't carry a history claim by themselves. When you
judge a source's trustworthiness, separate what it *proves* from what it
*feels*.

### Worked example 1: What can each source kind tell us?

**Question:** We want to learn how Ancestral Puebloans farmed in the
desert. We have four sources: (a) a Pueblo elder's spoken account of
dry-farming teachings, (b) a digging stick found at a village site, (c) a
Spanish explorer's 1500s map of the region, (d) a 1900 photograph of a
Pueblo cornfield. What does each contribute?

**Reasoning:** (a) The elder's **oral account** tells us the *knowledge*
itself — when to plant, how to read the rain — in the community's own
words; it can't give us the exact year a technique began. (b) The
**artifact** (digging stick) proves people *did* plant by hand with simple
tools and shows their skill; it can't tell us what the farmer was thinking.
(c) The **map** shows where the Spanish *believed* villages were — useful
for locations, but it leaves out everything the Spanish didn't care about,
and it's drawn from the explorer's perspective. (d) The **photograph**
shows a real cornfield with real people working — powerful evidence of
what farming looked like — but it's from 1900, centuries after the
Ancestral Puebloan villages, so it shows a *descendant* tradition, not the
original moment.

**Claim:** "No single source tells the whole story: the oral account gives
us knowledge and meaning, the artifact proves the practice, the map gives
locations from one perspective, and the photograph shows a later moment.
Together, they let us build a fuller picture — and each one's limits keep
us honest." That is C3 D2.His.9.3-5 and D2.His.10.3-5: summarizing how
different source kinds are used, and comparing what different sources tell
us.

### Worked example 2: Inferring audience and purpose from inside the source

**Question:** Read this museum exhibit label (original, composed for this
lesson — not a historical text):

> *"Look closely at this clay pot. Its maker pressed corn-cob patterns into
> the wet clay 800 years ago. Imagine her hands — quick, practiced — shaping
> a jar that would hold her family's corn through the winter. What would you
> want to ask her?"*

Who is the audience, and what is the purpose? Find your clues *inside the
label*.

**Reasoning:** *Clues:* "Look closely" and "Imagine" and "What would you
want to ask her?" — the label talks directly to *you*, the visitor, in
friendly second person. It doesn't use hard academic words; it tells a
little story ("her hands — quick, practiced"). A label written for
scholars would cite dates, sites, and debates. This one invites curiosity.
*Audience:* museum visitors, including kids and families. *Purpose:* to
teach and to spark wonder — to make you care about the maker as a real
person, not just to list facts.

**Claim:** "The label's audience is general museum visitors (including
children) and its purpose is to teach through curiosity: the direct
address ('Look closely,' 'What would you want to ask her?'), the simple
storytelling, and the invitation to imagine all point to teaching and
wonder rather than scholarly argument." Every reason comes from words
*inside* the source — that is the skill.

### A note on data as a source

Numbers are sources too. In Week 6 S3 you will use `resources/us_states.csv`
as a dataset — a source made by the unit's authors from public records.
Like every source, it has limits: its populations and areas are **rounded
approximations**, and it describes the present, not the past. You will
check its arithmetic yourself (population ÷ area = density) before you
trust it. Checking a source's math is source criticism too.

## Guided practice (S1 close / S2 opening)

Do these together:

1. For each of the 4 source kinds, name one thing it CAN tell us and one
   thing it CANNOT. (Make a two-column chart.)
2. Your adult shows you a source about an Indigenous nation. Together,
   answer: who made it? When? Who was it made for (audience)? Why (purpose)?
   Which clues inside the source told you?
3. Fact or opinion? Label each: (a) "The Grand Canyon is one mile deep."
   (b) "The Grand Canyon is the most beautiful place on Earth." (c) "Many
   visitors come to see the Grand Canyon each year." Explain each label.
4. A photograph shows a 1900s potlatch ceremony. List two things it proves
   and two things it can't tell you.

## Independent practice (S2 — 12 written tasks; adult reviews the same day)

1. Define **source**, **audience**, and **purpose** in your own words.
2. Name the 4 source kinds and write one sentence about what each is.
3. Oral accounts: explain two strengths and one limit of learning history
   from spoken stories.
4. Artifacts: a clay pot is found in a desert village. Write two things it
   can tell us and two things it cannot. (No guessing about its owner —
   what does the *object itself* prove?)
5. Maps: why should you always ask "whose map is this?" Give one example
   of something a European explorer's map might leave out.
6. Photographs: explain why a photograph is *not* the whole truth about an
   event, using "before and after" in your answer.
7. Read the museum label in Worked example 2 again. Find two more in-source
   clues about its audience or purpose that the example didn't mention.
8. Fact vs. opinion: label each and explain: (a) "Corn, beans, and squash
   are called the Three Sisters." (b) "The Three Sisters is the smartest
   way anyone ever farmed." (c) "Beans add nitrogen to the soil."
9. A website says: "This is the BEST video about totem poles EVER — you
   HAVE to watch it!!" Is that sentence fact or opinion? Should the
   ALL-CAPS excitement make you trust the video more or less? Why?
10. Two sources disagree: an elder's account says a village moved because
    the river changed course; an old map shows the village in the same spot
    for 100 years. List two possible explanations for the disagreement
    (neither source has to be lying).
11. Why do historians use *multiple* sources instead of trusting one?
    Connect your answer to the words "origin, structure, and context."
12. Data check preview: the CSV says Texas has `population_approx`
    29,145,000 and `area_sq_mi` 268,596. Estimate: is the listed density of
    108.5 people per square mile plausible? Show a rough division check.
    (Full dataset task: Week 6 S3.)

## Applied task (S2 close)

**Source detective:** Your adult gives you two sources about the same
nation (from the pre-approved list). For each, fill a source card: kind /
maker / date / audience (with in-source clue) / purpose (with in-source
clue) / one thing it proves / one thing it can't tell. Then write 3
sentences comparing what the two sources tell you — where they agree,
where they differ, and which question you would still need a third source
to answer.

## Exit check (S2 close)

1. Name the 4 source kinds and one limit of each (30-second oral quiz or
   written).
2. In one sentence: how do you infer a source's audience and purpose?

## The dataset task (Week 6 S3)

Using `resources/us_states.csv`, compare **Texas** and **Rhode Island**:

1. Find each state's row. Write its `name_common`, `capital`,
   `subregion`, `population_approx`, `area_sq_mi`, and
   `population_density_per_sq_mi`.
2. **Check the math:** compute each state's density yourself —
   `population_approx ÷ area_sq_mi` — and confirm it matches the CSV's
   `population_density_per_sq_mi` (round to one decimal). Show the division.
3. How many Rhode Islands (by area) would fit inside Texas? Divide and
   round to a whole number.
4. **Write an evidence claim** (3–4 sentences): which state has more people
   per square mile, and what does that suggest about how people are settled
   in each state? Use the numbers as evidence. (C3 D2.Geo.6.3-5:
   environmental and cultural characteristics shape population
   distribution.)

Remember: the CSV's figures are rounded approximations — say "about" or
"approximately" in your claim, the way geographers do.

## Supports and extension

- **Supports:** Source-kind cards with icons; fact/opinion sorting cards;
  the adult reads sources aloud; the dataset task may use a calculator for
  division (the skill assessed is setting up the comparison and writing
  the claim, not long division); sentence starters for source cards.
- **Extension:** Find one more source kind not covered here (e.g., a
  song, a building, a letter). Make a source card for it and explain what
  it can and cannot tell us.

## Teacher note — common misconceptions

- Learners treat photographs as complete truth. The "before and after"
  question (Independent practice #6) is the permanent antidote.
- Learners confuse "opinion" with "lie." Re-teach: opinions can be honest
  and still unprovable; the skill is knowing which kind of sentence you're
  reading.
- Learners infer purpose from the *topic* ("it's about history, so its
  purpose is teaching history") instead of from *in-source clues*. Insist:
  point to the words.
