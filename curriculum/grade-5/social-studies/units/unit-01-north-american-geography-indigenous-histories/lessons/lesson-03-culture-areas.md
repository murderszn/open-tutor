## The big idea: the land sets the table, people choose the meal

Long before European explorers arrived, hundreds of distinct Indigenous
nations lived across North America — each with its own language, stories,
and government. Geographers group them into **8 culture areas** — not
because the nations were the same (they were wonderfully different), but
because nations living in similar environments faced similar challenges and
often found similar solutions.

The pattern to watch in every area: **environment → food, shelter, travel**,
plus **adapt** (changing how you live) and **modify** (changing the land
itself). People were never just victims of their environment — they were
engineers of it.

### The 8 culture areas

**1. Arctic** (far north — present-day Alaska and northern Canada). A frozen
world: long dark winters, short summers, ice and sea. Nations like the Inuit
and Yupik **adapted** by hunting seals, whales, and caribou — animals that
gave food, warm clothing, oil for lamps, and bones for tools. They built
igluit (snow houses) and skin-covered boats: kayaks for one hunter, larger
umiaks for families. Nothing was wasted; in a land where a mistake could
mean freezing, every skill mattered.

**2. Northwest Coast** (coastal Washington, Oregon, British Columbia,
southeast Alaska). Mild, rainy, forested — and the ocean and rivers were
full of salmon. Nations like the Haida, Tlingit, and Chinook became expert
fishermen and navigators. Giant cedar trees gave them dugout canoes, plank
houses, boxes, and clothing. They carved **totem poles** — tall cedar poles
whose stacked animal and human figures record a family's history, clan
crests, and ancestors. (A totem pole is a family record, not an object of
worship.) The rich environment supported large, settled villages.

**3. Plateau** (inland Northwest — rivers cutting through high country).
Salmon ran the rivers; roots, berries, and game filled the hills. Nations
like the Nez Perce (Nimiipuu) moved with the seasons — fishing camps in
salmon season, root-gathering grounds in spring — and lived in earth-covered
houses in winter villages. Rivers were the highways.

**4. Great Plains** (the flat grasslands from Canada to Texas). Ocean-sized
fields of grass fed enormous herds of American bison (buffalo). Nations
like the Lakota, Cheyenne, and Comanche **adapted** by becoming skilled
hunters who used every part of the bison: meat for food, hides for clothing
and teepee covers, bones for tools, sinew for thread. Teepees — cone-shaped
tents of poles and hides — could be packed and moved to follow the herds.
(Tipis came into their fullest use after horses arrived with Europeans,
which changed Plains life enormously — an example of how cultures change
over time.)

**5. Eastern Woodlands** (forests from the Atlantic coast to the Great
Lakes). Rich forests, rivers, lakes, and good soil. Nations like the
Haudenosaunee farmed the **Three Sisters** — corn, beans, and squash
planted together so each plant helps the others: corn stalks hold up bean
vines, beans feed the soil, squash leaves shade the ground and hold
moisture. They built **longhouses** — huge bark-covered houses, sometimes
200 feet long, where several related families lived around shared fires —
and traveled forest trails and rivers in birchbark canoes.

**6. Southeast** (warm lands from the Carolinas to the Gulf Coast). Long,
hot growing seasons. Nations like the Cherokee and the Creek (Muscogee)
farmed corn, beans, and squash, hunted deer and turkey, and fished the
rivers. Earlier, Mississippian peoples built great earthen mounds and towns
— places like Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, were among the largest
cities in the world in their time. Warmth meant farming almost year-round.

**7. Southwest** (deserts of present-day Arizona and New Mexico). Hot, dry,
with little rain. Ancestral Puebloans and later Pueblo peoples, the Hopi,
and the Diné (Navajo) **modified** the desert: they built terraces, small
rock check dams, and canals to catch and carry scarce water to corn, beans,
and squash fields. They built thick-walled adobe (sun-dried clay brick)
homes and cliff dwellings that stayed cool in summer heat — architecture as
adaptation. Pottery and weaving turned desert materials into art.

**8. California** (mild coastal and valley lands). Perhaps North America's
mildest climate — no freezing winters, ocean and rivers nearby. Nations like
the Chumash and Pomo gathered acorns (a staple food, carefully prepared),
fished, and hunted. The Chumash built *tomol* — remarkable plank boats
sewn together with cord — for ocean travel and trade. Basket weavers made
some of the finest baskets in the world, so tightly woven they held water.

### Worked example 1: From environment to dinner (Eastern Woodlands)

**Question:** Why did the Haudenosaunee farm instead of only hunting?

**Reasoning:** *Environment:* the Eastern Woodlands offered deep forest
soil, reliable summer rain, and a growing season long enough for corn.
*Food:* farming the Three Sisters gave dependable harvests that could feed
large villages through winter — hunting alone couldn't do that reliably.
*Shelter:* dependable food meant people could live year-round in one place,
so they built permanent longhouses instead of portable tents. *Modify:*
they cleared fields and planted companion crops — they changed the forest
into farmland.

**Claim:** "The Eastern Woodlands environment made farming possible and
worthwhile: good soil and summer rain supported the Three Sisters, steady
harvests supported permanent longhouse villages, and people modified the
forest by clearing fields." **Evidence:** forest soils + summer rainfall on
the environment map; longhouse descriptions (bark houses holding many
families); Three Sisters planting (each crop helping the others).

### Worked example 2: Same challenge, different answer (Arctic vs. Southwest)

**Question:** Both the Arctic and the Southwest are harsh environments. How
did people adapt differently?

**Reasoning:** The harshness was opposite: the Arctic's problem was cold
and a short growing season — farming was impossible, so nations like the
Inuit perfected hunting sea mammals and caribou and built insulated snow
and skin shelters. The Southwest's problem was heat and almost no water —
so Ancestral Puebloans and Pueblo peoples engineered water: check dams,
terraces, and canals that carried rain and stream water to fields, plus
thick adobe walls against the heat.

**Claim:** "Harsh environments produce different adaptations: Arctic
nations adapted by mastering hunting and insulated shelter because farming
was impossible, while Southwest nations modified the desert with water
engineering because farming was possible *if* they could capture water."
**Evidence:** Arctic ice/cold on the climate map + kayak/umiak and iglu
descriptions; Southwest aridity + NPS-documented check dams, terraces, and
canals. Same human cleverness — opposite solutions, because the land was
opposite.

## Guided practice (S1 close / S2 opening)

Do these together:

1. Point to each of the 8 culture areas on the environment map. For two of
   them, name the key environmental challenge (too cold? too dry? too
   wet?).
2. Why did Plains nations build teepees instead of longhouses? Give the
   environment reason AND the way-of-life reason.
3. The Three Sisters: name the three crops and explain how each one helps
   the other two grow.
4. A learner says, "Desert people must have had an easy life because it's
   always warm." Use the Southwest section to agree or disagree with
   evidence.

## Independent practice (S2 — 12 written tasks; adult reviews the same day)

1. List the 8 culture areas in your own words (a phrase describing each
   one's environment is enough).
2. Arctic: name two animals hunted and explain what *two different* uses
   people found for each animal.
3. Northwest Coast: what is a totem pole, and what do its carved figures
   represent? What is a totem pole NOT?
4. Plateau: why were rivers called "the highways" of this culture area?
   Name two things rivers provided.
5. Great Plains: explain how the bison shaped *three* parts of life — food,
   shelter, and one more (clothing, tools, or travel).
6. Eastern Woodlands: draw and label a longhouse cross-section (or describe
   it in 3 sentences): what was it made of, who lived in it, and why did
   its size make sense for farming villages?
7. Southeast: how did the long, warm growing season shape daily life?
   Contrast with the Arctic's short summer in two sentences.
8. Southwest: name two ways people **modified** the desert to farm, and one
   way they **adapted** their homes to the heat.
9. California: what is a *tomol*, and what does it tell you about Chumash
   travel and trade?
10. Choose any two culture areas. For each, write one sentence explaining
    how the environment shaped shelter. Then write one sentence comparing
    the two.
11. Cahokia (Southeast/Mississippian) was one of the largest cities in the
    world in its time. What does a big city need (food, organization,
    building materials)? How did the Southeast environment provide those
    things?
12. Write an evidence claim: "Culture shaped how people modified their
    environments — not just the other way around." Support it with one
    example of people *changing* the land (not just living on it).

## Applied task (S2 close)

**Culture-area match challenge:** The adult reads 6 environment clues (e.g.,
"frozen sea, long winter night," "hot desert, little rain, corn fields with
canals," "rainy forest, salmon rivers, cedar trees"). For each, you name the
culture area, name one nation from it, and explain the environment → way of
life link in one sentence. Then flip it: you invent 2 clues and the adult
guesses.

## Teacher note — common misconceptions

- Learners picture all Indigenous peoples as Plains teepee-dwellers (the
  most-filmed image). The 8-area structure is the cure: keep asking "which
  area, and what does its land give?"
- Learners think "adapt" means "give up." Re-teach: adapting is engineering
  — check dams and kayaks are technology, as clever as anything modern.
- Totem poles are frequently misdescribed as gods or idols. Correct firmly
  and simply: family histories carved in cedar, belonging to clans.
