# Lesson 01 — How Geographers Read Maps

**Unit:** [U01 — North American Geography, Indigenous Histories, and Sources](../README.md) · **Week 3**
**Time:** three ~40-minute sessions (S1 launch, S2 practice, S3 investigation) + Week 3 S4 review.
**Student goal:** I can read a map like a geographer — using the title, key,
compass rose, scale bar, grid, and symbols — and make a claim about a place
that I can prove with a specific map feature.

**Materials:** a physical or printed political map of North America with a
key/legend, compass rose, scale bar, and grid (the adult prints or projects
one; see the [Resource Pack](../resource-pack.md) for verified map sources);
ruler; pencil; blank paper.

## Readiness check (S1 opening, ~5 minutes)

Answer aloud or on paper — this checks grade-4 map skills:

1. Point north on your map. How do you know that's north?
2. What does the key (legend) tell you?
3. Name the two countries that border the United States.

If the learner struggles with cardinal directions or the key, the adult
re-teaches those two items with the printed map before continuing (the map
in this lesson re-teaches scale and grid; the basics must be quick and
secure).

## The big idea: maps are evidence, not just pictures

A geographer is a person who studies places — where things are, and why
they are there. Geographers don't just *look* at maps. They *read* them,
the way you read a book. And just like a book, a map has parts that work
together to give you the story.

Think of a map as a witness in a courtroom. A witness says, "This is what I
saw." A map says, "This is what is there." But you should never take a
witness at their word without asking questions: *Who are you? What exactly
did you see? How do you know?* Reading a map means asking the map those
questions — and the map answers through its **tools**:

- **Title** — tells you what the map is about and what question it answers.
  "Rivers of North America" and "State Capitals of the United States" are
  two different maps that answer two different questions.
- **Key (legend)** — the box that explains what every symbol and color
  means. Without the key, a red dot could be a city, a volcano, or a
  campsite. With the key, you know.
- **Compass rose** — the symbol showing direction: north, south, east, west
  (and sometimes NE, NW, SE, SW). It tells you which way is which on the
  page.
- **Scale bar** — a marked line showing what a length on the map equals in
  real miles or kilometers. It lets you measure real distances.
- **Grid** — lettered columns and numbered rows across the map, like a game
  board (A1, B3, C2…). It lets you find places exactly and tell someone else
  where to look.
- **Symbols** — the small pictures, dots, lines, and colors that stand for
  real things: cities, rivers, mountains, borders, capitals.

When you put these together, you can do what geographers do: make an
**evidence claim** — a statement about a place that you can prove by pointing
to a specific feature on the map. "Denver is in the western part of the
United States" is not a claim you remember from a book — it is a claim you
can *show*: the title says it's a U.S. map, the symbol for Denver sits in
the western half, the compass rose tells you which side is west.

### Worked example 1: Reading every tool on one map

*(The adult reads this aloud with the learner, pointing at each tool on the
printed North America map.)*

Look at our map. First, the **title**: "North America — Countries and
Capitals." That tells me this map answers the question "Which countries are
in North America, and what are their capitals?" It will NOT tell me where
the rivers are — that would be a different map.

Next, the **key**. I see a star symbol next to the words "national capital"
and a dot next to "city." So when I see a star on Mexico, that star means
"this is Mexico's capital." When I see a dot, that's just a city, not a
capital. Without the key I might have guessed the star meant "important
place" — the key stops me from guessing.

Now the **compass rose** in the corner. The N arrow points toward the top of
the page. That means up is north, down is south, right is east, left is
west. So Canada is *north of* the United States, and Mexico is *south of*
the United States. I know that from the compass rose, not from memory.

The **scale bar** says a segment of this length = 500 miles. If I lay my
ruler between Washington, D.C. and Mexico City and it covers about 4 scale
segments, the real distance is about 4 × 500 = 2,000 miles. (Roughly —
maps are for estimating, and geographers say "about.")

The **grid**: the map has columns A–E across the top and rows 1–4 down the
side. Mexico City sits in square C3. If I tell you "look at C3," you can
find it without searching the whole map.

Finally, **symbols**: I see a blue wavy line running through the middle of
the United States. The key says blue wavy lines are major rivers. That's
the Mississippi River. I didn't need to memorize it — the key told me.

**Claim:** "Mexico's capital is farther south than the capital of the
United States, and the Mississippi River runs through the middle of the
United States." **Evidence:** the star symbol on Mexico City (key:
national capital) sits below — south of, per the compass rose — the star
on Washington, D.C.; the blue wavy line (key: major river) labeled
Mississippi crosses the central U.S. Every part of that claim points at a
map feature.

### Worked example 2: Two maps, two scales, one question

*(The adult shows a second map: a close-up state map of, say, Illinois —
different scale, same tools.)*

Here is a map titled "Illinois — Cities and Rivers." Its scale bar says one
segment = 50 miles. On the North America map, one segment was 500 miles.
Same tool, different scale — because the Illinois map is zoomed in, it
shows a smaller area with more detail. **That is what scale means:** the
relationship between the map and the real world. A *large-scale* map
(Illinois) shows a small area in great detail; a *small-scale* map (North
America) shows a huge area with less detail. It sounds backwards, but think
of it this way: the large-scale map shows things *larger*.

Now a question both maps can answer, but differently: "Is Chicago near a
Great Lake?" On the North America map, I see a dot for Chicago (key: city)
beside a big blue shape labeled Lake Michigan — yes, near. On the Illinois
map, I can measure: the scale bar says 50 miles per segment, and Chicago
sits right on the lake's shore — less than half a segment away, so just a
few miles. The small-scale map gave me the *relationship* (Chicago is by a
Great Lake); the large-scale map gave me the *precision* (right on the
shore).

**Claim:** "Maps of different scales answer different questions about the
same place." **Evidence:** both maps show Chicago; the North America map
(scale: 500 miles per segment) shows its region, while the Illinois map
(scale: 50 miles per segment) shows its exact position on Lake Michigan's
shore. That is C3 D2.Geo.3.3-5 in action: using maps of different scales
to describe locations.

## Guided practice (S1 close / S2 opening)

Do these together, talking through each step:

1. Read your North America map's title. What question does this map answer?
   What question does it NOT answer?
2. Find the compass rose. Which direction is Alaska from Texas — north,
   south, east, or west? Which map tool told you?
3. The key shows a triangle symbol for "mountain peak." Find one triangle in
   the western United States. What does it tell you — and what does it NOT
   tell you (elevation? name?)?
4. Using the scale bar, estimate the distance between any two capitals on
   your map. Show your multiplication.

## Independent practice (S2 — 12 written tasks; adult reviews the same day)

Use your North America map for all items. For every claim, name the map
tool that proves it.

1. Write the map's title. In one sentence, explain what question the map
   answers.
2. List the six map tools and write what each one tells you.
3. Find the grid square containing Ottawa, Canada. Write the square (like
   B2). Then write how the grid helps someone find it.
4. Name the ocean east of the United States and the ocean west of it. Which
   tool helped you know east from west?
5. Find the Mississippi River using the key's river symbol. Name two states
   it touches or borders.
6. Estimate the distance from Denver, Colorado to Dallas, Texas using the
   scale bar. Show your work (segments × miles per segment).
7. A friend says, "Cuba is north of Florida." Use the map to agree or
   disagree, and cite the tool that proves it.
8. Your map shows a star on Washington, D.C. What does the star mean? How do
   you know — did you guess, or did a tool tell you?
9. Find the Great Lakes using the key. Name all five lakes the map shows
   (hint: the acronym HOMES — Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, Superior).
10. Write one evidence claim about where the Rocky Mountains are, citing a
    map symbol and the compass rose.
11. Look at the scale bar on your North America map and the scale bar on the
    state close-up map. Which map is large-scale, and how do you know?
12. A map titled "Population of North American Cities" shows bigger dots for
    bigger cities. Your friend says the biggest dot must be the capital. Use
    the key to explain why that reasoning is wrong.

## Applied task (S3 investigation)

**Find two cities with a scale bar and a grid — then prove it.** Choose any
two state capitals on your map. (1) Write each city's grid square. (2)
Measure the distance between them with the scale bar and show the
multiplication. (3) Write a two-sentence evidence claim: which city is
farther north, and how far apart are they? Point to the exact map features
that prove each sentence. Oral, drawn, or written responses are all
acceptable — the assessed skill is map reading, not essay writing.

## Exit check (S3 close)

1. Name the six map tools in 30 seconds (oral or written).
2. Make one evidence claim about any place on the map, citing one specific
   map feature.

## Supports and extension

- **Supports:** The adult may read items aloud; the learner may answer
  orally or by pointing while the adult scribes; use a magnifier or
  large-print map if needed; allow the learner to trace the scale bar with
  a finger before measuring. Color is always paired with symbols and labels
  (never color-only).
- **Extension:** Compare a satellite image of North America with the
  political map. What does the satellite image show that the political map
  hides (mountains, deserts, forests)? What does the political map show that
  the satellite image hides (borders, capitals, names)? Write two evidence
  claims, one from each. (See the [Resource Pack](../resource-pack.md) for
  a satellite-image source.)

## Teacher note — common misconceptions

- Learners often read the biggest symbol as the most important place
  (Independent practice #12 targets this). Re-teach: the key defines every
  symbol; size on a thematic map encodes the theme (population), not
  importance.
- Learners confuse "large-scale map" with "map of a large area." Use the
  zoom analogy: zooming *in* makes things larger on screen — that is the
  large-scale map.
- Learners estimate distances without the scale bar ("it looks close").
  Insist on the multiplication every time in S2–S3; the habit is the skill.
