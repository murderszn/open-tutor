# Lesson 02 — North American Regions

**Unit:** [U01 — North American Geography, Indigenous Histories, and Sources](../README.md) · **Week 4**
**Time:** three ~40-minute sessions (S1 launch, S2 practice, S3 region
profile) + Week 4 S4 review.
**Student goal:** I can explain how landforms, climate, and water create

## The big idea: regions are nature's neighborhoods

A **region** is an area where places share characteristics — the same way a
neighborhood is a group of houses that share a street, a region is a group
of places that share landforms, climate, or ways of life. North America has
several great regions, and they are different for reasons you can explain —
not just describe.

Three forces shape every region's environment:

1. **Landforms** — the natural shapes of the land. The Rocky Mountains run
   like a spine down western North America; the Great Plains spread flat and
   wide in the middle; the Appalachian Mountains wrinkle the east. Mountains
   block rain (one side gets storms, the other stays dry), lift air into cold
   heights, and decide where rivers begin.
2. **Climate** — the usual weather pattern over many years. The far north is
   cold because it gets less direct sunlight; the south is warm because it
   gets more. Distance from oceans matters too: coastal places get steady,
   mild weather and rain, while places deep inside the continent swing
   between hot summers and freezing winters.
3. **Water** — oceans, the Great Lakes, and great rivers like the
   Mississippi and the Colorado. Water gives rain, gives transportation, and
   decides where people can farm and build cities.

**Why do environmental characteristics vary among regions?** (C3 D2.Geo.10.3-5)
Because these three forces combine differently in different places. The
Pacific Northwest is rainy because ocean winds hit coastal mountains and
drop their moisture. The Southwest is dry because those same winds have
already lost their moisture crossing mountains, and the region sits far
from eastern rain sources. The Midwest is flat and fertile because ancient
glaciers ground rock into deep soil and left the Great Lakes behind. Same
continent, different combinations — different regions.

### The unit's region model: 4 regions

Geographers group the United States in different ways. Some textbooks use
**5 regions** (Northeast, Southeast, Midwest, Southwest, West & Pacific).
But the dataset this unit works with — `resources/us_states.csv` — sorts
every state into **4 Census-style regions** in its `subregion` column:

| Our region (CSV `subregion` value) | Includes (examples) |
|---|---|
| **United States Northeast** | Maine, New York, Pennsylvania, Vermont |
| **United States Midwest** | Ohio, Michigan, Illinois, Kansas, Minnesota, Missouri |
| **United States South** | Texas, Oklahoma, Florida, Georgia, Alabama, Louisiana, Kentucky, Delaware |
| **United States West** | Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, California, Oregon, Washington, Montana, Nevada, Utah, Idaho, Hawaii, Alaska, Wyoming |

**This unit uses the 4-region model as its one official model**, because
every dataset task and sorting exercise reads the CSV's `subregion` column
directly. The legacy fifty-states assignment uses the 5-region model; when
it appears in Week 4 S3, the teacher guide maps the models (5-region
Southwest splits: Texas and Oklahoma → South; Arizona and New Mexico → West).

Each region has a character you can describe from a relief map:

- **Northeast:** Appalachian Mountains, rocky coasts (Maine's lobster
  shores), four clear seasons, dense cities along the Atlantic.
- **Midwest:** flat to rolling plains, the Great Lakes (HOMES), deep fertile
  soil — "America's Breadbasket," where wheat, corn, and soybeans grow.
- **South:** warm climate, long growing season, the Mississippi River system,
  Gulf and Atlantic coasts, the Rio Grande along the southern border.
- **West:** the Rockies and other ranges, deserts (the Southwest's saguaro
  country), the Grand Canyon carved by the Colorado River, Pacific coastline,
  plus Alaska's far north and Hawaii's volcanic islands.

### Worked example 1: Explaining a regional difference with evidence

**Question:** Why is the Southwest hot and dry while the Pacific Northwest
is cool and rainy — even though both are in the West?

**Reasoning:** Start with the three forces. *Landforms:* the Pacific
Northwest sits where ocean winds slam into coastal mountains (the Cascades);
the air rises, cools, and drops rain — lots of it. The Southwest sits
farther inland behind mountain ranges that already stole the moisture, so
the air arrives dry. *Climate:* the Northwest's ocean keeps temperatures
mild; the Southwest's interior position and southerly latitude bake it hot.
*Water:* the Northwest has the Pacific and huge rivers like the Columbia;
the Southwest has the Colorado River cutting the Grand Canyon through
desert.

**Claim:** "The Pacific Northwest and the Southwest are both in the West,
but their environments are opposites because of landforms and distance from
water: coastal mountains squeeze rain out of ocean winds in the Northwest,
while interior mountains leave the Southwest in a dry rain shadow."
**Evidence:** relief map (mountain ranges along the coast vs. interior),
the Colorado River and Grand Canyon in Arizona (desert), the rainy green
coast of Washington and Oregon on the climate map.

### Worked example 2: Sorting a state with the dataset

**Question:** Which region does Oklahoma belong to, and how do you know?

**Reasoning:** Open `us_states.csv` and find the Oklahoma row. Read the
`subregion` column: it says `United States South`. Check the map: Oklahoma
sits south of Kansas, west of Arkansas — plains grading into warmer
country, in the southern half of the continent per the compass rose. The
CSV and the map agree.

**Claim:** "Oklahoma belongs to the South." **Evidence:** the CSV's
`subregion` column for Oklahoma reads `United States South`; the map shows
it in the southern plains, south of the Midwest's Kansas. Two sources, one
answer — that is how geographers build confidence.

Note for the 5-region model: many textbooks put Oklahoma in the
"Southwest." That is not wrong — it is a different grouping system. In
*this* unit we always say which model we are using, and our official model
is the CSV's 4-region one.

## Guided practice (S1 close / S2 opening)

Do these together:

1. On the relief map, trace the Rocky Mountains with a finger. Name two
   states they run through. How do mountains change the climate on either
   side?
2. Find Texas in the CSV. Read its `subregion` aloud. In the 5-region
   model, Texas is "Southwest" — in our 4-region model, where does it go?
   Why might two models disagree?
3. The Midwest is called "America's Breadbasket." Using the three forces
   (landforms, climate, water), explain why so much food grows there.
4. Which region would you expect to have the most people per square mile —
   the Northeast or the West? Give one environmental reason and one human
   reason (cities, jobs), then check `population_density_per_sq_mi` for New
   York (370.3) vs. Wyoming (5.9) in the CSV. Does the data match your
   reasoning?

## Independent practice (S2 — 12 written tasks; adult reviews the same day)

Use the relief map, the climate map, and `resources/us_states.csv`
(`subregion` column) for every item.

1. In your own words, explain what a region is. Give one example.
2. Name the three forces that shape a region's environment, and write one
   sentence about how each one works.
3. Explain why the Southwest is dry and the Southeast is rainy, using
   landforms and distance from water. (Two–three sentences.)
4. Sort these states into our 4 regions using the CSV `subregion` column:
   Maine, Ohio, Georgia, Arizona. Write each state with its region.
5. Sort these states: Vermont, Kansas, Louisiana, Nevada. Write each state
   with its region and one map observation that fits (a mountain, river,
   coastline, or plain).
6. A classmate says, "Hawaii can't be in the West — it's in the middle of
   the ocean!" Using the CSV and the idea that regions group by shared
   characteristics, explain why Hawaii is grouped with the West.
7. Name the five Great Lakes (HOMES). Which of our 4 regions touches the
   most Great Lakes? How does lake water shape that region's environment
   and economy?
8. The Rio Grande forms the border between Texas and Mexico. Which region
   is Texas in (CSV)? What does a border river give the places along it —
   name two things (water, transportation, trade…)?
9. Four Corners is the only point where four states meet — Arizona, New
   Mexico, Utah, and Colorado. Which of our 4 regions are all four states
   in? What does that tell you about how the West's borders were drawn?
10. Santa Fe, New Mexico was founded in 1610 — the oldest state capital
    city in the United States. Which region is New Mexico in? Why would a
    capital be founded in the desert Southwest — what did the Spanish need
    there (think: water, routes, existing peoples)?
11. Write an evidence claim: "The Midwest's environment made it a farming
    region." Cite one landform, one climate fact, and one water feature.
12. A textbook uses the 5-region model and puts New Mexico in the
    "Southwest." Our CSV puts it in the West. Explain to a friend why both
    answers can be reasonable — and which one this unit uses.

## Applied task (S3 — region profile)

**Build a region profile sheet for all 4 regions.** For each region write:
(1) the region name; (2) two landforms or water features found there;
(3) a one-sentence climate description; (4) one landmark or fact (e.g., the
Grand Canyon in the West, the HOMES lakes in the Midwest); (5) three states
in the region with their capitals, verified in the CSV (`capital` column).
Then begin the kept `fifty-states-and-capitals-challenge.md` assignment
(challenge log + state-spotlight script), noting on the log which region
model that assignment uses vs. which model this unit uses.

## Exit check (S3 close)

1. Name the 4 regions of our model.
2. Explain in one sentence why environmental characteristics vary among
   regions.

## Teacher note — common misconceptions

- Learners treat regions as natural facts rather than human groupings.
  Re-teach: regions are *tools geographers choose*; the 4-vs-5-model
  discussion (Independent practice #12, Week 4 S4) is the cure.
- Learners sort by "where it feels like it goes" (Texas = Southwest because
  of cowboy movies). Insist on the CSV column + one map observation, every
  time.
- "Climate" vs. "weather" confusion is chronic: climate = the pattern over
  many years; weather = today. Correct gently each time.
