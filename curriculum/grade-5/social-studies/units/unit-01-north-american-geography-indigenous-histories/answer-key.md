## Lesson 01 — Map tools

**Readiness:** 1. North is found with the compass rose (N arrow). 2. The key
explains what each symbol/color means. 3. Canada and Mexico.

**Guided:** 1. Accept any accurate reading, e.g. "Which countries are in
North America and what are their capitals?" It does not answer river,
population, or climate questions. 2. Alaska is north of Texas (compass
rose). 3. A triangle marks a mountain peak's location; it does not give its
name or elevation. 4. Accept any two capitals with segments × miles shown,
e.g. 3 segments × 500 mi = about 1,500 miles.

**Independent:** 1–2. Title = what question the map answers; key = symbol
meanings; compass rose = direction; scale bar = map distance → real
distance; grid = exact finding; symbols = stand-ins for real things.
3. Method: read the column letter + row number where Ottawa sits; the grid
lets anyone find the same square without searching. (Square depends on the
printed map — check the method, not a fixed answer.) 4. Atlantic (east),
Pacific (west); the compass rose proves east from west. 5. Any two of:
Minnesota, Wisconsin, Iowa, Illinois, Missouri, Kentucky, Tennessee,
Arkansas, Mississippi, Louisiana. 6. Model: about 1.3 segments × 500 ≈ 650
miles; accept 500–800 with work shown. 7. Disagree — Cuba is south of
Florida (compass rose; Cuba sits below Florida on the map). 8. The star
means "national capital" because the key says so — a tool told you, not a
guess. 9. Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, Superior (HOMES). 10. Model:
"The Rocky Mountains run through the western United States" — evidence:
triangle/mountain symbols along the western states + compass rose showing
that side is west. 11. The state map (50 mi/segment) is large-scale: same
page shows a smaller area in more detail. 12. The key defines the dot-size
rule as population, not capital status — the biggest dot is the biggest
city by the theme; capitals have their own symbol (star).

**Applied task:** Check: two capitals named, grid squares read by the
method, distance = segments × scale with work shown, two-sentence claim
with each sentence pointing at a feature (compass rose for "farther
north," scale bar for distance).

**Exit:** 1. Six tools named. 2. Any claim with one cited map feature.

## Lesson 02 — Regions

**Readiness:** 1. Six tools. 2. Any evidence claim with a cited feature.
3. A region is an area grouped because places share characteristics
(landforms, climate, culture).

**Guided:** 1. Rockies run through e.g. Montana, Colorado (any two correct
states); mountains force air up (cool, wet on the windward side) and leave
a drier side behind. 2. Texas → `United States South`. Both reasonable:
"Southwest" groups by desert/western culture, "South" groups by the Census
system; this unit uses the CSV's 4-region model. 3. Landforms: flat/rolling
plains; climate: enough summer rain and a long growing season; water: the
Great Lakes and the Mississippi/Missouri system — deep glacial soil +
rain + water transport = enormous grain harvests. 4. Prediction: New York
(denser cities, Atlantic coast). CSV: NY 370.3 vs. WY 5.9 people/sq mi —
the data matches.

**Independent:** 1. Region: area grouped by shared characteristics; climate:
the usual weather pattern of a place over many years (not today's
weather). 2. Maine → Northeast; Minnesota → Midwest; Florida → South;
Oregon → West. 3. Pennsylvania → Northeast; Missouri → Midwest; Alabama →
South; Idaho → West — each with one map observation (e.g., Appalachians in
PA; plains in MO; Gulf coast in AL; Rockies in ID). 4. The Midwest touches
the most Great Lakes (Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie; Ontario touches NY
in the Northeast). Lakes give fresh water, moderate temperatures, shipping
routes, and fishing — shaping farming and industry. 5. Texas → United
States South. A border river gives fresh water, transportation/trade, and
irrigation (any two). 6. All four (AZ, NM, UT, CO) are in United States
West — western state borders were drawn along straight latitude/longitude
lines, grouping the whole interior West together. 7. New Mexico → United
States West. A desert capital needed water (the Santa Fe River area),
position on trade routes (El Camino Real), and a center for governing the
surrounding Pueblo peoples. 8. The Southwest sits inland behind mountain
ranges that strip moisture from winds (rain shadow) and far from eastern
rain; the Southeast sits near the warm Atlantic/Gulf, where moist air
brings regular rain. 9. Both reasonable — different grouping systems;
this unit uses the CSV's 4-region model (West for AZ/NM). 10. Model claim
with one landform (flat plains), one climate fact (summer rain + long
growing season), one water feature (Great Lakes / Mississippi system).
11. Utah and Colorado (both lie north of the Four Corners point; Arizona
and New Mexico lie south of it). 12. Illinois → Springfield (Midwest);
Texas → Austin (South); California → Sacramento (West).

**Applied (region profile):** Check all 4 regions have: name, 2 landforms/
water features, one-sentence climate, one landmark/fact, 3 states with
CSV-verified capitals; fifty-states challenge log started with the region
model noted.

**Exit:** 1. Northeast, Midwest, South, West. 2. Any one-sentence version
of: landforms, climate, and water combine differently in different places.

## Lesson 03 — Culture areas

**Readiness:** 1. Four regions + one environmental fact each. 2. A culture
area is a region where Indigenous nations shared similar ways of life
because of similar environments. 3. Adapt: change how you live (e.g.,
wear fur clothing); modify: change the land (e.g., build irrigation).

**Guided:** 1. Eight areas pointed out; Arctic challenge = extreme cold,
short growing season; Southwest challenge = heat + almost no water.
2. Corn, beans, squash: corn stalks support bean vines; beans feed nitrogen
to the soil; squash leaves shade the ground, holding moisture and blocking
weeds. 3. Plains nations followed bison herds, so homes had to move —
teepees pack down; Eastern Woodlands farming supported year-round villages,
so permanent longhouses made sense. 4. "Living nations" = Indigenous
nations existing today as sovereign communities; any one nation + one
present-day fact (e.g., the Diné Nation governs itself and its language is
widely spoken).

**Independent:** 1. Arctic (frozen north); Northwest Coast (rainy forested
coast); Plateau (inland river country); Great Plains (grasslands); Eastern
Woodlands (eastern forests); Southeast (warm farmlands); Southwest (desert);
California (mild coast/valleys) — accept equivalent phrases. 2. E.g.,
seals: meat/food + skins for clothing/boats; caribou: meat + hides for
clothing/shelter (any two animals, two distinct uses each). 3. Totem poles
are tall carved cedar poles whose figures record family histories, clan
crests, and ancestors; they are NOT objects of worship. 4. Rivers gave
salmon (food) and a travel route — canoes moved people and goods the way
roads do. 5. Food: bison meat; shelter: hides for teepee covers; plus one
more: bones for tools, sinew for thread, hides for clothing (any one).
6. Longhouses: wooden pole frames covered with bark (often elm), sometimes
200 ft long; housed several related families of one clan around shared
fires; large size fit farming villages that stayed in one place year-round.
7. Long growing seasons meant reliable corn/bean/squash harvests feeding
large settled towns; the Arctic's weeks-long summer meant no farming at
all — hunting was the only option. 8. Modify: terraces/check dams/canals
to catch and carry water (any two). Adapt: thick adobe walls that stay cool
in summer heat. 9. A *tomol* is a Chumash plank boat sewn together with
cord — it proves ocean travel and trade between the mainland and the
Channel Islands. 10. Any two areas with shelter linked to environment
(e.g., Arctic snow/skin shelters for cold and mobility vs. Northwest Coast
cedar plank houses for rain and settled fishing villages) + one comparison
sentence. 11. A big city needs dependable surplus food, organization, and
building material; the Southeast's long growing season gave farm surpluses,
rivers gave transport, forests gave timber — so towns like Cahokia could
grow large. 12. Any valid example of modifying the land shaped by culture:
Ancestral Puebloan check dams/terraces/canals, Eastern Woodlands field
clearing for Three Sisters planting, Northwest Coast clam gardens (any one
with the "people changed the land" reasoning).

**Image activity:** Accept any 3 observable features (evergreen forest,
rocky coastline, forested islands, snow-capped mountains, clear stream)
each with a food/shelter/travel use and reasoning (e.g., "cedar trees →
plank houses and canoes for shelter and travel"; "salmon stream → food";
"rocky coast/islands → canoe travel routes"). Final question: the rich,
reliable ocean/forest environment gave year-round food, so villages could
stay in one place instead of following herds.

**Exit:** 1. Environment → food, shelter, travel (the pattern). 2. One
adaptation (change how you live) + one modification (change the land).

## Lesson 04 — Living nations

**Readiness:** 1. Any two culture areas + one nation each. 2. Sovereign =
the right to make your own laws and govern your own community. 3. Wrong:
past tense ("lived") as if nations are gone; right: their histories do
stretch back thousands of years.

**Guided:** 1. Nations exist today as self-governing communities — e.g.,
they run governments/schools and speak their languages now. 2. A treaty is
a formal agreement between nations; only governments make treaties, so
treaties prove tribal nations are governments. 3. Model: "Some historians
argue the Haudenosaunee Confederacy influenced American democratic
thinkers, pointing to Franklin's 1751 letter and the 1988 Congressional
resolution; other historians disagree, saying the evidence shows admiration
more than direct influence." 4. Any present-day connection + the source's
kind; Indigenous-authored/tribal/museum sources matter because they carry
the nation's own voice rather than an outsider's description.

**Independent:** 1. Nation: a people with shared identity/history, and for
tribal nations the right to self-government; sovereign: self-governing;
homeland: a nation's original home territory. 2. Any three living nations
+ one present-day fact each (e.g., Diné Nation — self-government and
living language; Haudenosaunee — Grand Council at Onondaga; Pueblo peoples
— continuously inhabited villages like Taos). 3. "Tribes" alone can sound
small, past-tense, or storybook; "nations/peoples" names governments and
communities that exist now. 4. Using a nation's own name (Diné, "the
people") respects who they are instead of imposing an outsider's label.
5. Any nation: 2 sentences past + 2 sentences present + 1 continuity
sentence. 6. Two errors: "vanished" is false — the nation continues; the
past-tense framing erases living communities (accept any two valid
corrections). 7. A treaty is a formal agreement between nations; broken
treaties matter because they broke legal promises about land and rights
that nations still live with. 8. The "some historians argue" paragraph with
one evidence piece (Franklin's letter or the 1988 resolution) and one
reason for disagreement (thin evidence / admiration ≠ influence) — not
stated as fact. 9. Settler perspective: the railroad brings travel, trade,
connection (2 sentences); nation's member perspective: it cuts through the
homeland, breaks treaty promises, brings unwanted change (2 sentences) —
both sides reasoned, neither villainized. 10. Any living tradition/art/
public role + 3 sentences + named approved source.

**Exit:** 1. "…are **living, sovereign** communities with **homelands**."
2. Because historians disagree — the evidence is debated, so we report the
debate honestly instead of stating one side as fact.

## Lesson 05 — Sources

**Readiness:** 1. Any Lesson 03 fact + a guess at its source (stories,
objects, maps, photos). 2. Yes — spoken stories passed down are sources
when treated carefully. 3. Fact = checkable/provable; opinion = what
someone feels or believes.

**Guided:** 1. Two-column chart — e.g., oral accounts: can give a nation's
own stories/knowledge; can't give exact dates. Artifacts: can show
materials/skills; can't show thoughts. Maps: can show places and what the
maker valued; can't show what was left out. Photographs: can show one
moment's appearance; can't show before/after or meaning. 2. (a) Fact —
checkable. (b) Opinion — "smartest" can't be proven. 3. Maker/audience/
purpose with two in-source clues (depends on the adult-selected source —
check the method: clues quoted from inside the source). 4. Proves: what a
Pueblo cornfield and its workers looked like in 1900; can't tell: what
happened before/after, what the work meant to the people, or how fields
looked 300 years earlier.

**Independent:** 1. Source: anything giving information about the past;
audience: who it was made for; purpose: why it was made. 2. Four kinds,
one sentence each. 3. Strengths: the community's own words/knowledge;
preserves values and explanations. Limit: dates shift over generations;
needs comparison with other sources. 4. Can tell: materials available,
pottery-making skill; cannot tell: the maker's name/thoughts, exactly when
it was made, what it meant to its owner. 5. Because the maker's perspective
decides what appears — e.g., an explorer's map might show rivers and forts
but leave out long-standing Indigenous villages. 6. A photo freezes one
instant; it can't show what happened before or after, or why the
photographer chose that moment. 7. Accept any two unmentioned clues, e.g.,
"Step closer" (direct address to a visitor), "for hundreds of years"
(simple time language for general visitors), the invitation to imagine
(teaching through wonder, not scholarly citation). 8. (a) Fact — the name
is checkable. (b) Opinion — "most beautiful" can't be proven. (c) Fact —
checkable (Colorado River carved the Grand Canyon). 9. Opinion ("BEST…
EVER"); excitement should make you trust it *less* as evidence — hype is
marketing, not proof; check the video's actual content and maker instead.
10. Two explanations where neither lies: the river moved *after* the map
was drawn; the mapmaker never visited and copied an old map; "the village"
moved but kept its name; the elder's "river changed course" refers to a
different channel shift. (Any two plausible.) 11. One source has one maker
with one perspective and blind spots; comparing sources by origin (who
made it), structure (what kind it is), and context (when/why) lets you
weigh what to trust. 12. Rough check: 29,145,000 ÷ 268,596 ≈ 100–110
(e.g., 268,596 × 100 = 26,859,600, close to 29,145,000) — yes, 108.5 is
plausible.

**Dataset task (Week 6 S3):** 1. Texas: Austin, United States South,
29,145,000, 268,596 sq mi, 108.5/sq mi. Rhode Island: Providence, United
States Northeast, 1,097,000, 1,545 sq mi, 710.0/sq mi. 2. Texas:
29,145,000 ÷ 268,596 = 108.51 → 108.5 ✓ matches. Rhode Island: 1,097,000
÷ 1,545 = 710.03 → 710.0 ✓ matches. (Show the long division; calculator
allowed.) 3. 268,596 ÷ 1,545 = 173.85 → about **174** Rhode Islands fit in
Texas. 4. Model claim: "Rhode Island has far more people per square mile —
about 710 compared with about 109 in Texas, roughly six and a half times
as dense. That suggests Rhode Island's people live close together in cities
and towns across a small state, while Texas's people are spread across a
huge area with wide open spaces between cities. Both states have big
cities, but Texas's enormous land area — plains and desert in the west —
keeps its average density low." (Accept any claim with the computed
numbers, the denser-state verdict, and an environment/settlement link.)

**Exit:** 1. Four kinds + one limit each. 2. From clues inside the source
itself — its words, tone, and emphasis.

## Project rubric exemplar (second example — Diné)

A "Strong" (4) profile of the **Diné** might read: environment — high
desert of the Southwest (present-day Arizona/New Mexico), little rain, big
temperature swings; food — herded sheep and goats, farmed corn/beans/
squash where water allowed; shelter — hogans (round log-and-earth homes)
suited to the desert; travel — foot and horseback across wide distances;
adapt — sheep-herding lifeways and woven wool clothing for desert cold
nights; modify — dry-farming techniques and water-catchment to farm
without rivers; present-day — the Diné Nation is the largest tribal nation
in the U.S., with its own elected government, schools, and a living Diné
language. Sources: Diné tribal government pages + NMAI (named). Apply the
rubric: environment accuracy 4, links 4, adapt/modify 4, map 4 (tools +
annotation), evidence 4 — 20/20 when all elements are present and the
claim cites ≥3 evidence pieces from ≥2 named sources.
