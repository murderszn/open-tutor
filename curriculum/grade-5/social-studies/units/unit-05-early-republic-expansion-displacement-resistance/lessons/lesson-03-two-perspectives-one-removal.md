# Lesson 03 — Two Perspectives, One Removal: Reading Sources Like a Historian

**Goal:** I can compare two accounts of the same removal, tell what agrees
and what differs, infer audience and purpose, separate fact from opinion,
and judge how useful each account is for a question.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 21, S3, plus Week 21 S4 review).
**Materials:** the two model accounts below, the practice bank (Set C).

**Readiness check (adult asks aloud):** "In Unit 03 you asked five
questions of every source: who made it, when, for whom, for what purpose,
and what evidence does it give? Name the five questions." If this is
shaky, re-teach them for five minutes: this lesson runs entirely on that
routine.

## Teaching

**Read this first, out loud, together:** The two accounts below are
**original, invented teaching models written for this lesson. They are not
real documents from the 1830s.** No one in 1838 wrote these words. They
were written to let you practice the historian's questions on two very
different viewpoints — a Georgia newspaper editor who supported removal,
and a Cherokee writer who opposed it. Real sources are reached through the
Resource Pack, adult-selected. Whenever you discuss these accounts, practice
saying: "These are models, not real documents."

Historians compare accounts because no single account tells the whole
story. The same event can be described differently without either account
being a lie: the writers saw different things, wrote for different readers,
and wanted different results. Your job is not to pick the account you like
and call it true. Your job is to ask, for each account: who made it, when,
for whom, for what purpose — and then judge what each one is useful for.

### Account A — Model: a Georgia newspaper editor supports removal (1838)

> "At last the long trouble in our state is ending. The Cherokee have been
> given a fair bargain — new lands in the West, money for their farms, and
> the government's help on the journey. The army is only keeping order while
> families gather their things. Georgia's soil will now feed Georgia's
> farmers, and the state will grow rich and peaceful. Those who complain
> about the removal do not understand that a state must govern its own land.
> The treaty was signed, the money was paid, and the matter is settled."

### Account B — Model: a Cherokee writer opposes removal (1838)

> "Soldiers came to our houses before sunrise and ordered us out with only
> what we could carry. Our farms, our school, our printing press — all left
> behind. The paper they call a treaty was signed by men who had no right to
> sell our country, and our chief and our council said so in writing to
> Congress. We have followed the white man's laws, built our own government,
> and still they drive us out. Our old people and our children are dying on
> the road west. Remember this: we did not choose to leave."

### Worked example 1 — What agrees, what differs

*Question:* What do Accounts A and B agree on, and where do they differ?

*Reasoning, step by step:* (1) List the facts both accounts state: Cherokee
families are leaving Georgia; a treaty was signed; the government is
involved in the move. (2) List what differs: Account A calls it a "fair
bargain" and says the army is "only keeping order"; Account B says soldiers
ordered families out before sunrise and that the signers had "no right to
sell our country." (3) Notice the pattern: they agree on the skeleton
(people leaving, a treaty, government action) and disagree on the meaning
(fair bargain vs. forced removal) and on key facts (voluntary gathering vs.
soldiers at dawn). Agreement on the skeleton does not mean agreement on
the story — and the disagreement is exactly where the history lives.

### Worked example 2 — Audience, purpose, and usefulness

*Question:* For the question "Did the Cherokee agree to removal?", which
account is more useful, and why?

*Reasoning:* (1) Ask who each account is for: Account A is written for
Georgia readers who want removal to seem fair — its purpose is to persuade
them the matter is settled. Account B is written for readers who need to
know what happened to Cherokee families — its purpose is to protest and to
record. (2) For *this* question, Account B is more useful: it directly
states that the signers had no right to sell the country and that the chief
and council protested to Congress — evidence about agreement. Account A
only asserts "the treaty was signed," which does not prove the nation
agreed. (3) But Account A is still useful for a different question: "How
did Georgia supporters justify removal?" A persuasive source is not useless
— ask "useful for *which* question?" every time.

**Teacher note — common misconceptions:** (1) "One account is true and the
other is lying." Both are models; in real history, accounts can disagree
because writers saw different things or wanted different outcomes — check
each claim against other evidence. (2) "Account A is useless because it
supports removal." No — it is evidence of what removal supporters believed
and said, which is exactly what a historian needs for that question.
(3) "These accounts are real 1838 documents." No — they are invented
models for practice. Saying so every time is the habit that keeps classroom
history honest. (4) "Fact and opinion are the same thing." A fact can be
checked ("soldiers came before sunrise"); an opinion is a judgment ("a fair
bargain"). Both accounts mix them. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice

Work through these together, with the adult modeling the first answer.

1. List two facts both accounts agree on, in your own words.
2. Quote one opinion from Account A and one from Account B. How do you
   know each one is an opinion, not a fact?
3. Who is Account A's audience? Give one clue inside the text. (Hint:
   "Georgia's soil will now feed Georgia's farmers" — who benefits from
   hearing that?)
4. A learner says, "Account B is sad, so it must be true, and Account A is
   wrong." Use Worked example 2 to correct this: what is each account
   useful for, and what would you still need to check?

## Independent practice

1. For the question "What did Georgia supporters say about the treaty?",
   which account is more useful? Give one clue from the text.
2. For the question "How did Cherokee families experience the removal?",
   which account is more useful? Give one clue from the text.
3. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Account A and Account B are
   real letters from 1838, and Account A proves the removal was fair."
   Find the two errors (the accounts are invented models, not real letters;
   and one account's claim does not prove fairness — check it against other
   evidence) and rewrite the sentence correctly.
4. Explain in your own words why a historian reads *both* accounts instead
   of choosing the one they agree with. Two sentences.

## Application

Choose one question — "Was the removal fair?" or "How did the removal
happen?" — and write a four-sentence paragraph: state which account helps
more with your question and why, name one fact from the other account that
is still useful, and name one thing neither account can prove on its own
(for example, the exact number of deaths — models do not count). Honest
uncertainty earns full marks here — historians name what they do not know.

## Exit check

Without looking: (a) say in one sentence what Accounts A and B are (models,
not real documents); (b) finish this sentence: "A persuasive source is not
useless — ask useful for ______." The adult checks both against the
[answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Read both accounts aloud together, twice;
  let the learner underline facts in one color and opinions in another.
  Pair each account with a one-word label (A = "settled," B = "forced")
  and check the label against the text.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may answer the guided-practice
  questions orally with the two accounts side by side, pointing to the
  clue in the text for each answer, instead of writing.
- **Extension:** Find one real primary source about the Trail of Tears in
  the Resource Pack (adult-selected) and run the five questions on it:
  who made it, when, for whom, for what purpose, and what evidence does it
  give? Cite the source used.
