# Lesson 05 — Causes, Effects, and the Mini-Exhibit

**Goal:** I can explain probable causes and effects for one region's
transformation, and turn source evidence into a claim with reasons for my
mini-exhibit.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 23, S1–S2). **Materials:** the
[timeline diagram](../assets/early-republic-timeline.svg), the practice bank
(Set E), the learner's mini-exhibit notes from Week 22 S3.

**Readiness check (adult asks aloud):** "Name one probable cause and one
probable effect of the Erie Canal, from Lesson 04." If this is shaky,
re-teach cause-and-effect chains for five minutes: this lesson chains
everything the unit has taught.

## Teaching

Historians explain change with causes and effects — but they say
*probable* causes and *probable* effects, because history is not a
machine with one lever. The cotton gin is a good example. The machine
itself (1793) did not force anyone west. It made cotton farming profitable;
profitable farming made planters want more land; wanting more land made
them push for removal; removal pushed Indigenous nations west; and the
cotton system forced enslaved people west too. Each step is a probable
cause of the next — and each step also had other causes. The gin alone does
not explain removal; the Removal Act alone does not explain the cotton
boom. A good chain names several causes and says which ones mattered most.

Now turn that chain into a claim. A claim is a sentence you can argue
about, supported by evidence. "The Erie Canal opened in 1825" is a fact,
not a claim — no one argues about it. "The Erie Canal changed New York
more than any other single project of the early republic" is a claim: it
can be argued, and it needs evidence. Evidence is a fact from a source that
supports your claim, and a reason explains *how* the evidence supports it.
Two pieces of evidence from different sources make a claim much stronger
than one piece alone — that is the rule for your mini-exhibit.

Your mini-exhibit (see [project.md](../project.md)) is the unit's final
piece of work. You chose one place in Week 22 — a Cherokee town in Georgia,
a new Ohio farm town, a canal town, or a Mississippi cotton port — and
gathered two pieces of evidence about it. Now you finish it: a clear claim
about how expansion changed that place, two pieces of evidence with
reasons, and a drawn or written panel (or an oral exhibit) that teaches a
visitor. The exhibit's last line is a question you still have — historians
name what they do not know.

### Worked example 1 — A cause-and-effect chain for one region

*Question:* How did the cotton gin probably change the Mississippi region?

*Reasoning, step by step:* (1) Start with the event: the cotton gin
(1793) made separating cotton fiber from seeds fast and cheap. (2) Follow
one chain: cheap cotton → cotton farming profitable → planters wanted more
land → Mississippi became a state (1817) and cotton spread across it. (3)
Follow a second chain: more cotton land → more enslaved labor forced west
to work it. (4) Follow a third: more cotton → more shipping down the
Mississippi to New Orleans → New Orleans grew into a major port. One
invention, three chains — and the chains touch different people's lives.
Notice the word *probably*: the gin was one cause among several, including
the land itself and the demand from British mills.

### Worked example 2 — From evidence to a claim

*Claim:* "The Erie Canal changed Rochester from a small settlement into a
busy town because it made shipping cheap."

*Reasoning:* (1) Evidence 1 (from a map): Rochester sits on the canal
route between Buffalo and Albany — a source that shows location. (2)
Evidence 2 (from a fact in Lesson 04): canal boats cut the Buffalo–New
York trip from about three weeks to about eight days — a source that shows
speed and cost. (3) Reason linking Evidence 1: a town on a cheap, fast
route gets traders, shops, and settlers. (4) Reason linking Evidence 2:
faster, cheaper shipping is the reason traders would choose that route.
(5) Check: are the two pieces from different sources? Yes — a map and a
lesson fact. And is the claim arguable? Yes — someone could argue the
canal mattered less than the farmland around Rochester, and then you would
need more evidence. A claim that can be argued is a claim worth making.

**Teacher note — common misconceptions:** (1) "One cause explains
everything." No — big changes have several causes; the gin, the land, the
law, and the demand all mattered. (2) "A fact is a claim." A fact is not
arguable; a claim needs evidence and reasons. (3) "Two pieces of evidence
from the same source are enough." They are weaker than two from different
sources — a map and a text beat two sentences from the same page.
(4) "My exhibit has to prove I am right." No — it has to show your claim,
your evidence, and your reasons clearly, including what you are still
unsure about. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice

Work through these together, with the adult modeling the first answer.

1. Build a three-box chain for the Erie Canal: canal opens (1825) → ______
   → New York City grows. Fill the middle box and defend it with one
   reason.
2. Use Worked example 1's steps to build a two-chain cause/effect for the
   Louisiana Purchase: one chain about settlers, one chain about Indigenous
   nations.
3. A learner says, "The cotton gin caused the Trail of Tears." Is that a
   good claim? Why or why not? (Hint: the gin is one link in a long chain —
   name two other links between the gin and 1838.)
4. Turn this fact into a claim: "The Cherokee had a newspaper, a
   constitution, and a capital." Write one arguable sentence about what
   that shows, and name one piece of evidence that supports it.

## Independent practice

1. Write a probable-cause claim for the growth of St. Louis, using the
   pattern: "Because ______, St. Louis ______." (Hint: steamboats, the
   Mississippi, and the Missouri rivers.)
2. Give one reason the Louisiana Purchase alone does not explain westward
   expansion. (Hint: buying a claim is not the same as controlling land —
   what else had to happen?)
3. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The Erie Canal caused the
   cotton gin, because both happened in the early republic." Find the two
   errors (wrong order — the gin, 1793, came before the canal, 1825; and a
   later event cannot cause an earlier one) and rewrite the sentence
   correctly.
4. Draft your exhibit claim in one sentence, then list your two pieces of
   evidence underneath it, each with a one-sentence reason. Check: are the
   two pieces from different sources?

## Application

Finish the mini-exhibit panel (or oral exhibit): claim at the top, two
pieces of evidence with reasons, one drawing or map detail, and one
question you still have at the bottom. Practice presenting it in two
minutes: claim first, evidence second, question last. The adult scores it
with the project rubric.

## Exit check

Without looking: (a) name one probable cause and one probable effect of the
cotton gin; (b) finish this sentence: "A claim needs at least two pieces of
evidence from ______ sources, with reasons linking each piece to the
claim." The adult checks both against the [answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Build the cause/effect chains with cards on
  a table before writing them; let the learner move the cards and explain
  each arrow aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may present the mini-exhibit
  orally with the panel as a prop, instead of writing the full panel text.
- **Extension:** Add a critic's question to your exhibit: "What would
  someone who disagrees with my claim say, and what evidence would they
  cite?" Write one sentence answering it, and cite the source used.
