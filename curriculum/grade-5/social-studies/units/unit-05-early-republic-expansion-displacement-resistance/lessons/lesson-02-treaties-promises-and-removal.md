# Lesson 02 — Treaties, Promises, and Removal

**Goal:** I can explain what treaties promised, what the Indian Removal Act
allowed, what the Supreme Court decided, and what happened in the Cherokee
removal of 1838–1839 — and name how Indigenous nations resisted.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 21, S1–S2). **Materials:** the
[timeline diagram](../assets/early-republic-timeline.svg), the practice bank
(Set B), the Resource Pack's National Park Service Trail of Tears page
(adult-selected).

**Readiness check (adult asks aloud):** "In Unit 04 you learned that
treaties are part of the supreme law of the land. What does that mean a
treaty is supposed to do?" (It is a binding agreement between governments.)
If this is shaky, re-teach it for five minutes: this lesson turns on whether
treaties were kept.

## Teaching

A treaty is a formal, written agreement between governments. In the early
republic the United States signed hundreds of treaties with Indigenous
nations. In those treaties, nations often gave up some land in exchange for
promises: money, goods, schools, protection, and — most importantly — that
the land they kept would be theirs forever. The Cherokee, Choctaw,
Chickasaw, Muscogee (Creek), and Seminole nations in the Southeast had
signed many such treaties. By the 1820s the Cherokee had their own written
constitution, a newspaper (the *Cherokee Phoenix*, first published in
1828), schools, farms, and a capital at New Echota in Georgia. They had
done everything the United States had said would earn them respect as a
separate nation. It did not protect them.

The pressure came from land. Cotton farming, made profitable by the cotton
gin, made southeastern land valuable to white settlers, and the state of
Georgia wanted Cherokee land. In 1830 Congress passed the Indian Removal
Act, which allowed the president to trade land west of the Mississippi
River for Indigenous land in the East — and to pay for moving people west.
The law said removal was supposed to be by treaty, by agreement. In
practice, it became the legal cover for forced removal.

The Cherokee fought back in court. In 1831 the Supreme Court said it could
not hear their case (*Cherokee Nation v. Georgia*). Then in 1832, in
*Worcester v. Georgia*, the Court ruled 5–1 that Georgia's laws had no
force on Cherokee land — the Cherokee Nation was "a distinct community
occupying its own territory," and only the federal government could deal
with it. Chief Justice John Marshall wrote the decision. But President
Andrew Jackson declined to enforce the ruling, and Georgia ignored it.
Winning in court did not stop removal. That is one of the hardest lessons
in this unit: a law can be on your side and still not protect you, if the
people in power refuse to follow it.

In December 1835 a small group of about 500 Cherokee — without the approval
of the Cherokee government, and against the wishes of the Cherokee chief
John Ross and most Cherokee people — signed the Treaty of New Echota,
agreeing to give up all Cherokee land in the East and move west. The United
States used that treaty as its justification. In May 1838 U.S. soldiers
rounded up Cherokee families, held them in camps, and forced them west on a
journey of about 800 miles to Indian Territory (now Oklahoma). More than
16,000 Cherokee people were forced from their homes in Tennessee, Alabama,
North Carolina, and Georgia. About 4,000 died — from hunger, exposure, and
disease — on the journey, which ended in 1839. The Cherokee call it the
trail where they cried; in English it is known as the Trail of Tears.

The Cherokee were not the only ones. The Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee
(Creek), and Seminole were also forced west, and historians estimate that
about 60,000 people from the five southeastern nations were removed in
this era. Resistance took many forms: the Cherokee fought in court and
petitioned Congress; the Seminole fought back in Florida in the Second
Seminole War (1835–1842), one of the longest and costliest wars the United
States fought against an Indigenous nation; Tecumseh had earlier tried to
build a confederacy of nations to resist American expansion (defeated at
Tippecanoe in 1811); and Black Hawk led Sauk and Fox resistance in 1832.
Some people refused to leave and hid in the mountains — ancestors of the
Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, who live in North Carolina today.

### Worked example 1 — What a treaty promised vs. what happened

*Claim:* The Treaty of New Echota was used to justify removal, but it was
not an agreement the Cherokee Nation made.

*Reasoning, step by step:* (1) A treaty is an agreement between
governments. (2) The Cherokee government, led by Chief John Ross, opposed
removal and refused to sign. (3) About 500 Cherokee signed anyway, without
the government's approval. (4) The United States treated those signatures
as the whole nation's agreement. The reasoning shows the problem: a
signature is only an agreement if the signer has the right to agree for
the group. When the adult asks "who signed, and for whom?" — that question
decides whether a treaty was fair.

### Worked example 2 — Winning in court, losing the land

*Claim:* The Cherokee won in the Supreme Court in 1832, and still lost
their land — because a court decision only works if it is enforced.

*Reasoning:* (1) *Worcester v. Georgia* said Georgia's laws had no force
on Cherokee land. (2) The decision had to be enforced by the president.
(3) President Jackson declined to enforce it, and Georgia kept pushing
Cherokee families off their land. (4) Six years later, soldiers carried out
the removal anyway. Notice the chain: court → president → soldiers. If any
link refuses to act, the ruling is words on paper. A careful historian adds:
this is why the Cherokee also petitioned Congress, published their
newspaper, and tried every peaceful path — the court was one tool among
many, not the whole fight.

**Teacher note — common misconceptions:** (1) "The Cherokee agreed to move
because they signed a treaty." No — a minority signed without the nation's
approval; most Cherokee opposed removal. (2) "The Trail of Tears was a
migration." No — it was a forced removal by soldiers; "migration" suggests
a free choice. (3) "The Supreme Court protected the Cherokee." The Court
ruled for them, but the ruling was not enforced. (4) "Indigenous people
didn't resist." They resisted in court, in print, in petitions, in war, and
by refusing to leave. (5) "Only the Cherokee were removed." Five
southeastern nations were removed, about 60,000 people in all. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice

Work through these together, with the adult modeling the first answer.

1. In your own words, what is a treaty? Name one thing the Cherokee were
   promised in treaties, and one thing that actually happened instead.
2. Use Worked example 1's steps to explain why the Treaty of New Echota
   was not a fair agreement for the Cherokee Nation. Who signed, and for
   whom?
3. A learner says, "The Supreme Court saved the Cherokee in 1832." Use
   Worked example 2 to correct this in two sentences.
4. Name two different ways Indigenous nations resisted removal — one
   peaceful and one by force — and say which nation used each one.

## Independent practice

1. Order these: Indian Removal Act (1830) · Worcester v. Georgia (1832) ·
   Treaty of New Echota (1835) · Cherokee removal begins (1838). How many
   years passed between the Act and the removal?
2. The Cherokee removal covered about 800 miles and more than 16,000
   people were forced west; about 4,000 died. Write two sentences: one
   stating the numbers, one explaining why historians call the death toll
   "about 4,000" instead of an exact count. (Hint: records from a forced
   march are incomplete, and scholars count deaths differently.)
3. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The Cherokee chose to move
   west in 1838 because they signed a treaty, and the Trail of Tears was a
   long migration." Find the three errors (a minority signed without
   approval; the move was forced by soldiers; "migration" hides the force)
   and rewrite the sentence correctly.
4. Explain in your own words why the *Cherokee Phoenix* newspaper mattered.
   (Hint: a newspaper lets a nation speak for itself — to its own people,
   to Congress, and to the American public.)

## Application

Choose one resister — John Ross, the Cherokee newspaper editors, Osceola
and the Seminole, Tecumseh, or Black Hawk — and write a four-sentence
paragraph: who they were, what they did to resist, what happened as a
result, and what their resistance shows about the people who fought
removal. Use only facts from this lesson; do not invent speeches or
dialogue.

## Exit check

Without looking: (a) name the year of the Indian Removal Act and the year
the Cherokee removal began; (b) finish this sentence: "The Supreme Court
ruled for the Cherokee in 1832, but the ruling was not enforced because
______." The adult checks both against the [answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Read the timeline aloud together; let the
  learner point to each event as it is named. Pair each date with a plain
  phrase (1830 = the law; 1832 = the court win; 1835 = the disputed
  treaty; 1838 = the forced march).
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may explain the treaty chain
  orally with four cards (Act → Court → Treaty → Removal) instead of
  writing the paragraph.
- **Extension:** Read the adult-selected National Park Service Trail of
  Tears page (Resource Pack, Reference 1) and add one fact to the timeline
  with its source named. Cite the source used.
