# Unit 05 — Early Republic: Expansion, Displacement, and Resistance

**Grade 5 Social Studies · Weeks 20–23 of 36 · 16 sessions (~40 minutes each)**

**Status:** validated draft — delivered 2026-11-07; not yet educator-reviewed or merged.

Between 1800 and 1850 the United States grew from 16 states to 31, bought
a vast territory from France, dug canals, launched steamboats, and pushed
settlement far west of the Mississippi. That growth changed the map — and it
changed people's lives in very different ways. Learners trace the new states
on maps, explain what treaties promised and what actually happened to
Indigenous nations, compare two perspectives on the same removal, follow
goods along new trade routes, and build a small exhibit that makes a claim
with evidence. The unit's rule, stated from the first session, is that
expansion is never told as one side's triumph: settler, Indigenous, and
enslaved people's experiences are all part of the story.

**Essential question:** When a country grows bigger and richer, who gains,
who loses, and how do we know — whose story are we hearing, and whose
evidence counts?

**Sensitive-content note (for the guiding adult):** Westward expansion meant
forced removal, broken treaties, and death for Indigenous nations. This unit
names displacement plainly, centers Indigenous and resister perspectives
alongside settler accounts, and keeps all sources adult-selected. Forced
removal and enslavement are discussed plainly at an age-appropriate level,
with no graphic detail and no re-enactment of suffering. The Trail of Tears
is taught as a forced removal the Cherokee call the trail where they cried,
not as a journey or a migration. See the [teacher guide](teacher-guide.md)
for session-level guidance.

## Lessons

1. [Lesson 01 — The Nation in 1800 and 1850: A Map That Doubled](lessons/lesson-01-nation-in-1800-and-1850.md)

5. [Lesson 05 — Causes, Effects, and the Mini-Exhibit](lessons/lesson-05-cause-effect-and-mini-exhibit.md)
   — Probable causes and effects of one region's transformation; turning

## Prerequisites (from Units 01–04 and grade-4 end-of-year objectives)

- Read maps with title, key, compass rose, and scale; sort places into
  regions and justify with evidence (Unit 01).
- Sequence events across decades; compare accounts of the same event;
  explain probable causes and effects (Units 02–03).
- Name a perspective and its reasons; explain why people in the same period
  saw the same event differently (Units 02–03).
- State both sides of a claim fairly and judge a source by its creator,
  audience, and purpose (Unit 03).
- Explain the Constitution's structure, the Bill of Rights, and how laws
  change (Unit 04) — Lesson 02 builds directly on the treaty power and the
  Supreme Court's role.

Unit 05 re-teaches map-scale reading at the continental scale rather than
assuming it is secure, and turns Unit 03's source-comparison routine toward
a harder case: accounts that disagree about a forced removal.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of this unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Describe how the nation changed on the map, 1800–1850** — name the
   Louisiana Purchase (1803, about 828,000 square miles, $15 million),
   trace the Lewis and Clark route in general terms, and place at least ten
   of the fifteen states admitted 1803–1850 in order on a map.
   (C3 D2.Geo.3, 5, 6.3-5)
2. **Explain displacement plainly** — describe what treaties promised,
   what the Indian Removal Act (1830) allowed, what the Supreme Court
   decided in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), and what happened in the
   Cherokee removal of 1838–1839 (more than 16,000 people forced west;
   about 4,000 died), naming at least two ways Indigenous nations and
   individuals resisted.
   (C3 D2.His.4, 5, 14.3-5; D2.Civ.10.3-5)
3. **Compare two perspectives on the same removal** — for two accounts,
   state what agrees and what differs, infer each account's audience and
   purpose from clues inside it, separate fact from opinion, and judge how
   useful each account is for a stated question.
   (C3 D2.His.6, 10, 11, 13.3-5; D3.2.3-5)
4. **Explain how expansion changed places and trade** — explain how the
   Erie Canal, steamboats, and cotton reshaped where people lived and how
   goods moved; name who benefited and who was harmed when trade expanded;
   and trace how goods from many places reached one 1840s home.
   (C3 D2.Geo.7, 11.3-5; D2.Eco.14, 15.3-5)
5. **Develop a claim from evidence** — write a claim about an
   expansion-era development supported by at least two pieces of evidence
   from different sources, with reasons linking each piece to the claim.
   (C3 D2.His.14, 16.3-5; D3.4.3-5; D4.2.3-5)

## Vocabulary

| Term | Kid-friendly definition |
|---|---|
| territory | Land a country claims but that is not yet a state. |
| treaty | A formal, written agreement between governments — in this unit, between the United States and an Indigenous nation. |
| purchase | Buying something; the Louisiana Purchase was the U.S. buying France's claim to a huge territory in 1803. |
| expedition | A long, organized journey to explore a place, such as Lewis and Clark's trip west in 1804–1806. |
| removal | Being forced to leave your home and move somewhere else. In this unit it means the U.S. government forcing Indigenous nations west. |
| displacement | Being pushed out of the place where you live. |
| resistance | Standing up against something unfair — by going to court, writing, petitioning, refusing to leave, or fighting back. |
| sovereignty | A nation or people's right to govern itself and its own land. |
| canal | A human-made waterway that lets boats travel where no river goes. |
| steamboat | A boat pushed by a steam engine and a paddle wheel, able to travel upstream against a river's current. |
| interdependence | Depending on each other: different places make different goods, so they trade. |
| cotton gin | A machine (1793) that quickly separated cotton fiber from its seeds, making cotton farming much more profitable — and increasing demand for enslaved labor. |
| petition | A written request, signed by many people, asking a government to act. |
| compromise | An agreement in which each side gives up something to settle a disagreement — such as the Missouri Compromise of 1820. |
| exhibit | A small display that teaches visitors something, using objects, pictures, words, and a clear claim. |

## Standards and source notes

Framework: the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social
Studies State Standards (National Council for the Social Studies), grades
3–5 band — the same framework verified for this track's scope-and-sequence
(2026-10-04; codes and descriptions carried from that verified crosswalk).
**No state adoption, accreditation, or alignment certification is claimed.**
The 3–5 band spans three grades; this unit teaches its indicators at the
grade-5 upper-band level, as the scope-and-sequence specifies.

Key facts in this unit were checked 2026-11-07 against: the National
Archives Louisiana Purchase Treaty page (signed April 30, 1803; 828,000
square miles; $15 million); the U.S. Department of State Office of the
Historian (same figures; 15 modern states from the territory); the National
Park Service Trail of Tears page (Indian Removal Act 1830; forced Cherokee
removal began 1838; more than 16,000 Cherokee evicted from Tennessee,
Alabama, North Carolina, and Georgia; an estimated 4,000 died on the
about-800-mile journey; finished 1839); Britannica's Erie Canal entry
(built 1817–1825; opened October 26, 1825; 363 miles, Albany to Buffalo)
and Worcester v. Georgia entry (decided March 3, 1832, 5–1; Georgia's laws
had no force on Cherokee land; President Jackson declined to enforce the
ruling); and `resources/us_states.csv` (statehood years: fifteen states
admitted 1803–1850, 31 states by 1850). Where historians' estimates differ
— for example, the death toll on the Trail of Tears (about 4,000 is the
most-cited estimate; scholars' counts run higher and lower) — the unit
says so. Population figures are U.S. Census counts (about 5 million in
1800; about 23 million in 1850), rounded in the lessons.

**Original passages in this unit are classroom models, not quotations.**
Lesson 03's two "accounts" of a Cherokee removal are original, invented
teaching texts labeled as models. They are never presented as real
nineteenth-century documents, and learners practice saying exactly that.
Real sources are reached through the Resource Pack, adult-selected.
