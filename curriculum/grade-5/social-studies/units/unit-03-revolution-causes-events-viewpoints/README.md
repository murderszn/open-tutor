# Unit 03 — Revolution: Causes, Events, and Competing Viewpoints

**Grade 5 Social Studies · Weeks 11–14 of 36 · 16 sessions (~40 minutes each)**

**Status:** validated draft — delivered 2026-10-25; not yet educator-reviewed or merged.

This unit continues the story where Unit 02 left off: thirteen colonies that
had learned to govern themselves now argued with Britain about taxes,
representation, and who should make the rules. Learners build a timeline from
1754 to 1783, explain why the same tax looked fair to one group and unfair to
another, compare two accounts of the same event, and follow the war to its
turning points — always asking whose viewpoint they are hearing, and whose
voice is missing.

**Essential question:** Why did people who lived under the same king disagree
so sharply about taxes, rights, and independence — and how do we judge what
really happened when the accounts disagree?

**Sensitive-content note (for the guiding adult):** The Revolution is taught
here as a contest of competing claims, not a single heroic story. Loyalist,
Patriot, British, French, and Black perspectives appear through
adult-selected sources; every event is examined from at least two viewpoints.
War, enslavement, and displacement are discussed plainly and at an
age-appropriate level, with no graphic detail. The "Mohawk disguise" at the
Boston Tea Party is taught as the colonists' own description of their
costumes — Mohawk people were not the actors — and the adult names that
distinction explicitly. See the [teacher guide](teacher-guide.md) for
session-level guidance.

## Lessons

1. [Lesson 01 — The Road to Revolution: A Timeline, 1754–1776](lessons/lesson-01-road-to-revolution-timeline.md)
   — French and Indian War, Proclamation of 1763, Stamp Act, Townshend Acts,
   Tea Party, Intolerable Acts, First Continental Congress; cause-and-effect
   chains (Week 11).
2. [Lesson 02 — "No Taxation Without Representation": Boston Protests and Two Claims](lessons/lesson-02-no-taxation-boston-protests.md)

3. [Lesson 03 — Two Accounts, One Event: Reading Sources Like a Historian](lessons/lesson-03-two-accounts-one-event.md)
   — What agrees, what differs, who wrote each account and why; audience and

4. [Lesson 04 — Turning Points: Saratoga, Valley Forge, and Yorktown](lessons/lesson-04-turning-points-claims.md)
   — Lexington and Concord, Bunker Hill, Trenton, Saratoga, the French
   alliance, Yorktown, and the Treaty of Paris; turning evidence into a claim
   (Week 14).
5. [Lesson 05 — Harbor Scene Investigation: What a Picture Can and Cannot Prove](lessons/lesson-05-harbor-scene-investigation.md)
   — Observation of the generated Boston Harbor illustration (labeled AI
   reconstruction, never evidence); the deterministic road-to-revolution
   timeline diagram; what illustrations show and what only sources can prove
   (Week 12, S3).

## Prerequisites (from Units 01–02 and grade-4 end-of-year objectives)

- Read maps with title, key, compass rose, and scale; sort places into
  regions and justify with evidence.
- Sequence events across centuries; compare accounts of the same event;
  explain probable causes and effects.
- Name a perspective and its reasons; explain why people in the same period
  saw the same event differently (Unit 02, Lesson 04).
- Weigh benefits and costs of a choice; explain specialization, trade, and
  profit (Unit 02).

Unit 03 re-teaches timeline reading at the 1754–1783 scale rather than
assuming it is secure, and turns Unit 02's perspective routine into
source criticism: who made this source, for whom, and for what purpose?

## Measurable objectives

By the end of this unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Sequence the road to revolution** — place at least ten events from
   1754–1783 in order on a timeline and explain probable causes and effects
   for at least three of them. (C3 D2.His.1, 14.3-5)
2. **Explain two competing claims about taxation** — state the colonial
   claim ("no taxation without representation") and the British claim
   (Parliament's right to tax and pay war debts) fairly, each with its
   reasons, without taking one side as the whole story.
   (C3 D2.His.4, 5.3-5; D2.Civ.10.3-5)
3. **Compare sources about the same event** — for two accounts, state what
   agrees and what differs, infer each source's audience and purpose from
   clues inside the source, separate fact from opinion, and judge how
   useful each source is for a stated question.
   (C3 D2.His.6, 10, 11, 13.3-5; D3.2.3-5)
4. **Explain the war's turning points** — explain why Saratoga and Yorktown
   changed the war's course, name the French alliance's role, and describe
   contributions of Black patriots, women, and other groups whose stories
   are often left out. (C3 D2.His.14, 16.3-5)
5. **Develop a claim from evidence** — write a claim about the past
   supported by at least two pieces of evidence from different sources,
   with reasons linking each piece to the claim.
   (C3 D2.His.16.3-5; D3.4.3-5; D4.2.3-5)

## Vocabulary

| Term | Kid-friendly definition |
|---|---|
| tax | Money a government collects from people to pay for things like soldiers, roads, and courts. |
| representation | Having someone in the government who speaks for you and votes on laws and taxes. |
| Parliament | Britain's law-making body, which made the tax laws the colonists protested. |
| boycott | Refusing to buy goods as a protest, to pressure sellers and lawmakers to change. |
| Loyalist | A colonist who stayed loyal to Britain and opposed independence. |
| Patriot | A colonist who supported independence from Britain. |
| propaganda | A message made to persuade people to one side, often by choosing only flattering or alarming details. |
| primary source | A source made at the time of an event by someone who saw or took part in it. |
| secondary source | A later account, written by someone who studied the event afterward. |
| turning point | An event that changes the direction of a war or a story. |
| alliance | An agreement between countries to help each other, often in war. |
| grievance | A complaint that something is unfair and should be fixed. |
| petition | A written request, signed by many people, asking a ruler or government to act. |
| militia | Ordinary citizens trained to fight in an emergency, not full-time soldiers. |

## Standards and source notes

Framework: the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social
Studies State Standards (National Council for the Social Studies), grades
3–5 band — the same framework verified for this track's scope-and-sequence
(2026-10-04; codes and descriptions carried from that verified crosswalk).
**No state adoption, accreditation, or alignment certification is claimed.**
The 3–5 band spans three grades; this unit teaches its indicators at the
grade-5 upper-band level, as the scope-and-sequence specifies.

Key facts in this unit were checked against the track's scope-and-sequence
audit (which verified the kept Revolution quizzes and the Reporter's
Journal assignment) and re-confirmed 2026-10-25 against: the U.S. Census
Bureau's December 2023 Boston Tea Party history story (December 16, 1773;
the *Dartmouth*, *Eleanor*, and *Beaver*; 342 chests); the National Archives
Declaration transcript and Civics for All of US teacher guide; and the
Crash Course U.S. History #6 episode summary (road-to-revolution sequence).
Where sources disagree or a figure is an estimate — for example, the
5,000–8,000 Black soldiers and sailors — the unit says so.

**Original passages in this unit are classroom models, not quotations.**
Lesson 03's two "accounts" of the Boston Tea Party are original, invented
teaching texts labeled as models. They are never presented as real
eighteenth-century documents, and learners practice saying exactly that.
Real sources are reached through the Resource Pack, adult-selected.
