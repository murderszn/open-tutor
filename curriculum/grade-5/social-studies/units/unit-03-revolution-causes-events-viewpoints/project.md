# Project — Road to Revolution Reporter's Journal

**Built on the kept assignment:** `assignments/road-to-revolution-journal.md`
(three frontline dispatches), with the taught news-report model added by
this unit. The adult pre-selects all sources; learners write from the
unit's lessons and the adult-selected sources only.

**Deliverable:** Three dispatches, each 8–12 sentences, plus a one-paragraph
"Editor's note" at the end stating which parts are based on evidence and
which parts are the learner's imagining.

## The taught model (adult models Dispatch 1's opening, then learners write)

A news dispatch has four parts, in order:

1. **Headline** — what happened, in under ten words.
2. **Lead** — who, what, when, where, in one sentence.
3. **Evidence** — two facts with their sources named ("According to the
   unit's timeline…", "The Census history story reports…").
4. **Voices** — two viewpoints on the same event, each labeled ("A Patriot
   printer might have said…", "A British official reported…"). Imagined
   voices are labeled as imagining, never presented as quotations.

*Model opening (Dispatch 1, Boston Tea Party — adult reads aloud, learners
do not copy it):* "Headline: Tea Destroyed in Boston Harbor. Lead: On the
night of December 16, 1773, colonists in Boston boarded three ships and
destroyed 342 chests of tea. Evidence: the ships were the *Dartmouth*,
*Eleanor*, and *Beaver*, and the chests numbered 342 — facts the Census
history story confirms. Voices: a Patriot might have called it a protest
of principle; a British official would have called it destruction of
property. Both voices are my imagining, not quotations."

## The three dispatches

- **Dispatch 1 (Week 12 S3):** The Boston Tea Party, December 16, 1773.
  Must include: the three ships, 342 chests, the Tea Act's principle (not
  price), and two viewpoints. Must state the Mohawk-disguise distinction
  correctly.
- **Dispatch 2 (Week 12 S3, finished Week 13):** Lexington and Concord,
  April 19, 1775. Must include: the militia, the march to seize weapons at
  Concord, the fighting on the road back to Boston, and two viewpoints
  (for example, a militiaman and a British soldier's family, or a Loyalist
  neighbor).
- **Dispatch 3 (Week 14 S3):** A Black-patriot profile — choose one: Crispus
  Attucks, Peter Salem, James Armistead Lafayette, Wentworth Cheswell, or
  Phillis Wheatley. Must include: who the person was, what they did, what
  is known and what is estimated or traditional, and why their story
  matters to a full account of the Revolution.

## Rubric (20 points)

| Criterion | 5 — Strong | 3 — Developing | 1 — Beginning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy (5) | All dates, names, and numbers correct; estimates labeled as estimates | Most facts correct; one error or unlabeled estimate | Several errors, or facts invented |
| Two viewpoints (5) | Each dispatch shows two viewpoints fairly, each with reasons | Two viewpoints present but one is a cartoon | Only one viewpoint, or viewpoints confused |
| Evidence use (5) | Each dispatch names sources for its facts; imagining labeled as imagining | Sources named sometimes; imagining sometimes unlabeled | No sources named; imagining presented as fact |
| Reporter's craft (5) | Headline, lead, evidence, voices in order; clear sentences | Most parts present; order or clarity uneven | Missing parts; hard to follow |

**Editor's note (required, not scored separately — it is part of
"Evidence use"):** One paragraph at the end: "These are the facts I am
sure about… These are the parts I imagined… This is what I would still
need to check…"

## Safety and fairness notes

- No graphic descriptions of violence. "Soldiers fired and five colonists
  were killed" is the level of detail for this age.
- The Mohawk-disguise sentence must read: "The colonists dressed in
  costumes they described as Mohawk disguises; Mohawk people were not the
  actors." A dispatch that says Mohawk people dumped the tea loses the
  Accuracy points for that dispatch until corrected.
- Enslavement is named plainly where it belongs (Dispatch 3, Wheatley and
  Armistead Lafayette) and never used as a joke or a costume.
