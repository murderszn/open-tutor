# Lesson 01 — The Road to Revolution: A Timeline, 1754–1776

**Goal:** I can place the road-to-revolution events in order and explain how
one event probably caused the next.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 11, S1–S2). **Materials:** the
[timeline diagram](../assets/road-to-revolution-timeline.svg), paper timeline
strips, the practice bank (Set A).

**Readiness check (adult asks aloud):** "Name one way the colonies in Unit 02
governed themselves." (Town meetings, the House of Burgesses, colonial
assemblies.) If this is shaky, re-teach it for five minutes before starting:
the whole unit turns on colonists who were used to a say in their own rules.

## Teaching

In the 1750s, Britain and France both claimed land in North America, and
their rivalry became a war fought in America, Europe, and elsewhere: the
French and Indian War (1754–1763). "French and Indian" is the name the
British gave it; the war also involved many Indigenous nations, who made
their own choices about whom to ally with. Britain won, but winning was
expensive. Britain now owed a great deal of money, and it also had to pay
soldiers to guard a much larger North American territory.

Two decisions followed, and both angered many colonists. First, the
Proclamation of 1763 drew a line along the Appalachian Mountains and told
colonists not to settle west of it — Britain said this would prevent costly
new wars with Indigenous nations, but colonists who wanted that land heard
it as Britain closing land they had fought for. Second, Parliament began
taxing the colonies directly to raise money: the Sugar Act (1764), the Stamp
Act (1765, a tax on printed paper — newspapers, legal papers, playing
cards), and the Townshend Acts (1767, taxes on imports such as glass, paint,
paper, and tea). Colonists protested each one, and Parliament repealed some
taxes — but it kept insisting on its right to tax.

That insistence is the thread to follow on your timeline. A timeline is not
just a list of dates. It is a tool for asking: *what changed after this
event, and what probably caused that change?* A cause comes before its
effect, and a good cause-and-effect claim needs a reason, not just two events
in a row.

### Worked example 1 — From war debt to the Stamp Act

*Claim:* The Stamp Act of 1765 was probably caused, in part, by Britain's
war debt.

*Reasoning, step by step:* (1) The French and Indian War ended in 1763 and
left Britain with large debts. (2) Parliament looked for new money and
decided the colonies, which had benefited from British protection, should
help pay. (3) In 1765 it passed the Stamp Act, the first tax collected
directly inside the colonies rather than as a duty on trade. The order fits
(1763, then 1765), and the reason fits (a government in debt looks for
revenue). Notice the word *probably*: historians say "probable cause"
because other reasons — such as Parliament wanting to show it was in charge
— also played a part. One cause is rarely the whole story.

### Worked example 2 — From the Tea Party to the Intolerable Acts

*Claim:* The Coercive Acts of 1774 (which colonists called the Intolerable
Acts) were probably caused by the Boston Tea Party.

*Reasoning:* (1) On December 16, 1773, colonists destroyed 342 chests of tea
in Boston Harbor. (2) Parliament wanted to punish Boston and restore control.
(3) In 1774 it closed Boston Harbor until the tea was paid for, changed
Massachusetts government, and sent more soldiers. Again the order fits and
the reason fits: punishment follows the act it punishes. But a careful
historian adds: the Acts also punished a whole city for the actions of a
group, which is why colonists in *other* colonies — who had not dumped any
tea — were alarmed enough to call the First Continental Congress in
September 1774. One event's effect can travel farther than its actors
intended.

**Teacher note — common misconceptions:** (1) "The Revolution started on
July 4, 1776." No — July 4 is the Declaration; fighting had begun at
Lexington and Concord on April 19, 1775, and the argument about taxes had
run for a decade before that. (2) "Every colonist wanted independence."
No — many were Loyalists, many were undecided, and many enslaved and
Indigenous people had their own stakes that matched neither side neatly.
(3) "Taxes alone caused the war." Taxes were the spark in a longer argument
about who had the right to rule. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md).

## Guided practice

Work through these together, with the adult modeling the first answer.

1. Put these in order: Stamp Act (1765) · Proclamation of 1763 · French and
   Indian War ends (1763) · Boston Tea Party (1773). Say which two share a
   year and why the order of those two still matters.
2. Use Worked example 1's steps to explain why Parliament passed the
   Townshend Acts in 1767. What had happened just before, and what reason
   did Parliament give?
3. A learner says, "The Proclamation of 1763 caused the Stamp Act." Is that
   a good cause-and-effect claim? Why or why not? (Hint: both followed the
   war — are they causes of each other, or effects of the same cause?)
4. Draw a cause-and-effect chain with three boxes: war debt → ______ →
   colonial protest. Fill the middle box and defend it with one reason.

## Independent practice

1. Copy the ten events from the timeline diagram onto a strip in order.
   Circle the three you think mattered most, and write one sentence for
   each explaining why.
2. Write a probable-cause claim for the First Continental Congress (1774):
   what happened just before it, and why would colonies that had not dumped
   tea still send delegates?
3. **Error analysis:** A classmate's timeline puts the Boston Tea Party
   (1773) before the Stamp Act (1765) and writes, "The Tea Party caused the
   Stamp Act." Explain the two errors (wrong order; a later event cannot
   cause an earlier one) and fix the timeline.
4. Explain in your own words why historians say "probable cause" instead of
   just "cause."

## Application

Choose one event from 1763–1774 and write a four-sentence paragraph: what
happened, what probably caused it, what probably happened because of it,
and what you are still unsure about. Honest uncertainty earns full marks
here — historians name what they do not know.

## Exit check

Without looking: (a) name the years of the French and Indian War, the Stamp
Act, and the Boston Tea Party; (b) finish this sentence: "Parliament taxed
the colonies after the war because ______." The adult checks both against
the [answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Read the timeline aloud together; let the
  learner point to each event as it is named. Pair each date with a picture
  cue (a ship for 1773, a stamp for 1765).
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may build the timeline with
  cards on a table and explain it orally instead of writing the paragraph.
- **Extension:** Research one more event from 1764–1774 (for example, the
  Sugar Act or the Quartering Act) and add it to the timeline with a
  probable-cause sentence. Cite the source used.
