# Lesson 03 — Two Accounts, One Event: Reading Sources Like a Historian

**Goal:** I can compare two accounts of the same event, infer each writer's
audience and purpose, separate fact from opinion, and judge how useful each
source is for a question.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 13, S1–S2). **Materials:** practice
bank (Set C).

**Readiness check:** "What is the difference between a fact and an opinion?"
(Fact: checkable against evidence. Opinion: a judgment or feeling.)
"Between a primary and a secondary source?" (Primary: made at the time by a
participant or witness. Secondary: written later by someone studying the
event.) If either is shaky, re-teach with one example each before starting.

> **Important label — read this aloud first.** The two accounts below are
> **original classroom models invented for this lesson. They are NOT real
> documents from 1773.** Real eighteenth-century writing is harder to read
> and is chosen by the guiding adult from the Resource Pack. We use models
> here so you can practice the historian's questions on short, clear texts
> — and so you learn to say, honestly, "this is a model, not a source."

## Teaching

Historians do not pick the account they like best. They ask five questions
of every source — the same five, every time:

1. **Who made it, and when?** (Maker and date.)
2. **For whom was it made?** (Audience — clues inside the text: who is
   addressed, what is explained, what is assumed known.)
3. **Why was it made?** (Purpose — to inform, to persuade, to protest, to
   report to a superior?)
4. **What does it agree with, and what does it differ on, compared with
   other accounts?**
5. **How useful is it for my question?** A source can be excellent for one
   question ("What did Patriots want readers to feel?") and weak for
   another ("How many chests were destroyed?").

Fact and opinion help with question 4. "Colonists boarded three ships" is a
fact claim — checkable. "The colonists were brave heroes" is an opinion —
a judgment, not checkable the same way. A text can mix both, and a single
sentence can hide an opinion inside a fact: "The brave colonists punished
the wicked taxmen" states an action *and* two judgments.

### Account A — classroom model (Patriot printer's account)

> "Last night our brave townsmen struck a blow for liberty. Rather than let
> Parliament force its taxed tea upon us, they boarded the ships in the
> harbor and cast 342 chests into the sea. No person was harmed and no
> property but the tea was touched, for this was a protest of principle,
> not of rage. Let all lovers of freedom rejoice that Boston has shown the
> colonies the way."

### Account B — classroom model (British customs official's report)

> "On the night of December 16, a lawless mob boarded three merchant vessels
> in this harbor and destroyed their cargoes of tea. The officers on duty
> could not prevent the outrage, and the loss to the East India Company is
> very great. I request instructions and additional protection, as the
> authority of Parliament is openly defied in this town."

### Worked example 1 — What agrees, what differs?

*Agrees:* Both accounts say colonists boarded ships in Boston Harbor at
night and destroyed tea cargoes. Account A gives the number 342 chests;
Account B does not give a number, but "destroyed their cargoes" matches the
same action.

*Differs:* A calls the actors "brave townsmen" acting "for liberty" and
stresses that no person was harmed; B calls them a "lawless mob" and
stresses loss and defiance of Parliament. The *event* is shared; the
*judgment* of it is opposite. A also claims a motive ("protest of
principle"); B claims a different frame ("outrage" needing "protection").
Neither account, alone, tells you what a third person — say, an enslaved
dockworker watching from the wharf — thought.

### Worked example 2 — Audience, purpose, usefulness

*Account A:* Audience — fellow colonists who already sympathize with the
protest (clues: "our brave townsmen," "lovers of freedom"). Purpose — to
celebrate and rally support. Useful for: "How did Patriots want the Tea
Party remembered?" Weak for: an exact, neutral count of damage, because its
purpose is persuasion, not accounting.

*Account B:* Audience — a superior official (clues: "I request
instructions"). Purpose — to report a problem and ask for help. Useful for:
"What did British officials think had happened, and what did they want
done?" Weak for: the colonists' motives, because the writer did not share
them and had reason to present the event as a crime.

A source's purpose does not make it useless — it tells you *what question
it can answer*. A propaganda engraving is poor evidence for "how many
soldiers fired" and excellent evidence for "what did Patriots want
Americans to feel?"

**Teacher note — common misconceptions:** (1) "One of these must be lying."
Disagreement is not always lying: two honest witnesses can stress different
true details, and two persuaders can both select facts. Check claims against
a third source before calling anyone a liar. (2) "Primary sources are always
more trustworthy." A primary source can be biased, mistaken, or written to
persuade — that is exactly why historians ask who, for whom, and why.
(3) "If it is old, it is a primary source." A 1900 history of 1773 is a
secondary source, however old the book is.

## Guided practice

1. Underline one fact claim and one opinion in Account A. Do the same for
   Account B. Compare with the adult.
2. Who is Account B's audience? Quote the clue inside the text that tells
   you.
3. Both accounts agree ships were boarded. Name one thing they disagree
   about that is a judgment, not a fact.
4. For the question "Why did the Tea Party happen?", which account is more
   useful, and what is it missing? (Hint: neither gives the Tea Act or the
   tax principle on its own — you need Lesson 02 as well.)

## Independent practice

1. Write a three-column table: Agrees / A only / B only, with at least two
   entries per column.
2. Infer Account A's purpose in one sentence, with one quoted clue.
   Do the same for Account B.
3. **Error analysis:** A learner writes, "Account A is true and Account B
   is false because I agree with the Patriots." Explain the error (liking
   a side is not a source test) and rewrite the judgment using the five
   questions.
4. Judge usefulness: for the question "How many chests were destroyed?",
   which account helps, and why is even that answer better checked against
   a third source?

## Application

Write a four-sentence "historian's verdict" on the Tea Party that uses both
accounts: one sentence on what happened, one on what the accounts disagree
about, one on why they disagree (audience/purpose), and one on what you
would still need to check. End with the label: "Based on two classroom
models, not on original documents."

## Exit check

(a) Name the five historian's questions. (b) Is a persuasive source
useless? Answer in one sentence, and check against the
[answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Read both accounts aloud; let the learner
  mark fact claims in one color and opinions in another before writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may answer the guided practice
  orally while the adult scribes, then read the answers back.
- **Extension:** Take Revere's Boston Massacre engraving (described in
  Lesson 02, adult-selected image) and run the five questions on it in
  five sentences.
