# Lesson 02 — "No Taxation Without Representation": Boston Protests and Two Claims

**Goal:** I can explain the colonial claim and the British claim about
taxation fairly, each with its reasons, and describe the Boston protests
from more than one viewpoint.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 12, S1–S2). **Materials:** practice
bank (Set B), the [harbor illustration](../assets/boston-harbor-1773.png)
for preview only (full activity is Lesson 05).

**Readiness check:** "Yesterday's timeline: what did Parliament do in 1765,
and how did colonists respond?" (Stamp Act; protests, boycotts, the Stamp
Act Congress.) If the learner cannot name a colonial response, re-run Lesson
01's Worked example 1 before continuing.

## Teaching

By the late 1760s, the argument was no longer only about how *much* tax
colonists paid. It was about *who had the right* to tax them at all.

**The colonial claim** ran like this: "We have no representatives in
Parliament. No one we elected voted for these taxes. Taxing us without our
consent breaks the rights of English people, who are used to consenting to
taxes through their representatives." Colonists shortened it to a slogan:
"No taxation without representation." Notice what the slogan does: it turns
a long legal argument into words a crowd can chant. Slogans persuade; they
do not prove.

**The British claim** ran like this: "Every person in Britain is 'virtually
represented' in Parliament, whether or not they voted for a member — and
besides, Parliament must pay for the soldiers who protect the colonies, and
the colonies benefited from the war. The colonies are part of the empire,
and Parliament governs the whole empire." A British official would add that
Parliament had already repealed the Stamp Act when colonists protested, so
the system could listen.

A fair summary does not pick a winner. It states each side's reasons in
words that side would recognize. That is the skill this lesson practices,
and it is harder than it looks — because most of us already sympathize with
one side.

Now the Boston events, with both claims in mind:

- **Boston Massacre, March 5, 1770.** A crowd of colonists confronted
  British soldiers in Boston; soldiers fired, and five colonists were killed,
  including Crispus Attucks, a man of African and Wampanoag descent. A
  Patriot printer, Paul Revere, made a famous engraving showing soldiers
  firing in a neat line at unarmed colonists. The engraving was propaganda:
  it chose details that would anger viewers and left out the confusion,
  the snowballs and insults, and the soldiers' fear. Propaganda is not the
  same as lying about every detail — but it is a message built to persuade,
  and a historian reads it as evidence of what its maker wanted people to
  feel, not as a photograph of the night.
- **Boycotts.** Colonists — including the Daughters of Liberty, groups of
  women who organized non-importation — refused to buy British goods. A
  boycott is economics as protest: if sellers lose money, lawmakers may
  listen. Think about incentives: a merchant who loses sales has a reason to
  pressure Parliament, even if that merchant cares nothing about colonial
  rights.
- **Boston Tea Party, December 16, 1773.** After the Tea Act of 1773 let
  the British East India Company sell tea directly in the colonies (keeping
  a small tax on it), colonists in Boston refused to let the tea be unloaded.
  That night, colonists boarded the *Dartmouth*, *Eleanor*, and *Beaver* at
  Griffin's Wharf and destroyed 342 chests of tea in the harbor. They
  described themselves as disguised as Mohawk Indians. Say this carefully:
  these were colonists in costume, not Mohawk people — and the adult should
  name that distinction, because repeating the disguise without comment
  turns a real nation's identity into a costume.
- **The Intolerable Acts and the First Continental Congress (1774).**
  Parliament's punishment of Boston alarmed other colonies, which sent
  delegates to Philadelphia in September 1774 to coordinate a response.
  Colonists were organizing across colonies now, not just in one town.

### Worked example 1 — Summarizing the colonial claim fairly

*Prompt:* "Summarize the colonial claim in two sentences."

*Model answer:* "Colonists said Parliament could not tax them because they
elected no one to Parliament, so they had no consent in the taxes. They
pointed to their own assemblies, which had taxed them before, as the bodies
that should decide colonial taxes."

*Why this is fair:* It uses the colonists' own reason (consent through
representation) and names their evidence (their assemblies). It does not
add "and they were right" — fairness in a summary means the other side
would recognize its own argument, even if it disagrees.

### Worked example 2 — Summarizing the British claim fairly

*Prompt:* "Summarize the British claim in two sentences."

*Model answer:* "British officials said Parliament represented the whole
empire, including colonists, and had the right to tax all of it to pay for
defense and government. They said the colonies had benefited from British
protection in the war, so helping pay for it was fair."

*Why this is fair:* It gives Britain's reasons (empire-wide representation,
shared war costs) rather than a cartoon ("Britain was greedy"). A summary
that makes one side sound foolish is not a summary — it is a verdict
disguised as one.

**Teacher note — common misconceptions:** (1) "The Boston Massacre was a
massacre of hundreds." Five colonists were killed — a tragedy, and a word
chosen for effect. (2) "The Tea Party was about the price of tea." The tea
was actually cheaper under the Tea Act; the fight was about the *principle*
of being taxed without consent, and about protecting colonial merchants.
(3) "All Bostonians supported the Tea Party." Many did not — Loyalists,
merchants who lost business, and enslaved people watching a fight about
liberty that did not include them all had different views.

## Guided practice

1. In your own words, state the colonial claim. Then state the British
   claim. Swap papers with the adult: could each side recognize its own
   argument in your summary?
2. Revere's engraving: name one detail it emphasizes to anger viewers, and
   one thing a historian would need another source to check.
3. Boycott economics: a Boston merchant sells British cloth. Colonists
   boycott it. Name two incentives now pushing that merchant — one toward
   supporting the boycott, one toward opposing it.
4. Why did the Intolerable Acts alarm colonies that had dumped no tea? Give
   two reasons.

## Independent practice

1. Write two short paragraphs, one per claim, as in the worked examples.
   End each with the evidence that side would cite.
2. **Error analysis:** A learner writes, "The British taxed tea because they
   hated the colonists." Explain why this is a weak historical explanation,
   and replace it with the British claim from this lesson.
3. List three groups in Boston in 1773 who might have seen the Tea Party
   differently (for example, a Son of Liberty, a Loyalist merchant, an
   enslaved dockworker). Give each one sentence in that person's voice —
   and label it as your imagining, not a quotation.
4. Explain the Mohawk-disguise distinction in two sentences: who were the
   actors, and why does it matter to say so?

## Application

Imagine you are a Boston newspaper editor in January 1774 who must print
both claims on one page. Write the two headlines and one sentence under
each. Your headlines must be fair enough that a reader from either side
would not call them lies — even if that reader disagrees with the other
headline.

## Exit check

(a) What does "no taxation without representation" mean, in one sentence?
(b) Name the three Tea Party ships and the number of chests destroyed.
Check against the [answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Pre-teach *slogan, boycott, propaganda* with
  a modern, non-political example of each (a sports chant, refusing to buy
  a brand, an advertisement) before the history.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may record the two claims as a
  two-voice dialogue instead of writing paragraphs.
- **Extension:** Find one more Revere-engraving detail (adult-selected
  source) and write what it was designed to make a viewer feel, and what
  other source could check it.
