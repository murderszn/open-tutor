# Lesson 04 — Turning Points: Saratoga, Valley Forge, and Yorktown

**Goal:** I can explain why Saratoga and Yorktown changed the war's course,
describe the French alliance and the contributions of people often left out
of the story, and turn evidence into a claim.

**Time:** Two ~40-minute sessions (Week 14, S1–S2). **Materials:** practice
bank (Set D), the [timeline diagram](../assets/road-to-revolution-timeline.svg).

**Readiness check:** "Place Lexington (1775), the Declaration (1776), and
Saratoga (1777) in order, and say one thing that happened at each." If
Saratoga is blank, that is fine — this lesson fills it. If Lexington is
blank, spend five minutes on it first: April 19, 1775, British troops
marching to seize colonial weapons at Concord were met by colonial militia
at Lexington Green, then fought along the road back to Boston. The war had
begun before independence was declared.

## Teaching

The war did not go one way. Early Patriot victories were mixed with
defeats, and the Continental Army — led by George Washington, chosen as
commander in June 1775 — spent winters short of food, clothing, and pay.
Three moments changed what was possible:

- **Trenton, December 1776, and morale.** After defeats around New York,
  Washington crossed the Delaware River on Christmas night and surprised
  Hessian troops at Trenton. It was a small battle with a large effect:
  soldiers who were about to go home re-enlisted, and the cause looked
  alive again. Morale is a real military factor, not just a feeling.
- **Saratoga, October 1777 — the turning point.** American forces defeated
  a British army under General Burgoyne at Saratoga, New York, and forced
  its surrender. Why did this matter more than a single battle? Because
  France, which had been watching, now believed the Americans could win —
  and in 1778 France allied with the United States, sending money, ships,
  soldiers, and officers such as the Marquis de Lafayette. A turning point
  is not just a big event; it is an event after which the choices open to
  each side change. After Saratoga, Britain faced a wider war, not just a
  colonial rebellion.
- **Valley Forge, winter 1777–78 — endurance and training.** The army
  camped at Valley Forge, Pennsylvania, in a hard winter of cold and hunger.
  The Prussian officer Baron von Steuben drilled the troops into a more
  disciplined army. Valley Forge is taught here as endurance and
  organization, not as a battle — a common confusion to correct.
- **Yorktown, October 1781 — the end of major fighting.** With French ships
  under Admiral de Grasse blocking escape by sea and American and French
  troops besieging the town by land, British General Cornwallis surrendered
  at Yorktown, Virginia. The war's major fighting effectively ended there,
  though the Treaty of Paris was not signed until 1783, recognizing
  American independence and setting boundaries (the Mississippi River to
  the west, Spanish Florida to the south).

People often left out of a short war story were central to it:

- **Black patriots.** An estimated 5,000–8,000 Black soldiers and sailors
  served the Patriot cause — the range is an estimate because records are
  incomplete, and the unit always says so. Crispus Attucks died at the
  Boston Massacre in 1770; Peter Salem fought at Bunker Hill and Saratoga;
  James Armistead Lafayette, an enslaved man, spied for the Patriots while
  posing as a British servant and passed information that helped at
  Yorktown; Wentworth Cheswell, a Black teacher and town official, rode as
  a messenger. Many enslaved people also sought freedom with the British,
  who promised liberty to those who joined them — a reminder that "the
  Patriot side" and "the freedom side" were not the same thing for everyone.
- **Women.** Phillis Wheatley, an enslaved poet in Boston, published poems
  that argued for liberty in a voice the Patriot cause celebrated — while
  she herself remained enslaved, a contradiction the adult names plainly.
  Deborah Sampson served in the army disguised as a man. "Molly Pitcher"
  is a traditional story, probably a composite of several women who
  carried water to soldiers — teach it as a legend, not as one verified
  person's biography.
- **Indigenous nations and Loyalists.** Indigenous nations made their own
  strategic choices, allying with different sides or trying to stay out of
  the war, and most lost land whatever they chose. Loyalists — perhaps a
  third of colonists by some estimates, though the number is debated —
  faced confiscation, exile, or violence after the war. A complete account
  of "who won" must ask "who paid, and who was left out of the peace?"

### Worked example 1 — Why Saratoga was a turning point

*Claim:* Saratoga was a turning point because it changed France's choice.

*Reasoning:* (1) Before Saratoga, France helped secretly but would not
openly ally with a cause that might lose. (2) The surrender of a whole
British army at Saratoga in October 1777 showed the Americans could win a
major battle. (3) In 1778 France signed an alliance and sent ships and
troops. (4) With French help, the war became one Britain could not win by
fighting only in America. Each step is a fact; the *link* between them —
"France changed its mind because of Saratoga" — is the claim, and it is
supported because the timing and the French decision line up with the
reasons French officials gave at the time.

### Worked example 2 — Turning evidence into a claim

*Evidence:* (a) After Saratoga, France allied with the United States.
(b) At Yorktown, French ships blocked Cornwallis's escape by sea.

*Weak claim:* "France helped a lot." (Too vague to check.)

*Strong claim:* "French help after Saratoga was necessary for the Yorktown
victory, because without French ships blocking the Chesapeake, Cornwallis
could have been evacuated by sea." This claim can be argued against — a
critic could say the land siege alone might have forced surrender — and
that arguability is a virtue: a claim that cannot be disagreed with is
usually too vague to be interesting.

**Teacher note — common misconceptions:** (1) "The war ended at Yorktown."
Major fighting ended there; the peace treaty came in 1783. (2) "Valley
Forge was a battle." It was a winter camp. (3) "Everyone who fought for
independence was free." Many who fought were enslaved, and many who
cheered liberty owned enslaved people — hold both facts at once.

## Guided practice

1. In one sentence each, explain why Trenton mattered (morale), why
   Saratoga mattered (alliance), and why Yorktown mattered (end of major
   fighting).
2. Use Worked example 1's steps to explain why Yorktown, not Saratoga,
   ended major fighting. What was different at Yorktown?
3. Name one Black patriot, one woman, and one group from this lesson, and
   give each one's contribution in one sentence.
4. Improve this claim: "The war was long." What evidence would make it
   specific and checkable?

## Independent practice

1. Write a paragraph answering: "Was Saratoga or Yorktown the bigger
   turning point?" Defend your choice with two pieces of evidence.
2. **Error analysis:** A learner writes, "Valley Forge was the battle where
   Washington defeated the British in winter." Correct the two errors
   (not a battle; no such defeat there) and say what Valley Forge actually
   was.
3. Explain, in three sentences, why an enslaved person in 1778 might have
   chosen the British side, even while Patriots spoke of liberty.
4. Why is "France helped a lot" a weak claim? Rewrite it as a strong claim
   using one piece of Yorktown evidence.

## Application

Write a claim about the past — "The single most important reason the
Patriots won was ______" — supported by two pieces of evidence from
different lessons in this unit, with a reason linking each piece to the
claim. Then write one sentence a critic might use against your claim.
A claim that anticipates its critic is a stronger claim.

## Exit check

(a) Name the year of Saratoga, the year of the French alliance, and the
year of Yorktown. (b) Finish: "Saratoga was a turning point because ______."
Check against the [answer key](../answer-key.md).

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** Keep the timeline diagram visible
  throughout; point to each event as it is discussed.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may give the turning-point
  paragraph orally and then write only the claim sentence.
- **Extension:** Compare the Treaty of Paris boundaries (1783) with a
  modern U.S. map (adult-selected) and name two ways the country's shape
  has changed since — a bridge to Unit 05's expansion story.
