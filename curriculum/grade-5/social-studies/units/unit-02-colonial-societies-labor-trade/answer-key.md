## Lesson 01 — Three Colonial Regions

**Readiness check:** 1. Any Unit-01 example (e.g., Northwest Coast
salmon fishing; Plains bison hunting; Southwest irrigation). 2. A region
is an area grouped by shared characteristics (landforms, climate,
culture). 3. Any reasonable prediction (e.g., "farming would be hard; they
might fish or trade instead").

**Guided practice (A1–A3):**

- A1. New England: New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island,
  Connecticut. Middle: New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware.
  Southern: Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia.
- A2. a → New England; b → Southern; c → Middle.
- A3. Accept any correct chain, e.g.: New England → tall straight trees →
  shipbuilding; Middle → fertile soil → wheat farming/flour milling;
  Southern → warm climate + long season → tobacco/rice plantations.

**Exit check:** 1. Three regions + two colonies each (see A1). 2. Rocky
soil and short growing season made cash crops impractical; forests and
deep harbors made shipbuilding, fishing, and timber trade the easier
living. 3. Any one: Mayflower Compact (1620), House of Burgesses (1619),
or town meetings — with a sentence such as "they were early practice in
ruling themselves, leading toward the Constitution in Unit 04."

**Practice A (items A4–A10):**

- A4. 1. False — the three regions had different economies shaped by
  environment. 2. True. 3. False — plantations grew cash crops (tobacco,
  rice, indigo) mainly to be sold. 4. True.
- A5. 1607 Jamestown; 1619 House of Burgesses; 1620 Plymouth/Mayflower
  Compact; 1636 Rhode Island; 1681 Pennsylvania; 1732 Georgia.
- A6. Fishing family → New England (cold waters, harbors, poor farm
  soil). Rice family → Southern colonies (warm climate, long growing
  season, tidal rivers/wet lowlands).
- A7. Any self-rule practice from the lesson + one why-it-matters sentence.
- A8. Then/now: accept any two paragraphs using ≥3 lesson facts for the
  colonial day (e.g., chores at dawn, cornmeal mush, walking to the
  meeting house / river travel, candles after dark) and an honest today
  comparison; two differences + one similarity required (similarity often:
  families eating together, children having chores).
- A9. Weekly check: same as exit check (1–2) + one self-rule practice.
- A10. Challenge: accept any paragraph arguing that regions reveal the
  environment → economy pattern — e.g., "thirteen separate stories hide
  that climate and soil decided what work was possible; regions show the
  pattern."

## Lesson 02 — Colonial Economic Life

**Readiness check:** 1. Any correct triple (see Lesson 01). 2. Focusing
on producing what you make best/most easily, then trading for the rest.
3. Trade with each other.

**Guided practice (B1–B3):**

- B1. Ship's mast — natural: tall straight trees; human capital:
  shipwrights' knowledge; physical capital: axes, saws, docks. Flour —
  natural: fertile soil, wheat, water; human capital: farming/milling
  skill; physical capital: plow, mill, barrels. Indigo — natural: warm
  climate, suitable soil; human capital: planting/processing knowledge
  (much of it African expertise); physical capital: hoes, vats, drying
  sheds.
- B2. Accept: "The Middle colonies grew wheat better than almost anything
  else and New England built ships better than almost anything else; by
  each specializing in what its environment made easiest and trading,
  both ended up with more than if each made everything poorly alone."
- B3. Costs: what she pays the supplier + shipping + shop costs. Price:
  what customers will pay (higher for British cloth). Profit = price −
  costs; she stocks whichever leaves the bigger profit after all costs.

**Exit check:** 1. Natural resources, human capital, physical capital +
one colonial example each (see B1). 2. "Because each region produces what
its environment makes easiest, no region makes everything — so regions
must exchange goods with each other." 3. "Money lets you sell now and buy
later from anyone; barter requires finding someone who wants exactly what
you have." 4. Any one: Navigation Acts (goods on British ships through
British ports) or mercantilism's raw-materials-out/manufactured-goods-in
rule — plus one shaping effect (e.g., "planters sold mostly to Britain,"
"colonists bought British cloth and tools").

**Practice B (items B4–B10):**

- B4. field → **drying/curing barn** → packed hogshead → **wagon and
  river to port** → ship to London → sold.
- B5. Benefits: more total goods (trade surplus buys cloth/tools/tea);
  each hour of work earns more. Costs: dependence on traders and wheat
  prices; less self-sufficient if trade is disrupted.
- B6. (1) Money separates selling from buying — no need for a double
  match of wants. (2) Profit = money kept after paying all costs.
  (3) When tobacco prices were high, planters planted more tobacco; when
  prices fell, some switched crops — profit pointed their choices.
- B7. Effect on colonial merchants: higher shipping costs / fewer
  choices of buyers (must use British ships and ports). Effect on British
  tax collectors: more goods passing through British ports = more taxes
  collected.
- B8. Plan-a-trade: accept any coherent plan using regional
  specializations (e.g., flour → Caribbean sugar islands, return with
  sugar/molasses; or flour → Britain, return with cloth/tools); must name
  one British rule (Navigation Acts / must trade through Britain) and a
  profit expectation + a risk (storms, price drops, spoilage).
- B9. Weekly check: (1) three kinds + examples; (2) specialization
  sentence; (3) one rule + one effect; (4) any good traced in 3 steps
  around the triangle (see Lesson 05 key).
- B10. Challenge: the merchant's perspective — protected shipping,
  steady work (stake: his livelihood). The planter's perspective — forced
  to accept lower British prices instead of better French/Spanish offers
  (stake: his income). Both sincere because the same rule helps one and
  hurts the other.

## Lesson 03 — Labor Systems

**Readiness check:** 1. Natural/human-capital/physical-capital triple for
flour. 2. Mostly enslaved workers, paid nothing. 3. Any reasonable
prediction (e.g., "because the owners keep everything the workers
produce").

**Guided practice (C1–C3):**

- C1. Apprentice → hired/skilled; spinning woman → family; passage worker
  → indentured; girl born on plantation → enslaved.
- C2. Accept: "Indentured servitude was a temporary contract — usually
  4–7 years of work, often for the ocean passage, ending in freedom and
  freedom dues. Enslavement was lifelong and hereditary: forced, unpaid,
  and passed to children by law."
- C3. Two forms + what each shows, e.g.: spirituals — faith and hope
  kept alive under oppression; Anansi tales — West African culture
  preserved and passed to children; slowing work / breaking tools —
  everyday refusal to cooperate fully; running away — the ultimate
  rejection of the system, at great risk.

**Exit check:** 1. Family, hired/skilled (apprentices), indentured,
enslaved. 2. Same as C2 (two sentences). 3. Cause: labor-hungry cash
crops with too few willing workers (plus 1619 showing forced labor was
possible; planters seeking West African rice expertise). Effect:
plantation economy built on enslaved labor; profits resting on unpaid
work. 4. Any resistance form + what it shows (see C3).

**Practice C (items C4–C9):**

- C4. The Middle Passage was the forced voyage across the Atlantic
  carrying captive Africans to the Americas to be enslaved. (Plain,
  2–3 sentences; no graphic detail required.)
- C5. Same as guided C3 — any two forms with "what it shows" sentences.
- C6. Indentured: chose (signed contract); 4–7 years; could not leave
  during contract, free after; no wages but freedom dues at end.
  Enslaved: no choice (forced); for life and children too; could not
  leave (punished by law); paid nothing ever.
- C7. Cause → effect: West African peoples held generations of
  rice-growing expertise (flooding, draining, processing) = the human
  capital Carolina plantations needed → planters paid high prices for
  Rice Coast captives → Carolina's rice economy boomed on stolen
  expertise.
- C8. Kept assignment: modeled rows are given; learner rows must include
  (a) one resistance form + what it shows; (b) then/now: two differences
  (e.g., no choice of work, no pay, no schooling vs. free child's chores
  and lessons) + one similarity (e.g., both did chores, both played);
  (c) one open supporting question (e.g., "Why did some colonies use
  more enslaved labor than others?") — yes/no questions are not
  acceptable; source kinds named in D1.5 terms.
- C9. Weekly check: (1) four kinds; (2) C2 sentence pair; (3) one cause +
  one effect; (4) one resistance form + what it shows.

## Lesson 04 — Perspectives

**Readiness check:** 1. Temporary contract vs. lifelong/hereditary/forced.
2. A point of view shaped by who you are and what you want. 3. No —
different positions (owner vs. forced laborer) give different experiences
and stakes.

**Guided practice (D1–D3):**

- D1. Account A (merchant): speaks as a distant owner of the cargo; saw
  prices and ships, not the fields; wants a fast voyage and strong price;
  missing: everyone who grew, loaded, and will buy the tobacco. Account B
  (enslaved worker): speaks from inside the labor; saw the heat and hours;
  the stake is the unfairness — profit without pay; missing: the same
  list, plus the merchant's own view.
- D2. Master's perspective: the notice protects his investment — the
  servant owes him contracted labor (reason: the contract + the money he
  paid for the passage). Servant's perspective: running away is a bid for
  freedom from harsh terms (reason: the work or treatment felt unbearable,
  or the contract felt unjust). Both perspectives get one reason each.
- D3. Any two with a "seen differently" sentence, e.g.: an enslaved
  porter — the market as a workplace, not a shopping trip; a Lenape
  trader — the market on land that was once his people's; a colonial girl
  — the market as one of few public outings.

**Exit check:** 1. Who is speaking (and their place)? What did they
experience/see? What do they want (what's at stake)? Whose voice is
missing? 2. "The merchant's stake is profit and the worker's experience
is unpaid labor — different positions give different accounts of the same
shipment." 3. "It reminds the historian that every set of accounts is
incomplete, and the missing voices might change the picture." 4. "The
settler's context (charter, fenced-farm idea of land) made the shore look
empty and available; the Powhatan context (generations of homeland use)
made it look taken."

**Practice D (items D4–D9):**

- D4. Accept a paragraph applying the four questions to each view, e.g.:
  the farmer speaks as a town member wanting a gathering place (stake:
  community life); the widow speaks as a poor taxpayer who experiences
  the tax as hardship (stake: feeding her family); missing: the
  carpenter who would be paid to build it.
- D5. (a) Tax collector: the smuggling is theft from the king's revenue —
  his job and income depend on collecting. (b) Smuggler: the night
  landing is honest business dodging an unfair tax — his profit depends
  on avoiding it. (c) Enslaved dock worker: the event is night work he
  did not choose — his stake is surviving it, not the sugar's price.
- D6. Same as exit check 4 — require the word **context** used correctly
  (the background that gives each view its meaning).
- D7. Two-voice account: check (a) two accounts clearly differ by
  speaker's place/stake; (b) labeled as the learner's own writing;
  (c) facts consistent with Lessons 01–03; (d) four-questions paragraph
  present including a missing voice.
- D8. Weekly check: (1) four questions; (2) differing-accounts sentence;
  (3) missing-voice sentence.
- D9. Challenge: accept agree or disagree with one unit example and
  reasoning. Strong answers land near: disagree — fairness means hearing
  every perspective and weighing with evidence (e.g., the enslaver's
  perspective explains the system but does not justify it).

## Lesson 05 — Port-Town Trade Investigation

**Readiness check:** 1. Within a region / between colonies / across the
Atlantic. 2. A harbor town where ships load and unload. 3. Any reasonable
prediction.

**Observation task:** accept any three genuine see → infer → wonder rows,
e.g.: see tall-masted ships at docks → infer ships are the town's
lifeline for heavy goods; see stacked barrels/crates → infer large trade
volume; see warehouses with chimneys → infer storage and workshops behind
the docks; see the town on the hill → infer the port supports a whole
community. "What can't it tell us?": who loaded the barrels (free,
indentured, enslaved); destinations; the year; whether workers were paid;
what the barrels hold. (Any two are sufficient.)

**Trace-one-good:** Rum — (1) distilled in New England from Caribbean
molasses; (2) shipped to West Africa and traded; (3) exchanged ultimately
for goods/people that feed the triangle; (4) profits return to New England
merchants. Sugar — (1) grown in the Caribbean with enslaved labor; (2)
shipped to New England; (3) made into rum or sold; (4) profits to
merchants/planters. Tobacco — (1) grown in Virginia with enslaved labor;
(2) packed and shipped to Britain; (3) sold in London; (4) profits to
planter, ship owner, merchant. Who benefited / who was harmed: merchants,
planters, ship owners benefited through profit; enslaved Africans were
harmed — captured, transported by force, worked without pay or freedom.

**Exit check:** 1. Any two supported inferences + one thing the image
cannot show. 2. "A common pattern of trade routes linking New England,
West Africa, and the Caribbean/Americas." 3. "Because it describes a
pattern many voyages followed, not a rule every ship obeyed."

## Unit Assessment — solutions and scoring notes

**A1.** (4) Three regions + two colonies each — 1 pt per region (both
colonies correct). **A2.** (4) One region: 2 pts for two correct
environmental facts, 2 pts for correctly linking environment → settlement
pattern and → livelihood. **A3.** (2) One self-rule practice (Mayflower
Compact 1620 / House of Burgesses 1619 / town meetings) + one
why-it-matters sentence (roots of American self-government → Constitution
in U04).

**B1.** (4) Four labor kinds, one accurate sentence each. **B2.** (3) The
plain difference (2 pts — must include temporary/contract vs.
lifelong/hereditary/forced) + why "who chose?" matters (1 pt — it reveals
freedom vs. force, the moral and legal core). **B3.** (3) One cause + one
effect that connect (e.g., labor-hungry cash crops → too few willing
workers → laws making enslavement permanent → plantation economy on
enslaved labor). **B4.** (2) Two differences + one similarity, both
plausible.

**C1.** (3) One correct item per resource kind for flour. **C2.** (3) Must
use *specialize* and *trade* correctly: Middle colonies' land grew wheat
best → focusing on wheat and trading the surplus beat making everything
poorly. **C3.** (4) Four ordered steps (field → barn → hogshead →
river/port → ship → London; any four correct consecutive steps); 1 pt for
naming a worker + labor kind at one step; 1 pt for a final profit sentence
naming who profited. **C4.** (2) Money sentence + profit-influence
sentence (see B6).

**D1.** (4) Per account: who is speaking + place (1 pt), what they want /
what's at stake (1 pt). Shopkeeper: German immigrant merchant; stake =
market-day income and community trade. Porter: enslaved laborer; stake =
enduring forced work he cannot refuse. **D2.** (3) Why the accounts differ
(1 pt — different positions/experiences/stakes) + one context sentence per
account (2 pts). **D3.** (2) One missing voice (e.g., the farmer who grew
the wheat, a Lenape trader, a colonial child) + one "seen differently"
sentence. **D4.** (2) One open supporting question (not yes/no) + one
fitting source kind (e.g., "What did market day sound and smell like?"
→ a traveler's diary / a merchant's account book — adult-selected).

## Project exemplar notes

Use the rubric in [project.md](project.md). A proficient submission shows
all four deliverables; the trade chain is ordered and labeled; labor kinds
are correct for ≥3 steps; the profit/harm paragraph names beneficiaries
and harmed people plainly; the two voices differ by speaker's place and
stake; the four-questions paragraph names a missing voice; the supporting
question is genuinely open and the source kinds fit it. Common deductions:
chain steps out of order (−1 chain accuracy); "workers" unnamed by labor
kind (−1 labor honesty); two accounts that sound like the same person
(−1 perspectives); yes/no supporting question (−1 sources question).
