# Lesson 01 — Three Colonial Regions

**Unit:** [U02 — Colonial Societies: Labor, Trade, and Diverse Perspectives](../README.md) · **Week 7 (S1–S2)**
**Time:** two ~40-minute sessions (S1 launch + guided practice, S2 independent
practice + application).
**Student goal:** I can name the three colonial regions and their colonies,
explain how each region's environment shaped how people settled and lived,
and compare colonial daily life to life today.

**Materials:** Atlantic-coast colonies map (any accurate school map showing
the 13 colonies grouped by region); [Practice bank](../practice.md) region
set; notebook.

## Readiness check (S1 opening, ~5 minutes)

2. What is a **region**? (An area grouped together because places in it
   share characteristics like landforms, climate, or culture.)
3. Predict: how might rocky soil and a long, cold winter change how people
   earn a living? (Answers will vary; revisit after the lesson.)

## The big idea: the same coast, three different lives

Between 1607 and 1732, English settlers founded thirteen colonies along
North America's Atlantic coast. The colonists did not all live the same
way. Look at a map: the coast stretches from cold, rocky New England in
the north to warm, flat lands in the south. Just as environment shaped
Indigenous nations' ways of life in Unit 01, it shaped the colonists' —
what they could grow, what they could build, and how they made a living.

Historians group the thirteen into **three regions**: New England, the
Middle colonies, and the Southern colonies. Each region has its own
environment, its own economy, and its own daily rhythm.

### New England — forests, harbors, and towns

**Colonies:** New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut.
(Settled first at Plymouth in 1620; the Massachusetts Bay Colony followed
in 1630.)

**Environment:** thin, rocky soil; short growing season; long, cold
winters; thick forests; deep natural harbors and a long coastline.

**Economy:** Farming was hard — the rocky soil grew mostly enough for a
family to eat. So New Englanders turned to the sea and the forest: fishing
(cod was a huge catch), whaling, cutting timber, building ships, and
trading. New England became the colonies' shipyard: its ships carried
goods up and down the coast and across the Atlantic.

**Daily life:** People lived close together in towns, often around a
meeting house where the community gathered. Because towns made decisions
together, New England developed **town meetings** — gatherings where
(mostly adult male) townspeople voted on local rules. Daily life was
organized around family, church, and the rhythms of the sea and seasons.

### The Middle colonies — the "breadbasket"

**Colonies:** New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware.

**Environment:** rich, fertile soil; moderate climate; longer growing
season than New England; broad, navigable rivers (the Hudson, Delaware,
and Susquehanna).

**Economy:** This land grew grain — wheat, barley, and rye — so well that
the region earned the nickname **"breadbasket colonies."** Farmers grew
more than their families needed and sold the surplus; mills ground grain
into flour. The region also had ironworks, livestock farms, and busy
ports. Its people were the most diverse of any region: Dutch settlers in
New Netherland (which England took over in 1664 and renamed New York),
English Quakers in Pennsylvania (founded 1681 by William Penn, who promised
religious tolerance), plus German, Scots-Irish, and other families.

**Daily life:** a patchwork of farms, market towns, and growing port
cities — Philadelphia and New York City. Neighbors might speak different
languages and worship in different churches.

### The Southern colonies — plantations and cash crops

**Colonies:** Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia.
(Virginia's Jamestown, founded 1607, was the first permanent English
settlement; Georgia, founded 1732, was the last of the thirteen.)

**Environment:** warm climate; long growing season; rich coastal soil;
wide, slow tidal rivers reaching far inland.

**Economy:** The long growing season made it possible to grow **cash
crops** — crops raised mainly to be sold. Virginia and Maryland grew
tobacco; South Carolina and Georgia grew rice and indigo (a plant used to
make blue dye). Growing these crops on a huge scale required **plantations**:
very large farms worked by many laborers. Most of that labor was done by
enslaved people — you will study this honestly in Lesson 03.

**Daily life:** more spread out than New England towns — plantations sat
miles apart along rivers, which carried crops to port. Social life
centered on the plantation household, the county courthouse, and the
Anglican parish church.

### A thread that runs through all three: ruling themselves

Even under a distant king, colonists practiced making their own rules:
the **Mayflower Compact** (1620), in which Plymouth's settlers agreed to
form a government and obey its laws; the **House of Burgesses** (1619) in
Virginia, the first assembly of elected representatives in the English
colonies; and New England's town meetings. These early experiments in
self-rule are roots you will trace all the way to the Constitution in
Unit 04.

### Worked example 1: Why did New England become shipbuilders?

**Question:** Why did New Englanders build ships instead of growing tobacco
like Virginia?

**Modeled reasoning:** Start with the environment. New England has rocky
soil, a short growing season, and cold winters — poor conditions for a
labor-hungry cash crop like tobacco. But it has two things in abundance:
tall, straight trees (timber, masts, tar) and deep harbors. Add human
capital: sailors, carpenters, and merchants. So New Englanders specialized
in what their environment made easiest — fishing, timber, shipbuilding,
and carrying other people's goods. Virginia's warm climate and long season
made tobacco the easier choice there. **The pattern:** environment → what
grows or is available → what people specialize in.

**Check yourself:** Which mistake would a careless reader make here?
Thinking "New Englanders were just better at building ships." The lesson
is not about who was better — it is about how environment opened some
doors and closed others.

### Worked example 2: Which region fits a wheat farmer?

**Question:** A family arriving in 1700 wants to grow wheat to sell. Which
region should they choose, and why?

**Modeled reasoning:** Wheat needs fertile soil and a growing season long
enough for grain to ripen — but it does not need the extreme heat of a
cash-crop climate. The Middle colonies have the richest soil of the three
regions, a moderate climate, and rivers to carry grain to mills and ports.
New England's soil is too rocky; the Southern colonies' economy is built
around plantations growing tobacco and rice. So the family should choose
the Middle colonies. **Notice the method:** list what the crop needs, then
match those needs against each region's environment.

## Guided practice (S1, with the adult)

Use the region set in the [practice bank](../practice.md):

1. Sort the 13 colonies into the three regions from memory, then check
   against the map. (Prompts: name all four New England colonies; all
   four Middle colonies; all five Southern colonies.)
2. For each region, name one environmental feature and one way people
   earned a living that fits that feature. (Hint: use the pattern
   environment → available resources → work.)
3. True or false, and fix the false ones: "All thirteen colonies had the
   same economy." / "The Middle colonies were called the breadbasket
   because of their wheat." / "Southern plantations grew mostly food for
   the planter's own family."

## Applied task: then and now (S2, finish in Week 7 S3 if needed)

Compare one colonial day to one of your days. Choose a colonial child in
one region and write two short paragraphs: (1) describe their day using at
least three facts from this lesson (chores, food, travel, town or
plantation life); (2) describe your day, then name two differences and one
similarity. (D2.His.2.3-5)

## Exit check (end of S2)

1. Name the three colonial regions and two colonies in each.
2. In one sentence, explain why New England specialized in shipbuilding
   rather than cash crops.
3. Name one way colonists practiced ruling themselves, and one sentence on
   why it matters for later units.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult pre-reads the region descriptions aloud;
  the learner follows on the map, pointing to each region as it is named.
- **Alternate response mode:** the then/now task may be drawn as two
  labeled panels (a colonial day / a today day) with a dictated caption
  scribed by the adult.
- **Extension:** research one real colonial port city (Boston, New York,
  Philadelphia, or Charleston) and explain in a paragraph why its location
  made it a good port — harbor depth, rivers, and what goods passed
  through. (Feeds Lesson 05.)

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** learners merge "the colonies" into one place
  with one way of life. Keep pressing the environment → economy link and
  make them name the region before answering any "colonial life" question.
- **Likely misconception:** "colony" vs. "state." The colonies were ruled
  by Britain; they became states only after independence. Correct gently
  and often.
