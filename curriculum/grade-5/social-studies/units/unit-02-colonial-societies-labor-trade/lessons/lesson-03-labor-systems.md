# Lesson 03 — Labor: Who Worked, and Who Chose

**Unit:** [U02 — Colonial Societies: Labor, Trade, and Diverse Perspectives](../README.md) · **Week 9 (S1–S2)**
**Time:** two ~40-minute sessions (S1 launch + guided practice, S2 independent
practice + application).
**Student goal:** I can describe the kinds of labor in the colonies, explain
plainly the difference between indentured servitude and enslavement, and
explain who did which work and why.

**Materials:** [Practice bank](../practice.md) labor set; notebook.

**A note before we begin (read aloud by the adult):** This lesson talks
about enslaved labor — people who were forced to work without pay or
freedom. That is a hard, true part of American history, and this unit tells
it honestly and plainly. We will not use graphic detail. We will focus on
how the system worked, on the different perspectives of the people in it,
and on the people who resisted. If anything feels upsetting, it is okay to
pause and talk about it.

## The big idea: four kinds of workers, one hard question

In the colonies, work was done by four kinds of labor. The question that
matters most about each kind is simple: **who chose?**

1. **Family labor.** Most colonial families farmed, and everyone worked —
   parents, children, grandparents. Children had chores from a young age:
   fetching water, feeding animals, spinning, helping at harvest. Nobody
   was paid; the family survived together.
2. **Hired and skilled labor.** Blacksmiths, coopers (barrel-makers),
   carpenters, weavers, and sailors sold their skill for wages or took on
   **apprentices** — young people who worked for a master craftsman for
   years to learn a trade.
3. **Indentured servants.** Many poor Europeans wanted to come to America
   but could not pay for the voyage. A captain or merchant paid their
   passage, and in return the servant signed a **contract** — usually 4
   to 7 years of work. When the contract ended, the servant became free,
   and many received "freedom dues": some clothes, tools, or a small plot
   of land. Servitude was hard and sometimes cruel — but it was
   **temporary**, and the servant had signed the contract.
4. **Enslaved labor.** Enslaved people — the great majority of them
   African — were **forced** to work. They signed nothing. Enslavement
   was **lifelong**, and the law made it **hereditary**: children born to
   an enslaved mother were born enslaved. Enslaved people could be bought
   and sold like property, were paid nothing, and could not legally leave.
   This is the plain legal and human difference between servitude and
   enslavement: **one ends; the other was designed never to end.**

### Why did enslavement grow in the colonies?

Cause and effect, step by step (D2.His.14.3-5):

- **Cause:** Southern plantations grew labor-hungry cash crops — tobacco,
  rice, indigo — on thousands of acres. There were never enough willing
  workers.
- **Cause:** In 1619, a ship called the *White Lion* brought "20 and odd"
  captive Africans to Virginia. Colonists discovered they could force
  people to work for life — and wrote laws making it permanent and
  hereditary.
- **Cause:** Growing rice, in particular, required expert knowledge of
  flooding fields and water control. Many enslaved Africans came from
  West Africa's "Rice Coast," where their peoples had grown rice for
  generations. Planters specifically sought their expertise — the
  Southern economy depended on African knowledge even as it denied
  African freedom.
- **Effect:** By the 1700s, enslaved labor was the foundation of the
  Southern colonial economy — and a growing part of the Middle colonies'
  as well. The profits from Lesson 02's tobacco chain rested on unpaid
  labor.

### The Middle Passage

Enslaved Africans were captured, marched to the coast, and forced aboard
ships for the **Middle Passage** — the voyage across the Atlantic to the
Americas. We name it plainly and we do not dwell on its horrors at this
age; what matters for this unit is the system: human beings treated as
cargo in a trade network, feeding the plantations that fed the profits.

### Resistance: people fought back

Enslaved people were never passive. They resisted in ways large and
small, and this unit names that resistance as part of the story:

- **Keeping culture alive:** spirituals (songs of faith and hope),
  stories like the Anansi spider tales carried from West Africa, family
  bonds fiercely protected.
- **Quiet resistance:** slowing work, breaking tools, pretending not to
  understand orders.
- **Open resistance:** running away to free communities, and — rarely,
  at great risk — armed revolts.

Resistance is evidence of something important: enslaved people never
accepted that the system was right.

### Worked example 1: Two workers, two contracts

**Question:** In 1720, Thomas signs as an indentured servant for 5 years,
and Amina is purchased as an enslaved worker. Compare their situations
using the question "who chose?"

**Modeled reasoning:** Ask four questions about each worker:

| Question | Thomas (indentured) | Amina (enslaved) |
|---|---|---|
| Who chose this? | Thomas signed the contract himself. | No one asked Amina; she was forced. |
| For how long? | 5 years — then free, with freedom dues. | For life — and her children too. |
| Can they leave? | Not during the contract, but afterward, yes. | No — running away is punished by law. |
| Are they paid? | Not in wages, but the contract promises an end and a start in freedom. | Nothing, ever. |

**Conclusion:** Both situations are hard, but they are not the same.
Indentured servitude was a temporary contract, however unfair; enslavement
was a lifelong system built on force and race, designed to be permanent.
**The method to reuse:** when comparing two historical situations, ask the
same questions of each — especially *who chose?* and *could they leave?*

### Worked example 2: Why rice planters wanted African expertise

**Question:** South Carolina planters paid high prices for enslaved people
from West Africa's Rice Coast. Why those workers specifically?

**Modeled reasoning:** Rice is a difficult crop: fields must be flooded
and drained at exactly the right times, and the grain must be processed
skillfully. West African peoples had grown rice this way for centuries —
they held the **human capital** (Lesson 02's term) that made Carolina
rice plantations possible. Planters were not just buying labor; they were
stealing expertise. **Cause:** African rice-growing knowledge →
**effect:** planters sought Rice Coast captives → **effect:** Carolina's
rice economy boomed on knowledge its owners refused to credit. Notice how
this connects to Unit 01's big idea: human capital matters as much as
soil and tools.

## Teacher note

- **Sensitive-content guidance:** keep language plain and factual; do not
  add graphic detail or ask the learner to imagine themselves enslaved
  ("how would you feel as a slave?" centers the wrong perspective and
  can distress). The unit's stance: name the system, honor the
  resistance, and keep the focus on causes, effects, and perspectives.
- **Likely misconception:** "Slavery was only in the South." Correct
  gently: enslaved labor existed in all thirteen colonies, including New
  England and the Middle colonies — it was *largest* in the plantation
  South.
- **Likely misconception:** "Indentured servants were basically slaves."
  Use Worked example 1's table: acknowledge the hardship, then insist on
  the plain legal difference (temporary contract vs. lifelong hereditary
  force).
- **Likely misconception:** "Enslaved people just accepted it." The
  resistance section exists to prevent this; return to it whenever the
  misconception surfaces.
