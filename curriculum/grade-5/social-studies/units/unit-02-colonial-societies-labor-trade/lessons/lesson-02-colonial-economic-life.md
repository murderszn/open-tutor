# Lesson 02 — Colonial Economic Life: Resources, Specialization, and Trade

**Unit:** [U02 — Colonial Societies: Labor, Trade, and Diverse Perspectives](../README.md) · **Week 8 (S1–S2)**
**Time:** two ~40-minute sessions (S1 launch + guided practice, S2 independent
practice + application).
**Student goal:** I can name the three kinds of resources behind colonial
goods, explain why regions specialized and traded, and describe how money
and profit shaped colonial choices.

**Materials:** [Practice bank](../practice.md) economy set; notebook; the

## Readiness check (S1 opening, ~5 minutes)

1. Name one good each region was known for. (New England: fish, ships,
   timber; Middle: wheat/flour; Southern: tobacco, rice, indigo.)
2. In your own words: what does it mean to **specialize**? (To focus on
   producing what you can make best or most easily, then trade for the
   rest.)
3. Predict: if New England makes ships but cannot grow wheat, and the
   Middle colonies grow wheat but need ships — what should they do?
   (Trade with each other.)

## The big idea: three kinds of resources, one connected economy

Every good the colonists made — a barrel of flour, a ship's mast, a
hogshead of tobacco — came from **three kinds of resources** working
together:

- **Natural resources:** gifts of the environment — fertile soil, tall
  trees, fish-filled waters, navigable rivers, a warm climate.
- **Human capital:** people's skills and knowledge — farmers who knew
  wheat, shipwrights who knew hulls, sailors who knew the winds,
  merchants who knew markets. (And, honestly, the forced labor of
  enslaved people — Lesson 03.)
- **Physical capital:** the tools and structures people built — plows,
  mills, forges, wharves, warehouses, and the ships themselves.

No region had all three in the right mix for everything. So each region
**specialized** in what its resources made easiest, and then **traded**
for the rest. The Middle colonies grew more wheat than they could eat and
sold the surplus; New England built more ships than it needed and carried
other colonies' goods; the Southern colonies grew tobacco and rice the
world wanted and bought food, tools, and cloth in return.

### Trade routes: near and far

Trade moved in widening circles:

1. **Within a region:** a farmer sold wheat to a miller; the miller sold
   flour to a baker and a ship's captain.
2. **Between colonies:** New England ships carried Southern tobacco and
   Middle-colony flour up and down the coast.
3. **Across the Atlantic:** colonial ships carried raw materials —
   tobacco, rice, indigo, timber, tar, fish — to Britain and Europe, and
   returned loaded with manufactured goods: cloth, tools, tea, glass,
   guns, and books the colonies could not make well themselves.

### The rules of the game: mercantilism

Britain set the rules. Under a policy called **mercantilism**, the
colonies existed to make Britain richer: they were expected to send raw
materials to Britain and buy finished British goods. Laws called the
**Navigation Acts** said that most colonial goods had to travel on
British (or colonial) ships and pass through British ports, where taxes
were collected. Colonists could not freely trade with France or Spain,
even when those countries offered better prices.

Was this fair? Colonists disagreed about that — and their disagreement
is one of the roots of the Revolution you will study in Unit 03. For now,
notice the cause-and-effect: Britain's rules shaped **what** colonists
produced and **where** they could sell it.

### Money makes exchange easier

Imagine trading without money: a shoemaker who needs wheat must find a
wheat farmer who happens to need shoes — and agree on how many bushels a
pair of shoes is worth. Colonists did **barter** like this, but it is
slow and awkward. **Money** — coins (often Spanish or British, and often
scarce) and eventually colonial paper money — made exchange easier: sell
your shoes for coins, then spend the coins on wheat whenever you like.
Money does not have to be valuable in itself; it works because everyone
agrees to accept it.

### Profit points the way

A **profit** is what a seller keeps after paying every cost — seeds,
tools, labor, shipping, taxes. Profits influenced choices: when tobacco
prices were high, Virginia planters planted more tobacco; when prices
fell, some switched crops. Merchants chose which goods to carry by asking
"where is the profit greatest?" This is neither good nor evil — it is a
cause-and-effect you can trace: **high expected profit → more sellers
choose that good → supply grows.**

### Worked example 1: The journey of a tobacco hogshead

**Question:** Trace a hogshead (a large barrel) of Virginia tobacco from
field to London buyer. At each step, name the resources used and who
profits.

**Modeled reasoning:**

1. **Field (Virginia):** natural resource — warm climate, rich soil;
   human capital — planting and curing knowledge; labor — mostly enslaved
   workers (Lesson 03 explains why). The planter pays the costs of seeds,
   tools, and labor.
2. **Packing:** the dried tobacco is pressed into a hogshead; physical
   capital — the cooper's barrels, the press.
3. **To the river:** rolled or wagon-hauled to a wharf on a tidal river;
   the river carries it cheaply to port — natural resource doing the
   work of roads.
4. **Across the Atlantic:** loaded onto a British merchant ship (the
   Navigation Acts require it); the ship's captain and owner take a share
   for freight.
5. **London:** sold to a tobacco merchant, who sells to shops. Each
   handler adds a cost and takes a cut.

**Who profits?** The planter profits if the London price covers all
costs; the ship owner profits from freight; the London merchant profits
from resale. The enslaved workers who grew the tobacco are paid nothing —
profit flows to everyone except the people who did the hardest work.
**That gap between who works and who profits is the unit's hardest and
most important pattern.** (D2.Eco.3, 7.3-5; D2.His.14.3-5)

### Worked example 2: Why specialize? A Middle-colony miller

**Question:** A Pennsylvania family could grow their own food, make their
own cloth, and build their own furniture. Why did many families instead
grow mostly wheat and buy everything else?

**Modeled reasoning:** List the benefits and costs (D2.Eco.1.3-5). The
family's land grows wheat better than almost anything else — that is
their advantage. An hour spent growing wheat earns more, once sold, than
an hour spent weaving rough cloth. By specializing in wheat and trading
the surplus for cloth, tools, and tea, the family ends up with **more of
everything** than if each household made all its own goods poorly. The
cost: they depend on traders and on wheat prices. **The principle:**
specialization plus trade lets everyone consume beyond what they could
make alone — at the price of depending on others.

## Guided practice (S1, with the adult)

Use the economy set in the [practice bank](../practice.md):

1. For each of these goods — a ship's mast, a barrel of flour, a bundle
   of indigo — name one natural resource, one piece of human capital, and
   one piece of physical capital behind it. (Prompt: "What did nature
   give? What did people know? What tools did they use?")
2. Explain in two sentences why the Middle colonies and New England
   traded with each other instead of each producing everything.
   (Hint: use the word **specialize**.)
3. A colonial shopkeeper must choose between stocking British cloth and
   colonial-made cloth. British cloth costs her more but customers will
   pay more for it. Walk through her profit thinking aloud. (Prompt:
   "What are her costs? What price can she charge? Where is the profit?")

## Applied task: plan a trade (S2, finish in Week 8 S3 if needed)

You are a Philadelphia merchant in 1750 with a warehouse of flour and a
small ship. Plan one trading voyage: (1) where will you sail and why —
choose using the regions' specializations; (2) what will you carry there
and what will you bring back; (3) name one British trade rule that
affects your plan; (4) where do you expect the most profit, and what
could go wrong? Write it as a one-page merchant's plan. (D2.Eco.1, 4,
7.3-5)

## Exit check (end of S2)

1. Name the three kinds of resources and give one colonial example of each.
2. In one sentence, explain why specialization leads to trade.
3. In one sentence, explain how money makes exchange easier than barter.
4. Name one British trade rule and one way it shaped what colonists produced.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the worked examples aloud while the
  learner traces each step on paper, numbering the chain 1–5.
- **Alternate response mode:** the merchant's plan may be drawn as a
  labeled voyage map (port → route → port, goods in each direction) with
  a dictated profit sentence scribed by the adult.
- **Extension:** compare colonial mercantilism to a modern trade rule the
  adult names (for example, a tariff). In a paragraph, explain who the
  rule helps, who it hurts, and how you know. (Keep it to one rule; the
  adult supplies the facts.)

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** "Trade means everyone benefits equally." Push
  back with Worked example 1's ending: ask who is missing from the profit
  chain and why that matters.
- **Likely misconception:** learners treat "the economy" as modern and
  separate from daily life. Keep anchoring every abstract term (profit,
  specialization, money) to a concrete colonial good.
- **Vocabulary watch:** *mercantilism* and *Navigation Acts* are
  labels for patterns, not people. Make sure learners can explain the
  pattern without the label first.
