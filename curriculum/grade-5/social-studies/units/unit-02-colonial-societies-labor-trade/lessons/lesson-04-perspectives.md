# Lesson 04 — Two Viewpoints, One Event

**Unit:** [U02 — Colonial Societies: Labor, Trade, and Diverse Perspectives](../README.md) · **Week 10 (S1–S2)**
**Time:** two ~40-minute sessions (S1 launch + guided practice, S2 independent
practice + application).
**Student goal:** I can explain why people in the same time period saw the
same event differently, name each perspective and its reasons, and connect
a perspective to its historical context.

**Materials:** [Practice bank](../practice.md) perspective set; notebook.

## Readiness check (S1 opening, ~5 minutes)

1. From Lesson 03: state the plain difference between indentured
   servitude and enslavement. (Temporary contract vs. lifelong,
   hereditary, forced.)
2. What is a **perspective**? (A point of view — how a person sees an
   event, shaped by who they are and what they want.)
3. Predict: would a Virginia planter and an enslaved field worker describe
   a tobacco harvest the same way? Why or why not? (No — their positions
   in the system give them different experiences and different stakes.)

## The big idea: every event has more than one witness

A **perspective** is not just an opinion. It is a whole point of view,
shaped by four things:

- **Who you are:** a planter, an enslaved worker, a sailor, a Powhatan
  hunter, a colonial child.
- **What you experience:** what you actually see and live through each day.
- **What you want:** freedom, profit, safety, land, a full harvest.
- **What you know:** what your community has taught you about how the
  world works.

Historians do not pick the "right" witness and ignore the rest. They
**compare accounts**: they ask who is speaking, what that person wants,
what they saw, and whose voice is missing — and then they build the
fullest picture they can. When two accounts of the same event disagree,
that disagreement is itself evidence: it tells you what was at stake.

**A historian's four questions** (use these on every account in this
lesson):

1. **Who** is speaking, and what is their place in the story?
2. **What** did they experience — what did they actually see?
3. **What** do they want — what is at stake for them?
4. **Whose voice is missing** — who else was there that we have not heard?

### Worked example 1: One tobacco shipment, two accounts

**The event:** In 1750, a ship leaves Virginia loaded with tobacco for
London. (The two short accounts below are **original teaching material
written for this lesson** — they model how perspectives differ. They are
not historical documents.)

**Account A — the London merchant, writing to his partner:**

> "The *Good Fortune* sails with 200 hogsheads of fine Virginia leaf.
> Prices in London are strong this season. If the voyage goes well, our
> profit will be the best in three years. I have instructed the captain to
> make all haste."

**Account B — an enslaved field worker, speaking years later:**

> "We worked the tobacco from sunup to sundown, in heat that made the air
> swim. When the ship sailed, the master celebrated. We went back to the
> fields. None of that profit ever touched our hands."

**Modeled reasoning:** Apply the four questions.

- **Account A:** Who? A merchant — far from the fields, close to the
  money. What did he experience? Prices, ships, and profits — not the
  growing. What does he want? A fast voyage and a strong price. His
  account is about **profit** because profit is his stake.
- **Account B:** Who? An enslaved worker — inside the labor, outside the
  profit. What did they experience? The heat, the hours, the work itself.
  What do they want? The account does not ask for anything directly, but
  its last sentence is a claim: the system was unfair. Their account is
  about **labor** because labor is their experience.
- **Whose voice is missing?** The ship's captain, the indentured dock
  worker, the Virginia planter, the London shopkeeper — each would add a
  different piece.

**The point:** neither account is "lying." Each is true to what its
speaker saw and wanted. But either account **alone** gives you only half
the event. The full picture needs both — plus the missing voices.

### Worked example 2: Settlers arrive — two views of the same shore

**The event:** English settlers build Jamestown in 1607 on land where
Powhatan peoples have lived for generations.

**View 1 — an English settler (modeled on settler letters of the period):**
"We have planted a town in a fruitful land. The woods are full of game,
the rivers full of fish. With God's help we will build a new England
here."

**View 2 — a Powhatan person (modeled on what we know of Indigenous
responses to English settlement):** "Strangers have built wooden houses
on our hunting grounds. They fence the land and will not share the river
as we do. They take corn when their own stores run out, yet call us
thieves when we take back what is ours."

**Modeled reasoning:** Same event, different context (D2.His.5.3-5). The
settler sees **empty land waiting to be used** — because in his context,
land that is not fenced and farmed looks unused, and his charter says the
king gave it to him. The Powhatan person sees **homeland being taken** —
because in her context, the land has been hunted, fished, and managed for
generations, and sharing the river is the law of the place. The
disagreement is not about facts (both agree settlers built houses); it is
about what those facts **mean** — and meaning comes from context. This is
why Unit 01 taught Indigenous nations as living communities with
homelands: the perspective only makes sense if you know whose land it was.

**Check yourself:** A careless reader concludes "the settler was wrong
and the Powhatan person was right" — or the reverse. The historian's move
is different: explain **why each saw it that way**, using their context
as evidence. Judgment comes later, and it must be built on both views,
not one.

## Guided practice (S1, with the adult)

Use the perspective set in the [practice bank](../practice.md):

1. Re-read Account A and Account B from Worked example 1. For each,
   answer the four historian's questions aloud. (Prompt: "Who is
   speaking? What did they see? What do they want? Who is missing?")
2. A colonial newspaper prints a notice offering a reward for a runaway
   indentured servant. (The adult reads the modeled notice in the
   practice bank.) Name two perspectives on this notice — the master who
   placed it and the servant who ran — and give one reason for each
   perspective.
3. "Whose voice is missing?" — list two people who were present at a
   colonial market day but whose accounts we rarely have. (Prompts:
   enslaved porters, Indigenous traders, women shopkeepers, children.)

## Independent practice (S2)

Complete the perspective set (items 4–9) in the [practice bank](../practice.md):
four-questions drills on modeled accounts, perspective-and-reason matching,
and the "two views of town meeting" task.

## Applied task: a two-voice account (S2, finish in Week 10 S3 if needed)

Choose one colonial event from this unit: a tobacco shipment sailing, a
town meeting vote, market day in Philadelphia, or the founding of a new
plantation. Write **two short accounts of the same event** (4–6 sentences
each), each from a different person's perspective — for example, a
planter and an enslaved worker, or a merchant and a sailor. Then add a
final paragraph answering the four historian's questions about your own
two accounts: who is speaking in each, what each wants, and whose voice
is still missing. (D2.His.4, 5.3-5; D2.Civ.10.3-5)

**Rules for this task:** your accounts must be clearly labeled as your
own writing ("my account," not a real document); they must stay
consistent with the facts you learned in Lessons 01–03; and the "missing
voice" paragraph is required — no event has only two witnesses.

## Exit check (end of S2)

1. Name the historian's four questions.
2. In one sentence, explain why the merchant and the enslaved worker
   described the same shipment differently.
3. In one sentence, explain what "whose voice is missing?" adds to a
   historian's work.
4. Give one reason the Powhatan view of Jamestown differed from the
   settler view.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads each modeled account aloud with a
  different voice or posture for each speaker; the learner holds up a
  "who / what seen / what wanted / missing" card for each question.
- **Alternate response mode:** the two-voice account may be presented as
  a dialogue (two speakers, labeled) with the four-questions paragraph
  dictated to the adult.
- **Extension:** find one real primary source about colonial life in the
  [resource pack](../resource-pack.md) (adult pre-selects; e.g., the
  Mayflower Compact text or a colonial newspaper page at the Library of
  Congress). Apply the four questions to it and write a paragraph on what
  the source can and cannot tell us. (D1.5.3-5)

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** "One account is true and the other is lying."
  Redirect to the method: both can be sincere and partial; the
  historian's job is comparison, not choosing a winner.
- **Likely misconception:** "Being fair means giving every perspective
  equal weight." Not quite: fairness means hearing every perspective and
  then weighing them with evidence. An enslaver's perspective explains
  the system; it does not justify it.
- **Sensitive-content guidance:** the modeled accounts are deliberately
  plain. Do not ask the learner to role-play an enslaved person in
  distress; the two-voice task keeps the writer in the analyst's seat
  (the final paragraph), not inside someone else's suffering.
