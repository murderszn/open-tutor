# Answer Key — Unit 04: Grammar, Sentence Variety, Conventions, and Editing

For the guiding adult only. Never place this file beside a student
question. Wording may vary where the key says "sample" or "accept
any wording that states the same rule" — the rule, the fix, or the
idea must match, not the exact sentence.

## Lesson 01 — Conjunctions, prepositions, interjections

**Guided practice**

1. *Yet* is a coordinating conjunction joining two independent
   clauses ("The drums beat loudly" and "the baby slept"); it shows
   contrast with surprise. The comma is there because a comma +
   coordinating conjunction joins two independent clauses.
2. Two prepositional phrases: "over the river" (object: *river*)
   and "during the festival" (object: *festival*). Either one
   earns full marks.
3. Mild: "Wow, the lights are bright." Strong: "Wow! The lights are
   bright."
4. Comma splice. Fixes: "We bought tickets, and we found seats near
   the water." / "We bought tickets. We found seats near the water."
5. "The wind rose, so the banners waved harder."

**Independent practice**

6. Sample: "The lanterns hung **above the river**." (Phrase
   underlined; accept any sentence with a *where* phrase.)
7. Sample: "The line was long, but it moved fast." (Comma before
   *but* joining two clauses.)
8. *Or* joins two words (*Sam, Alex*), not two clauses, so the
   compound-sentence comma rule does not apply. No comma.
9. "The parade ended. The lanterns stayed lit." / "The parade
   ended, but the lanterns stayed lit."
10. *Oh* — interjection. *Through* — preposition (phrase "through
    the market"). *And* — conjunction joining two verb phrases
    ("walked … and bought …"). *But* — conjunction joining two
    independent clauses (comma before it is correct).

**Applied task:** accept any four sentences meeting the four
requirements, with jobs labeled. Check: *when* phrase, *where*
phrase, compound sentence with correct comma, interjection with
comma or exclamation mark.

## Lesson 02 — Correlative conjunctions and sentence variety

**Guided practice**

1. "Both Maya **and** Jon will carry the banner." Broken rule:
   wrong partner (*both* pairs with *and*, never *or*).
2. Sample: "Not only did the band play, but also the choir sang."
   (Parallel verb phrases.)
3. When: "At dusk, the lanterns glowed." Where: "The lanterns
   glowed above the river."
4. Sample: "When the rain stopped and the clouds parted, the parade
   began." (All three ideas kept.)
5. Sample: "The food stalls along the river sold hot, tasty food to
   hungry people."

**Independent practice**

6. Samples: "Either Maya or Jon will steer." / "Neither rain nor
   wind stopped the parade." / "Both drums and flutes played." /
   "The festival offers not only music but also food." (Each pair
   matched, halves parallel.)
7. Sample: "After Maya painted a banner and Jon hung it, the wind
   caught it."
8. Sample: "The boat drifted slowly down the wide river."
9. Sample: "The long parade through town is watched by many people
   along the street."
10. Error: not parallel — a clause ("we can watch the fireworks")
    is paired with a noun phrase ("the parade route is long" is a
    clause too, but the two halves do not match in structure or
    meaning; *either/or* must offer two choices of the same kind).
    Rewrites: "Either we can watch the fireworks or walk the parade
    route." / "We can either watch the fireworks or follow the
    parade route."

**Applied task:** accept any three-sentence rewrite that keeps all
six ideas (opens at noon; two stages; bread and soup; banner
painting; dusk lantern lighting; boats carrying lanterns), uses one
correlative pair, and shows one combined and one reduced sentence.

## Lesson 03 — Perfect verb tenses and tense consistency

**Guided practice**

1. "They **have finished**." "She **had eaten**."
2. Gathering happened first. The past perfect (*had gathered*)
   proves it: past perfect marks the earlier of two past events.
3. Not an error. Present habit ("practise every week") plus a past
   event ("performed last Friday") is a real time change — the
   clock test passes.
4. "The band played at noon and marched in the parade at two."
5. Sample: "We **will have sold** every ticket before the parade
   begins."

**Independent practice**

6. "The crew **has built** / **had built** / **will have built**."
7. *Broken*: the past participle of *break* is *broken*; *broke* is
   the simple past, and perfect tenses need the participle.
8. "She painted the sign yesterday and will hang it tomorrow."
9. Sample: "I have eaten breakfast. I had woken up before sunrise."
   (Waking is the earlier event, in past perfect.)
10. Error: "has started" is present perfect, but "by the time we
    arrived" sets a past moment — the show's starting is earlier
    than arrival, so past perfect is needed: "By the time we
    arrived, the show **had started**." The second sentence is
    correct: present perfect ("have bought," completed before now)
    plus present-tense scheduled future ("starts at seven") is a
    real time difference.

**Applied task:** accept any six-sentence report in time order with
all three perfect tenses labeled: past perfect before the gates
opened, present perfect by now, future perfect before the
fireworks.

## Lesson 04 — Commas, titles, spelling

**Guided practice**

1. "We need paint, brushes, water, and paper."
2. "After lunch, we painted banners."
3. "Jon, please pass the paint." — spoken **to** Jon (direct
   address).
4. "The festival starts at noon, doesn't it?"
5. Novel: *The Lighthouse Cat* (italics, or underlined in
   handwriting — longer work). Poem: "Rain on Tin Roofs"
   (quotation marks — shorter work).

**Independent practice**

6. "Yes, I packed apples, cheese, and juice. After the picnic,
   Maya, please bring the blanket."
7. Samples: tag — "The gates open at noon, don't they?" Direct
   address in the middle — "I think, Maya, you are right."
8. "We read the article "How Bridges Stand" and watched the movie
   *River of Lights*. It was really good."
9. "Let's eat, Grandma!" invites Grandma to eat (direct address —
   comma around the person spoken to). "Let's eat Grandma!" makes
   Grandma the thing being eaten (no comma — she is the object).
   Rule: direct address.
10. Words to check: *accommodate, necessary*. Both are spelled
    correctly in the sentence as printed — the check confirms, not
    corrects.

**Applied task:** accept any six-sentence invitation containing a
series with a serial comma, an introductory element, a yes/no
reply, a tag question, direct address, and one correctly formatted
invented title, each labeled in the margin.

## Lesson 05 — Editor's desk, varieties, formal/informal

**Guided practice**

1. Notebook — holds the draft and its revisions. Colored pencils —
   mark the different editing passes. Books — references (a
   dictionary and a grammar guide). Sticky notes — questions for
   the author.
2. "We goed to the store" — verb pass (*goed* → *went*).
   "Apples bananas and grapes" — comma pass (series commas).
   "Me and Sam runs fast" — sentence pass (subject form and
   agreement → "Sam and I run fast").
3. Sample formal version: "Dear Teacher, may I please go to the
   library? I need to finish my poster." (No slang, no "plz" or
   "gotta," complete sentences.)
4. Neither is "wrong." The character's line is a dialect — a
   consistent variety tied to a place or community. The narrator's
   line is formal/standard register. Dialect is identity, not error.
5. Passes: sentence pass — split the run-on: "The parade starts
   soon. We have not painted the banners yet." Verb pass — *has* →
   *have* (plural subject *we*).

**Independent practice**

6. Fixed paragraph: "Last week we read the novel *The Lighthouse
   Cat*. We have finished it on Friday, and we talked about apples,
   cheese, and juice at the party. It was definitely our favorite
   book." Fixes: title (italics), *has* → *have*, *friday* →
   *Friday*, comma before *and* joining two clauses, series commas,
   *definately* → *definitely*.
7. Samples: informal — "Hey! Come to the art show Thursday? It's
   gonna be awesome!" Formal — "You are invited to the class art
   show on Thursday. Student work will be on display." Facts
   identical (art show, Thursday).
8. A character's dialect follows consistent patterns shared by a
   community; it signals who the character is. A spelling error
   breaks a shared convention with no meaning of its own. One is
   variety; the other is a mistake.
9. Words to check: *principal* (not *principle*), *separate*,
   *schedule*, *exhibition*. All are spelled correctly here.
10. Lost: the character's voice, warmth, and sense of place — the
    revision sounds like a stranger, not the character. The original
    was not an error: it is a dialect line, consistent and
    meaningful, not a broken rule.

**Applied task:** the marked Practice Set F draft (see Practice Set
F key below) with one color per pass and one sticky-note question
about sentence 6's "them all."

## Practice Set A

1. a. *After* — preposition (begins "after lunch," telling when).
   b. *But* — coordinating conjunction joining two independent
   clauses (comma before it is correct). c. *Wow* — interjection
   showing strong feeling. d. *And* — coordinating conjunction
   joining two words (*Maya, Jon*); no comma.
2. Conjunctions: *and, because, or, yet, so*. Prepositions: *under,
   between, during, beside*. Interjections: *ouch, hooray, whew*.
3. a. "above the boats" — object: *boats*. b. "near the stone
   wall" — object: *wall*. c. "Before sunset" — object: *sunset*.
4. Mild: "Ouch, that hurt." / "Aha, I found it." Strong: "Ouch! That
   hurt!" / "Aha! I found it!"
5. a. Comma splice → "The band played, and the crowd danced." /
   "The band played. The crowd danced." b. Run-on → same two fixes.
   c. Correct.
6. a. "The rain stopped, so the parade began." b. "We wanted front
   seats, but we arrived late."
7. "Fish and chips" joins two words, not clauses — no comma. "Fish
   was served, and chips were served later" joins two independent
   clauses — comma + *and*.
8. Sample: preposition — "This gift is for Maya." Conjunction —
   "We waited, for the show was late." Difference: as a preposition
   *for* begins a phrase about the gift; as a conjunction *for*
   joins two clauses and means "because."

## Practice Set B

1. a. "Either Maya **or** Jon will steer." (Wrong partner.)
   b. "Both the drums and **the flutes** woke the baby" — or any
   parallel noun: "Both the drums and the flutes played loudly."
   (Not parallel: noun paired with a verb phrase.) c. "Not only did
   it rain, but also the wind blew." The halves are parallel
   (clause/clause); the error is the missing comma before *but
   also*. d. "Neither the cake nor the pie **was** eaten." (Verb
   agrees with the nearer subject, *pie*, singular.)
2. a. Sample: "Either we row **or walk**." b. Sample: "Not only did
   the choir sing, but also **the band played**." c. Sample: "Both
   the banners **and the flags waved**."
3. Samples: when — "In the morning, the boat moved." Where — "The
   boat moved across the lake." How — "The boat moved slowly."
4. a. Sample: "When the gates opened, the crowd rushed in and the
   music started." b. Sample: "Maya sewed a flag and Jon painted a
   sign, and they hung both by the gate."
5. a. Sample: "The yearly festival is enjoyed by many townspeople."
   b. Sample: "Because it rained, the parade was delayed."
6. Sample: "The market opens at nine, when bakers sell bread and
   farmers sell cheese. Both children and adults play games. At
   noon, a band plays and everyone dances." (All ideas kept; one
   correlative pair; one combined sentence.)
7. Error: verb agreement — *Maya and Jon* is a plural compound
   subject, so *likes* must be *like*. Fix: "Both Maya and Jon like
   lemonade." Rules: correlative pair correct (*both/and*),
   parallel (noun/noun), verb agrees with plural subject.
8. Samples: "Neither hot dogs nor burgers were left." / "The parade
   offers not only music but also dancing." (Pairs matched, halves
   parallel.)

## Practice Set C

1. *To paint* (she): has painted / had painted / will have
   painted. *To write* (they): have written / had written / will
   have written.
2. a. Present perfect. b. Past perfect. c. Future perfect.
   d. Simple past. e. Simple future.
3. a. *Gone* — participle of *go* is *gone*; *went* is simple past.
   b. *Written* — participle of *write* is *written*; *wrote* is
   simple past. c. *Eaten* — participle of *eat* is *eaten*; *ate*
   is simple past. d. *Broken* — participle of *break* is *broken*;
   *broke* is simple past.
4. a. The band's playing happened first; the past perfect (*had
   played*) proves it: past perfect marks the earlier of two past
   events. b. Visiting the market happened first (present perfect,
   completed before now); returning is future.
5. a. Not an error (habit + past event = real time change).
   b. Error: *paints* with *yesterday* must be *painted*; *watches
   ... now* is correct. c. Not an error (present rehearsal + future
   performance = real time change).
6. a. "The crew decorated the hall. They **hung** lights, and they
   will open the doors at six." (Only *hang* → *hung* changes; the
   future shift is correct.) b. "Maya read the notice and ran to
   tell Jon yesterday." (Both verbs past; *reads/runs* with
   *yesterday* were errors.)
7. Samples: present perfect — "I have visited the stalls." Past
   perfect — "I had bought tickets before the gates opened."
   Future perfect — "I will have seen every stall before ten
   o'clock." (Labels in the margin.)
8. Error: *have bought* is present perfect, but the sentence sets a
   past moment (the show started), and the buying happened before
   that moment — past perfect is needed: "By the time the show
   started, we **had bought** tickets." The second sentence is
   correct: present perfect (bought, completed before now) plus
   present-tense scheduled future (starts at seven) is a real time
   difference, not a wrong shift.

## Week-04 Quiz

1. *And* joins three words in a series (*Maya, Jon, and Lee*), not
   clauses. The serial comma before it is the series rule.
2. Past perfect. It orders two past events: painting the banners
   happened before carrying them.
3. Pair: *either/or*. Halves parallel: noun/noun (*Maya/Jon*).
4. Tag-question rule (comma sets off a tag question).
5. "We packed apples, cheese, and juice."
6. "Neither Maya **nor** Jon will stay." (*Neither* pairs with
   *nor*.)
7. "She **painted** the sign yesterday and **will hang** it
   tomorrow."

9. Sample: "You are invited to join us on Saturday. It will be

## Unit Assessment

**Part A**

1. *And* joins two independent clauses ("Lanterns hang above the
   tables" and "musicians play near the fountain"); the comma is
   there because a comma + coordinating conjunction joins two
   clauses.
2. Sample: "above the tables" — object: *tables*. (Also accept:
   "near the fountain" — object: *fountain*; "On the first Friday"
   — object: *Friday*.)
3. Future perfect. It tells the reader the visiting will be
   finished before the later event, going home.
4. Four errors: (a) *buyed* → *bought* (irregular past tense).
   (b) *bread cheese and jam* → *bread, cheese, and jam* (series
   commas, including the serial comma). (c) *morning train* →
   "Morning Train" (song title in quotation marks, capitalized).
   (d) *cheered didn't they* → *cheered, didn't they?* (comma
   before a tag question).

**Part B**

5. Sample: "When the stalls opened, the crowd arrived and the music
   started."
6. "Both Maya **and** Jon will sing." Broken rule: wrong partner
   (*both* pairs with *and*, never *nor*).
7. "They **walked** to the market yesterday and **will buy**
   tickets tomorrow."
8. Novel: *The Lighthouse Cat*. Song: "Morning Train".
9. Sample: "You are invited to the night market. It will be
   wonderful." (Facts kept: market, invitation, enjoyable.)

## Project — Editor's Portfolio

**The 18 planted errors (original → fix → rule)**

1. *me and my family* → *my family and I* (subject pronoun form
   and order; the subject of a sentence uses *I*, and the other
   person comes first in formal English).
2. *goes* → *went* (past tense for a last-year story; also agrees
   with a plural subject).
3. *buyed* → *bought* (irregular past tense of *buy*).
4. *has* → *have* (plural subject *we* takes *have*).
5. *apples cheese crackers and juice* → *apples, cheese, crackers,
   and juice* (series commas, including the serial comma).
6. *After lunch we* → *After lunch, we* (comma after an
   introductory element).
7. *watched the band it played* → *watched the band. It played*
   (comma splice split into two sentences; joining with ", and"
   also accepted).
8. *morning train* → "Morning Train" (song title in quotation
   marks, capitalized).
9. *lantern dance* → "Lantern Dance" (song title in quotation
   marks, capitalized).
10. *Either … nor* → *Either … or* (correlative pair must match).
11. *We stayed for the parade. Because the rain stopped.* →
    *We stayed for the parade because the rain stopped.*
    (Fragment joined to the sentence it belongs with.)
12. *stoped* → *stopped* (spelling: double the final consonant
    before *-ed* after a short vowel; check in a reference).
13. *welcomed everyone and the crowd* → *welcomed everyone, and the
    crowd* (comma before *and* joining two independent clauses).
14. *cheered didn't they* → *cheered, didn't they?* (comma before
    a tag question).
15. *friday* → *Friday* (capitalization of a day of the week).
16. *will have saw* → *will have seen* (future perfect needs the
    past participle *seen*, not the simple past *saw*).
17. *definately* → *definitely* (spelling; check in a reference).
18. *The festival is fun it is* → *The festival is fun, and it is*
    (run-on joined with comma + *and*; splitting into two sentences
    also accepted).
