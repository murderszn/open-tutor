# Lesson 02 — Correlative Conjunctions and Sentence Variety

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can use correlative conjunction pairs
correctly, and I can expand, combine, and reduce sentences to make
my writing clearer and more interesting."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil.
**Standards:** L.5.1.e, L.5.3.a.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "Yesterday we joined clauses with FANBOYS. Listen:
'Either we row or we walk.' What two words work as a team here?"
Accept: *either/or*. "The team matters: the two words must match, and
the words after each one must be the same kind of thing." If the
learner cannot spot the pair, point to both words and ask what each
one introduces.

## Explicit teaching

### Correlative conjunctions: pairs that work together

A **correlative conjunction** is a pair of words that join equal
things. The pairs for grade 5:

- **either/or** — choice: "Either Maya or Jon will steer."
- **neither/nor** — negative choice: "Neither rain nor wind stopped
  the parade."
- **both/and** — addition: "Both drums and flutes played."
- **not only/but also** — addition with emphasis: "The festival
  offers not only music but also food from twelve stalls."

Two rules:

1. **The pair must match.** *Either* pairs with *or*, never with
   *nor*. *Neither* pairs with *nor*, never with *or*.
2. **Parallel structure:** the words after each half of the pair
   must be the same kind of thing — noun with noun, verb with verb,
   phrase with phrase. "Either Maya or Jon" (noun/noun) is parallel.
   "Either Maya will steer or walking" is not: a clause is paired
   with a single word, and the sentence wobbles.

Note: *both/and* and *not only/but also* do not take a comma between
their halves. A comma appears only if the ordinary compound-sentence
rule creates one elsewhere in the sentence.

### Sentence variety: expand, combine, reduce

**Expand:** add detail to a thin sentence — a prepositional phrase,
a describing word, or a clause. Thin: "The boat moved." Expanded:
"After sunset, the painted boat moved slowly down the dark river."
Expansion answers *when, where, how,* or *why*.

**Combine:** join short, choppy sentences into one longer sentence
that keeps every idea. Choppy: "The band played. The crowd danced.
The lanterns glowed." Combined: "As the band played, the crowd
danced beneath the glowing lanterns." Combining uses conjunctions,
commas, and reordering — never just a comma between two clauses
(that would be a comma splice from Lesson 01).

**Reduce:** cut words that repeat or add nothing, without losing
meaning. Wordy: "The festival, which happens every year in the
summer, is an event that people enjoy." Reduced: "The yearly summer
festival is popular." Reduction is not deletion of ideas; it is
deletion of empty words.

## Worked example 1 — Repair the pair

Broken: "Neither the drums nor the flutes was playing, and either
the crowd cheered nor sang along."

Step 1: check the pairs. *Neither/nor* is a correct pair; *either*
must pair with *or*, not *nor*. Fix: "either the crowd cheered or
sang along."

Step 2: check parallelism. "Neither the drums nor the flutes" —
noun/noun, parallel, good. But "either the crowd cheered or sang
along" is not parallel: a clause ("the crowd cheered") is paired
with a verb phrase ("sang along"). Move the pair so it sits before
matching parts: "the crowd either cheered or sang along" —
verb/verb, parallel, good.

Step 3: check the verb. "Neither the drums nor the flutes was
playing" — with *neither/nor*, the verb agrees with the nearer
subject (*flutes*, plural), so it should be *were*: "Neither the
drums nor the flutes were playing." (This agreement rule is a
grade-5 stretch goal: the adult may accept *was* if the pair and
parallelism are right, and note the verb rule for later.)

Fixed: "Neither the drums nor the flutes were playing, but the crowd
either cheered or sang along." Note the comma before *but*:
it joins two independent clauses.

## Worked example 2 — Combine, then reduce

Choppy draft: "The river was calm. The boats lined up. The race
started. People cheered."

Combine step: keep every idea, join with time order and one
conjunction: "When the river calmed, the boats lined up, the race
started, and people cheered." That sentence is grammatical but
heavy — three commas and a list of clauses.

Reduce step: fold the list into phrases: "On the calm river, the
lined-up boats started the race to loud cheers." All four ideas
survive — calm river, boats lined up, race started, cheers — in
about half the words.

Reasoning shown: combining first (so nothing is lost), then reducing
(so the sentence is readable). A common error is reducing first and
losing an idea, or combining with commas alone and making a splice.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Repair this pair and say which rule it broke: "Both Maya or Jon
   will carry the banner."
   *(Hint: check the pair first, then parallelism.)*
2. Complete with a parallel second half: "Not only did the band
   play, but also __________."
3. Expand this thin sentence twice — once for *when*, once for
   *where*: "The lanterns glowed."
4. Combine these three sentences without losing an idea: "The rain
   stopped. The clouds parted. The parade began."
5. Reduce this sentence without losing meaning: "The food stalls,
   which were set up along the river, sold food that was hot and
   tasty to the people who were hungry."

## Independent practice

6. Write one correct sentence for each pair: *either/or*,
   *neither/nor*, *both/and*, *not only/but also*.
7. Combine these sentences into one: "Maya painted a banner. Jon
   hung it. The wind caught it."
8. Expand this sentence with a prepositional phrase and one
   describing word: "The boat drifted."
9. Reduce this sentence: "The parade, which is a long event that
   goes through the town, is watched by many people who stand along
   the street."
10. Error analysis: explain what is wrong with "Either we can watch
    the fireworks or the parade route is long" — and rewrite it
    correctly two ways.

## Applied task

Take this choppy paragraph and rewrite it as three sentences that
use at least one correlative pair, one combined sentence, and one
reduced sentence. Keep every idea:

"The festival opens at noon. Bands play on two stages. Food stalls
sell bread and soup. Children paint banners. At dusk, lanterns are
lit. Boats carry the lanterns down the river."

## Exit check

The learner says, without looking: the four correlative pairs and
one rule for using them — then combines "The drums stopped. The
crowd went quiet." into one sentence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the choppy drafts
  aloud; the learner may dictate combined sentences and the adult
  scribes.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may cut printed
  sentence strips apart and physically reorder them into combined
  sentences before writing them out.
- **Extension:** write a sentence using *whether/or* ("Whether we
  row or walk, we will arrive by dusk") and explain why it is also
  a correlative pair; this pair is enrichment, not required.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: matching *either* with *nor* or *neither*
with *or* because both sound negative. Reteach move: chant the
pairs as fixed teams — "either-or, neither-nor, both-and,
not-only-but-also" — and have the learner cover one half and guess
its partner. Likely misconception: reducing by deleting an idea
instead of empty words. Reteach move: list the ideas first, then
check each one off in the reduced sentence. Answers: see
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 02.
