# Lesson 03 — Perfect Verb Tenses and Tense Consistency

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can form the present perfect, past
perfect, and future perfect tenses, use tense to show time and
sequence, and fix sentences where the tense shifts by mistake."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil.
**Standards:** L.5.1.b, L.5.1.c, L.5.1.d.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "Say these three: 'I walk.' 'I walked.' 'I will
walk.' What changes?" Accept: the verb form changes to show past,
present, or future time. "Now listen: 'I have walked.' That verb has
two words. Today you will learn what two-word verbs can say that
one-word verbs cannot." If the learner cannot name simple past
forms of common verbs yet, practise *walk/walked, sing/sang,
eat/ate, write/wrote* before continuing.

## Explicit teaching

### The three perfect tenses

Every perfect tense is built the same way: a form of **have** +
the **past participle** of the main verb. For regular verbs the past
participle ends in *-ed* (*walked, painted*). For irregular verbs
it must be memorized: *eaten* (not *ate*), *written* (not *wrote*),
*gone* (not *went*), *seen* (not *saw*), *broken, chosen, sung,
taken, given*.

| Tense | Formula | Example | What it tells the reader |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present perfect | have/has + past participle | "I **have walked** to the river." | An action completed at some unnamed time before now, with a link to the present (the walk is done; I am here now). |
| Past perfect | had + past participle | "I **had walked** to the river before the rain started." | An action completed **before** another past action — the earlier of two past events. |
| Future perfect | will have + past participle | "I **will have walked** to the river before noon." | An action that will be completed **before** another future time or event. |

Think of a timeline with three marks: *before then* (past perfect),
*before now* (present perfect), *before a later time* (future
perfect). The helping verb is the time signal; the participle
carries the action.

### Using tense for sequence, state, and condition

- **Sequence:** past perfect marks the earlier event when two past
  events are told out of order: "By the time the gates opened, the
  band **had tuned** its instruments." Tuning happened first.
- **State:** perfect tenses can describe a state that results from
  an action: "She **has finished** the banner" — the banner is in a
  finished state now.
- **Condition:** future perfect sets a condition for a later event:
  "We **will have sold** every ticket before the parade begins."

### Tense shifts: when they are errors and when they are not

A **tense shift** is a change of tense inside a sentence or passage.
Some shifts are correct — when the time really changes: "We **are
painting** banners now, and we **will hang** them tonight" (present
to future, because tonight is later). A shift is an **error** when
the time does not change but the verb does: "We **painted** banners
and **hang** them on the wall" (past to present, same afternoon) —
fix to "painted … and **hung**." The test: does the clock move? If
not, the tense must not move either.

## Worked example 1 — Build all three perfect tenses

Verb: *to paint* (regular; participle *painted*).

- Present perfect: "Maya **has painted** three banners." The
  painting is complete; the banners exist now. *Has* (not *have*)
  because the subject *Maya* is singular.
- Past perfect: "Maya **had painted** three banners before the
  festival opened." Two past events: painting, then opening.
  Painting is earlier, so it takes *had painted*.
- Future perfect: "Maya **will have painted** three banners before
  the festival opens." By opening time, the painting will be done.

Now an irregular verb, *to write* (participle *written*): "Jon
**has written** the announcement" (present perfect); "Jon **had
written** the announcement before lunch" (past perfect); "Jon
**will have written** the announcement before the gates open"
(future perfect). A common error is using the simple past instead of
the participle: "has wrote" is wrong; "has written" is right.

## Worked example 2 — Find and fix the shift

Passage: "The crew decorated the boats in the morning. They hang
lanterns in the afternoon, and they will light them at dusk."

Step 1: mark the times. Morning decorating is past (*decorated*).
Afternoon hanging is also past — it happened before "now" in the
story — but the verb says *hang* (present). Dusk lighting is future
(*will light*), and that shift is correct, because dusk is later.

Step 2: fix only the wrong shift. "The crew decorated the boats in
the morning. They **hung** lanterns in the afternoon, and they will
light them at dusk." Now the verbs march in order: past, past,
future — and the clock moves only where the story moves.

A common error: "fixing" the correct future shift too, and flattening
the whole passage into one tense. That would erase real time
information.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Form the present perfect of *to finish* for "they," and the past
   perfect of *to eat* for "she."
   *(Hint: participle of eat is eaten, not ate.)*
2. Which event happened first? "After the crowd had gathered, the
   mayor spoke." Name the tense that proves it.
3. Is this shift an error? "We practise every week, and we performed
   last Friday." Explain with the clock test.
4. Fix the shift: "The band played at noon and marches in the
   parade at two."
5. Write a future-perfect sentence about festival tickets using
   *will have sold* and a later event.

## Independent practice

6. Give all three perfect tenses for *to build* with the subject
   "the crew."
7. Choose the correct participle: "They have (broke / broken) the
   old record for attendance." Explain why.
8. Fix both shifts: "She paints the sign yesterday and hangs it
   tomorrow."
9. Write two sentences about the same morning: one in present
   perfect, one in past perfect, where the past-perfect event
   happened first.
10. Error analysis: explain what is wrong with "By the time we
    arrived, the show has started" — and rewrite it correctly.

## Applied task

Write a six-sentence festival report that uses all three perfect
tenses at least once each, in time order: something that had
happened before the gates opened, something that has happened by
now, and something that will have happened before the fireworks.
Underline each perfect verb phrase and label its tense in the
margin.

## Exit check

The learner says, without looking: the formula for a perfect tense,
the participle of *write*, and the clock test for a tense shift —
then fixes "He practises daily and won yesterday's race" if it
needs fixing (it does not: present habit plus a past event is a real
time change).

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the timeline aloud
  while the learner points to *before then / before now / before
  later*; the learner may answer orally and the adult scribes.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may place verb cards on
  a drawn timeline instead of writing sentences, then read the
  timeline aloud as sentences.
- **Extension:** form the perfect progressive ("has been painting")
  for one verb and explain what extra information the *-ing* adds
  (the action continued over time); perfect progressive is
  enrichment, not required.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: using the simple past as the participle ("has
went," "have ate"). Reteach move: keep a short irregular-participle
wall list (*eat/ate/eaten, write/wrote/written, go/went/gone,
see/saw/seen, break/broke/broken, choose/chose/chosen*) and require
a check against it before any present-perfect sentence is accepted.
Likely misconception: "correcting" every tense change, including
real time changes. Reteach move: the clock test, said aloud, before
any fix. Answers: see [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 03.
