# Lesson 01 — Conjunctions, Prepositions, and Interjections: Jobs in Sentences

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can explain what a conjunction, a
preposition, and an interjection do in a sentence — and name the job
of each joining word I meet."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil.
**Standards:** L.5.1.a.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "A sentence needs a subject — who or what it is
about — and a predicate — what the subject does or is. Listen: 'The
lanterns glow.' What is the subject? What is the predicate?" Accept:
subject *the lanterns*; predicate *glow*. Then: "Now listen: 'The
lanterns glow, and the crowd cheers.' What new word joined two
complete thoughts?" Accept: *and*. If the learner cannot split
subject from predicate yet, practise with three short sentences
before continuing — every job in this lesson hangs on that split.

## Explicit teaching

**Subject** = who or what the sentence is about. **Predicate** = what
the subject does or is. A **clause** is a group of words with its own
subject and verb. An **independent clause** can stand alone as a
sentence; a **dependent clause** cannot.

### Conjunctions: joining words

A **conjunction** joins words, phrases, or clauses. The most common
are the **coordinating conjunctions**, remembered by **FANBOYS**:

- **F**or — reason: "We stayed, for the parade was not over."
- **A**nd — addition: "Drums and flutes played."
- **N**or — negative choice: "She did not sing, nor did she dance."
- **B**ut — contrast: "The line was long, but it moved fast."
- **O**r — choice: "You may sit on the grass or on the wall."
- **Y**et — contrast with surprise: "It was late, yet the crowd stayed."
- **S**o — result: "The rain stopped, so the parade began."

Rule: when a coordinating conjunction joins **two independent
clauses**, put a **comma before it**. When it joins two words or two
phrases (not two clauses), do **not** use a comma: "Drums and flutes
played" has no comma, because *drums* and *flutes* are just words.

A **compound sentence** is two independent clauses joined by a comma
plus a coordinating conjunction (or by a semicolon, which this unit
does not require). Two errors to avoid: a **comma splice** joins two
independent clauses with only a comma and no conjunction ("The band
played, the crowd danced" — wrong), and a **run-on** joins them with
nothing at all ("The band played the crowd danced" — wrong).

### Prepositions: relationship words

A **preposition** shows the relationship between a noun or pronoun
and another word — usually place, time, direction, or manner.
Common prepositions: *in, on, under, over, beside, between, through,
across, before, after, during, with, without, about, for, from, to*.

A **prepositional phrase** = a preposition + its **object** (the noun
or pronoun that follows it) + any words describing that object:
"under the old oak tree," "before the parade," "with her brother."
A prepositional phrase never contains the subject or the main verb
of the sentence — a useful test when you are hunting for the subject.

### Interjections: feeling words

An **interjection** is a word or short phrase that shows strong
feeling and is not grammatically connected to the rest of the
sentence: *wow, ouch, hooray, oh, whew, aha*. Punctuate it with a
comma when the feeling is mild ("Oh, I see the lanterns.") and with
an exclamation mark when it is strong ("Wow! The lanterns are
beautiful!"). An interjection at the start of a sentence is followed
by a comma unless the exclamation mark ends it.

## Worked example 1 — Name the job

Sentence: "After the parade, Maya and Jon walked across the bridge,
but they stopped to watch the boats."

- *After* — preposition: it begins the phrase "After the parade,"
  which tells **when**.
- *and* — coordinating conjunction: it joins two words, *Maya* and
  *Jon* (no comma, because they are words, not clauses).
- *across* — preposition: it begins "across the bridge," which tells
  **where** they walked.
- *but* — coordinating conjunction: it joins two independent
  clauses ("Maya and Jon walked across the bridge" and "they
  stopped to watch the boats"), so a comma comes before it.

Reasoning shown: first find the clauses, then decide whether the
conjunction joins words or clauses — that decision controls the
comma.

## Worked example 2 — Fix the splice, keep the meaning

Broken: "The food stalls opened, the smell of bread filled the air."

Why it is broken: two independent clauses ("The food stalls opened"
and "the smell of bread filled the air") are joined by a comma alone.
That is a comma splice.

Fix 1 (compound sentence): "The food stalls opened, and the smell of
bread filled the air." The comma plus *and* now joins the clauses
correctly.

Fix 2 (two sentences): "The food stalls opened. The smell of bread
filled the air." Splitting is always a legal fix.

A common error: fixing a splice by removing the comma only ("The
food stalls opened the smell of bread filled the air") creates a
run-on, which is worse, not better.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. In "The drums beat loudly, yet the baby slept," name the job of
   *yet* and say why the comma is there.
   *(Hint: find the two clauses first.)*
2. Find the prepositional phrase in "Lanterns hung over the river
   during the festival." What is the object of the preposition?
3. Punctuate this interjection two ways — mild and strong: "Wow the
   lights are bright"
4. Is this a comma splice, a run-on, or correct? "We bought tickets,
   we found seats near the water."
5. Join these two clauses with *so* and correct punctuation: "The
   wind rose. The banners waved harder."

## Independent practice

6. Write one sentence that uses a prepositional phrase to tell
   *where*, and underline the phrase.
7. Write one compound sentence about a festival using *but*, with
   correct punctuation.
8. Explain in your own words why "Sam or Alex will carry the banner"
   has no comma before *or*.
9. Correct this run-on two different ways: "The parade ended the
   lanterns stayed lit."
10. Name the job of each underlined word: "Oh! We walked through the
    market and bought apples, but we forgot the bread."
    *(Underlined: Oh, through, and, but.)*

## Applied task

Write four sentences about a festival morning: one that uses a
prepositional phrase for *when*, one that uses a prepositional
phrase for *where*, one compound sentence joined by *and* or *but*,
and one sentence that begins with an interjection. Circle each
conjunction, preposition, and interjection you used, and label its
job in the margin.

## Exit check

The learner says, without looking: what a conjunction joins, what a
prepositional phrase is made of, and how a mild interjection is
punctuated — then names the job of *before* in "Before lunch, we
painted banners."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads each example aloud
  while the learner follows; the learner may answer orally and the
  adult scribes.
- **Alternate response mode:** instead of writing, the learner may
  sort word cards into three piles (conjunction / preposition /
  interjection) and explain one card from each pile.
- **Extension:** find one subordinating conjunction (*because,
  when, although, if*) in a book at home and explain what it joins;
  subordinating conjunctions join a dependent clause to an
  independent clause and are enrichment here, not required.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: the learner puts a comma before every *and*,
including "peanut butter and jelly" cases. Reteach move: ask, "Can
each side stand alone as a sentence?" If not, no comma. Likely
misconception: calling every long word group a prepositional phrase.
Reteach move: a prepositional phrase must start with a preposition
and end with a noun or pronoun. Answers: see
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 01.
