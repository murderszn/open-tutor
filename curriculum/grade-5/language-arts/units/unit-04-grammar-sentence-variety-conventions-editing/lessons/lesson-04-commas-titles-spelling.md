# Lesson 04 — Commas, Titles of Works, and Spelling with References

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can use commas in a series, after
introductory elements, with yes/no, tag questions, and direct
address; punctuate titles of works correctly; and check my spelling
with a reference."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil; a
dictionary or glossary (print or digital).
**Standards:** L.5.2.a, L.5.2.b, L.5.2.c, L.5.2.d, L.5.2.e.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "Read this aloud and clap where you pause: 'In the
morning Maya Jon and Lee painted banners.' Where did you want a
pause?" Accept: after *morning* (introductory element) and between
the names (series). "Commas are pause marks with rules. Today you
learn five comma rules, one title rule, and one spelling habit."
If the learner cannot hear any pause, read the sentence twice —
once flat, once with pauses — and ask which version is easier to
follow.

## Explicit teaching

### Five comma rules

**Rule 1 — Series.** Use commas to separate three or more items in
a series: "We packed apples, cheese, crackers, and juice." The
comma before *and* in a series is the **serial comma** (also called
the Oxford comma). This unit uses it always, because it prevents
misreading: "We invited Maya, Jon and Lee" could mean two people
(Jon and Lee as one pair) or three; "Maya, Jon, and Lee" is always
three.

**Rule 2 — Introductory elements.** Use a comma to separate an
introductory word, phrase, or clause from the rest of the sentence:
"After the parade, we ate." "Finally, the gates opened." "When the
rain stopped, the band played." Short introductory words (*Now,
Well, Yes*) also take a comma: "Well, the show must go on."

**Rule 3 — Yes and no.** Set off *yes* and *no* with a comma when
they begin a reply: "Yes, I would love to come." "No, thank you."
(If *yes* or *no* is the whole reply, it takes a period instead:
"Yes." — but as soon as more words follow, the comma appears.)

**Rule 4 — Tag questions.** Set off a tag question — a short
question tagged onto the end of a statement — with a comma: "The
lanterns are beautiful, aren't they?" "You bought tickets, didn't
you?" The tag repeats the helping verb and subject in question
form, and its verb matches the statement's tense.

**Rule 5 — Direct address.** Set off the name of the person being
spoken **to** with commas: "Maya, please hold the banner." "Please
hold the banner, Maya." "I think, Maya, you are right." Test: if
you are talking **about** Maya, no comma — "Maya held the banner"
names who acted, and no comma belongs there. If you are talking
**to** Maya, commas belong around her name.

### Titles of works

Longer works — books, movies, plays, albums, newspapers — are
**italicized** in print or **underlined** in handwriting: the novel
*The Lighthouse Cat*, the movie *River of Lights*. Shorter works —
poems, songs, short stories, articles, chapters — go in
**quotation marks**: the poem "Rain on Tin Roofs," the song
"Morning Train," the article "How Bridges Stand." The title's own
capitalization is kept: capitalize the first word, the last word,
and all important words; small words (*a, an, the, and, of, in, on*)
stay lowercase unless they are first or last.

### Spelling with references

Grade-5 spelling is not memorized whole; it is **checked**. When you
are unsure of a word, look it up in a dictionary or glossary and
copy the spelling exactly — that is what L.5.2.e means by
"consulting references as needed." Two habits: (1) circle every
word you are unsure of during drafting, then check them all in one
editing pass; (2) keep a personal list of words you have looked up
twice — those are your words to learn, not just to check.

## Worked example 1 — One sentence, four comma decisions

Sentence to fix: "Well Maya Jon and Lee arrived early and they
brought apples cheese and juice didn't they"

Step 1 — introductory word: *Well* opens the sentence, so "Well,
Maya …" Step 2 — direct address: Maya is spoken to, so "Well, Maya,
Jon and Lee arrived early …" Wait — is Maya spoken to, or is she
one of the arrivals? Read on: "Jon and Lee arrived early" — the
sentence is about arrivals, and Maya is addressed ("Well, Maya, Jon
and Lee arrived early" = telling Maya that Jon and Lee arrived).
The comma after *Maya* marks direct address; without it, the series
would read as three arrivals. This is exactly why the serial comma
matters.

Step 3 — series: "apples, cheese, and juice" with the serial comma.
Step 4 — tag question: "didn't they" tags the statement, so a comma
before it and a question mark at the end.

Fixed: "Well, Maya, Jon and Lee arrived early, and they brought
apples, cheese, and juice, didn't they?" Note the comma before *and*:
it joins two independent clauses ("Jon and Lee arrived early" and
"they brought …"), which is the Lesson 01 compound-sentence rule,
not the series rule.

## Worked example 2 — Titles and a spelling check

Draft lines: "We read the novel the lighthouse cat in class. Then
we sang the song morning train. I definately loved the poem rain on
tin roofs."

Step 1 — titles. *The Lighthouse Cat* is a novel (longer work), so
in handwriting it is underlined; in print it would be italicized:
Step 1 — titles. *The Lighthouse Cat* is a novel (longer work), so
in handwriting it is underlined; in print it would be italicized.
*Morning Train* is a song (shorter work), so it takes quotation
marks. *Rain on Tin Roofs* is a poem (shorter work), so it also
takes quotation marks.

Step 2 — spelling. *Definately* is circled as unsure. A dictionary
check gives **definitely** (from *definite* + *-ly*; the middle is
*-ite-*, not *-ate-*). Copy it exactly.

Fixed: "We read the novel *The Lighthouse Cat* in class. Then we
sang the song "Morning Train." I definitely loved the poem "Rain on
Tin Roofs.""

A common error: putting every title in quotation marks, including
books. Reteach move: long work = italics/underline; short work =
quotation marks — "long gets slanted, short gets quoted."

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. Add commas to this series, using the serial comma: "We need
   paint brushes water and paper."
   *(Hint: count the items — there are four.)*
2. Punctuate the introductory element: "After lunch we painted
   banners."
3. Punctuate the direct address, and say whether the name is spoken
   to or about: "Jon please pass the paint."
4. Add the tag question comma: "The festival starts at noon doesn't
   it"
5. Format these titles: a novel called *The Lighthouse Cat*; a poem
   called *Rain on Tin Roofs*. Say which rule each follows.

## Independent practice

6. Fix all comma errors: "Yes I packed apples cheese and juice.
   After the picnic Maya please bring the blanket."
7. Write one sentence that uses a tag question correctly, and one
   that uses direct address in the middle of the sentence.
8. Fix the titles and spelling: "we read the article how bridges
   stand and watched the movie river of lights. it was realy good."
   *(Check "really" in a reference if unsure.)*
9. Explain in your own words why "Let's eat, Grandma!" and "Let's
   eat Grandma!" mean different things. Which comma rule is at work?
10. Circle the words you would check in a dictionary in this
    sentence, then check them: "The committee will accommodate the
    necessary equipment for the procession."
    *(Words to check: accommodate, necessary — both are commonly
    misspelled; the sentence as printed is correct.)*

## Applied task

Write a six-sentence invitation to the River Festival that uses:
a series with a serial comma, an introductory element, a yes/no
reply, a tag question, direct address, and one correctly formatted
title (a song or poem performed at the festival, invented by you).
Label each comma rule in the margin.

## Exit check

The learner says, without looking: the five comma rules by name —
then punctuates "No Maya we have not finished the banners have we"
correctly on paper.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads each unpunctuated
  sentence aloud twice — once flat, once with pauses — and the
  learner places commas where the pauses fall, then names the rule.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may use comma cards
  (small paper commas) placed on printed sentences instead of
  rewriting them.
- **Extension:** punctuate a sentence with a series of phrases
  ("We painted in the morning, on the porch, and with new brushes")
  and explain why the serial comma still applies; series of phrases
  follow the same rule as series of words.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: commas "wherever you pause" — pauses are a
clue, not the rule; some pauses take no comma (before *and* joining
two words) and some commas mark no pause (direct address in the
middle). Reteach move: after placing a comma by ear, always name
the rule; if no rule fits, remove the comma. Likely misconception:
capitalizing every word in a title, including *and* and *of*.
Reteach move: first word, last word, important words only. Answers:
see [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
