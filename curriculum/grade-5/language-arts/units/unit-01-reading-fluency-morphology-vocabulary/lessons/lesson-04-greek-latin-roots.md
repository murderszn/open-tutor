# Lesson 04 — Root Keys: Greek and Latin Pieces That Open Hundreds of Words

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find the Greek or Latin root inside a
big word, say what it means, and use it to figure out the word."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; the [unit word
sets](../README.md#unit-word-sets-authored-for-this-unit-reused-across-lessons-and-practice);
word cards; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** L.5.4.b; RF.5.3.a.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult writes *telephone, television, telescope, telegraph*. "Four
long words. What piece do they all share?" (*tele-.*) "And what do all
four have to do with?" (distance — far.) "One piece, four words, one
idea. Today we collect eleven keys like it."

## Explicit teaching

A **root** is a word piece that carries the core meaning. Many English
roots come from **Greek** and **Latin**, languages spoken thousands of
years ago — but the pieces are still alive inside English words. Learn
one root and you unlock a family of words.

The detective method (same as Lessons 02–03, now with roots at the
center):

1. **Find the root** (cover the front and back; the meaningful middle
   is usually the root).
2. **Name each piece's meaning** (prefix + root + suffix).
3. **Break into syllables and read** — every syllable has one vowel
   sound.
4. **Assemble the meaning.** Then **run the meaning test** (from Lesson
   02): does the root's meaning make sense inside the whole word? If
   not, it is not that root.

Today's eleven keys:

| Root | Means | From | Hook |
|---|---|---|---|
| graph- | write | Greek | *autograph* — self-writing (your signature) |
| tele- | far | Greek | *telephone* — sound from far away |
| photo- | light | Greek | *photograph* — writing with light |
| port- | carry | Latin | *transport* — carry across |
| dict- | say | Latin | *dictate* — say the words for someone to write |
| scrib- / script- | write | Latin | *describe* — write down about something |
| struct- | build | Latin | *construct* — build together |
| spect- | look, see | Latin | *inspect* — look into closely |
| tract- | pull, draw | Latin | *attract* — pull toward |
| form- | shape | Latin | *transform* — change shape |
| ject- | throw | Latin | *reject* — throw back |

**Root vs. base:** a base word can stand alone (*happy* in *unhappy*);
a root usually cannot (*struct* is not an English word by itself — it
needs pieces around it: *construct, structure, instruct*).

### Modeled example 1 — *transportation* (prefix + root + suffix + syllables)

*Adult writes* transportation. "Cover the front: *trans-* (a bonus
prefix meaning 'across'). Cover the back: *-tion* ('act or state of,'
makes a noun). The middle: *port-* ('carry'). Syllables: trans | por |
ta | tion — four. Assemble: 'the act of carrying across.' Check: 'Public
transportation carries people across the city.' Fits. One word, four
pieces, and every piece did its job."

### Modeled example 2 — *inspect* and *spectator* (one root, two words)

*Adult writes both.* "*Inspect*: *in-* ('into') + *spect-* ('look') =
'look into closely.' Check: 'The mechanic will inspect the engine'
(look into it closely) — fits. *Spectator*: *spect-* ('look, see') +
*-ator* ('one who') = 'one who looks' — a watcher. Check: 'The
spectators cheered' (the watchers cheered) — fits. The root *spect-*
repeats; once you own it, both words come free."

### Modeled example 3 — the meaning test catches a fake root

*Adult writes* *important* and *import*. "*Import*: *im-* ('in') +
*port-* ('carry') = 'carry in' — goods carried into the country. Real
root; the meaning test passes. *Important*: does 'carry' make sense
inside 'important'? 'Full of carry'? No. The letters *port* are there by
coincidence. Fake root — do not use it. Letter-matching without the
meaning test is the #1 trap in this lesson."

### Why roots matter for reading

A fifth grader meets roughly ten thousand new words in a year of
reading — far too many to memorize one by one. But those words are
built from a few hundred repeating roots. Eleven roots, learned well,
unlock hundreds of words: every *spect-* word you ever meet will have
something to do with looking. Roots are the highest-leverage
vocabulary investment in this unit.

### Modeled example 4 — a root family: *form-* ("shape")

*Adult writes* transform, inform, format. "*Transform*: *trans-*
('change, across') + *form-* ('shape') = 'change shape.' Check: 'The
caterpillar will transform into a butterfly' — change shape. Fits.
*Inform*: *in-* ('into') + *form-* ('shape') = 'shape into' — to put
knowledge into someone's mind; to tell. Check: 'Please inform me of
any changes' — put the knowledge into me. Fits (the 'shape' here is
the shape of an idea, not a physical shape — roots stretch).
*Format*: *form-* + *-at* = 'the shape of' something — the way a
document is shaped. Check: 'The essay's format confused him' — the
shape/layout of the essay. Fits. One root, three words, one idea
running through all of them: shape."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Break *dictate* into pieces and syllables; assemble." (*dict-* "say"
   + *-ate*; dic-tate; 'say the words' — to speak for writing down.)
   **Hint:** "What does a boss do when she dictates a letter?"
2. "What does *scrib-* mean? Use *describe* and *prescribe* to prove
   it." (*scrib-* "write": describe = write down about; prescribe =
   write before/order in writing.) **Hint:** "Cover the front of each
   word — what's left in the middle?"
3. **Syllable surgery:** "Break *construction* and read it." (con |
   struc | tion; *con-* "together" + *struct-* "build" + *-tion* = the
   act of building together.) **Hint:** "Every syllable needs one vowel
   sound — clap them."
4. "Build a word: 'throw back' + root *ject*." (*reject* — *re-* "back"
   + *ject* "throw.") **Hint:** "Which prefix means 'back'?"
5. **Error-analysis:** The adult assembles *photograph* as "'far' +
   'write' = write from far away." The learner fixes it. ("*photo-* is
   'light,' not 'far' — that's *tele-*. *Photograph* = light-writing.
   Mixing up the two Greek keys.")

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Break, read, and define: *telegraph, portable*.
2. Break, read, and define: *subtract, transform*.
3. "List every unit word containing *struct-*. What do the words have
   in common?" (construct, structure, instruct, destruction — all about
   building, or un-building.)
4. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why is it useful that *spect-* means
   'look' in *inspect*, *spectator*, and *spectacle* — but useless to
   treat *port* in *important* as 'carry'?" (3–4 sentences; must
   mention the meaning test.)
5. Speed round: the adult shows 8 root-word cards from the unit set;
   the learner breaks, reads, and states the assembled meaning.
   Record the count correct.

## Application

**Root keyring.** The learner makes 11 key cards — one per root: root,
meaning, Greek or Latin, hook word, and one *new* unit word using it
(verified by breaking and reading). The keyring joins the shared card
set and is used in the project journal.

## Exit check

The adult writes two unpracticed unit words — for example, *inject*
and *export*. The learner breaks each into pieces and syllables, reads
it, and assembles the meaning. Success: both read correctly with pieces
named and sensible meanings. (The bonus prefixes *in-* "into" and *ex-*
"out" may be supplied by the adult; the root assembly is the skill
being checked.)

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads each word aloud first;
  roots may be color-coded; syllable beats can be tapped or clapped.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may point to pieces, clap
  syllables, or dictate the assembly for the adult to write.
- **Extension:** hunt the eleven roots in the learner's own reading;
  log 5 new finds with assembled meanings; find one "fake root" word
  and write why it fails the meaning test.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** letter-matching without the meaning test
(*port* in *important*, *form* in *formal* — "shape" fails inside
*formal*); also treating every long word as root-based (some are just
long — *calendar*, *window* — or base + affix only). Teach the
removal test: cover the suspected root; if the meaning falls apart,
keep it; if the meaning never needed it, drop it.
