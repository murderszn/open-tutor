# Lesson 03 — Suffixes: The Endings That Change a Word's Job

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find the suffix at the end of a word,
say what it means, and name the word's new part of speech."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; the [unit word
sets](../README.md#unit-word-sets-authored-for-this-unit-reused-across-lessons-and-practice);
word cards; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** L.5.4.b; RF.5.3.a.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult writes *care* and *careful* and *careless*. "Same base, two
different endings. How do the meanings differ?" (full of care vs.
without care — the endings are opposites.) "Today's endings do two
jobs at once: they change meaning *and* they change what the word can
do in a sentence."

## Explicit teaching

A **suffix** is a word piece attached to the **end** of a base word.
Unlike prefixes, suffixes often change the word's **part of speech** —
whether it acts as a noun, verb, adjective, or adverb. The detective
method:

1. **Find the suffix** (cover the end; the base is what's left).
2. **Name the suffix's meaning and its job** (what part of speech it
   makes).
3. **Assemble:** base meaning + suffix meaning, in the new part of
   speech.
4. **Check in a sentence.**

Today's eight endings:

| Suffix | Means | Makes the word a... | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| -ful | full of | adjective | *joyful* — full of joy |
| -less | without | adjective | *fearless* — without fear |
| -able / -ible | can be | adjective | *readable* — can be read |
| -ly | in that way | adverb | *quickly* — in a quick way |
| -ment | act, state, or result of | noun | *enjoyment* — the result of enjoying |
| -tion / -sion | act or state of | noun | *celebration* — the act of celebrating |
| -ness | state or quality of | noun | *kindness* — the quality of being kind |
| -er / -or | one who | noun | *teacher* — one who teaches |

**-able or -ible?** They sound the same ("uh-bull") and mean the same
("can be"). Which spelling? There is no rule you can hear — you check
a dictionary or memorize the common ones. (*readable, breakable* use
-able; *visible, flexible, terrible* use -ible.) When in doubt, verify.

**Spelling changes when you add a suffix** (the three moves):

1. **Drop the e:** *navigate* → *navigation* (drop the *e* before
   -tion); *care* → *caring*.
2. **Double the consonant:** *run* → *runner* (short vowel + one
   consonant doubles); *swim* → *swimmer*.
3. **y → i:** *happy* → *happiness*; *carry* → *carried*.

### Modeled example 1 — *careful* vs. *careless* (opposite endings)

*Adult writes both.* "Base: *care* (noun/verb — caring about things).
*-ful* = 'full of' → adjective: *careful* = full of care. *-less* =
'without' → adjective: *careless* = without care. Same base, opposite
endings, opposite meanings. Check: 'Be careful with the glass' (full
of care — handle it well) vs. 'He was careless with the glass' (without
care — it broke). The suffix is the whole difference."

### Modeled example 2 — *-tion* changes the job

*Adult writes* celebrate → celebration. "*Celebrate* is a verb — an
action you do. Add *-tion* ('act or state of') and drop the *e*: *the
act of celebrating*, now a **noun** — a thing. Check the job in
sentences: 'They **celebrate** every June' (verb — what they do). 'The
**celebration** lasted all day' (noun — the thing itself; it has 'the'
in front, like all nouns). The suffix didn't just lengthen the word; it
changed its job from action to thing."

### Modeled example 3 — *-ly* makes an adverb

*Adult writes* quick → quickly. "*Quick* is an adjective — it describes
a noun: 'a quick rabbit.' Add *-ly* ('in that way'): *quickly* = 'in a
quick way,' now an **adverb** — it describes *how* something is done:
'The rabbit ran quickly.' Check: adjectives describe nouns; adverbs
describe verbs. 'Quick rabbit' (noun described) vs. 'ran quickly' (verb
described). *-ly* is the most reliable part-of-speech signal in
English: if it ends in -ly and describes *how*, it is almost always an
adverb."

### Why suffixes matter for writing

Prefixes change *what* a word means; suffixes change *what a word can
do*. When you write, you constantly need the same idea in different
jobs: "They **celebrate**" (the action) becomes "the **celebration**"
(the event you're describing). Writers who own suffixes never get
stuck with a verb where they need a noun — they reshape the word and
keep the sentence moving.

### Modeled example 4 — *-ness* vs. *-ment*: two noun-makers

*Adult writes* happy → happiness; enjoy → enjoyment. "Both endings make
nouns, but they make different *kinds* of nouns. *-ness* = 'the state
or quality of being': *happiness* is the state of being happy — a
condition. *-ment* = 'the act, state, or result of' a verb: *enjoyment*
is the result of enjoying — something that happened. Check: 'Her
*happiness* was obvious' (a state she was in). 'Their *enjoyment* of
the concert was obvious' (the result of the concert). Same sentence
frame, different endings, different meanings. When you need a noun
from an adjective (*happy, kind, dark*), reach for *-ness*. When you
need a noun from a verb (*enjoy, move, pay*), reach for *-ment* or
*-tion*."

### The spelling moves — memory hooks

- **Drop the e:** "The *e* is silent, so it leaves before a vowel
  suffix moves in" (*navigate → navigation*). It stays before a
  consonant suffix (*care → careful* — the *e* stays because *-ful*
  starts with a consonant).
- **Double the consonant:** "Short vowel + one consonant at the end?
  Double up to protect the vowel" (*run → runner* — without doubling,
  *runer* would rhyme with *tuner*).
- **y → i:** "The *y* changes its costume to *i*" (*happy →
  happiness*, *carry → carried*). It keeps its costume only before
  *-ing* (*carry → carrying*) — the one suffix where the *y* stays.

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. "Break *fearless*: base, suffix, part of speech, meaning." (*fear*
   + *-less* "without" → adjective: without fear.) **Hint:** "Start
   with the base's meaning."
2. "Build the word: *enjoy* + *-ment*. What job does it do now?" (*enjoyment*
   — noun, the result of enjoying; "their enjoyment of the game.")
   **Hint:** "Try 'the ___' in front — if it fits, it's a noun."
3. **Build-a-word:** "*invent* + noun ending meaning 'act of'." (*invention*;
   drop the e? No — *invent* has no final e; just add -ion.) **Hint:**
   "Which of today's endings means 'act of'? Check for a final e."
4. "What part of speech is *suddenly*? How do you know?" (Adverb — *-ly*
   "in that way": in a sudden way.) **Hint:** "What question does it
   answer — what kind, or how?"
5. **Error-analysis:** The adult writes *hopeful* → "full of hope,
   noun." The learner fixes the job label. (Adjective, not noun — *-ful*
   makes adjectives; test: "a hopeful child," describes the noun.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Break, name the part of speech, and define: *powerful, hopeless,
   flexible, bravely*.
2. Break, name the part of speech, and define: *movement, kindness,
   decision, actor*.
3. Apply the spelling moves: *happy* + *-ness*; *run* + *-er*;
   *celebrate* + *-tion*; *fear* + *-less*. (Watch for y→i, doubling,
   dropped e.)
4. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why does English need *-ful* and *-less*
   to be a matched pair? What would go wrong if we only had one of
   them?" (2–3 sentences.)
5. Write 4 original sentences, each using a different suffix word, and
   label each word's part of speech.

## Application

**Suffix ladder.** The learner builds a ladder on paper: pick 4 base
words and add a different suffix to each, writing the base → suffixed
word → new part of speech → meaning. Then swap ladders with the adult
(or a partner) and check each other's rungs for the spelling moves.

## Exit check

The adult writes two unpracticed words — for example, *thoughtless*
and *payment*. For each, the learner names base + suffix, the new part
of speech, and the assembled meaning. Success: 2 of 2 with parts of
speech correct.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads each word aloud first;
  suffixes may be color-coded; the learner may tap base then suffix.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort printed word cards
  into noun/adjective/adverb piles instead of writing; may dictate
  sentences for the adult to scribe.
- **Extension:** collect 5 more -able/-ible words from reading and
  sort them by spelling (-able vs. -ible); write the rule you notice
  (there isn't a sound rule — note that the dictionary is the tool).

is not *bu* + *-s*; *this* is not *thi* + *-s*) — run the meaning test

same, so either is fine" — spelling still matters; teach dictionary
