# Lesson 01 — Read It Like You Mean It: Accuracy, Rate, and Expression

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can read a passage aloud with accuracy,
good speed, and expression — and I can make my third reading smoother
than my first."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; the fluency passage
below; pencil; a way to count errors (paper tally or adult's marks).
**Standards:** RF.5.4.b, RF.5.4.c.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult reads the first two sentences of the passage below at racing
speed, mumbling through punctuation. Then reads them again at a
storyteller's pace, pausing at commas and lifting the voice at the
question. Ask: "Which version would you rather listen to? What did the
second one do that the first one did not?" (It was accurate, not too
fast, and the voice showed the meaning.)

## Explicit teaching

**Fluency** is reading that sounds the way the author meant it. It has
three parts — the **fluency trio**:

1. **Accuracy** — reading the words right. No skipping, no guessing,
   no swapping one word for another.
2. **Rate** — reading at a talking speed. Not racing, not dragging.
   Good rate changes: faster for action, slower for something important.
3. **Expression** — reading with a voice that matches the meaning.
   Punctuation is the author's stage directions: a period says *stop*,
   a comma says *short pause*, a question mark says *lift your voice*,
   an exclamation says *energy!*

Good readers also **self-correct**. When the words stop making sense —
"I sailed the bout across the lake" — a fluent reader stops, looks back,
and fixes it: "boat, not bout." That stop-and-fix is a strength, not a
failure.

The **three-read routine** builds fluency the way rehearsal builds a
play:

- **Read 1 — Accuracy read.** Read every word right, slowly if needed.
  Count errors.
- **Read 2 — Smoothness read.** Read at talking speed. Follow the
  punctuation.
- **Read 3 — Performance read.** Read like the author is listening.
  Voice matches meaning.

### The fluency passage — "The Night of the Lanterns" (original)

> The town of Maribel had not celebrated the harvest in twenty years,
> but tonight the sky would remember. At dusk, children carried paper
> lanterns to the old stone bridge. "Light them together," called the
> mayor, "and make a wish before the flame burns low!" One by one, the
> lanterns lifted into the dark like a flock of glowing birds. Nora
> watched hers drift past the church tower. Was it the highest? She
> could not tell, but she did not care. The whole river shimmered, and
> for once the town was quiet enough to hear the water.

(130 words. Used in sessions 1, 8, and 16 for repeated reading.)

### Modeled example 1 — three reads of the first paragraph

*Adult reads the first three sentences aloud three times, narrating the
plan between reads.*

**Read 1 (accuracy):** Reads slowly, fingers on each word, stumbles
deliberately on "shimmered" — "shim... shimmered" — then keeps going.
Marks two errors on paper. Says: "Read 1 was about accuracy. I fixed
'shimmered' by sounding it out instead of guessing."

**Read 2 (smoothness):** Reads at talking speed, pausing at the commas
in "At dusk, children carried paper lanterns to the old stone bridge."
Says: "The comma told me to pause — that's the author saying 'take a
breath here.'"

**Read 3 (performance):** Reads the mayor's line — "Light them
together," called the mayor — with a raised, calling voice, and "make
a wish before the flame burns low!" with warmth. Says: "The exclamation
marks and the dialogue told my voice what to do."

### Modeled example 2 — a self-correction caught live

*Adult reads: "One by one, the lanterns lifted into the dark like a
flock of glowing birds." Then "re-reads" a line as:* "Nora watched hers
drift past the church tower — *tower*? No — the word is 'tower,' wait,
I said it right. Let me do a real one." *Adult then reads:* "for once
the town was quiet enough to hear the *water* — hmm, that doesn't fit,
the next word... 'hear the *water*' — yes it does fit; the river
shimmers, you hear water." *Then demonstrates a genuine catch:* reads
"the whole river *shivered*" — stops — "shivered? No. S-H-I-M-M-E-R-E-D,
*shimmered*. The river shimmered — that makes sense with glowing
lanterns. If I had kept 'shivered,' the sentence would mean the river
was cold and shaking, which is wrong." The lesson: when the meaning
breaks, stop, look at the word, fix it, keep going.

### Why three reads work

Your brain has two jobs while reading: **figuring out the words**
(decoding) and **understanding the story** (comprehension). On a first
read, most of your effort goes to the words — that is why the first
read sounds choppy. On the second and third reads, the words are
already familiar, so your brain spends its effort on phrasing and
meaning. That is not cheating; that is how every actor rehearses a
play and every musician rehearses a song. Repeated reading is
practice, and practice is the point.

### Modeled example 3 — punctuation as stage directions

*Adult writes on the board:*

> "Light them together," called the mayor, "and make a wish before the
> flame burns low!"

"Watch what the punctuation tells my voice. The comma after
'together,' says: short pause — the speaker is catching a breath. The
quotation marks say: this is someone talking, so use a talking voice,
not a narrating voice. The exclamation mark says: energy — the mayor
is excited. If I read this flat — 'light them together called the
mayor and make a wish before the flame burns low' — I have deleted the
author's directions. Fluent readers follow the directions."

*Adult then reads the sentence "Nora watched hers drift past the church
tower." twice — once stopping hard at the period mid-thought, once
flowing through it — and asks which honored the period's full stop.*

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 5 tasks)

1. **Accuracy read:** The learner reads "The Night of the Lanterns"
   aloud. The adult tallies errors without interrupting (mark them,
   keep going). **Hint:** "Slow is fine. Your only job on this read is
   getting the words right."
2. **Error review:** Together, look at the tallied errors. For each:
   was it a skipped word, a swapped word, or a misread? **Hint:**
   "Point to the word and say what letter tricked you."
3. **Smoothness read:** The learner reads again, this time marking the
   punctuation with a finger pause. **Hint:** "Every comma is a yellow
   light; every period is a red light."
4. **Expression coaching:** The adult reads the mayor's line with flat
   voice, then with calling voice. The learner chooses and copies the
   better one. **Hint:** "Exclamation marks are the author's way of
   shouting directions at you."
5. **Read 3 vs. Read 1:** The learner performs the final read. Compare:
   errors, pauses, voice. **Hint:** "Tell me one thing your third read
   did better — errors, speed, or voice."

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 5 tasks)

1. Read the passage a fourth time alone. Tally your own errors. Did the
   count drop from Read 1?
2. Circle the three punctuation marks that changed your voice the most.
   Write what each one told you to do (stop / pause / lift / energy).
3. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why is the second read smoother than
   the first, even though the words are the same?" (Write 2–3
   sentences.)
4. **Error-analysis:** The adult reads one sentence with a deliberate
   error ("the town was quiet enough to *smell* the water"). The learner
   must catch it, stop the adult, and fix it using the meaning.
5. Record (paper tally): Read 1 errors ___, Read 3 errors ___. Write
   one sentence: "I got smoother because ___."

## Application

**Fluency log.** The learner starts a fluency log (used in sessions 8
and 16). Copy this template:

> Date: ___ · Passage: ___
> Read 1 errors: ___ · Read 3 errors: ___
> What improved (circle one): accuracy / rate / expression
> One sentence: "I got smoother because ___."

Today's entry is the first. The log lives with the Word Detective
Field Journal from the project, and the learner adds an entry every
time a passage is repeated (sessions 8 and 16 at minimum).

## Exit check

The learner reads the final paragraph aloud one more time. Success: ≤3
uncorrected errors, audible pauses at commas and periods, and the
mayor's line read with a calling voice. Then the learner names the
three parts of the fluency trio without looking.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the passage aloud first
  as a model; the learner may track with a finger or a reading window;
  unfamiliar words (maribel, shimmered) may be pre-taught.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may record audio instead of
  tallying on paper; may answer the reasoning task orally for the adult
  to scribe.
- **Extension:** read the passage to a real audience (family member,
  pet, stuffed animal) and ask which read they liked best and why;
  rewrite the mayor's line three ways (whisper, command, plea) and read
  each.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** speed = fluency (racing while dropping words)
or, at the other end, word-by-word dragging treated as "being careful."
Both come from making only one of the trio the goal. Teach the trio as
three judges that all have to agree. **Answer-key reference:**
