# Lesson 05 — Word Detective Toolkit: Context Clues, Dictionaries, and Precise Words

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can solve an unknown word with context
clues, verify it in a dictionary, and choose the most precise word when
I write."
**Time:** 40 minutes × 2 parts (Part A in session 9; Part B in session
11) · **Materials:** this lesson; the [unit word
sets](../README.md#unit-word-sets-authored-for-this-unit-reused-across-lessons-and-practice);
a print or digital dictionary and thesaurus; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** Part A — L.5.4.a, L.5.4.b; Part B — L.5.4.c, L.5.5.c,
L.5.6.

## Readiness check (2 min, Part A)

The adult writes: "The hike was **arduous**, and by mile six everyone's
legs were burning." "Without a dictionary, what might *arduous* mean?
What in the sentence told you?" (Hard, exhausting — "legs burning" is
the clue.) "Today you learn the names of four clue types — then the
tools that verify your guesses."

# PART A — Context clues (session 9)

## Explicit teaching (Part A)

A **context clue** is a hint in the words *around* an unknown word. Four
types do most of the work:

| Clue type | What to look for | Signal words |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition clue** | The meaning is stated right there | *is, are, means, or, refers to* |
| **Example clue** | Examples show what the word covers | *such as, like, for example, including* |
| **Restatement clue** | The meaning is said again in other words | commas or dashes setting off a rephrase |
| **Contrast clue** | An opposite shows what the word is not | *but, however, unlike, although, on the other hand* |

The routine — **Clue → Parts → Verify**:

1. **Clue:** name the clue type and guess the meaning.
2. **Parts:** cross-check with prefix/root/suffix (Lessons 02–04).
3. **Verify:** check a dictionary (Part B). Clue first, parts second,
   dictionary last — the dictionary confirms, it does not do the
   thinking for you.

### Modeled example 1 — definition clue

*Adult writes:* "A **cartographer**, or mapmaker, spends hours drawing
coastlines." "The commas set off *or mapmaker* — a restatement that
works like a definition: a cartographer *is* a mapmaker. Clue type:
definition/restatement. Guess: someone who makes maps. Parts check:
*graph-* 'write' — writing maps. Both agree."

### Modeled example 2 — contrast clue

*Adult writes:* "Unlike his **gregarious** brother, who loved parties,
Marcus was quiet and kept to himself." "Signal word: *unlike* — a
contrast. The brother loves parties; Marcus is the opposite. Guess:
*gregarious* = outgoing, sociable. Parts check: no helpful prefix or
root here — that's fine; the clue carries the work. This is a contrast
clue."

### Modeled example 3 — example clue + parts cross-check

*Adult writes:* "Celestial bodies, **such as** the sun, moon, and
stars, have fascinated humans for centuries." "Signal: *such as* —
examples. The examples are the sun, moon, stars. Guess: *celestial* =
having to do with the sky. Parts check: no obvious root — but the
examples are strong enough. Example clue."

### Modeled example 4 — restatement with dashes

*Adult writes:* "The old lighthouse — a **relic** of the whaling
days — still guides ships into the harbor." "The dashes set off a
restating phrase: 'a relic of the whaling days' re-says what *relic*
is. Clue type: restatement. Guess: something left over from an
earlier time. Parts check: *re-* ('back, again') + *-lic*... the parts
are murky here — and that's fine. The clue is strong enough on its
own. Not every word yields to word parts; the routine says clue
first, and sometimes the clue is the whole answer."

## Guided practice (Part A; adult with learner; 4 tasks)

1. "The room was **frigid**, and we could see our breath." Name the
   clue type and meaning. (No labeled type — pure inference from
   "see our breath": bitterly cold.) **Hint:** "What does seeing your
   breath tell you about the air?"
2. "She was **reluctant** to try the high dive; her friends, however,
   could not wait to jump." Name the clue type. (Contrast — *however*:
   the friends are eager, she is the opposite: unwilling, hesitant.)
   **Hint:** "Find the signal word first."
3. "The **debris** — broken branches, scattered papers, and an
   overturned bench — covered the park after the storm." Name the
   clue type. (Example/restatement: the dashes list what debris is:
   scattered wreckage.) **Hint:** "Read what's between the dashes."
4. **Error-analysis:** The adult solves "He felt **tranquil** after
   the loud parade" as "tranquil = loud, because the parade was loud."
   The learner repairs it. (The sentence says *after* the parade —
   contrast with loud: calm, peaceful. The clue was misread; *after*
   flips it.)

## Independent practice (Part A; 4 tasks)

1. "The **luminous** dial of the old clock glowed green in the dark
   hallway." Name the clue type and meaning.
2. "Their **quarrel** started over a borrowed book and ended with
   slammed doors." Name the clue type and meaning.
3. "The explorers were **astonished** to find fresh water; they had
   expected the island to be barren." Name the clue type and meaning.
4. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "When would you trust a contrast clue
   more than a definition clue? When would you trust it less?" (3–4
   sentences.)

## Exit check (Part A)

The adult writes one new sentence with an unknown word and one clue
type. The learner names the clue type, the signal words, and the
meaning. Success: all three correct.

# PART B — Dictionary, thesaurus, shades of meaning, precise words (session 11)

## Explicit teaching (Part B)

A dictionary **verifies** your guess; it does not replace it. How to
read an entry:

> **tranquil** *adjective* \ ˈtraŋ-kwəl \ **1** free from agitation:
> calm, peaceful **2** free from disturbance

- **Headword** (*tranquil*) — the word you looked up.
- **Part of speech** (*adjective*) — its job.
- **Pronunciation key** (\ ˈtraŋ-kwəl \) — how to say it (the ˈ marks
  the stressed syllable).
- **Numbered senses** (**1**, **2**) — the meanings, most common first.
  **Trap:** meaning #1 is not always the one your sentence needs. Pick
  the numbered sense that fits the sentence.

**Thesaurus** gives **synonyms** (same meaning) and **antonyms**
(opposite). The swap-and-check rule: a synonym must fit the *sentence*,
not just the dictionary. "A *slim* chance" works; "a *skinny* chance"
does not — same thesaurus column, wrong sentence. If the swap sounds
wrong, reject it.

**Shades of meaning:** synonyms sit on an intensity ladder:

*glanced → looked → stared → glared*

All mean "looked," but the strength climbs. Choosing the right rung is
**precision** — saying exactly what you mean. Vague words (*said, big,
good, walked*) make the reader guess; precise words do the work.

**Signal words** for academic writing (L.5.6): words that show logical
relationships — contrast (*however, although, on the other hand*),
addition (*moreover, in addition, furthermore*), similarity
(*similarly, likewise*). They are the road signs of an argument.

### Modeled example 1 — picking the right numbered sense

*Adult writes:* "The bank was **steep**." Then the entry: **steep**
*adjective* **1** having a sharp slope **2** (of prices) too high.
"Sentence: 'The bank was steep' — a riverbank, sharp slope → sense 1.
But 'The tickets were steep' — prices → sense 2. Same word, different
number. The sentence picks the number, not the dictionary's order."

### Modeled example 2 — thesaurus swap-and-check

*Adult writes:* "She **said** the news loudly." Thesaurus column for
*said*: *murmured, exclaimed, stammered, whispered*. "Try each in the
sentence: 'She *murmured* the news loudly' — contradiction, reject.
'She *exclaimed* the news loudly' — fits perfectly. 'She *stammered*
the news loudly' — stammering is halting, not loud; reject. Winner:
*exclaimed*. The swap-and-check caught two bad fits the thesaurus
alone would have allowed."

### Modeled example 3 — shade ladder

*Adult writes:* "He **walked** into the room." Ladder: *strolled →
walked → marched → stormed*. "If he is relaxed, *strolled*. If he is
angry, *stormed*. The sentence needs the rung that matches the
feeling. Now: 'He ___ into the room, fists clenched.' Which rung?
*stormed* — fists clenched tells you the intensity. The context picks
the rung."

### Modeled example 4 — a thesaurus trap

*Adult writes:* "He gave a **slim** chance of winning." Thesaurus
column for *slim*: *thin, skinny, slender, narrow*. "Try the swap:
'He gave a *skinny* chance of winning.' Read it aloud — it sounds
wrong. *Skinny* describes bodies, not chances. Try *slender*: 'a
*slender* chance' — better, but still odd. The truth: in this idiom,
*slim* is nearly irreplaceable — 'a slim chance' is a fixed phrase.
The swap-and-check just saved us from a thesaurus trap. Rule: the
thesaurus suggests; the sentence decides. When no synonym survives the
swap-and-check, keep the original word — it was precise already."

## Guided practice (Part B; adult with learner; 4 tasks)

1. Look up **perish** in the dictionary. Read the headword, part of
   speech, and first two senses aloud. Which sense fits "The old
   custom will perish if no one practices it"? **Hint:** "Read both
   senses before choosing."
2. Thesaurus swap-and-check: "The **big** dog barked." Try *enormous*
   and *colossal* in the sentence. Keep or reject each, and say why.
   **Hint:** "Read the full sentence aloud with the swap — does it
   sound right?"
3. Build a shade ladder for **happy**: place *content, happy,
   delighted, ecstatic* in order. **Hint:** "Which is the quietest
   happy? Which is the loudest?"
4. Add a signal word: "The first bridge design was cheap. ___ , it
   could not hold the weight." (*However* — contrast.) **Hint:**
   "Are the two sentences agreeing or disagreeing?"

## Independent practice (Part B; 4 tasks)

1. Look up **murmur**. Write the part of speech and the numbered
   sense that fits "A murmur ran through the crowd." 
2. Upgrade 3 vague sentences with precise words (use the swap-and-check):
   "She **looked** at the test." / "It was a **good** plan." /
   "He **said** sorry."
3. Build a shade ladder for **cold**: *cool, cold, chilly, frigid,
   freezing*. Then write one sentence using the strongest rung
   correctly.
4. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "A classmate says the dictionary's
   meaning #1 is always the right one. Explain why that is wrong,
   using an example." (3–4 sentences.)

## Application (both parts)

**Detective's notebook page.** The learner takes one unknown word from
their own reading and fills the four boxes: **Clue** (type + signal
words + guess) → **Parts** (prefix/root/suffix breakdown) →
**Verify** (dictionary entry copied: part of speech + chosen numbered
sense) → **Use** (an original sentence using the word precisely).
This page becomes the template for the project journal.

## Exit check (Part B)

The learner looks up one new word, reads the entry's parts aloud, picks
the sentence-correct numbered sense, and uses the word in an original
sentence. Success: entry read correctly, correct sense chosen, sentence
uses the word precisely.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads dictionary entries
  aloud first; pronunciation keys may be skipped in favor of the
  adult modeling the word; sentence frames provided ("The clue type
  is ___ because I see ___").
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort clue-type cards,
  build ladders with word cards, or dictate upgrades for the adult to
  scribe.
- **Extension:** keep a running "shade ladder wall" for the unit —
  add one ladder per day from reading; find a multiple-meaning word
  with 3+ senses and write a sentence for each sense.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** dictionary as first resort (skipping
clue → parts → verify); grabbing numbered sense #1; thesaurus swaps
that break the sentence ("slim chance" vs. "skinny chance" —
synonyms are not interchangeable). Enforce the order with the
four-box notebook page. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-05). Reteach plan:
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-05).
