# Unit 02 Answer Key (adult-held)

Every practice item, quiz question, assessment task, and project
deliverable solved independently. Keep separate from learner pages.

## §L1 — Lesson 01 guided/independent practice

1. Accept: topic = the subject in a word or two ("a boy's kite");
   theme = the message about life in a full sentence ("asking for help
   takes courage"). Must use "The Broken Kite" as the example.
2. a. "Kites" — no: topic word, not a sentence (rule 1). b. "It is
   embarrassing to fail in front of people." — yes, defensible: it's a
   sentence, it's general, and the story shows it ("Sam's face burned.
   Everyone had watched"; he hides the kite). c. "Hiding a problem never
   fixes it." — yes: sentence, general, proven (the kite stays broken
   until he stops hiding).
3. Accept any detail different from the worked example, e.g.: "He shoved
   the backpack behind the porch steps" — hiding, not asking; or "Asking
   felt worse than the crash" — he avoids the courageous act. Must name
   the detail and tie it to asking/help.
4. (Independent wording; check the three rules + two details.) Sample:
   "It takes more courage to ask for help than to hide a failure."
   Details: "did not want to say the words out loud" (the fear); the kite
   flying "past the oak that had broken it" (the result of asking).
5. Two mistakes: (1) it's a plot summary, not a general sentence about
   life (rule 2 — remove-the-names test fails); (2) it states what
   *happened*, not what the story *says* (rule 1 — "the theme is that…"
   must be followed by a message, not an event).
6. Check: topic is 1–2 words; theme is a full general sentence; one
   starred proving detail. Adult judges reasonableness.

## §L2 — Lesson 02

1. Priya chart (honest limits): trait *loyal/supportive* — "Three… two…
   one!" she shouts for him; "Priya cheered from the sidewalk"
   (indirect, actions). Trait *observant* — she notices his red ears
   (from the retelling; indirect). Must note: with two appearances,
   claims like "Priya is brave" would be guessing — flag unsupported
   traits as guessing.
2. The imagined mother-question shows Sam expects judgment at home,
   which is part of why hiding feels safer than confessing — his motive
   for hiding includes avoiding that conversation, not just the
   festival embarrassment.
3. *Kind* fits best: "Ms. Okafor didn't laugh" + "let him do the last
   knot himself" (kindness with respect). *Patient* is defensible but
   thinner (one scene). *Strict* is contradicted — nothing she does is
   strict. Reject with the contradicting detail.
4. Before: proud ("did not want to say the words out loud"); careful
   ("tied every knot twice"). After: brave ("laid the torn kite on her
   picnic table"); grateful ("'Thank you,' he said quietly"). Each with
   its detail.
5. *Careless* is wrong: eleven days of work, sanded sticks, knots tied
   twice — the text proves care. The kite broke because "a gust hit
   sideways," an accident, not carelessness. Confusing the accident with
   a trait is the error.
6. Direct: "Ms. Okafor was a kind neighbor." (told outright). Indirect:
   "Ms. Okafor cut a new stick without being asked." (shown). Labels must
   match.

## §L3 — Lesson 03

1. As Sam (first person): notices the torn paper, the snapped stick,
   Ms. Okafor's hands with the tape; feels shame easing into relief,
   gratitude he can barely voice. As Ms. Okafor: notices the boy's red
   ears, his hesitation, the clean snap of the stick; feels matter-of-
   fact kindness, satisfaction at teaching. Sam knows his own shame;
   Ms. Okafor knows kites ("forty years") and sees Sam's embarrassment
   from outside.
2. Third person: "He stuffed the torn kite into his backpack"; "She cut
   a new stick." First person: "I was the one who shouted"; "I felt bad
   the whole walk home."
3. *Probably* marks a guess — Priya cannot hear Sam's thoughts, so she
   infers. It shows first-person narrators report their own mind fully
   and everyone else's only by observation.
4. Accept: "The detail is 'He dragged it out and laid the torn kite on
   her picnic table.' This shows Sam acting against his shame — the
   verbs prove the courage." Must quote exactly and explain.
5. Sample fix: "First-person narrators were there, but they still can't
   read minds — Priya has to guess with 'probably,' and she never sees
   the repair scene at all. Being present doesn't remove the limits."
6. Open — score the defense: e.g., first person as the kite for humor
   and strangeness, or third person to show *everyone's* pity. Must name
   the POV and give a reason tied to what it reveals/hides.

## §L4 — Lesson 04

1. False: "both helpers are adults" (Devon is a peer stagehand); "both
   problems are solved in one scene" (Sam's spans crash + repair visit).
   True: both feel fear/embarrassment about being watched; both endings
   show the character changed.
2. Open with required defense. Strong either way: Ms. Okafor (without
   her skill the kite never flies — the theme's proof depends on her) or
   Devon (one whispered sentence at the exact freezing moment; Maya does
   the rest herself).
3. Sample: "Courage means acting even when you're afraid of failing in
   front of people." Rules check: sentence ✓, general ✓. Proof: Sam —
   "laid the torn kite on her picnic table"; Maya — "She started from
   'the crown is heavy.'"
4. Venn: outer Sam — kite festival, adult helper with tools, failure
   repaired, shame→asking. Outer Maya — audition, peer helper with
   words, fear carried not erased, freezing→trying. Overlap (2+):
   realistic fiction; courage about being watched; a helper's small act
   at the turning point; shared-theme sentence labeled.
5. The mistake: comparing *topics* (kite vs. play) instead of *themes*.
   Topics differ in every pair of stories; themes are the big ideas
   underneath — here, courage — which is what the lesson compares.
6. Paragraph: must compare (not just describe each), with one quoted
   detail per story. Sample judgment: Sam changed more visibly (hiding
   → public asking, kite flying past the oak) while Maya's change is
   quieter (freezing → finishing); or the reverse, defended.

## §L5 — Lesson 05

4. Score with the self-review checklist: ☐ theme-sentence claim; ☐ two
   exact quotations in quotation marks; ☐ explanation after each
   ("this shows…"); ☐ details from two story parts; ☐ closing restates
   theme in new words. See §UA Task 1 sample for the standard.
5. Sample explanations for "Asking felt worse than the crash": "This
   shows Sam's real obstacle was never the broken stick — it was the
   shame of needing help, which is exactly what the theme calls
   courage." / "The comparison 'worse than the crash' proves asking felt
   like the bigger risk, so choosing to ask is the story's bravest act."
6. Short paragraph: claim about trying again + one quote ("Start from
   'the crown is heavy.'" or "You did it anyway") + explanation. Check
   the three moves in miniature.

## §PR — Practice sets

**A1.** a. T · b. Th · c. T · d. Th.
**A2.** b. Sentence ✓ (rule 1), general beyond kites ✓ (rule 2), story
proves it ✓ (rule 3). a is plot summary; c is a topic fragment.
**A3.** Proofs: "shoved the backpack behind the porch steps" (the kite
stays broken — hiding fixed nothing); "did not want to say the words
out loud" (the hiding persists until he speaks). Non-proof: "The kite
leapt, wobbled, and climbed" (pre-crash; irrelevant to hiding) or
"Ms. Okafor was watering her roses" (irrelevant detail).
**A4.** Sample: "Courage means trying again even when you're afraid."
Best detail: "She started from 'the crown is heavy,' and the lines came
back — shaky at first, then stronger."
**A5.** Disagree: the narrator never states the theme; it emerges from
events — the hiding, the asking, the kite flying "past the oak." The
reader infers it; no character announces it.

**B1.** (i)→careful · (ii)→proud · (iii)→kind · (iv)→respectful.
**B2.** a. indirect (action clue) · b. indirect (thought clue — we infer
the trait) · c. indirect (action clue).
**B3.** "Sam hid the kite because he was ashamed and did not want to say
the words out loud" — **stated** (text gives both). "Ms. Okafor helped
because she saw a struggling kid and knew kites" — **inferred** (her
skill is stated; her kind motive is implied by "didn't laugh" and
"Bring it here").
**B4.** Turning point: Ms. Okafor's "Bring it here" and Sam dragging the
backpack out / laying the kite on the table. The theme is *about*
asking — without the turn, the story would end on a hidden kite and the
theme would be unproven.
**B5.** Mildest→strongest: **embarrassed** ("Sam's face burned") <
**ashamed** (hiding; "did not want to say the words out loud") <
**humiliated** (the feared extreme — "in front of everybody").

**C1.** a. third person, outside narrator · b. first person, Priya ·
c. third person, outside narrator.
**C2.** Priya reveals: nobody was laughing ("We weren't"); she admired
the work ("sad his eleven days of work ended like that"); her own guilt
("I felt bad"). Priya hides: Sam's private thoughts ("did not want to
say the words out loud"); the entire repair scene (she wasn't there);
the second flight.
**C3.** Could describe: seeing Sam walk home "fast, head down" from her
yard before he spoke; her forty years of kite knowledge; her own
thoughts as she watches. Could not: Sam's private thoughts; the crash
itself (she only heard about the "tough morning"); Priya's feelings.
**C4.** Qualify/disagree: this narrator stays close to Sam — his
thoughts yes; Ms. Okafor's and Priya's thoughts never. Third person is
a *position*, not omniscience; this one chose Sam's shoulder.

**D1.** Kite: town festival, snapped wooden stick, tape repair — no
magic. Audition: forgetting lines on a school stage, a whispered
prompt — ordinary life.
**D2.** Fears: Sam — public shame ("in front of everybody"); Maya —
freezing/forgetting lines on stage. Helpers: Ms. Okafor (adult neighbor;
rebuilds the stick, teaches the lashing, lets Sam finish); Devon (peer
stagehand; whispers "Start from 'the crown is heavy.'"). Change: Sam —
hiding → asking; kite flies past the oak. Maya — nearly leaving →
trying again; earns the servant part, proud.
**D3.** Sample: "Courage means acting even when you're afraid of failing
in front of people." Differently: Sam's courage is *letting someone in*
("laid the torn kite on her picnic table"); Maya's is *not walking
out* ("She started from 'the crown is heavy'").
**D4.** Errors: (1) it ranks the characters instead of comparing what
each faced — different fears, different help; needing help isn't a
courage deficit. (2) The premise is false: Maya *did* get help (Devon's
whisper), and Sam's help *required* courage to accept ("worse than the
crash").

**E1.** 1 claim · 2 quote 1 · 3 explanation of quote 1 · 4 quote 2 ·
5 explanation of quote 2.
**E2.** Before: "The turning point comes when Sam finally stops hiding:"
After: "The verbs do the work — *dragged* shows asking still costs him,
and *laid* shows he does it anyway. That is the courage the theme
names."
**E3.** b. a describes the pre-crash flight — no help, no courage. b
captures the courageous act itself: reluctance (*dragged*) plus action
(*laid*).
**E4.** Sample: "Maya's shaking voice didn't stop her — and that is the
point: courage isn't the absence of fear, it's going on while your
hands shake." (New words, same theme.)

## §QZ — Formative quiz (week-08)

1. **b.** Full sentence (rule 1), general (rule 2), story-proven
   (rule 3). a is a title/topic; c is a single word.
2. (1) The ending contradicts it — the kite *does* fly near trees ("past
   the oak"), so the story disproves it (rule 3). (2) It's a rule about
   kite-flying, not a general message about life (rule 2).
3. Accept: **proud** — "did not want to say the words out loud";
   **careful** — "tied every knot twice." (Alternatives with proof
   accepted.)
4. **Indirect** — the narrator shares Sam's thought; we *infer* the
   trait (pride/shame) from the clue rather than being told it.
5. Accept stated: "…because Ms. Okafor said 'Bring it here' and told him
   a snapped stick is a ten-minute fix" — **stated**. Accept inferred:
   "…because hiding hadn't fixed anything and her offer made asking feel
   possible" — **inferred**. The label must match the answer given.
6. Proofs: "He stuffed the torn kite into his backpack" (he/him);
   "She cut a new stick" (she). Knows that Priya can't: Sam's private
   thoughts ("Asking felt worse than the crash") or the whole repair
   scene (Priya wasn't there).
7. *Probably* marks a guess — Priya can't read Sam's mind. First-person
   narrators know their own thoughts fully and others' only by
   observation; the word draws that boundary.
8. Sam: courage as **asking for help** — "laid the torn kite on her
   picnic table." Maya: courage as **trying again** — "She started from
   'the crown is heavy,' and the lines came back."
9. **b.** a shows the kite working before the crash — nothing about help
   or courage. b is the courageous act: *dragged* (reluctance) +
   *laid* (action anyway).
10. 1 claim · 2 quoted evidence · 3 explanation · 4 closing sentence.
    The explanation comes after because it answers "so what?" about the
    *specific words just quoted* — it translates the proof for the
    reader, which is impossible before the proof appears.

## §UA — Unit assessment samples and scoring notes

**Task 1 sample (claim A, 8 sentences):** "In 'The Broken Kite,' the
theme is that asking for help takes courage, and that courage is how
broken things get fixed. At the beginning, Sam hides the wrecked kite
'behind the porch steps' because he 'did not want to say the words out
loud.' This shows his real problem isn't the snapped stick — it's the
shame of admitting he needs help. In the middle, the turn comes when Ms.
Okafor says, 'Bring it here,' and Sam 'dragged it out and laid the torn
kite on her picnic table.' The verbs prove the theme: *dragged* shows
asking still costs him, and *laid* shows he does it anyway — that gap
between cost and action is courage. At the end, the kite climbs 'past
the oak that had broken it,' which proves the fix only happened because
Sam asked. The same tree that caused the shame becomes the measure of
the repair. Courage, the story argues, isn't never breaking — it's
carrying the broken thing to someone who can help."

Score 4 if: theme sentence + 2 exact quotes + explanation after each +
2+ story parts + new-words closing. Deduct per the teacher guide's
1–4 table.

**Task 2 sample (Sam):** (a) Beginning: **proud** — "did not want to say
the words out loud." End: **brave** — "laid the torn kite on her picnic
table." (b) Turning point: Ms. Okafor's "Bring it here" and Sam
dragging the backpack into the open. It connects the parts: the
beginning's hiding only matters because the middle reverses it, and the
ending's flight ("past the oak") only happens because of the reversal —
the parts fit together as problem → turn → proof. (c) "Sam finally asks
because Ms. Okafor's calm, practical offer made help feel safe instead
of shameful" — **inferred** (her words are stated; the effect on Sam is
assembled from his actions).

**Task 3 sample:** (a) Third person **can**: Sam's private thoughts
("Asking felt worse than the crash"). **Cannot**: Ms. Okafor's thoughts
as she watches; Priya's feelings on the walk home. Priya **can**: her
own feelings ("I felt bad the whole walk home") and what she witnessed
(the crash, Sam walking off). **Cannot**: Sam's thoughts ("he probably
believes" = guessing); the repair scene (absent). (b) "In the
third-person version the crash is Sam's humiliation — we sit inside his
burning face and his dread of speaking, so the scene feels like an
exposure. In Priya's version the same crash is a friend's sad
spectacle: 'We weren't' laughing, and the eleven days of work matter
more than the fall. The facts don't change, but the emotional truth
does — POV decides whose feelings set the meaning of the scene."

**Task 4 sample chart:** Kind of courage — Kite: *asking for help*
("laid the torn kite on her picnic table"); Audition: *trying again*
("started from 'the crown is heavy'"). Helper — Kite: Ms. Okafor, adult
neighbor, rebuilds the stick and teaches the lashing. Audition: Devon,
peer stagehand, whispers the restart line. Ending proof — Kite: the kite
flies "past the oak that had broken it" (failure repaired through
help). Audition: Maya earns the servant part and walks home proud
("You did it anyway" — fear carried, not erased).
**Sample paragraph:** "Both stories argue that courage means acting even
when you're afraid of failing in front of people, but they prove it
with different actions. Sam's courage is letting someone in — he 'laid
the torn kite on her picnic table' after hiding it, so his bravery is
*accepting* help. Maya's courage is refusing to leave — she 'started
from the crown is heavy' after freezing, so her bravery is *continuing*
alone. One story says courage opens the door; the other says courage
stays in the room. Together they show courage isn't one shape."

## §PJ — Project scoring notes

- **Know/can't-know list (3+3):** e.g., as Ms. Okafor — knows: saw Sam
  walk home fast and head-down; her forty years of kite-building; the
  clean snap of the stick. Can't know: Sam's thought "worse than the
  crash"; the festival crash itself; Priya's walk home.
- **Sample rewrite opening (Ms. Okafor, first person):** "I was watering
  the roses when the boy came up the walk fast, head down, a backpack
  dragging behind him. I had seen the red kite go up that morning — the
  whole park saw it — and I had seen it come down into the oak. He
  stopped at his porch and just stood there, and I knew that standstill.
  It is the moment a person decides whether to hide a thing or carry it
  somewhere. 'Tough morning at the festival,' I said, to give him an
  opening. He mumbled about the kite breaking in front of everybody. I
  did not laugh — there is nothing funny about eleven days of work in a
  tree. 'Bring it here,' I said. 'A snapped stick is a ten-minute fix.'"
  (Note how the narrator *wonders* rather than mind-reads: "I knew that
  standstill" is her interpretation, flagged as such.)
- **Sample theme paragraph (5–8 sentences):** "Telling the repair scene
  through Ms. Okafor changes what the theme 'asking for help takes
  courage' feels like. In my rewrite she says, 'there is nothing funny
  about eleven days of work in a tree,' which shows the helper sees the
  *work*, not the failure — Sam's shame was never the whole picture. The
  original story gives us Sam's version: he 'did not want to say the
  words out loud.' Putting the two together proves the theme twice: from
  Sam's side, asking costs pride; from Ms. Okafor's side, asking is met
  with respect, not ridicule. The new perspective reveals that courage
  isn't just enduring shame — it's discovering the shame was
  one-sided."
- Score with the project rubric (15–20 share-ready). The Strong column
  rewards voice distinctness, using a POV limit as tension, a fitting
  invented detail, and the "why did the author choose the original POV"
  reflection.
