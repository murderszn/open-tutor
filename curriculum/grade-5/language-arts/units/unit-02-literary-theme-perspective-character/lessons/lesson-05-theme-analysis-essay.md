# Lesson 05 — Proving It on Paper: The Theme-Analysis Paragraph

**Goal:** I can write a paragraph that states a theme claim, supports it
with quoted evidence, and explains how the evidence proves the claim.
**Time:** two sessions, ~40–45 minutes each · **Materials:** this lesson,
a notebook, your Theme Evidence Chart (Lesson 01), character timeline
(Lesson 02)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Say your theme statement for "The Broken Kite" out loud. Now imagine a
friend says, "Prove it." What would you point to in the story? A claim
without evidence is just an opinion — today you build the whole
argument.

## Teaching: claim → evidence → explanation

A **theme-analysis paragraph** has three moves, in order:

1. **Claim:** your theme statement, in your own words. ("In 'The Broken
   Kite,' the theme is that asking for help takes courage.")
2. **Evidence:** 2–3 **quoted or closely paraphrased details** from the
   story. Quote accurately — copy the words exactly and put them in
   quotation marks (RL.5.1). Short quotes beat long ones: quote the
   *sharpest* words.
3. **Explanation:** 2–3 sentences connecting each detail back to the
   claim. Never drop a quote and run — tell the reader *what the detail
   shows*.

A strong explanation also shows **how the story's parts fit together**
(RL.5.5): the beginning sets up the problem, the middle turns it, the
ending proves the theme. Name where your details come from (beginning /
middle / end) to show the theme *developing*, not just appearing.

### Worked example 1 — a full model paragraph (study, don't copy)

> In "The Broken Kite," the theme is that asking for help takes courage,
> and that courage is how broken things get fixed. At the beginning, Sam
> hides the wrecked kite "behind the porch steps" because he "did not
> want to say the words out loud" — he is more afraid of asking than he
> was of the crash itself. This shows that his real problem isn't the
> broken stick; it's his pride. In the middle, the turning point comes
> when Ms. Okafor says, "Bring it here," and Sam "dragged it out and
> laid the torn kite on her picnic table." The verbs matter: *dragged*
> shows it still costs him, and *laid* shows he does it anyway — that is
> the courage the theme names. At the end, the kite climbs "past the oak
> that had broken it," which proves the theme: the same tree that caused
> the shame becomes the measuring stick for the fix, and the fix only
> happened because Sam asked.

Notice: claim first, then three details in story order (beginning →
middle → end), each followed by its explanation. The paragraph quotes
exactly ("behind the porch steps") and explains word choices ("The verbs
matter").

### Worked example 2 — fixing a weak paragraph

**Weak draft:** "The theme is courage. Sam was sad when his kite broke.
Ms. Okafor helped him. Then it flew. This shows courage."

**Diagnosis (three problems):**
1. The claim is a topic word, not a theme sentence (Lesson 01, rule 1).
2. No quotations — "Sam was sad" is a summary, not evidence (RL.5.1).
3. No explanation — "This shows courage" doesn't say *how*.

**Revision moves:** (a) rewrite the claim as a sentence about asking for
help; (b) replace "Sam was sad" with the quoted detail "did not want to
say the words out loud"; (c) add: "This shows his fear was about *asking*,
not about the kite — which is why the theme is about courage, not about
kites." Same story, honest argument.

## Guided practice (with an adult)

1. Using your Theme Evidence Chart, pick your two strongest details.
   Practice saying each as: "The detail is ___. This shows ___."
2. Together, outline a paragraph on this claim: *"In 'The Broken Kite,'
   Sam changes from hiding problems to facing them."* Choose one
   beginning detail, one middle detail, and one end detail from your
   character timeline (Lesson 02).
3. The adult reads the model paragraph aloud. You mark: where is the
   claim? Where does each explanation begin? Put a star by the sentence
   you think is the strongest explanation, and say why.

## Independent practice

4. Draft your own theme-analysis paragraph on "The Broken Kite"
   (6–10 sentences): claim → 2–3 quoted details in story order → an
   explanation after each detail → a closing sentence restating the theme
   in new words.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate's paragraph ends with three quotations
   in a row and no explanations. Write the two missing explanation
   sentences for one of those quotes (invent a plausible quote from the
   story, or use "Asking felt worse than the crash").
6. Write a second, shorter paragraph (4–5 sentences) arguing a theme for
   "The Quiet Audition" with one quoted detail ("Start from 'the crown
   is heavy'" or "You did it anyway") and your explanation.

## Applied task

**Revise like an editor:** re-read your draft and check the
self-review list — ☐ claim is a full theme sentence; ☐ at least two
exact quotations in quotation marks; ☐ every quote has an explanation
sentence; ☐ details come from at least two different parts of the story;
☐ closing sentence restates the theme in new words. Fix every unchecked
box, then read the paragraph aloud to the adult. If any sentence sounds
like a plot summary, convert it to explanation ("this shows…").

## Exit check

Without looking back: what are the three moves of a theme-analysis
paragraph, in order? And why must a quotation never stand alone?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** sentence frames for explanations: "This detail
  shows ___ because ___." / "The word ___ matters because ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** the paragraph may be dictated; the adult
  scribes while the learner directs every quotation and explanation.
- **Extension:** write a counter-paragraph arguing a *different* theme
  for the same story (e.g., about embarrassment or persistence) with its
  own evidence. Which argument is stronger, and why? (Evaluates evidence
  quality — a U03 preview.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) summary in place of analysis — the "this
shows…" test catches it; (2) theme statements that merely restate the
prompt; (3) dropped quotations with no explanation — model the
explanation as *translation* ("in other words, this detail means…").
