# Lesson 01 — What Is This Story Really About? (Theme)

**Goal:** I can tell the difference between a story's *topic* and its
*theme*, and I can write a theme statement supported by details from the
text.
**Time:** two sessions, ~40–45 minutes each · **Materials:** this lesson,
a notebook, the story below (read-aloud first, then re-read)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Say or write: what is the *topic* of your favorite movie or book — in one
or two words? (Examples: "sharks," "a soccer team," "a haunted house.")
Now finish this sentence about the same movie or book: "I think the movie
is really trying to say that ___." That second sentence is reaching for
the **theme**. Today we learn to find it on purpose.

## Teaching: topic is the subject; theme is the message

The **topic** is what a story is *about* — usually one or two words:
*kites, a festival, a broken toy*. The **theme** is what the story *says
about* that topic — a full sentence, a message about life that the reader
can carry into other situations: *Asking for help takes courage.*

Three rules for a good theme statement:

1. **It is a sentence, not a word.** "Courage" is a topic. "It takes
   courage to ask for help" is a theme.
2. **It says something about life in general**, not just this story's
   plot. A theme should still make sense if you remove the character
   names.
3. **The story must prove it.** Every theme statement needs at least one
   detail from the text that shows it is true.

## Anchor story — "The Broken Kite" (original; read together)

*Sam had worked on the red kite for eleven days. He sanded the sticks
smooth, tied every knot twice, and painted a white lightning bolt down
the middle. On festival morning the wind was perfect — the kind of wind
that lifted flags straight out from their poles.*

*"Three… two… one!" Priya shouted, and Sam ran. The kite leapt, wobbled,
and climbed. For one shining minute it was the highest thing in the
park. Then a gust hit sideways. The kite spun, dove, and smashed into
the oak by the playground. The frame snapped with a sound like a dry
twig.*

*Sam's face burned. Everyone had watched. He stuffed the torn kite into
his backpack and walked home fast, head down. At his porch he stopped.
He could not carry the wreckage inside — Mom would ask what happened,
and he did not want to say the words out loud. He shoved the backpack
behind the porch steps.*

*Ms. Okafor was watering her roses next door. "Tough morning at the
festival," she said. Sam stared at his shoes. "My kite broke," he
mumbled. "It broke in front of everybody."*

*"Bring it here," Ms. Okafor said. "I built kites for forty years before
I retired. A snapped stick is a ten-minute fix." Sam hesitated. Asking
felt worse than the crash — like admitting he couldn't do it alone. But
the backpack was already in his hands. He dragged it out and laid the
torn kite on her picnic table.*

*Ms. Okafor didn't laugh. She cut a new stick, showed Sam how to lash it
with tape, and let him do the last knot himself. "Test it," she said.
The wind had calmed. Sam ran, the kite rose — wobbling, then steady —
and climbed past the oak that had broken it. Priya cheered from the
sidewalk. Sam laughed out loud, and it surprised him.*

*"Thank you," he said quietly. Ms. Okafor smiled. "The kite didn't need
a perfect builder," she said. "It just needed a builder who wasn't
afraid to ask."*

### Worked example 1 — topic vs. theme

**Topic of "The Broken Kite":** *a boy's kite* (two words — that's the
subject).

**Weak theme statement:** "The story is about courage." ✗ This is just a
topic word. It doesn't say anything.

**Strong theme statement:** "Asking for help takes courage, and courage
is how broken things get fixed." ✓ It is a sentence, it applies beyond
kites (homework, friendships, anything hard), and the story proves it.

**Proof from the text:** Sam hides the kite behind the porch steps
because he "did not want to say the words out loud" — asking feels
"worse than the crash." Then he *does* ask, Ms. Okafor helps, and the
kite flies "past the oak that had broken it." The change from hiding to
asking is exactly what the theme describes.

### Worked example 2 — testing a wrong theme

**Proposed theme:** "Never build kites."

Test it against the three rules: (1) It's a sentence ✓. (2) Does it say
something about life in general? Barely — it's really just about kites
✗. (3) Does the story prove it? No — the story *ends with the kite
flying*. A theme the ending contradicts cannot be the theme. ✗

**Proposed theme:** "Practice makes perfect."

Test: it's a sentence ✓, it's general ✓ — but the story doesn't prove
it. Sam doesn't get better by practicing alone; he gets the kite flying
by *accepting help*. The evidence points to courage and help, not solo
practice. Good readers let the evidence choose the theme, not their
first guess.

## Guided practice (with an adult)

1. In your own words, explain the difference between topic and theme to
   the adult. Use "The Broken Kite" in your explanation.
2. Which of these could be a theme of "The Broken Kite"? Say why each
   yes or no, using the three rules:
   a. "Kites"
   b. "It is embarrassing to fail in front of people."
   c. "Hiding a problem never fixes it."
3. Find one more detail from the story — a different one from the worked
   example — that supports the theme "Asking for help takes courage."

## Independent practice

4. Write a theme statement for "The Broken Kite" in your own words
   (different wording from the worked example is fine). Then list two
   story details that prove it.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The theme is that Sam's kite
   broke." Explain the two mistakes in that sentence (hint: check the
   three rules).
6. Think of a movie or book you know well. Write its topic (1–2 words)
   and a theme statement (full sentence). Star the one detail from it
   you remember best that proves your theme.

## Applied task

Make a two-column **Theme Evidence Chart** for "The Broken Kite" in your
notebook. Left column: three details from the story (quote or
paraphrase). Right column: for each detail, one sentence explaining how
it supports your theme statement. You will reuse this chart in Lesson 05.

## Exit check

Without looking back: (1) What is the difference between topic and
theme? (2) Write a theme statement for "The Broken Kite" and name one
detail that proves it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the story may be read aloud by the adult first;
  the learner follows along, then re-reads silently. Pre-teach *hesitated*
  and *lashed* with the U01 dictionary routine.
- **Alternate response mode:** the theme statement may be dictated to the
  adult; the evidence chart may be drawn as three picture panels with
  captions.
- **Extension:** write a second, different theme the story could support
  (for example, about embarrassment or persistence) and argue for it with
  two details. Strong stories often hold more than one theme.

("Sam fixed his kite") — redirect with rule 2, the "remove the names"
test; (2) learners pick a moral the story doesn't earn ("always obey
adults") — redirect with rule 3, the ending test; (3) one-word "themes"
