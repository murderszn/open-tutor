# Lesson 02 — Characters from the Inside Out

**Goal:** I can describe a character's traits, motives, and change using
specific details about what the character says, does, and thinks.
**Time:** two sessions, ~40–45 minutes each · **Materials:** this lesson,
a notebook, "The Broken Kite" (Lesson 01)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Name one character from any story you know. Say one **trait** (what kind
of person they are — *brave, sneaky, generous*), and tell how you know:
what did they *do* or *say* that showed it? Traits need proof, just like
themes do.

## Teaching: reading people on the page

A **character trait** is a quality that describes how a character tends to
act: *proud, kind, nervous, determined*. Authors reveal traits two ways:

- **Direct characterization** — the narrator tells you outright: "Sam
  was embarrassed."
- **Indirect characterization** — you figure it out from clues: what the
  character **says** (dialogue), **does** (actions), **thinks** (thoughts
  the narrator shares), and how **others react** to them.

A character's **motive** is *why* they act — the want underneath the
action. And the most interesting characters **change**: compare who they
are at the beginning with who they are at the end, and find the moment
that changed them.

**Precise-word habit (U01):** traits are adjectives, and near-synonyms
are not identical. *Proud* (thinking well of yourself) is not the same as
*stubborn* (refusing to change course), and *nervous* is not the same as
*afraid*. Pick the word the evidence actually earns.

### Worked example 1 — Sam's traits from indirect clues

**Trait: proud (at the start).**

- **Action clue:** "He stuffed the torn kite into his backpack and
  walked home fast, head down." He hides the damage instead of asking
  anyone at the festival for help.
- **Thought clue:** "Asking felt worse than the crash — like admitting
  he couldn't do it alone." He would rather suffer than admit he needs
  help. That is pride: protecting how he looks instead of fixing the
  problem.

Why *proud* and not *lazy*? A lazy character wouldn't have "worked on
the red kite for eleven days" and "tied every knot twice." The evidence
rules *lazy* out — good trait-naming checks the whole story.

**Trait: brave (at the end).**

- **Action clue:** "He dragged it out and laid the torn kite on her
  picnic table." After hiding it, he brings it into the open — the
  opposite of his earlier hiding.
- **Dialogue clue:** "Thank you," he said quietly. He speaks the words
  he "did not want to say out loud" earlier.

### Worked example 2 — motive and change

**Motive question:** Why does Sam finally bring the kite to Ms. Okafor?

The text says: "But the backpack was already in his hands." He has
carried the hidden kite all the way home, and hiding it hasn't fixed
anything. His motive is not suddenly loving help — it is that **hiding
stopped working**. Readers can state a motive the text implies without
the text saying it word-for-word, as long as the details support it.

**Change:** Beginning Sam hides problems ("shoved the backpack behind
the porch steps"); ending Sam faces them ("laid the torn kite on her
picnic table," then flies it "past the oak that had broken it"). The
**turning moment** is Ms. Okafor's line: "Bring it here." Her calm,
practical offer — "A snapped stick is a ten-minute fix" — makes asking
feel possible. Character change needs a cause; here the cause is another
character's kindness lowering the cost of asking.

**Ms. Okafor in brief (direct + indirect):** Direct: "I built kites for
forty years" (experienced). Indirect: "Ms. Okafor didn't laugh" (kind —
she refuses to shame him); "let him do the last knot himself"
(respectful — she helps without taking over).

## Guided practice (with an adult)

1. Fill a three-column chart for **Priya** (trait | detail | direct or
   indirect?). Priya has only two appearances — what can you honestly
   claim, and what would be guessing?
2. Sam's mother never appears on the page, but Sam imagines her asking
   "what happened." What does that imagined moment tell you about Sam's
   motive for hiding the kite?
3. Which precise trait word fits Ms. Okafor best — *kind, patient,* or
   *strict*? Defend your pick with one detail and explain why you
   rejected the other two (U01 shades-of-meaning habit).

## Independent practice

4. Make a **before/after chart** for Sam: two traits at the beginning
   with one detail each, two traits at the end with one detail each.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Sam is careless. That's why
   the kite broke." Use story details to show why *careless* is the
   wrong trait — and name what actually broke the kite.
6. Write two sentences about a real or fictional person you know: one
   using direct characterization, one using indirect characterization.
   Label which is which.

## Applied task

Draw a **character-change timeline** for Sam with four points: (1) the
festival crash, (2) hiding the backpack, (3) Ms. Okafor's offer,
(4) the second flight. Under each point, write what Sam does and one
trait word for how he is acting. Circle the turning point. (A
deterministic plot-diagram SVG for this timeline is in `assets/`.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: name one trait of Sam's at the beginning and one
at the end, each with the story detail that proves it. Then state his
motive for finally asking for help in one sentence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** re-read only the four timeline scenes rather than
  the whole story; the adult reads dialogue aloud with expression.
- **Alternate response mode:** traits may be shown as labeled drawings
  (facial expressions, body language) with dictated captions.
- **Extension:** write a paragraph from Ms. Okafor's point of view
  (preview of Lesson 03): what did *she* notice about Sam that morning?
  What was her motive for offering help?

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) trait lists without evidence — require the
detail every time; (2) confusing what *happens to* a character with the
character's trait (the crash doesn't make Sam clumsy — "the wind" and "a
gust" caused it; eleven days of careful work rules out carelessness);
(3) inventing motives the text can't support ("Ms. Okafor was lonely")
— allow inferences but demand a detail. Answer-key reference:
`answer-key.md` §L2. Next: Lesson 03 changes the camera — point of view.
