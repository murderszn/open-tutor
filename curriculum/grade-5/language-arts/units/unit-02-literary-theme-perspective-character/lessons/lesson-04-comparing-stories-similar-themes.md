# Lesson 04 — Same Big Idea, Different Story

**Goal:** I can compare and contrast two stories in the same genre on how
they approach a similar theme, using evidence from both texts.
**Time:** two sessions, ~40–45 minutes each · **Materials:** this lesson,
a notebook, "The Broken Kite" (Lesson 01)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

"The Broken Kite" is **realistic fiction** — a made-up story that *could*
really happen (no magic, no talking animals). Name one other realistic
story you know (book, movie, or show). What makes it realistic rather
than fantasy?

## Teaching: comparing stories is comparing choices

**Compare** means finding what is alike; **contrast** means finding what
is different. When two stories share a **genre** (type of story) and a
similar **theme**, comparing them shows you something one story alone
can't: how different authors — different characters, events, and choices
— can explore the *same big idea* in different ways.

Useful comparison points for stories: the **characters** (who changes?),
the **problem**, the **kind of courage or choice** the theme requires,
**how the problem gets solved** (alone? with help?), and the **ending**.

## Second anchor story — "The Quiet Audition" (original; read together)

*Maya knew every line of the play. She had whispered them into her
pillow for two weeks — the brave queen's big speech, the funny servant's
jokes, all of it. But knowing the lines in her bedroom and saying them
on the auditorium stage were two different planets.*

*At auditions, her name was called third. She walked up the steps, and
her voice came out thin as thread. Halfway through the queen's speech
she forgot the next line. Silence filled the whole auditorium. Maya
stared at the exit sign and thought about leaving.*

*Then Devon, the stagehand holding the clipboard, whispered, "Start from
'the crown is heavy.' You know this part." Maya breathed in. She started
from "the crown is heavy," and the lines came back — shaky at first,
then stronger. She didn't get the queen. She got the servant, the funny
one. On the cast list her name looked small and wonderful.*

*Walking home, Devon said, "You almost left." Maya nodded. "My voice was
shaking the whole time." Devon grinned. "Yeah. You did it anyway."*

### Worked example 1 — what the two stories share

Both stories are **realistic fiction**, and both circle the same big
idea: **courage**.

- "The Broken Kite": Sam is ashamed of failing in public; courage for
  him means **asking for help** — dragging the hidden kite into the open
  and letting Ms. Okafor teach him the repair.
- "The Quiet Audition": Maya is afraid of failing in public; courage
  for her means **trying again after freezing** — starting over from
  "the crown is heavy" instead of walking out.

Same theme family (courage), different *kind* of courage. A theme can
wear different clothes in different stories.

### Worked example 2 — how the stories differ

| Comparison point | "The Broken Kite" | "The Quiet Audition" |
|---|---|---|
| Main character's problem | Shame after a public failure; hides the broken kite | Fear before a public try; nearly quits the audition |
| Who helps? | Ms. Okafor, an adult neighbor, with tools and skill | Devon, a peer, with a whispered reminder |
| How is it solved? | Sam **accepts help** he didn't want | Maya **keeps going** on her own voice |
| Ending | The kite flies past the oak — the failure is repaired | Maya gets a smaller part — the fear isn't erased, it's carried |

**The contrast teaches:** courage isn't one shape. Sam's courage is
*letting someone in*; Maya's is *not walking out*. If the theme of both
stories is "courage means acting even when you're afraid," each story
proves it with a different kind of action — and that is exactly what
comparing reveals.

## Guided practice (with an adult)

1. Together, sort these into "alike" and "different": both main
   characters feel embarrassment or fear about being watched; both
   helpers are adults; both problems are solved in one scene; both
   endings show the character changed. (Two of these are false — find
   them.)
2. Which story's helper matters more to the ending — Ms. Okafor or
   Devon? Defend your answer with one detail from each story. (Defensible
   answers either way; demand the details.)
3. Write a one-sentence theme that fits **both** stories. Test it with
   the Lesson 01 rules and one detail from each story.

## Independent practice

4. Complete a **Venn diagram** (two overlapping circles): at least three
   items in each outer section and two in the overlap, all tied to story
   details. Label the overlap with your shared-theme sentence.
5. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The stories are completely
   different because one is about a kite and one is about a play."
   Explain the mistake using the words *topic* and *theme*.
6. Which character changed more — Sam or Maya? Write a paragraph (5–7
   sentences) comparing their changes with at least one quoted detail
   from each story.

## Applied task

Record a 2-minute **"book talk"** (spoken to the adult, or written as a
script): recommend *one* of the two stories to a friend who is nervous
about trying something hard. Name the story's theme, describe the main
character's change in two sentences, and explain why this story — not the
other one — fits your friend. (Speaking/listening: SL.5.1.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: (1) What genre are both stories, and what makes
that genre realistic? (2) State one similarity and one difference in how
the two stories show courage, each with a story detail.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads "The Quiet Audition" aloud first;
  the learner tracks Maya's feelings with sticky-note marks (nervous →
  frozen → trying → proud).
- **Alternate response mode:** the Venn diagram may be built with
  moveable cards instead of writing; the book talk may be audio-recorded.
- **Extension:** add a third story — an original 8–10 sentence fable
  with animal characters about courage — and place it on the comparison:
  how does changing the *genre* change what the theme can do?

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) comparing topics instead of themes ("both
have kids") — push back to the big idea; (2) plot summary disguised as
comparison — require the "so what" sentence for each point ("this shows
courage looks different when…"); (3) treating "different" as "worse" —
contrast is neutral. This lesson's comparison chart is reused in the
unit assessment. Answer-key reference: `answer-key.md` §L4. Next:
