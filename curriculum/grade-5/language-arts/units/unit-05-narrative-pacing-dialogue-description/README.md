# Unit 05 — Narrative Craft: Pacing, Dialogue, and Description

**Grade:** 5 · **Subject:** Language Arts · **Track weeks:** 19–22
**Backlog:** [issue #28](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/28), section U05
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

A story is not a list of things that happened. It is a writer choosing
where to begin, who gets to speak, which moment to slow down for, and
which words will make the reader feel the cold iron of the stairs under
their own feet. In this unit the learner follows one original story —
"The Night the Light Went Out," about Maya, eleven, and her
grandmother Gran Rosa, keeper of the Gull Point lighthouse — through
its whole story mountain, then takes the story apart to learn its
craft: how an opening orients a reader, how dialogue shows character,
how pacing stretches the important moment and hurries past the rest,
how concrete words and the five senses replace flat telling, and how
an ending must follow from everything that came before. The unit ends
with the learner writing, revising, and reading aloud a complete
original narrative of their own.

## Essential question

How does a writer decide what to show slowly, what to tell quickly,
and what to let the characters say for themselves?

- Summarize and paraphrase in one's own words with a source list
  (U03; RI.5.2, W.5.8)

  interest, and style (U04; L.5.3.a); use perfect verb tenses and
  keep tense consistent (U04; L.5.1.b–d); punctuate series,

  L.5.2.d) — all reused in the learner's own narrative draft

Lesson 01 re-teaches the story mountain and the orientation move
rather than assuming they are secure. Nothing in this unit assumes
the learner has read the Unit 02 stories or the Unit 03 Willow Creek

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. **Orient the reader** by establishing a situation and introducing
   a narrator and/or characters, and **organize an event sequence
   that unfolds naturally**, using a variety of transitional words,
   phrases, and clauses to manage the sequence (W.5.3.a, W.5.3.c).
2. **Use dialogue, description, and pacing** as narrative techniques
   to develop experiences and events and to **show the responses of
   characters to situations**, punctuating dialogue correctly —
   quotation marks around exact words, commas inside the closing
   quotation mark before a tag, a new paragraph for each speaker,
   and a capital letter for the first spoken word (W.5.3.b;
   dialogue-punctuation rules applied from L.4.2.b; L.5.2.b for
   introductory-element commas in tags).
3. **Control pacing**: stretch an important moment into a scene with
   detail, dialogue, and character thought, and speed past less
   important time with summary and transitions (W.5.3.b); expand,
   combine, and reduce sentences for meaning and style while doing
   so (L.5.3.a).
4. **Use concrete words and phrases and sensory details** to convey
   experiences and events precisely — showing through actions,
   senses, and exact verbs instead of flatly telling the reader what
   a character feels (W.5.3.d, L.5.3.a).
5. **Provide a conclusion that follows from the narrated
   experiences**, produce **clear and coherent writing** appropriate
   to task, purpose, and audience, and **develop and strengthen
   writing by planning, revising, and editing** with guidance from
   peers and adults — including punctuating the titles of works
   (W.5.3.e, W.5.4, W.5.5, W.5.10, L.5.2.d); compare and contrast
   two characters' interactions as a craft model (RL.5.3).

## Anchor texts (all original, authored for this unit)

- **"The Night the Light Went Out"** — an original narrative
  (~600 words) printed in full in Lesson 01. Maya, eleven, spends
  a stormy October weekend with her grandmother Gran Rosa, keeper
  of the Gull Point lighthouse. The lamp goes out in the storm;
  they climb ninety-seven dark steps to fix it; the light returns
  as a fishing boat rounds the point. Used in Lessons 01–05, in
  the practice sets, and as the craft model for the project. The
  people, the lighthouse, and Gull Point are invented for
  teaching; no real place or person is described.
- **"The Night the Light Went Out" — fast and slow versions of the
  tower climb** — two original retellings of the same climb
  printed side by side in Lesson 03: a three-sentence summary and
  a stretched scene. Used in Lesson 03 and Practice C.
- **"The Lamp Room" — telling and showing versions** — two
  original versions of the same paragraph printed in Lesson 04:
  one that names feelings flatly, one that shows them through
  senses and actions. Used in Lesson 04 and Practice D.
- **Three endings for the lighthouse story** — one that follows
  from the story, one that invents a new problem, and one that
  preaches a moral, printed in Lesson 05. Used in Lesson 05 and
  Practice E.
- **"The Map in the Attic"** — an original assessment narrative
  (~230 words) printed only in the culminating assessment. It is
  new to the learner at assessment time.
- **Lighthouse-storm illustration** — a generated illustration in
  [assets/lighthouse-storm-scene.png](assets/lighthouse-storm-scene.png),
  used in Lesson 05 as a story prompt. It is an AI illustration,
  not a photograph, and it shows no real place or people.
- **Story-mountain diagram** — a deterministic SVG in
  [assets/story-mountain-diagram.svg](assets/story-mountain-diagram.svg),
  used in Lesson 01. Its labels are exact (exposition, inciting
  incident, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution)
  and its shape is the teaching model, not a measurement.

No copyrighted texts are reproduced. The one book title mentioned
in the unit (*The Lighthouse Cat*) is invented for teaching; any
resemblance to a real work's title is coincidental, and no real
work is quoted.

Dialogue-punctuation note: the specific rules this unit teaches —
quotation marks around a speaker's exact words, a comma inside the
closing quotation mark when a tag follows, a new paragraph each time
the speaker changes, and a capital letter for the first spoken word —
are spelled out in the grade-4 framework (L.4.2.b, "use commas and
quotation marks to mark direct speech") and in grade 3 (L.3.2.c).
Grade 5 (L.5.2) states the general command of capitalization,
punctuation, and spelling; this unit applies the grade-4 dialogue
rules inside grade-5 narrative writing (W.5.3.b) and does not claim
them as a grade-5 code. Supporting standards used sparingly: L.5.6
(precise words such as *meanwhile, at last, suddenly* as sequence
signals), SL.5.4 (report on the finished story in a logical
sequence, speaking clearly at an understandable pace), SL.5.6
(formal English for the read-aloud). RL.5.8 does not exist and
RF.5.1–2 do not exist at grade 5; neither is cited anywhere in this
unit.

activity and success criterion; repeated labels are never reused
without a specific new task.

## How lessons, practice, reading, investigation, and review fill the unit

Five explicit lessons (sessions 1, 3, 5, 7, 9) introduce each craft
tool; each is paired with a practice session that applies it to new
material (sessions 2, 4, 6, 8, 10). Connected reading runs through
the whole unit: the same lighthouse story is reread for new purposes
— first for its shape, then for its dialogue, then for its pacing,
then for its sensory detail, then for its ending — so the learner
sees that rereading with a new question finds new craft. The
investigation is the learner's own complete narrative
(sessions 11–12, revised in session 14): it is the unit's synthesis
task, not an add-on. Retrieval and review (sessions 13–14) precede
the formative quiz (session 15); the culminating assessment
(session 16) uses a new passage so it measures transfer, not memory
of the lighthouse story. Feedback is same-day on practice, and the
teacher guide gives reteach moves for each lesson.
