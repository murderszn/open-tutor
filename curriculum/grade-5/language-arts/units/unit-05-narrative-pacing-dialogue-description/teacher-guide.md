# Teacher Guide — Unit 05: Narrative Craft

For the guiding adult. This guide never goes to the learner with the
lessons; keep it separate from student pages. All question solutions
are in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md); this guide explains how to
teach, what to watch for, and what to do next when the learner
struggles.

## How to use this unit

- **Session length:** about 40 minutes, four sessions per week, as
  paced in the unit README. If a lesson runs long, split it at the
  guided-practice break and finish independent practice the next
  session — do not skip the exit check.
- **Reading routine:** read each new passage aloud first while the
  learner follows, then have the learner reread it for the lesson's
  question. Rereading with a new question is a taught skill here,
  not a sign of failure.
- **Feedback:** review practice the same day. A wrong answer that is
  corrected the same day, in the learner's own words, counts as
  learning; a wrong answer left uncorrected until the quiz does not.
- **What "proficient" means:** the success criteria in the unit
  README's pacing table. The formative quiz is formative: a score
  below 8/10 triggers reteaching (below), not a grade penalty.
- **Read-aloud, not peer work:** this track serves one learner at a
  time with a guiding adult. Every "share" moment in this unit is a
  read-aloud to the adult, who listens and retells — never work
  shared between learners.

## Per-lesson notes

### Lesson 01 — Orienting the reader; story mountain

- **Likely misconception:** the storm is named as the inciting
  incident, or the light going out is named as the climax. Keep
  returning to the test: *the inciting incident breaks the
  ordinary world; the climax decides the outcome.* The storm is
  setting; the blown fuse is the trouble; the thrown switch is the
  turning point.
- **Reteach move:** if the learner cannot tell exposition from
  inciting incident, cover ¶3 and ask, "What was ordinary before
  this paragraph?" The answer (lamp lit, storm coming, supper
  routines) is the exposition; the paragraph itself is the break.
- **Watch for:** orientation paragraphs that list facts ("Maya is
  11. She likes lighthouses.") instead of placing the reader in a
  scene. Require who, where, and when inside a sentence that also
  shows something happening.

### Lesson 02 — Dialogue that shows character

- **Likely misconception:** the comma goes outside the quotation
  marks, or a period is used before the tag ("The fuse has blown."
  she said). Keep returning to the test: *if a tag follows, the
  period becomes a comma inside the marks.* Also: capitalizing the
  tag after a question mark ("What if we can't fix it?" She asked)
  — the tag is part of the same sentence, so it stays lowercase.
- **Reteach move:** if punctuation keeps slipping, have the learner
  read each line aloud with the punctuation spoken ("quote …
  comma … close quote … lowercase tag") before writing it. Hearing
  the rules first makes the written form stick.
- **Watch for:** dialogue that only reports information ("As you
  know, the fuse has blown") instead of sounding like a person.
  Ask, "Would anyone really say that out loud?"

### Lesson 03 — Pacing: scene and summary

- **Likely misconception:** stretching the wrong moment — usually
  the beginning, because it is easiest to describe — and
  summarizing the climax because it is hardest to write. Keep
  returning to the test: *stretch the choice, skip the routine.*
- **Reteach move:** if the learner stretches everything, give them
  a five-event outline and a budget: "You may stretch two events
  and must summarize three." The budget forces the choice.
- **Watch for:** "slow" mistaken for "long sentences." Slow means
  more detail, dialogue, and thought per moment of story time; a
  stretch can use short sentences too ("She stopped." is the most
  tense sentence in the climb).

### Lesson 04 — Sensory detail; show, don't tell

- **Likely misconception:** replacing one vague word with another
  (*scared* → *terrified*) and calling it showing. Keep returning
  to the three tests: a sense, an action, an exact word.
- **Reteach move:** if showing will not come, ask the learner to
  close their eyes and describe what their character's hands are
  doing. Hands almost always show the feeling: shaking, gripping,
  steady.
- **Watch for:** sensory detail added to every sentence, including
  summaries. Showing belongs to the stretched moments (Lesson 03);
  a summary can tell plainly. Also: "show, don't tell" applied to
  facts the reader needs quickly (the fuse blew; the spare was in
  the chest) — those are told, and that is correct.

### Lesson 05 — Endings that follow; revision

- **Likely misconception:** any ending that mentions the story's
  topic is a good ending ("And they all lived happily ever after
  at the lighthouse"). It names the place but answers nothing and
  shows no change. Make the learner point to the exact sentence
  that passes each of the three tests.
- **Reteach move:** if endings keep inventing new problems, forbid
  new nouns in the last paragraph: no new person, place, or thing
  may appear. The ending must be built from what is already there.
- **Watch for:** revising by only fixing spelling. Editing is the
  last step; revision (re-seeing) comes first. And the "woke up
  and it was all a dream" ending — name the trick and why it
  breaks trust: it tells the reader none of it mattered.

## Practice, quiz, and assessment

- **Practice sets A–E** follow Lessons 01–05 in order. Sets A and B
  rebuild the legacy dialogue and story-mountain quiz items with
  lighthouse material; Set D rebuilds the legacy sensory quiz the
  same way. Do not assign the legacy files — their keys sit beside
  the questions.
- **Week-22 quiz (10 points):** give it in session 15, after the
  review session. Score 8–10: ready for the assessment. Score
  6–7: reteach the missed lesson's worked examples, then the
  assessment. Below 6: reteach the whole lesson for the missed
  objective and repeat the matching practice set before the
  assessment.
- **Unit assessment (24 points):** give it in session 16. Part A
  uses a new passage ("The Map in the Attic") so it measures
  transfer. Part B is a writing task scored against its four
  sub-criteria in the answer key; accept any scene that meets the
  stated requirements, even if its events differ from the sample.
- **Project (20 points):** score with the rubric in
  [project.md](project.md). The read-aloud is part of the
  deliverable: listen for the orientation, the dialogue scene, the
  stretched moment, the sensory paragraph, and the ending, and
  name one strength and one next step afterward.

## Readiness to move on

The learner is ready for Unit 06 (opinion writing) when they can,
without prompting: open a story with who/where/when; punctuate a
four-line exchange with at most one error; name whether a passage
is a scene or a summary and why; rewrite one telling sentence as
showing with a sense and an action; and write an ending that passes
the three tests. If dialogue punctuation is still unsteady, keep the
six-rules card on the desk through Unit 06 — opinion writing needs
the same quotation rules for evidence.
