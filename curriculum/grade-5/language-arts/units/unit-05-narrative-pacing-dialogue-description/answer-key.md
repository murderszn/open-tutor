# Answer Key — Unit 05: Narrative Craft

For the guiding adult only. Do not give this file to the learner
before practice, quizzes, or assessments are complete. Wording may
vary where noted; facts, punctuation rules, structure names, and
ending-test judgments must not vary.

## Lesson 01 — Guided practice

1. Who: Maya, eleven, the viewpoint character. Where: the Gull
   Point lighthouse. When: the second weekend in October.
2. ¶3 is the inciting incident: the beam stops — the light goes
   out at half past nine. The storm itself is not the inciting
   incident: the storm is the setting, and the lamp was lit and
   working in the storm (¶2) until the fuse blew. The *one event*
   that breaks the ordinary world is the light going out.
3. ¶4: Gran decides to climb, takes the lantern and tools, and
   gives Maya a job (carry the lantern). ¶5: the climb itself —
   counting the steps, the lantern shadows, the wind, Gran's hand
   on Maya's shoulder. ¶6: Maya's legs shake and she doubts they
   can fix it; Gran steadies her and they climb on.
4. Acceptable sample: "Maya stood at the bottom of the lighthouse
   stairs, her lantern shaking in her hand. The light had gone out,
   and Gran was waiting for her at the top." Any rewrite that names
   Maya and the lighthouse stairs before anything scary happens is
   acceptable.

## Lesson 01 — Independent practice

1. One more climax proof from ¶7: "Gran fitted it into place."
   (Also acceptable: "Light flooded the room." or "Maya held the
   lantern high so its light fell across the work.")
2. Acceptable sample: "Eleven-year-old Maya spends a stormy
   October weekend with her grandmother Gran Rosa, the keeper of
   the Gull Point lighthouse." Any one sentence naming who, where,
   and when is acceptable.
3. Story order: 1 (e) Maya arrives; 2 (c) the beam stops; 3 (b)
   Maya counts the steps; 4 (a) Gran throws the switch; 5 (d)
   Maya writes in her notebook. Joined sample: "First, Maya
   arrived at the lighthouse for the weekend. Then the beam stopped
   at half past nine. After that, Maya counted the iron steps as
   they climbed. At last, Gran threw the switch. In the morning,
   Maya wrote in her notebook."
4. Any two sentences naming who, where, and when, with no trouble
   started yet. Acceptable sample: "On a rainy Saturday night,
   Ana and her little brother stayed at their aunt's cottage by
   the lake. The cottage was small and warm, and the rain drummed
   on the roof."
5. Quote: "A light is a promise, Maya. Someone out there is
   counting on it." Explanation: Gran treats the light as a duty to
   people she cannot see, not just a job to do. (Also acceptable:
   "Gran Rosa lit the lamp at dusk, the way she did every
   evening" — she keeps the light every night without fail.)
6. Maya: "What if we can't fix it?" Gran: "Then we fix it faster."
   Explanation: Maya asks worried questions and looks for
   reassurance, while Gran gives short, practical answers and a
   plan — Maya is the frightened one, Gran is the steady one.

## Lesson 02 — Guided practice

1. "Are we almost there?" Maya asked.
   "Not yet," Gran said. "Keep climbing."
2. The error is the period outside the quotation marks. Correct:
   "The lamp is out," whispered Maya. (The period becomes a comma
   inside the marks because the tag follows.)
3. (b) *laughed* — Gran is delighted, and *laughed* matches delight;
   *muttered* fits someone annoyed or trying not to be heard.
4. Acceptable sample: Maya stopped on the step. "I can't see the
   next one," she whispered. / Gran's hand found her shoulder.
   "One step at a time," she said. The exchange shows Maya's fear
   through her words and her stopping, and Gran's calm through her
   steady hand and her short instruction — without using the words
   *frightened* or *calm*. (Any exchange meeting those conditions
   is acceptable.)

## Lesson 02 — Independent practice

1. a. "The stairs are dark now," said Gran. (Comma inside the
   closing quotation mark.) b. Maya whispered, "The light went
   out." (Capital *Maya*; capital *The*.) c. "Hand me the fuse,"
   Gran said. (Comma inside the closing quotation mark.)
   d. "What if we can't fix it?" she asked. (Question mark, not a
   period, inside the marks.)
2. "Ready?" Maya asked.
   "Ready," Gran said.
   "Then throw the switch," Maya said.
3. Acceptable sample: "The wind is shaking the tower," Gran said,
   "and the stairs are dark." (Comma inside the first quote,
   lowercase tag, comma after the tag, lowercase continuation.)
4. a. "I don't want to go up there," whispered Maya.
   b. "There," laughed Gran.
   c. "What is that big glass thing?" asked Maya.
   d. "Keep your eyes on the step," said Gran.
5. Acceptable sample: Maya held the lantern high. "It's back on!"
   she cried. / "There," Gran laughed, pulling her close. / "Did
   you see the boat turn?" Maya asked. / "I saw it," Gran
   whispered. "Promise kept." Check: new paragraph per speaker; one
   action beat ("Maya held the lantern high"); *said* used zero
   times.
6. "Keep your eyes on the step in front of you," Gran said. "Don't
   look at how far there is to go." Tags: *Gran said* (twice, one
   per quoted sentence). What Gran's words show: she is steady and
   practical — she gives Maya a concrete job (watch the next step)
   instead of comfort words, and she keeps Maya's attention on
   what she can do right now.

## Lesson 03 — Independent practice

1. ¶9 is a summary. Evidence: it covers a whole night in one time
   transition ("In the morning"); it has no dialogue and no
   stretched moment. The writer chose summary because the trouble
   is over — the light is fixed, the boat is safe — and the story
   needs to close, not linger.
2. Acceptable sample (three sentences or fewer, facts kept):
   "Maya and Gran ate supper, washed the dishes, and Gran told
   Maya about the first storm she ever kept the light through."
3. Acceptable sample (at least five sentences, one line of dialogue,
   one sensory detail): "The causeway was narrow, and the wind
   pushed at Maya's coat as she walked toward the white tower.
   Salt stung her lips. Gulls cried over the waves. 'There it is,'
   Maya said, pointing. Gran smiled. 'Home for the weekend,' she
   said."
4. (a) stretch — the boat in the dark is the reason the light
   matters; the reader must feel it waiting. (b) skip — locking a
   door is routine; nothing changes. (c) skip — choosing a
   notebook changes nothing in the story. (d) stretch — handing
   over the fuse is the climax; the outcome is decided here.
5. Acceptable sample: "The beam stopped. The dark pressed in like
   a hand over a candle. Maya's stomach dropped. 'Gran?' she
   whispered."
6. "The next morning." The other options do not fit: *an hour
   later* is wrong (a whole night passed and the storm blew
   itself out); *at once* is wrong (Maya stands on the rocks in
   daylight, not in the same moment); *by Friday* is wrong (the
   storm was Friday evening; the morning after is Saturday).

## Lesson 04 — Independent practice

1. Sight: "the great beam began its slow turn across the water"
   (or "the white tower rose above the sea"). Sound: "the wind
   moaned through the tower" (or "the iron sing"). Smell: "smelled
   oil and salt and rain" (or "the smell of salt on the wind").
   Taste: the learner's own invention, labeled as such — for
   example, "the air tasted of salt" — because the story names no
   taste. Touch: "ninety-seven steps of cold iron" (or "Gran's
   hand was warm").
2. Acceptable sample (at least four sentences, two senses, one
   precise verb; none of *scared, dark, brave*): "Maya's legs shook
   on the cold iron steps, and she counted under her breath — ten,
   twenty, thirty. The lantern swung in her hand, throwing shadows
   that leapt ahead of her like startled cats. The wind moaned
   through the tower. Gran's hand was warm on her shoulder, and
   Maya took a deep breath and climbed on."
3. Acceptable sample: "The boat turned toward the harbour, its
   engine chugging low over the black water, and its horn sounded —
   one long, low note — as the golden beam swept across its deck."
   Check: two sensory details (sound: chugging, horn; sight: black
   water, golden beam) and one precise verb (*chugging* or *swept*).
4. (a) "nice morning" → "The sea lay flat and shining, and gulls
   circled the tower in the clear air." (b) "big tower" → "The
   white tower rose above the sea like a candle in a bottle."
   (c) "Maya felt bad" → "Maya's stomach dropped, and her legs
   shook." (d) "storm was bad" → "The wind shook the tower, and
   rain tapped the glass like impatient fingers." (Any concrete
   replacement is acceptable.)
5. Acceptable samples: Maya: "The stairs went up and up and UP,
   and my lantern made the shadows jump like cats!" Gran:
   "Ninety-seven steps, same as always. Mind the third step from
   the top — it rattles." Explanation: Maya's short excited bursts,
   repetition, and exclamation show a child; Gran's counting, flat
   statements, and practical warning show an adult who knows the
   tower.
6. "Gran laughed, a sound like a kettle coming to the boil, and
   pulled Maya close." The laugh (sound, compared to a kettle) and
   the action (pulling Maya close) show delight without naming it.

## Lesson 05 — Independent practice

1. Acceptable sample: "Leo climbed the last branch, reached out,
   and caught the kite's tail. He carried it down carefully, and
   when his sister saw it, she laughed — and Leo knew he had kept
   his promise." Tests: (1) answers — yes, he gets the kite;
   (2) change — Leo proves he can keep a promise; (3) details —
   the branch, the kite's tail, the sister.
2. It fails test 1 (it un-answers the story's question: if it was
   all a dream, Leo never found the kite) and test 2 (no change can
   survive, because nothing happened). It tells the reader none of
   it mattered.

   open the kitchen door after school and stopped. The room was
   cold, and the floorboards were bare under his shoes. A board
   creaked behind him. 'Is anyone here?' he asked. 'Yes,' a voice
   said from the corner. Sam let out the breath he had been
   holding." Fixes: orientation added (kitchen, after school);
   dialogue punctuated (question mark inside, comma before the

   ending ("The end") cut.

   The fuse was loose, that's all." Maya: "And the shadows jumped

   done; Maya tells it with wonder and images — the same climb,
   two different people.
5. (a) the learner's own story title in quotation marks — short
   work. (b) a book title in italics — long work. (c) a poem title

   acceptable if the form matches the length.)
6. Acceptable samples: orientation — "Just before the picture, Maya

1. One more rising-action obstacle from ¶5 or ¶6: "The wind moaned

   like startled cats," or "Gran's hand was warm on Maya's

   Maya counts the steps; 4 (a) Gran throws the switch; 5 (d)

   Climax: Gran throws the switch and the light returns (¶7).
   Difference: the inciting incident *starts* the trouble; the
   climax *decides* the outcome.
5. False. The light going out is the inciting incident — it starts
   the trouble. The climax is Gran throwing the switch in ¶7.
6. Any two sentences naming who, where, and when, with no trouble

## Unit assessment

### Part A

1. Who: June (and her grandfather). Where: her grandfather's house
   — the desk drawer, then the attic. When: the first Saturday of
   summer.
2. Story order: 1 (b) June finds the key; 2 (d) June unlocks the
   chest; 3 (a) June finds the map; 4 (c) June climbs down with
   the map.
3. "Did you draw this map?" June asked. There is no comma before
   the tag because the question mark is the mark that ends the
   spoken words — a comma replaces a period, never a question mark
   (or an exclamation mark).
4. ¶6 is a summary. Evidence: it covers the trip downstairs and the
   walk to the wall in a few sentences, and it compresses past time
   ("she had walked past it a hundred times"); it has no dialogue
   and no stretched moment. The writer chose summary because the
   discovery is about to happen at the wall — the paragraph hurries
   the reader there.
5. Dominant sense: smell — "The attic smelled of dust and old
   paper." One other sense: sight — "under a canvas sheet, stood a
   wooden chest with a brass lock." (Touch is also acceptable:
   "with the key in her pocket and her flashlight in her hand.")
6. June: "Did you draw this map?" Grandfather: "I never found what
   the X marks. Maybe you will." Explanation: June asks quick,
   eager questions and wants answers now, while her grandfather
   answers with a story about his own childhood and a challenge —
   he is the patient keeper of the mystery, and she is the eager
   finder.
7. Acceptable sample: "June walked to the stone wall and ran her
   hand along it, looking for anything different. Between two
   stones she found a small brass box, and inside it, a note in
   her grandfather's handwriting: FOR THE ONE WHO LOOKS." Tests:
   (1) answers — yes, she looks, and finds; (2) change — June
   becomes the finder her grandfather never was; (3) details —
   the stone wall, the brass, the grandfather's handwriting.

### Part B — sample scene (10 points)

Acceptable sample (any scene meeting the four sub-criteria is
acceptable, even with different events):

> On Saturday morning, June and her grandfather walked together to
> the stone wall behind the house. The map was folded in June's
> pocket, and the grass was wet under their shoes.
>
> "Which stone?" June asked, running her hand along the wall.
>
> "The one that looks like all the others," her grandfather said.
> "That's the trick of it."
>
> June stopped. One stone sat a little lower than the rest, and
> moss grew thick around its edges. She pressed it. It moved.
> Behind it, cold and dark, was a hollow just big enough for her
> hand — and inside, a small brass box, green with age.
>
> June lifted it out and held it up to the light, and her
> grandfather laughed, a sound like a kettle coming to the boil.

Scoring: orientation in the first two sentences (who: June and her
grandfather; where: the stone wall behind the house; when: Saturday
morning) — 3 points. Dialogue: two lines, new paragraph per
speaker, question mark inside the quotes, comma inside before the
tag — 3 points. Stretched moment: the stone, the moss, the press,
the hollow — at least three sentences at the moment June looks
closely — 2 points. Sensory detail (wet grass, cold dark hollow)
and an ending that follows (the box found; the grandfather's laugh
answers the story's question) — 2 points.

## Project — scoring notes

Score with the rubric in [project.md](project.md). Notes per
criterion:

- **Orientation and sequence (4):** full points only if the opening
  names who, where, and when before the trouble *and* the events
  unfold naturally with transitions. A story that orients late
  (after the trouble) is developing, not proficient.
- **Dialogue and character (4):** count the punctuation errors in
  the dialogue scene. 0–1 errors and distinct voices: proficient.
  2–3 errors, or both speakers sounding alike: developing. No
  dialogue scene, or errors on most lines: beginning.
- **Pacing (4):** the stretched moment must be a scene (detail,
  dialogue, or thought) at the climax or hardest choice — not
  merely a long paragraph. The summary must carry a time
  transition.
- **Sensory detail (4):** the paragraph must show feeling through
  senses and actions *without naming the feeling*. If the feeling
  is named as well as shown, that is developing.
- **Ending, revision, and title (4):** test the ending against the
  three tests in the answer key's Lesson 05 notes. The revision log
  needs one entry per checklist item (five entries). The title must
  be punctuated for its length (story: quotation marks).
