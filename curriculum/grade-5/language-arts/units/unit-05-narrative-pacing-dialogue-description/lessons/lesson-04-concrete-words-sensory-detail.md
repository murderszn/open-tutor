# Lesson 04 — Concrete Words and Sensory Detail: Show, Don't Tell

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can replace flat telling words with
concrete words and details from the five senses, so the reader feels
what my character feels instead of just being told about it."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil; the
lighthouse story from Lesson 01.
**Standards:** W.5.3.d, L.5.3.a.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "Close your eyes. I am going to say two sentences.
Tell me which one you can *see* better. One: 'The storm was bad.'
Two: 'Rain tapped the glass like impatient fingers, and the wind
pushed at the windows.'" Accept the second. Then ask: "What did the
second sentence give you that the first one did not?" The answer —
sounds, pictures, comparisons — is the whole lesson.

## Explicit teaching

**Sensory detail** = a detail that reaches the reader through one of
the five senses: **sight, sound, smell, taste, touch**. A story that
uses two or more senses in an important scene feels real; a story
that uses none feels like a report.

**Concrete words** = words that name something specific you could
point at, hear, or touch: *cold iron steps, brass lantern, wet
slate sky*. **Vague words** = words that could mean almost anything:
*nice, bad, big, scary, good*. A vague word asks the reader to do
the writer's work. A concrete word does it for them.

**Show, don't tell** = instead of naming a feeling or a fact
directly ("Maya was scared"), show it through what the character
does, what their body does, and what their senses take in ("Maya's
legs shook on the cold iron steps, and her breath came fast"). The
reader *feels* the fear instead of reading the label for it. Telling
is not forbidden — a summary needs telling — but the important
moments of a story must be shown.

**Precise verbs** = verbs that say exactly how something happens:
*trudged, whispered, squeezed, moaned, swung* instead of *went,
said, touched, made a sound, moved*. A precise verb carries its own
detail; a vague verb needs an adverb to rescue it ("walked slowly"
is weaker than "trudged").

Three tests for a showing sentence:

1. **Sense test:** does it give the reader something to see, hear,
   smell, taste, or touch?
2. **Action test:** does the character *do* something, or does
   their body react, instead of the narrator naming the feeling?
3. **Exact-word test:** could this sentence only belong to *this*
   story, or could it belong to any story? ("It was scary" fits
   any story. "The iron sang with every gust" fits only this
   tower.)

### Modeled example 1 — the lamp room: telling and showing

*Adult reads both versions aloud; the learner names the senses in
the second one.*

**Telling version (flat):**

> Maya was scared in the dark tower. The stairs were cold. Gran
> was calm. It was a frightening climb.

Four sentences, four labels: *scared, cold, calm, frightening*.
The reader learns nothing they could picture.

**Showing version (from the story, ¶5–6):**

> The stairs wound up and up, ninety-seven steps of cold iron.
> Maya counted them under her breath — ten, twenty, thirty — and
> the lantern swung in her hand, throwing shadows that leapt ahead
> of her like startled cats. [sight, touch] The wind moaned
> through the tower, and each gust made the iron sing. [sound]
> Gran's hand was warm on Maya's shoulder. [touch] "Keep your
> eyes on the step in front of you," Gran said. "Don't look at how
> far there is to go." [sound — dialogue] Halfway up, Maya's legs
> began to shake. [touch — the body shows the fear] She stopped. "What if we can't fix it?" she asked.
> "What if a boat is out there right now, looking for the light?"
> Gran squeezed her shoulder. "Then we fix it faster," Gran said.
> "A light is a promise, remember? And we keep our promises."
> Maya took a deep breath, smelled oil and salt and rain, and
> climbed on. [smell]

The showing version never uses the word *scared* — and the reader
knows Maya is scared, because her legs shake, her breath is counted,
and she asks what if. It never uses the word *calm* — and the reader
knows Gran is calm, because her hand is warm and steady and her
sentences are short orders. Showing trusts the reader to feel what
the details prove.

### Modeled example 2 — precise verbs

*Adult shows the upgrade chain; the learner supplies the last one.*

| Vague | More precise | Why it is better |
|---|---|---|
| Maya *went* up the stairs | Maya *climbed* the stairs | *Climbed* says up, with effort |
| The wind *made a sound* | The wind *moaned* through the tower | *Moaned* gives the sound and the mood |
| Gran *touched* her shoulder | Gran *squeezed* her shoulder | *Squeezed* says comfort and urgency at once |
| Maya *said* the words quietly | Maya *whispered* | *Whispered* carries the volume and the fear |
| The light *came back* | The light *flooded* the room | *Flooded* says sudden, everywhere, unstoppable |
| The boat *made a noise* | The boat's horn *sounded* — one long, low note | *Sounded* plus the detail lets the reader hear it |

The rule: if you need an adverb to make a verb work (*walked
slowly, said quietly*), look for a stronger verb first.

### Modeled example 3 — expanding a flat sentence

*Adult thinks aloud, adding one sense at a time.*

Flat: "The lamp room was dark."

Add sight: "The lamp room was dark, and the great lens stood still."

Add touch and sound: "The lamp room was dark, and the great lens
stood still, a giant glass eye that could not see, while the wind
moaned through the tower."

That is the story's own sentence (¶7), built one sense at a time.
Expanding a sentence (L.5.3.a) is not decoration — each added detail
is a fact the reader needs to feel the moment.

## Guided practice

With the adult, do these together.

1. Name the dominant sense in each detail from the story:
   (a) "rain tapped the glass like impatient fingers";
   (b) "the smell of salt on the wind"; (c) "Gran's hand was warm
   on Maya's shoulder"; (d) "the iron sing."
2. This sentence tells: "Gran was happy when the light came back."
   Rewrite it as showing, using one action and one sound from ¶8.
   Do not use the word *happy*.
3. Upgrade these vague verbs to precise ones that fit the
   lighthouse story: (a) Maya *went* down the rocks in the
   morning. (b) The waves *hit* the rocks. (c) Gran *said* the
   words with a laugh.
4. Read ¶2 of the story. List one detail for each of two different
   senses, and say what each detail helps the reader feel.

## Independent practice

1. Write the five senses. For each one, write a detail from the
   lighthouse story that appeals to it. (Taste may come from your
   own invention — what would the salt air taste like? Label it as
   your own detail, not the story's.)
2. Rewrite this telling paragraph as showing, in at least four
   sentences. Use at least two senses and one precise verb. Do not
   use the words *scared*, *dark*, or *brave*:
   "Maya was scared on the stairs. It was dark. Gran was brave.
   Maya kept going."
3. Expand this flat sentence with two sensory details and one
   precise verb: "The boat turned toward the harbour."
4. Find the vague word in each sentence and replace it with
   concrete words: (a) "It was a nice morning." (b) "The tower was
   big." (c) "Maya felt bad." (d) "The storm was bad."
5. Voice (from the legacy sensory quiz): how does word choice show
   a narrator's age or personality? Write two sentences about the
   same staircase — one as Maya (eleven, excited, a little scared)
   would tell it, and one as Gran (calm, practical, used to the
   tower) would tell it.
6. Read ¶8 of the story. Copy the sentence that shows Gran is
   delighted *without* using the word *delighted* or *happy*.
   Then write one sentence explaining which sense or action does
   the showing.

## Application

Open your project draft. Find the paragraph where your main
character faces the hardest moment. Rewrite it as showing: at least
two senses, one precise verb, no naming of the feeling. Read both
versions aloud to the adult and ask which one they can feel more.

## Exit check

On a slip of paper, rewrite this telling sentence as showing, with
one sense and one action: "Leo was nervous before the race." The
adult checks that the feeling is shown, not named, and that a sense
or an action does the work.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the telling version first,
  then the showing version, and the learner points to the exact
  words that changed — so the lesson is visible, not abstract.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may draw the lamp-room scene
  and label two senses on the drawing, then dictate the showing
  sentences for the adult to scribe.
- **Extension:** take one paragraph of the story and rewrite it
  using a *different* dominant sense — for example, retell the
  climb through smell and touch only, with no visual details. What
  does the reader gain, and what do they lose?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners replace one vague word with another
(*scared* → *terrified*) and call it showing. Keep returning to the
three tests: a sense, an action, an exact word. Another trap: adding
sensory detail to *every* sentence, including summaries — remind the
learner that showing belongs to the stretched moments (Lesson 03),
and a summary can tell plainly. A third: "show, don't tell" applied
to facts the reader needs quickly (the fuse blew; the spare was in
the chest) — those are told, and that is correct. Answer-key
reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
