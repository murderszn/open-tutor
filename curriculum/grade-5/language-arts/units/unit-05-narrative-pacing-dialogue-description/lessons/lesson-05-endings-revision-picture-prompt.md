# Lesson 05 — Endings That Follow, and Revising Like an Editor

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can write an ending that follows
from my story, revise my draft with a checklist, and use a picture
to start a new scene."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil; the
lighthouse illustration below (or its text alternative); your
project draft.
**Standards:** W.5.3.e, W.5.4, W.5.5, W.5.10, L.5.2.d, RL.5.3.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "A story ends like this: 'And then a dragon ate
the lighthouse. The end.' What is wrong with that ending?" Accept:
nothing in the story set up a dragon. An ending must follow from
what came before — and this lesson teaches how to test that.

## Explicit teaching

**Conclusion (ending)** = the last part of a story, which must
follow from the narrated experiences. A good ending answers the
story's question, shows how a character has changed, and uses the
story's own details — it does not invent a new problem, and it does
not preach a moral at the reader.

**The three ending tests.** A finished ending must pass all three:

1. **Does it answer the story's question?** The lighthouse story
   asks: *will the light be kept?* The ending must say yes or no —
   not change the subject.
2. **Has the character changed?** Maya begins the story wanting to
   visit a lighthouse; she ends it wanting to *keep a promise like
   that herself*. The ending shows the change.
3. **Does it use the story's own details?** The notebook, the white
   tower, the promise — all of them were in the story already. An
   ending that needs brand-new things (a dragon, a treasure, a
   stranger) is not an ending; it is a new story starting too late.

**Revise** = re-see the draft and change it to make the story
better (add, cut, move, or rewrite). **Edit** = fix the surface:
spelling, punctuation, capitals. Revise first, edit last — there is
no point polishing a paragraph you are about to cut. This unit's
revision checklist (used again in the project):

- **Orientation:** does the opening name who, where, and when?
- **Dialogue:** is every speaker's turn punctuated, with a new
  paragraph per speaker?
- **Pacing:** is the important moment stretched and the routine
  summarized?
- **Sensory detail:** does the hardest moment show through senses
  and actions?
- **Ending:** does it pass the three tests?
- **Title:** is the story's title punctuated correctly?

**Titles of works (L.5.2.d).** The title of a short work — a story,
a poem, a chapter — goes in **quotation marks**: "The Night the
Light Went Out". The title of a longer work — a book — goes in
*italics* (or underlined by hand): *The Lighthouse Cat*. A title
does not need quotation marks *and* italics; choose the form that
fits the length of the work.

**Observe and imagine (3 min):** Name three things you can see in
the picture (the glowing lamp room, the lantern, the waves, the open
door, the storm clouds). Then ask: what might have happened *just
before* this picture — and what might happen *just after* it? A
picture cannot tell you the order of events; the writer chooses it.
That choice is the beginning of a scene, and you will write one in
the application below.

### Modeled example 1 — three endings, one that follows

*Adult reads all three; the learner applies the three tests to each.*

**Ending A (follows — the story's own ending, ¶9):**

> In the morning, the storm had blown itself out. The sea lay flat
> and shining, and gulls circled the tower in the clear air. Maya
> stood on the rocks with her notebook and wrote: *The light went
> out, and we climbed the dark stairs, and we fixed it. A light is
> a promise.* She looked up at the white tower with its red top and
> thought that one day, when she was older, she would like to keep
> a promise like that herself.

Tests: (1) answers the question — yes, the light was kept, and the
boat found the harbour; (2) Maya changed — from a visitor to
someone who wants to keep a promise; (3) uses the story's own
details — the notebook, the tower, the promise. Passes all three.

**Ending B (invents a new problem):**

> Just then, a pirate ship appeared out of the storm, and Maya had
> to fight the pirates all by herself while Gran hid in the lamp
> room. The end.

Tests: (1) does not answer the story's question — the pirates have
nothing to do with the light; (2) no change is shown — Maya simply
does a new thing; (3) pirates were never in the story. Fails all
three. The problem is not that pirates are bad — it is that they
arrive with no setup, in the last paragraph.

**Ending C (preaches a moral):**

> And so Maya learned that you should always be brave and never
> give up, and that everyone should always keep their promises no
> matter what happens, because promises are the most important
> thing in the whole world. The end.

Tests: (1) it answers nothing — it lectures instead; (2) Maya's
change is *announced* ("Maya learned that…") rather than shown;
(3) it uses no detail from the story at all — it could end any
story. Fails all three. A moral the reader can feel is stronger
than a moral the narrator states.

### Modeled example 2 — revising a flawed paragraph

*Adult shows a draft paragraph, then the revised version, naming
each change.*

**Draft:**

> Maya and Gran went up the stairs. It was scary. "Are we almost
> there" Maya asked. "Not yet" Gran said. "Keep going" Gran said.
> They got to the top and fixed the light. The end.

**Revised:**

> The stairs wound up and up, ninety-seven steps of cold iron.
> Maya counted them under her breath — ten, twenty, thirty — and
> the lantern swung in her hand. Halfway up, her legs began to
> shake. She stopped.
>
> "Are we almost there?" Maya asked.
>
> "Not yet," Gran said. "Keep your eyes on the step in front of
> you."

Changes: orientation added (ninety-seven steps of cold iron);
telling ("It was scary") replaced with showing (legs shaking,
counting, stopping); dialogue punctuated (question mark inside the
quotes, comma before the tag, new paragraph per speaker); pacing
fixed (the climb is stretched, the fixing is saved for its own
scene); "The end" cut — the story is not over, and an ending must
pass the three tests, not just announce itself.

### Modeled example 3 — punctuating titles

*Adult shows the rule in the story's own terms.*

- The story you read is called "The Night the Light Went Out" —
  quotation marks, because it is a short work.
- In the project you will name your story. If you call it "The
  Secret of the Old Shed", it also takes quotation marks.
- If a character in your story reads a book, the book's title is
  italicized: Maya read *The Lighthouse Cat* before bed. (Handwriting?
  Underline it instead: *The Lighthouse Cat*.)
- A common error: putting a story title in italics and a book title
  in quotation marks — the forms are swapped. Short work: quotes.
  Long work: italics.

## Guided practice

With the adult, do these together.

1. Apply the three ending tests to Ending B above. Name the test it
   fails most clearly, and say in one sentence what the writer
   should have done instead.
2. Apply the three tests to Ending C. Which test does it fail, and
   what detail from the story could replace the preaching sentence?
3. Read the revised paragraph in Modeled example 2. Name one change
   that fixed the **pacing** and one change that fixed the
   **dialogue**.
4. Punctuate these two titles correctly: (a) the story *the night
   the light went out*; (b) the book *the lighthouse cat*.

## Independent practice

1. Write an ending of two or three sentences for this unfinished
   story, and check it against the three tests: "Leo found his
   little sister's lost kite tangled in the big oak behind the
   school. He had promised to find it before dark. The branches
   were high, and the wind was rising." (Your ending must answer:
   does he get the kite? What changes for Leo?)
2. Read this ending and name the test it fails: "And then Leo woke
   up and it was all a dream, and there was no kite at all. The
   end." Explain in one sentence why it fails.
3. Revise this flawed paragraph using the checklist. Fix at least
   three things (orientation, dialogue punctuation, telling vs.
   showing, pacing):
   "Sam was nervous. He walked into the room. 'Is anyone here' he
   asked. 'Yes' a voice said. 'I am here' the voice said. Sam was
   relieved. The end."
4. Compare characters (RL.5.3): imagine Gran and Maya each telling
   the story of the climb the next morning. Write one sentence each
   would say about it, in their own voices — Gran's practical,
   Maya's wondering — and one sentence explaining how the two
   sentences show they are different people.
5. Punctuate these titles: (a) your own project's story title
   (invent one if you have not chosen yet); (b) a book a character
   in your story might read; (c) a poem your character might
   recite. Label each as short work or long work.
6. Use the illustration: write one sentence of orientation for a
   scene that happens *just before* the picture (who, where, when),
   and one line of dialogue a character in the picture might say
   *just after* it. Punctuate the dialogue correctly.

## Application

Write a scene from the illustration: choose one character's point
of view (the girl's or the older woman's), write what happened just
before the picture or just after it, in at least six sentences, with
orientation, one line of dialogue, one stretched moment, and one
sensory detail. Then revise it once with the checklist — and write
the revision you made in your revision log (see
[project.md](../project.md)).

## Exit check

On a slip of paper: "A good ending must (1) ___ the story's
question, (2) show the character has ___, and (3) use the story's
own ___." The adult checks that the learner can state all three
tests without looking.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the three endings aloud in
  different voices (A warm, B rushed, C lecturing) so the learner
  hears the difference before analyzing it.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may tell the illustration
  scene aloud to the adult first, then write it, rather than
  writing cold.
- **Extension:** write a *fourth* ending for the lighthouse story —
  one that follows from the story but takes it somewhere the real
  ending did not go (for example, the boat's captain comes ashore
  the next morning). Check it against the three tests, and explain
  which test is hardest to pass when you invent new material.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners think any ending that mentions the
story's topic is a good ending ("And they all lived happily ever
after at the lighthouse") — it names the place but answers nothing
and shows no change. Keep returning to the three tests, and make the
learner point to the exact sentence that passes each one. Another
trap: revising by only fixing spelling — remind the learner that
editing is the *last* step, and revision (re-seeing) comes first.
A third: "woke up and it was all a dream" endings — name the trick
and why it breaks trust: it tells the reader none of it mattered.
