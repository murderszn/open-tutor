# Lesson 03 — Pacing: Slowing Down and Speeding Up

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can stretch the important moment of
a story into a scene and hurry past the rest with a summary — and I
can tell the reader how much time has passed."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil; the
lighthouse story from Lesson 01.
**Standards:** W.5.3.b, L.5.3.a.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "Tell me about your morning today — but you only
have ten seconds. Now tell me about the best minute of your morning
— and take a whole minute over it." Accept both answers. Then ask:
"Why did the second telling take longer, even though it covered less
time?" The answer is pacing — and this lesson teaches how writers
choose it on purpose.

## Explicit teaching

**Pacing** = how fast or slow a story feels as the reader moves
through it. A writer controls pacing with two tools: **scene** and
**summary**.

A **scene** stretches a moment out: dialogue, sensory detail, and a
character's thoughts, written as if the moment is happening right
now. A scene makes the reader live inside the moment. Scenes belong
at the important parts of the story — the inciting incident, the
climax, the moment a character changes their mind.

A **summary** speeds past time: a few sentences that tell the reader
what happened over hours, days, or weeks, without showing every
moment. Summaries belong at the unimportant parts — travel, waiting,
ordinary days — so the story does not drag.

**Stretch** what matters; **skip** what does not. The test is the
story mountain: stretch the climax and the moment of decision; skip
the walk to the place where the decision happens, unless the walk
*is* the decision.

Two more pacing tools:

- **Sentence length.** Short sentences speed a reader up — they feel
  urgent, breathless. Longer sentences, joined with commas and
  conjunctions, slow a reader down — they feel calm, or heavy, or
  dreamy. (This is L.5.3.a: expand, combine, and reduce sentences
  for meaning and style.)
- **Transitions.** A summary needs a transition that says how much
  time passed: *an hour later, the next morning, by Friday, after a
  while*. Without one, the reader does not know whether a minute or
  a month went by.

A common error: stretching everything. A story that shows every
single step of every single day is not careful — it is boring. And
the opposite error: summarizing the climax. A climax told in one
sentence ("Then she fixed it") cheats the reader out of the moment
they waited for.

### The tower climb — fast and slow

*The same climb, told two ways. Both are correct writing; they do
different jobs.*

**Fast (summary — 3 sentences, ~30 words):**

> Gran and Maya climbed ninety-seven dark steps to the lamp room.
> Maya's legs shook, but she kept going. Gran fitted the spare
> fuse, threw the switch, and the light came back on.

**Slow (scene — ~145 words):**

> The stairs wound up and up, ninety-seven steps of cold iron. Maya
> counted them under her breath — ten, twenty, thirty — and the
> lantern swung in her hand, throwing shadows that leapt ahead of
> her like startled cats. The wind moaned through the tower, and
> each gust made the iron sing. Gran's hand was warm on Maya's
> shoulder. "Keep your eyes on the step in front of you," Gran
> said. "Don't look at how far there is to go." Halfway up, Maya's
> legs began to shake. She stopped. "What if we can't fix it?" she
> asked. "What if a boat is out there right now, looking for the
> light?" Gran squeezed her shoulder. "Then we fix it faster," Gran
> said. "A light is a promise, remember? And we keep our promises."
> Maya took a deep breath, smelled oil and salt and rain, and
> climbed on.

The fast version tells the reader the climb happened. The slow
version makes the reader climb it. The slow version stretches the
climb because the climb *is* the story's test: Maya is afraid, and
she climbs anyway. The writer earns that slowness by choosing it
only here — the walk along the causeway in ¶1 is summarized in a
single image ("a fist of grey rock"), and the whole night after the
light returns is summarized in one morning paragraph (¶9).

### Modeled example 1 — stretch or skip?

*Adult reads the outline; the learner calls stretch or skip for
each part, with a reason.*

Maya's weekend at the lighthouse, as an outline:

| Part | Stretch or skip? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The drive to the causeway on Friday afternoon | **Skip** (summary) | Nothing is decided on the drive; one sentence of travel is enough |
| The lamp going out at half past nine | **Stretch** (scene) | The inciting incident — the moment the trouble starts, told with dialogue and detail |
| Maya brushing her teeth before bed | **Skip** | Ordinary routine; no story purpose |
| The climb, and Maya's question halfway up | **Stretch** (scene) | The moment Maya doubts — the story's test of her courage |
| Gran fitting the fuse | **Stretch** (scene) | The climax — the outcome is decided here |
| The boat sounding its horn | **Stretch, briefly** | One or two sentences of falling action; the reader needs to hear the answer |
| The morning after | **Skip** (summary) | The storm is over; one paragraph closes the story |

Rule of thumb: if a character makes a choice, feels something change,
or learns something — stretch it. If nothing changes — skip it.

### Modeled example 2 — sentence length changes the pace

*Adult reads both versions aloud; the learner says which feels
faster and why.*

Choppy (fast, urgent): "The wind shook the tower. The iron sang.
Maya stopped. Her legs shook."

Flowing (slow, heavy): "The wind shook the tower until the iron
sang, and Maya stopped on the cold steps, her legs shaking beneath
her."

The choppy version is four short sentences — the reader hurries
through them, the way Maya hurries up the stairs. The flowing
version joins the same facts into one long sentence, and the reader
slows down. Combining sentences is not just grammar (L.5.3.a); it
is a pacing tool. Break a long sentence to make a moment feel sudden;
join short ones to make a moment feel long.

### Modeled example 3 — adding a transition that tells the time

*Adult shows the gap, then fills it.*

Without a time transition: "Gran threw the switch. Maya stood on
the rocks with her notebook." The reader does not know whether
seconds or days passed.

With one: "Gran threw the switch. **In the morning**, Maya stood on
the rocks with her notebook." Three words — *in the morning* — skip
a whole night honestly, and the reader trusts the jump.

## Guided practice

With the adult, do these together.

1. Which version of the climb (fast or slow) is a scene, and which
   is a summary? Name one thing the slow version has that the fast
   version does not.
2. Maya's arrival at the lighthouse (¶1) is mostly summary. Name
   one sentence in ¶1 that *stretches* a moment into a small scene,
   and say what it shows.
3. Stretch-or-skip: Maya eats breakfast on Saturday morning before
   the storm. Stretch or skip? Give a reason using the rule of
   thumb.
4. Combine these three short sentences into one flowing sentence
   that slows the pace: "The rain tapped the glass. The wind
   pushed at the windows. The sky turned the colour of wet slate."

## Independent practice

1. Read ¶9 (the morning after). Is it a scene or a summary? Give
   two pieces of evidence from the paragraph, and explain in one
   sentence why the writer chose that pace for the ending.
2. Write a fast (summary) version — three sentences or fewer — of
   this slow moment: Maya and Gran eat supper, wash the dishes,
   and Gran tells Maya about the first storm she ever kept the
   light through. Keep the facts; drop the detail.
3. Write a slow (scene) version — at least five sentences, with one
   line of dialogue and one sensory detail — of this fast moment:
   "Maya arrived at the lighthouse on Friday." Keep the facts;
   add the detail.
4. Stretch-or-skip: for each part below, write **stretch** or
   **skip** and one reason. (a) The boat circling the point in the
   dark. (b) Gran locking the lighthouse door in the morning.
   (c) Maya choosing which notebook to bring. (d) The moment Maya
   hands Gran the fuse.
5. Break this long sentence into two or three short ones to make
   the moment feel sudden: "When the beam stopped, the dark pressed
   in like a hand over a candle, Maya's stomach dropped, and she
   whispered Gran's name into the dark."
6. Add a time transition to join these two sentences honestly:
   "The light flooded the room. Maya stood on the rocks with her
   notebook." Choose from *an hour later, the next morning, at
   once, by Friday* — and explain why the others do not fit.

## Application

Open your project draft. Find the moment where your main character
makes their hardest choice. Stretch it into a scene of at least five
sentences, with one line of dialogue. Then find one part of your
story where nothing changes, and summarize it in one sentence with
a time transition.

## Exit check

On a slip of paper: "A scene stretches a moment; a summary ___.
The climax of the lighthouse story should be told as a ___ because
___." The adult checks that the learner names the right tool and
gives a story-based reason.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the fast version, then the
  slow version, aloud with a timer — the learner hears that the
  slow version takes longer to read even though it covers less
  story time.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may act out the climb (slow
  steps, counting aloud) and then describe it in three fast
  sentences, rather than writing all six independent items.
- **Extension:** rewrite ¶8 (the light returning) as a summary of
  two sentences — then explain what the reader loses. Rewrite ¶1
  (the arrival) as a stretched scene — then explain what the reader
  gains, and whether the gain is worth the extra length.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners stretch the wrong moment — usually
the beginning, because it is easiest to describe — and summarize the
climax because it is hardest to write. Keep returning to the test:
*stretch the choice, skip the routine.* Another trap: thinking
"slow" means "long sentences" — slow means *more detail, dialogue,
and thought per moment of story time*; a stretch can use short
sentences too (see the fast/slow climb: the slow version's shortest
sentences — "She stopped." — are the most tense). Answer-key
