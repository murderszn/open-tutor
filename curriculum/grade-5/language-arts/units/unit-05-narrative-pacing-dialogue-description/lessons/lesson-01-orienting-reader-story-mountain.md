# Lesson 01 — Orienting the Reader: Situation, Narrator, Characters, Natural Sequence

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can open a story so the reader knows
who is in it, where and when it happens, and what happens first,
next, and last — and I can map any story onto the story mountain."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil; the
story-mountain diagram below.
**Standards:** W.5.3.a, W.5.3.c, RL.5.3 (craft model).

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "Tell me about a time something went wrong and you
had to fix it — in two sentences. Who was there? Where were you?"
Accept any true two-sentence answer. Then ask: "If a stranger read
only those two sentences, would they know who you are, where you
were, and what happened first?" If the learner cannot tell, that is
exactly what this lesson teaches — a story must orient its reader
before it can surprise them.

## Explicit teaching

**Orientation** = the opening of a story that tells the reader who
is in it, where it happens, and when it happens — before the trouble
starts. A reader who does not know who, where, or when is lost, and
a lost reader stops reading. Orientation usually takes one or two
paragraphs, never the whole story.

**Narrator** = the voice telling the story. In this unit's story the
narrator is *not* Maya: the story says "Maya had been waiting" and
"her stomach dropped," not "I had been waiting." That is
**third-person narration** — the narrator stands outside the story
and tells it about someone else. The narrator follows Maya closely
enough to know what she feels, so Maya is the **viewpoint
character**: the story is told from her side of events, even though
she is not the one speaking it.

**Event sequence** = the order events happen in the story. A story's
events must unfold **naturally**: one thing leads to the next, and
the reader can follow the chain. **Transitions** are the words and
phrases that manage the sequence — *first, then, after a while, at
last, in the morning, meanwhile, suddenly* — and they tell the reader
how much time has passed and how the events connect.

**Story mountain** = the shape of a story's structure, with six
stages:

1. **Exposition** — the ordinary world: who, where, when, before
   the trouble.
2. **Inciting incident** — the spark: the one event that breaks the
   ordinary world and starts the trouble.
3. **Rising action** — the climb: a series of obstacles and
   complications that make the trouble worse.
4. **Climax** — the peak: the turning point, the most tense moment,
   where the outcome is decided.
5. **Falling action** — the slope down: what happens right after
   the turning point.
6. **Resolution** — the new ordinary world: how things end, and how
   the characters have changed.

A common error: putting the climax first, or skipping the
exposition and starting with the trouble. A reader who meets the
trouble before meeting the people does not care yet. Orientation
first; trouble second.

### The story — "The Night the Light Went Out" (original)

> **1.** Maya had been waiting all year for the second weekend in
> October, when she was allowed to stay with Gran Rosa at the Gull
> Point lighthouse. The lighthouse stood on a fist of grey rock at
> the end of a narrow causeway, and its white tower rose above the
> sea like a candle in a bottle. Maya loved the smell of salt on
> the wind, the gulls crying over the waves, and the way Gran Rosa
> always said, "A light is a promise, Maya. Someone out there is
> counting on it."
>
> **2.** On Friday evening, the sky turned the colour of wet slate.
> The wind began to push at the windows, and rain tapped the glass
> like impatient fingers. Gran Rosa lit the lamp at dusk, the way
> she did every evening, and the great beam began its slow turn
> across the water. "Storm's coming in fast," Gran said, pulling
> her shawl tight. "We'll have a wild night."
>
> **3.** At half past nine, the beam stopped. One moment the room
> was washed with gold as the light swung past the window; the
> next, the dark pressed in like a hand over a candle. Maya's
> stomach dropped. "Gran?" she whispered. "The light went out."
>
> **4.** Gran was already on her feet. "The fuse has blown," she
> said. "There's a spare in the lamp-room chest, but the stairs
> are dark now, and the wind is shaking the tower." She reached for
> the brass lantern by the door. "You'll carry this. I'll carry
> the tools. We go together, slow and steady."
>
> **5.** The stairs wound up and up, ninety-seven steps of cold
> iron. Maya counted them under her breath — ten, twenty, thirty
> — and the lantern swung in her hand, throwing shadows that leapt
> ahead of her like startled cats. The wind moaned through the
> tower, and each gust made the iron sing. Gran's hand was warm on
> Maya's shoulder. "Keep your eyes on the step in front of you,"
> Gran said. "Don't look at how far there is to go."
>
> **6.** Halfway up, Maya's legs began to shake. She stopped.
> "What if we can't fix it?" she asked. "What if a boat is out
> there right now, looking for the light?" Gran squeezed her
> shoulder. "Then we fix it faster," Gran said. "A light is a
> promise, remember? And we keep our promises." Maya took a deep
> breath, smelled oil and salt and rain, and climbed on.
>
> **7.** In the lamp room, the great lens stood dark and still, a
> giant glass eye that could not see. Gran set down her tools, and
> her hands moved quickly — a twist here, a click there — while
> Maya held the lantern high so its light fell across the work.
> "Hand me the fuse," Gran said, and Maya passed it over, her
> fingers steady now. Gran fitted it into place. "Ready?" she
> asked. Maya nodded. Gran threw the switch.
>
> **8.** Light flooded the room. It poured through the lens and out
> across the black water in a great golden road. Down below, a
> fishing boat that had been circling the point turned toward the
> harbour, and its horn sounded — one long, low note, like a
> thank-you. Gran laughed, a sound like a kettle coming to the
> boil, and pulled Maya close. "There," she said. "Promise kept."
>
> **9.** In the morning, the storm had blown itself out. The sea lay
> flat and shining, and gulls circled the tower in the clear air.
> Maya stood on the rocks with her notebook and wrote: *The light
> went out, and we climbed the dark stairs, and we fixed it. A
> light is a promise.* She looked up at the white tower with its
> red top and thought that one day, when she was older, she would
> like to keep a promise like that herself.

### Modeled example 1 — mapping the story onto the mountain

*Adult builds the table aloud; the learner copies it.*

| Stage | What happens in this story | Proof (paragraph) |
|---|---|---|
| Exposition | Maya arrives for her October weekend with Gran Rosa at Gull Point lighthouse; we learn Gran keeps the light and calls it a promise | ¶1 |
| Inciting incident | At half past nine the beam stops — the light goes out in the storm | ¶3 |
| Rising action | Gran and Maya climb ninety-seven dark steps; Maya's legs shake; she doubts they can fix it | ¶4–6 |
| Climax | Gran fits the spare fuse and throws the switch; the light returns | ¶7 |
| Falling action | The fishing boat turns toward the harbour and sounds its horn; Gran laughs and holds Maya close | ¶8 |
| Resolution | Morning: the storm is over; Maya writes in her notebook that a light is a promise, and wishes to keep one herself one day | ¶9 |

Notice that the inciting incident is *one* event (the light going
out), not the whole storm. The storm is the setting; the blown fuse
is the trouble. A common error is naming the setting as the trouble.

### Modeled example 2 — an opening that orients, and one that does not

*Adult reads both aloud; the learner says which one orients the
reader and why.*

**Opening A (does not orient):** "It was dark and scary. She ran up
the stairs. 'Hurry!' someone shouted. The light came back on."

The reader does not know who "she" is, where the stairs are, why the
light matters, or who shouted. The trouble arrives before the reader
has anyone to care about.

**Opening B (orients):** "Maya had been waiting all year for the
second weekend in October, when she was allowed to stay with Gran
Rosa at the Gull Point lighthouse. The lighthouse stood on a fist of
grey rock at the end of a narrow causeway, and its white tower rose
above the sea like a candle in a bottle."

Two sentences give who (Maya, Gran Rosa), where (Gull Point
lighthouse), and when (the second weekend in October) — and the
image of the candle in a bottle tells the reader what kind of place
this is before anything goes wrong.

### Modeled example 3 — transitions manage the sequence

*Adult shows the same three events twice.*

Without transitions: "Maya counted the steps. Her legs shook. Gran
fitted the fuse."

With transitions: "**First**, Maya counted the steps. **Halfway up**,
her legs began to shake. **At last**, Gran fitted the fuse."

The events are the same; the transitions tell the reader how the
events connect in time. Without them the story reads like a list.
With them it reads like a climb.

## Guided practice

With the adult, do these together.

1. Name the who, where, and when of "The Night the Light Went
   Out" in one sentence each — who the viewpoint character is,
   where the story happens, and when it happens.
2. Which paragraph is the inciting incident, and what *one* event
   in it breaks the ordinary world? Say why the storm itself is not
   the inciting incident.
3. The story's rising action has three parts (¶4, ¶5, ¶6). Name one
   obstacle from each paragraph.
4. Read Opening A in Modeled example 2 again. Rewrite its first two
   sentences so the reader learns who "she" is and where the stairs
   are — before anything scary happens.

## Independent practice

1. Copy the story-mountain table from Modeled example 1. For the
   **climax** row only, add one more proof detail from ¶7 that is
   not already in the table.
2. Write one sentence that tells the story's exposition in your own
   words — who, where, when — without copying the story's first
   paragraph.
3. These four events are out of order. Number them 1–4 in story
   order, then join them with transitions (*first, then, after
   that, at last*): (a) Gran throws the switch. (b) Maya counts
   the iron steps. (c) The beam stops at half past nine.
   (d) Maya writes in her notebook in the morning.
4. Write an orientation of two sentences for a story of your own
   about a place you know well (a kitchen, a park, a bus, a
   garden). Name who is there, where it is, and when it happens.
   Do not start the trouble yet.
5. Inference (RL.5.1): Quote one line from ¶1 that supports the
   inference that Gran Rosa takes her job seriously, and explain
   in one sentence what the line shows.
6. Character comparison (RL.5.3): Gran and Maya both speak in ¶6.
   Quote one thing each of them says, and write one sentence
   explaining how their words show they are different kinds of
   people in this moment.

## Exit check

On a slip of paper: "The inciting incident of the lighthouse story
is ___ (one event). The climax is ___ (one event)." The adult checks
that the two events are different and in the right order.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the story aloud first while
  the learner follows; paragraph numbers stay visible for quoting.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may point to the paragraph
  that proves each stage and dictate the table for the adult to
  scribe, then read it back and correct it.
- **Extension:** rewrite ¶3 (the light going out) from Gran's point
  of view instead of Maya's — what does Gran notice first, and what
  does she say to herself? Keep the same events; change only what
  the viewpoint character notices and feels.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners name the storm as the inciting
incident, or name the climax as "the light went out" (that is the
trouble starting, not the turning point). Keep returning to the test:
*the inciting incident breaks the ordinary world; the climax decides
the outcome.* Another trap: orientation paragraphs that list facts
("Maya is 11. She likes lighthouses.") instead of placing the reader
inside a scene — require who, where, and when inside a sentence that
also shows something happening. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 01.
