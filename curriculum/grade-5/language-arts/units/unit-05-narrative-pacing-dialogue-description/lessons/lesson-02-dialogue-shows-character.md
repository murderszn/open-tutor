# Lesson 02 — Dialogue That Shows Character

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can write dialogue that sounds like
two different people, punctuate it correctly, and start a new
paragraph every time the speaker changes."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil; the
lighthouse story from Lesson 01.
**Standards:** W.5.3.b, L.5.2.b; dialogue-punctuation rules applied
from L.4.2.b.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "Read this aloud, exactly as written: *Maya said
the light went out Gran said the fuse has blown.* What is wrong
with it?" Accept: you cannot tell where one person's words end and
the next person's begin. That confusion is what dialogue punctuation
fixes — and this lesson teaches the rules from scratch.

## Explicit teaching

**Dialogue** = the exact words a character speaks, written inside
quotation marks. Dialogue does two jobs at once: it moves the story
forward, and it shows what kind of person is speaking — without the
narrator having to explain it.

**Dialogue tag** = the words that name the speaker, such as *she
said* or *Gran asked*. An **action beat** is different: it is a
separate sentence describing what a character does, such as *Gran
squeezed her shoulder.* A tag is glued to the spoken words with a
comma; a beat stands on its own with a period.

### The six dialogue rules

**Rule 1 — Quotation marks around the exact words.** Only the words
the character actually says go inside the marks. The tag stays
outside.

> "The fuse has blown," she said.

**Rule 2 — Comma inside the closing quotation mark when a tag
follows.** When the spoken sentence would end with a period but a
tag follows, the period becomes a comma, and the comma sits *inside*
the quotation marks.

> "Keep your eyes on the step in front of you," Gran said.

A common error is putting the comma outside: "Keep your eyes on the
step in front of you", Gran said. In American punctuation the comma
always goes inside.

**Rule 3 — A new paragraph every time the speaker changes.** Even
a one-word reply gets its own paragraph. Two speakers in one
paragraph is called a **character clash**, and it confuses the
reader about who is talking.

> "What if we can't fix it?" she asked.
>
> "Then we fix it faster," Gran said.

**Rule 4 — Capitalize the first word the speaker says.** The first
spoken word begins with a capital letter, even when the tag comes
first.

> Gran said, "Hand me the fuse."

**Rule 5 — A tag in the middle of one sentence.** When a tag
interrupts a single spoken sentence, put a comma inside the first
closing quotation mark, write the tag in lowercase, put a comma
after the tag, and reopen the quotation marks with a lowercase
letter (unless the second part starts a new sentence or a proper
noun).

> "We have three minutes," whispered Maya, "before the lamp goes
> out for good."

**Rule 6 — Questions and exclamations keep their own mark.** A
question mark or exclamation mark stays inside the quotation marks,
and the tag after it is still lowercase — the mark does *not*
become a comma.

> "What if a boat is out there right now?" she asked.
>
> "Promise kept!" Gran said.

And one more rule from this year's grammar work (L.5.2.b): when a
tag begins with an introductory element, put a comma after it —
"**After a long pause,** Gran said, 'We'll go together.'" The comma
after *pause* separates the introductory element from the rest of
the sentence.

### Tags that fit the feeling

*Said* is never wrong, but it never tells the reader *how* the words
were spoken. A precise tag does: *whispered, asked, laughed,
shouted, muttered*. Choose the tag that matches the feeling — a
frightened character whispers; a delighted character laughs. But a
little goes far: if every line has a fancy tag, the tags shout louder
than the dialogue. Use *said* most of the time, a precise tag when
the feeling matters, and an action beat when the body should do the
talking.

### Modeled example 1 — punctuating a raw exchange

*Adult shows the same exchange twice: first as the learner might
draft it, then corrected.*

**Draft (wrong):**

> Maya said what if we can't fix it Gran said then we fix it faster
> a light is a promise remember

**Corrected:**

> "What if we can't fix it?" Maya asked.
>
> "Then we fix it faster," Gran said. "A light is a promise,
> remember? And we keep our promises."

What changed: quotation marks around the exact words (Rule 1); the
question mark kept inside, tag lowercase (Rule 6); the comma inside
the closing quotation mark before *Gran said* (Rule 2); a new
paragraph for the new speaker (Rule 3); capital letters on the first
spoken words (Rule 4). The second sentence, "A light is a promise,
remember?" stays in the same paragraph because Gran is still
speaking.

### Modeled example 2 — the same news, two different voices

*Adult reads both aloud; the learner names what each speaker's words
show about them.*

When the light goes out, Maya says:

> "Gran?" she whispered. "The light went out."

One word — her grandmother's name — as a question, in a whisper.
Maya's dialogue shows fear and a need for an adult. She asks
questions: "What if we can't fix it?" "What if a boat is out there
right now?"

Gran answers in short, practical sentences:

> "The fuse has blown," she said. "There's a spare in the lamp-room
> chest, but the stairs are dark now, and the wind is shaking the
> tower."

Gran names the problem, names the solution, and gives orders:
"You'll carry this. I'll carry the tools. We go together, slow and
steady." Her dialogue shows steadiness and a plan. The reader learns
who these two people are from how they talk — not from the narrator
explaining it. That is dialogue *showing character* (RL.5.3): compare
how the two characters speak, and you can tell them apart with your
eyes closed.

### Modeled example 3 — a tag in the middle, and a tag first

*Adult thinks aloud.*

Mid-sentence tag: "We go together," Gran said, "slow and steady."
The spoken sentence is *We go together slow and steady*, split by
the tag. Comma inside the first quote, lowercase *Gran said*,
comma after the tag, lowercase *slow* to reopen — because the
sentence continues.

Tag first: Gran said, "Keep your eyes on the step in front of you."
Comma after the tag, capital *Keep*. The tag-first form is useful
when the reader needs to know *who* is speaking before the words
begin — for example, after a long stretch of description.

## Guided practice

With the adult, do these together.

1. Punctuate this exchange correctly, with a new paragraph for each
   speaker: *Maya asked are we almost there Gran said not yet keep
   climbing.*
2. This sentence has one punctuation error. Find it and fix it:
   *"The lamp is out". whispered Maya.*
3. Choose the better tag for this line, and say why: Gran has just
   heard the boat's horn and is delighted. *"There," ___ Gran.*
   (a) *muttered* (b) *laughed*
4. Write two lines of dialogue between Maya and Gran on the stairs
   that show Maya is frightened and Gran is calm — without using
   the words *frightened* or *calm*. Use one action beat instead of
   a tag in one of the lines.

## Independent practice

1. Correct the punctuation in each sentence. There is one error in
   each.
   a. "The stairs are dark now", said Gran.
   b. maya whispered, "the light went out."
   c. "Hand me the fuse" Gran said.
   d. "What if we can't fix it." she asked.
2. Rewrite this character clash as separate paragraphs, one for
   each speaker turn, with correct punctuation: *"Ready?" Maya asked. "Ready," Gran said. "Then
   throw the switch," Maya said.*
3. Write a mid-sentence-tag sentence of your own, using the
   lighthouse story: begin with *"The wind is shaking the tower,"*
   and split it with a tag.
4. Gran and Maya hear the boat's horn (¶8). Write two lines of
   dialogue — one for each — that show Gran is relieved and Maya
   is proud. Give each line a tag or action beat that fits.
5. Tag bank: list four tags that fit a frightened speaker and four
   that fit a delighted speaker. Do not repeat *said*.
6. Read ¶5 of the story. Copy the two lines of dialogue in it, and
   underline the tag in each line. Then write one sentence
   explaining what Gran's words show about her character.

## Application

Open your project story map from Lesson 01. Write the first dialogue
scene of your story: two characters, at least four lines, one
mid-sentence tag, and one action beat. Check every line against the
six rules before you move on.

## Exit check

On a slip of paper, punctuate this line correctly: *maya whispered
are we there yet* — with a tag, a new paragraph is not needed for
one line. The adult checks quotation marks, comma placement,
capitalization, and the lowercase tag.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads each corrected example aloud
  with the punctuation spoken ("quote … comma … close quote") so
  the learner hears the rules before seeing them.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may act out the exchange with
  the adult (one voice each) and point to the rule that applies to
  each line, rather than writing all six independent items.
- **Extension:** rewrite ¶4 of the story so that *Maya* is the calm
  one giving orders and *Gran* is the frightened one asking
  questions. Keep the same events; change only the dialogue and
  the tags. What does the story feel like now?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners put the comma outside the quotation
marks, or use a period before the tag ("The fuse has blown." she
said — the period ends the sentence, so the tag becomes a fragment).
Keep returning to the test: *if a tag follows, the period becomes a
comma inside the marks.* Another trap: capitalizing the tag after a
question mark ("What if we can't fix it?" She asked) — the tag is
still part of the same sentence, so it stays lowercase. A third:
writing dialogue that only reports information ("As you know, the
fuse has blown") instead of sounding like a person — ask, "Would
anyone really say that out loud?" Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 02.
