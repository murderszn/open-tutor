# Lesson 04 — Two Accounts, One Event; Reasons Matched to Evidence

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare two accounts of the same
event, tell how their points of view differ, and match each reason to
the evidence that supports it."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil.
**Standards:** RI.5.6, RI.5.8, RI.5.1.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "Two people watch the same rain. One says, 'Great —
my garden needed it.' The other says, 'Terrible — my picnic is
ruined.' Did it rain, or not?" (It rained. Both accounts agree on the
event; they differ on what it meant, because they wanted different
things.) An **account** is one person's or one text's version of an
event. A **point of view** is the position from which they see it —
what they care about, what they notice, and what they want to happen
next.

## Explicit teaching

**Multiple accounts (RI.5.6):** when two accounts describe the same
event, first find what they **agree** on (the shared facts), then
find where they **differ** — and ask whether the difference is about
facts or about point of view. Most honest disagreements are about
point of view: the same water is a problem for one person and a gift
for another.

**Reasons and evidence (RI.5.8):** an author makes **points**
(claims) and supports them with **reasons** (why the point is true)
and **evidence** (facts, examples, quotations, or observations that
back the reason). The skill is matching: *which* reason and *which*
evidence support *which* point? Evidence for one point does not
prove a different point.

**Fact vs. opinion:** a **fact** can be checked ("the field flooded
for three days"). An **opinion** is a judgment ("the dam should be
removed"). Opinions are not lies — but they cannot serve as evidence
for a fact claim without a reason behind them.

### The accounts — "Two Reports on Willow Creek" (original)

Both accounts describe the same spring flood at Willow Creek.

> **Account A — Tomas, farmer.** "In spring, the pond rose and
> covered my low field for three days. I lost part of my hay crop.
> The water stayed too long for the young plants. The dam should be
> lowered so the field drains faster. I am not against the beavers,
> but a field that cannot drain cannot feed anyone."
>
> **Account B — Ana, pond watcher.** "In spring, the pond rose and
> spread into the marsh beside the creek. By summer, frogs sang there
> every night, and a heron hunted there each morning. In dry July,
> the pond still held water when a nearby stream shrank. The dam
> should stay, because the pond is the reason the marsh is alive
> again."

### Modeled example 1 — agreements and point-of-view differences

*Adult builds the chart aloud; the learner supplies the quotations.*

| | What the accounts show |
|---|---|
| Agree | The pond rose in spring and water spread beyond the creek. Both mention the spring rise and standing water lasting days. |
| Agree | The water mattered to living things: Tomas's crop plants and Ana's frogs and heron both depended on it. |
| Differ — what each notices | Tomas notices the field and the lost hay crop. Ana notices the marsh, frogs, and heron. |
| Differ — what each wants next | Tomas: "The dam should be lowered so the field drains faster." Ana: "The dam should stay." |
| Differ — point of view | Tomas speaks as someone whose living depends on the field. Ana speaks as someone who watches the pond's wildlife. Neither account is lying; each values a different outcome. |

The test for a point-of-view difference: could both accounts be true
at the same time? Here, yes — the field flooded *and* the marsh came
alive. When both can be true, the disagreement is about values and
wants, not about facts.

### Modeled example 2 — matching reasons and evidence to points

Read this short author paragraph (original, written for this lesson):

> **Point 1:** Beaver ponds help towns in dry summers. **Reason:**
> they store water and release it slowly. **Evidence:** Willow
> Creek's pond still held water in dry July while a nearby stream
> shrank to a trickle. **Point 2:** Beaver ponds also help wildlife.
> **Reason:** still, deep water gives animals a place to live.
> **Evidence:** frogs laid eggs among the pond plants, and a heron
> hunted along the bank.

Now match (answers in the teacher key — try first):

- Which evidence supports Point 1? The July water detail — it is
  about dry-summer water, not about animals.
- Which evidence supports Point 2? The frogs and heron — they are
  about wildlife, not about town water supply.
- Which sentence is a distractor? "The dam was built of branches
  and mud." True, but it supports neither point — it describes
  construction, not dry-summer help or wildlife help.

A common error: attaching *all* the evidence to *both* points
("everything proves everything"). Evidence must be matched, point by
point, or the argument falls apart.

### Modeled example 3 — fact, opinion, and a hidden reason

- "The field flooded for three days." — **Fact** (checkable).
- "The dam should be lowered." — **Opinion** (a recommendation).
  It becomes stronger when Tomas adds a reason: "so the field
  drains faster" and evidence: "I lost part of my hay crop."
- "The pond is the reason the marsh is alive again." — **Opinion
  with a reason**: Ana's evidence is the frogs and heron that
  returned. A reader may still disagree with her recommendation and
  accept her observations — that separation is the whole skill.

## Guided practice

1. Quote one sentence from Tomas and one from Ana that describe the
   same spring rise. What do they agree on?
2. In your own words, state what Tomas wants to happen next and what
   Ana wants to happen next. Quote the sentence that proves each.
3. Read: "Beaver ponds are wonderful." Fact or opinion? What reason
   or evidence would turn it into a supported point?
4. Match these to Point 1 or Point 2 from Modeled example 2, or mark
   "neither": (a) "The pond held water in July." (b) "A heron hunted
   along the bank." (c) "Beavers gnaw trees with strong teeth."

## Independent practice

1. Write two sentences: one thing Tomas and Ana agree on, and one
   thing they disagree on, each with a quotation.
2. Explain, in two sentences, why their disagreement is mainly about
   point of view rather than about facts. Use the test: can both
   accounts be true at the same time?
3. Read this paragraph (original): "Point: School gardens help
   students learn. Reason: students see plants grow. Evidence:
   the class measured bean plants every week and watched them climb.
   Evidence: the garden is near the parking lot." Which evidence
   supports the point, and which is irrelevant? Explain why.
4. Read: "Ana says the pond should stay because the marsh is alive
   again." Is "the marsh is alive again" a fact, an opinion, or an
   opinion backed by observations? Name the observations.
5. Attribute these unattributed sentences to Tomas or Ana, with a
   reason: (a) "The hay will not recover this year." (b) "The frogs
   sang every night by summer." (c) "Three days of standing water
   is too long for young plants."
6. Inference (RI.5.1): Tomas says, "I am not against the beavers."
   Quote a detail that supports the inference that Tomas accepts
   beavers in general but objects to this dam's height. Explain
   your reasoning in one sentence.

## Application

Two learners (or the learner and the adult) each take one account —
Tomas or Ana — and argue that side for one minute, using only details
from that account. Then switch sides. Switching sides is the test:
if you can argue both, you understand point of view instead of just
picking a team.

## Exit check

On a slip of paper: "Tomas and Ana agree that ___. They disagree
about ___, because Tomas cares about ___ and Ana cares about ___."
The adult checks that the agreement is a shared fact and the
disagreement names a point of view, not a made-up fact.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the two accounts are read aloud in two voices
  (adult as Tomas, learner as Ana, then swapped); key recommendation
  sentences are underlined before matching begins.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may sort agreement/difference
  cards onto a chart and dictate the exit check.
- **Extension:** write a third account of the same flood — from the
  heron's point of view, or from a child who plays near the creek —
  in three sentences. What would that account notice that Tomas and
  Ana do not?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "one account is true and the other is lying."
Keep returning to the both-can-be-true test. Another trap: treating
an opinion as evidence ("Ana says the pond should stay, so ponds are
good" — that is a recommendation, not a fact). Require a reason and
an observation behind every opinion used as support. Answer-key
