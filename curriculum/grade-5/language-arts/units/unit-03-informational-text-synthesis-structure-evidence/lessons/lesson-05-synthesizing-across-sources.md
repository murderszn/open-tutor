# Lesson 05 — Synthesizing Across Sources: Paraphrase, Quote, Source Notes

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can put information from several sources
together in my own words, quote short bits exactly when they prove a
point, and list my sources."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; Lessons 01, 03,
and 04 passages; pencil.
**Standards:** RI.5.7, RI.5.9, W.5.8, SL.5.3.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "One text says beavers build dams. Another says dams
make ponds. A third says ponds help frogs. If I put those together and
write, 'Beaver dams make ponds that help frogs,' did I copy anyone?"
(No — that sentence is new. It *synthesizes*: it combines
information from several sources into one idea that no single source
stated in that form.) Synthesis is not copying, and it is not
summarizing one text — it is building something new from several.

## Explicit teaching

**Synthesize** = combine information from several sources to answer
a question or explain a topic knowledgeably (RI.5.9). A synthesis
shows its work: the reader can tell which idea came from which source.

**Paraphrase** = restate information in your own words, keeping the
meaning. A good paraphrase changes the words *and* the sentence
shape. Changing one word ("big" → "large") and keeping the rest is
not paraphrasing — it is copy-close writing, and it must be fixed.

**Quotation** = the author's exact words in quotation marks. Quote
short bits that prove a point — a vivid phrase, a number, a
recommendation — not whole paragraphs. Every quotation needs a
source: *who said it, and in which text?*

**Source notes** = a table you build *while* reading, not after
writing. Four columns:

| Source | What it says, in my own words | Exact words I might quote | Question it helps answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| "The Pond Builders" | … | … | … |
| "Two Streams" | … | … | … |
| Tomas's account / Ana's account | … | … | … |

**Source list** = the list at the end of finished work naming each
source used, so a reader can check it. For this unit, source titles
and lesson numbers are enough (for example: "'The Pond Builders,'
Lesson 01"). A source list is not decoration — it is how a reader
knows you did not invent the information.

**Summarizing a speaker (SL.5.3):** when someone speaks — in a
discussion, a presentation, or a read-aloud account — listen for
their *points* and the *reasons and evidence* behind each point, then
retell them in your own words. Do not retell every word; retell the
argument.

### Modeled example 1 — a paraphrase that works, and one that does not

Original sentence (from "The Pond Builders"): "Because the dam
slowed the water, the pond grew deep and calm."

- **Copy-close (fix this):** "Because the dam slowed the water, the
  pond became deep and calm." Only one word changed. This is too
  close to copy.
- **Paraphrase (good):** "Once the dam held the stream back, the
  water settled into a deep, still pond." New words, new shape,
  same meaning. Check: does it still say the dam caused the change?
  Yes — the cause/effect link survived the rewrite. A paraphrase
  that drops the cause ("The pond was deep and calm") has lost
  meaning, not just words.

### Modeled example 2 — source notes for one question

Question: *Should Willow Creek keep its beaver dam?*

| Source | In my own words | Exact words I might quote | Question it helps answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| "The Pond Builders" (L01) | Dams turn a fast stream into a pond that helps wildlife but floods a low field. | "held back the water, and a pond began to form" | What does the dam do? |
| "Two Streams" (L03) | A stream with beavers held water in dry July; one without shrank to a trickle. | "while Dry Fork shrank to a trickle" | Does the pond help in dry times? |
| Tomas (L04) | The spring flood covered his field for three days and hurt his hay crop. | "covered my low field for three days" | What does the dam cost? |
| Ana (L04) | The same flood spread into the marsh, and frogs and a heron returned. | "the pond is the reason the marsh is alive again" | What does the dam give back? |

Notice: the table includes a counter-point (Tomas's cost) alongside
the benefits. A synthesis that hides the counter-point is not
synthesis — it is one-sided reporting.

### Modeled example 3 — a synthesis paragraph

> Willow Creek's dam changed a fast stream into a pond, and that
> change helped and hurt in different ways. The pond held water in
> dry July "while Dry Fork shrank to a trickle," so wildlife that
> needs still water — frogs and a heron — had a home when a nearby
> stream did not. But the same pond "covered [Tomas's] low field for
> three days" in spring and damaged his hay crop. Keeping the dam
> means keeping both results, not just the welcome one.

Check it: ideas from three sources, in the writer's own words except
for two short quotations; a counter-point included; no source is
copied at length. The bracket in "[Tomas's]" shows a small change
inside a quotation so it reads clearly — the adult should explain
that brackets mark the writer's change, not the author's words.

## Guided practice

1. Paraphrase this sentence in your own words, changing both words
   and shape: "A heron began to hunt along the bank." Then check:
   does your version keep the meaning?
2. Repair this copy-close paraphrase of "Frogs laid eggs among the
   pond plants": "Frogs placed eggs among the pond plants." What
   else must change besides one verb?
3. Build one source-note row for the illustration (Lesson 01):
   what does it show, in your own words, and what question does it
   help answer? (It shows the dam, lodge, and heron — it helps
   answer "What does a beaver pond look like?" It cannot answer
   "What happened in spring," because a picture has no dates.)
4. Listen as the adult reads Tomas's account aloud. Summarize his
   points in two sentences: what happened, what he wants, and what
   evidence he gives. Do not copy his sentences.

## Independent practice

1. Paraphrase: "In the dry month of July, when a nearby stream
   shrank to a trickle, Willow Creek's pond still held water."
   Keep the comparison (pond vs. stream) in your version.
2. Paraphrase: "The dam should be lowered so the field drains
   faster." Keep it as a recommendation, not a fact.
3. Write a source-note row for "Two Streams" answering: "How are the
   two streams different in July?" Include one exact phrase you
   might quote.
4. Write a 3-sentence synthesis answering: "Why do people disagree
   about the Willow Creek dam?" Use at least two sources and
   include one short quotation with its source named.
5. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "Ana said the pond is great
   and Tomas is wrong." Explain two problems with that sentence as
   synthesis: it copies no evidence, and it dismisses a source
   instead of weighing it. Rewrite it fairly in one sentence.
6. Summarize a speaker: the adult reads Ana's account aloud once.
   Write her two main points and one piece of evidence for each,
   without looking at the text.

## Application

Start the project: fill the source-note table for all four project
sources (the three passages plus the illustration) before drafting
anything. A brief drafted without source notes almost always copies
one source and ignores the rest — the table prevents that.

## Exit check

On a slip of paper: one paraphrase of a sentence the adult chooses
from "The Pond Builders," plus the source it came from. The adult
checks: changed words *and* shape, same meaning, source named.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult models one paraphrase aloud before
  the learner writes one; sentence starters are allowed ("In other
  words, …").
- **Alternate response:** the learner may dictate paraphrases and
  source notes for the adult to scribe, then read them back and fix
  copy-close wording.
- **Extension:** synthesize a second question — "What should a town
  do when a beaver dam floods a field?" — using the pond-leveler
  paragraph from Lesson 03 as a fourth source, and name its
  trade-off in one sentence.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: paraphrase = changing one word; synthesis =
summarizing the longest passage and adding "also" + one detail from
another. Require the table first, and require at least two sources in
every synthesis paragraph. Another trap: quotations without sources
("someone said …"). Every quotation in this unit names its source in
the same sentence or the one before it. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 05.
