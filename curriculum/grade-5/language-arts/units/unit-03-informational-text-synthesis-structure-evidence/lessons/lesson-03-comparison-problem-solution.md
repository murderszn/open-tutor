# Lesson 03 — Text Structures: Comparison and Problem/Solution

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare two things by what is alike
and different, and I can name a problem, a solution, and what the
solution costs."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; pencil.
**Standards:** RI.5.3, RI.5.5, RI.5.4.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult says: "A pond and a puddle are alike because both hold
water. They are different because a pond lasts and a puddle dries up
fast." Ask: "Which sentence tells a similarity, and which tells a
difference?" Then: "A problem is something that needs fixing. A
solution is a way to fix it. If the fix creates a new problem, is it
still a solution?" Accept: yes, but a solution with a cost must be
named honestly — that trade-off is the interesting part.

## Explicit teaching

**Comparison** — showing how two or more things are alike and
different. Ask: *What is the same? What is different?* Signal words:
*similarly, alike, both, also, in common, however, although, unlike,
on the other hand, in contrast, while, whereas*.

**Problem/solution** — naming a problem and proposing one or more
ways to solve it. Ask: *What is the problem? What is being tried?
What does it cost or risk?* Signal words: *problem, difficulty,
challenge, solution, solve, therefore, as a result, one way, another
way*.

Comparison and problem/solution often share signal words (*however,
as a result*). Again, the main job decides: is the author mainly
lining two things up side by side, or mainly fixing something?

### The passage — "Two Streams" (original; practice dataset)

> **1.** Willow Creek and Dry Fork both run through farm fields, and
> both flow into the same river. But that is where the likeness ends.
> Willow Creek has beavers. Dry Fork does not.
>
> **2.** In Willow Creek, the dam slows the water, so the stream
> becomes a deep, calm pond. In contrast, Dry Fork stays shallow and
> fast. In the dry month of July, Willow Creek's pond still held
> water, while Dry Fork shrank to a trickle.
>
> **3.** The wildlife differs, too. In one practice count — invented
> for this lesson, not a real field survey — observers noted 12 kinds
> of birds at Willow Creek in a week and 4 kinds at Dry Fork. Frogs
> sang at Willow Creek on warm nights. At Dry Fork, the banks were
> quiet. Similarly, both streams had minnows, but only Willow Creek
> had a heron that returned every morning.
>
> **4.** The difference is not all in one direction. Willow Creek's
> pond sometimes flooded a low field in spring. Dry Fork never
> flooded a field, because its water ran off quickly. A stream that
> holds water helps wildlife in a dry summer — and a stream that
> lets water go quickly spares a field in a wet spring.

*Note on the numbers:* the bird counts in paragraph 3 are a fictional
practice dataset written for this lesson. Do not quote them as real
observations, and do not use them outside this unit. The lesson's
point is how comparison works, not how many birds live anywhere.

### Modeled example 1 — a comparison chart

*Adult fills the chart from the passage; the learner checks each row
against a quoted detail.*

| Feature | Willow Creek (with beavers) | Dry Fork (without beavers) |
|---|---|---|
| Alike | Runs through farm fields; flows into the same river; has minnows | Runs through farm fields; flows into the same river; has minnows |
| Water | Deep, calm pond; still held water in dry July | Shallow and fast; shrank to a trickle in July |
| Wildlife (practice count) | 12 kinds of birds in a week; frogs sang; a heron returned daily | 4 kinds of birds in a week; quiet banks; no heron mentioned |
| Flooding | Sometimes flooded a low field in spring | Never flooded a field; water ran off quickly |

A good comparison names *both* sides for each row. A chart that lists
only Willow Creek's features is a description, not a comparison.

### Modeled example 2 — problem, solution, trade-off

Read this paragraph (original, written for this lesson):

> The problem was the spring flood: Willow Creek's pond covered the
> farmer's low field for days. One solution was a pond leveler — a
> pipe through the dam that lets some water out slowly while the pond
> stays deep enough for the beavers. As a result, the field flooded
> less, but the pond sat a little lower, and the farmer still watched
> the spring rain.

Work it through:

- **Problem:** the pond flooded the low field in spring.
- **Solution:** a pond leveler pipe that releases some water slowly
  while keeping the pond deep enough for beavers.
- **Trade-off:** the field flooded less, but the pond sat lower and
  the farmer still had to watch the rain. The solution helped; it did
  not erase the problem.

A common error: naming the *cause* ("the dam") as the problem, or
naming a wish ("everyone should be happy") as a solution. A solution
must be something someone actually does.

### Modeled example 3 — comparison across two texts, not just two streams

Compare the structure of "The Pond Builders" (Lesson 01) with "Two
Streams" (this lesson):

- "The Pond Builders" is mostly **chronology + cause/effect**: it
  follows the dam build in time, then explains what the dam caused.
- "Two Streams" is mostly **comparison**: it lines two streams up
  feature by feature.
- Both passages teach about beaver ponds, but they organize the
  information differently. That is RI.5.5: comparing the *overall
  structure* of two texts, not just their facts.

## Guided practice

1. From "Two Streams," name one similarity and one difference that
   are *not* in the chart above, each with a quoted detail.
2. The practice count says 12 bird kinds at Willow Creek and 4 at Dry
   Fork. In your own words, explain why that count supports a
   comparison claim but must not be quoted as a real survey.
3. Read: "Dry Fork's banks were quiet." Is that a fact from the
   passage or an opinion? How can you tell? (It is presented as an
   observation in the passage; "quiet" here describes what observers
   heard, not a judgment about whether quiet is good.)
4. Name the problem, solution, and trade-off in Modeled example 2
   without looking back. Then check.

## Independent practice

1. Complete a comparison chart for "a pond" and "a puddle" using
   this lesson's ideas (depth, how long water lasts, wildlife).
   Each row must have both sides.
2. Read this paragraph (original): "The town's pond dried up every
   August. The town dug the pond deeper and planted shade trees at
   its edge. The pond still shrank in August, but it no longer dried
   out completely." Name the problem, the solution, and the
   remaining trade-off.
3. Read this paragraph (original): "Unlike Dry Fork, Willow Creek
   held water in July. Similarly, both streams had minnows. However,
   only Willow Creek had a heron." Underline the comparison signal
   words and name the structure.
4. Compare the structures of "The Pond Builders" paragraph 3 and
   "Two Streams" paragraph 2 in two sentences: which structure does
   each use, and what signal words prove it?
5. Vocabulary in context (RI.5.4): use the sentences around it to
   explain *in contrast* and *similarly*. Then use both words in two
   sentences about the two streams.
6. Relationships (RI.5.3): explain the relationship between "water
   held in July" and "wildlife at Willow Creek" in one sentence,
   using a detail from the passage.

## Application

Choose a pair you know (two pets, two games, two seasons) and write
a 4-sentence comparison paragraph: one similarity sentence, two
difference sentences, and one sentence that says which difference
matters most and why. Read it aloud and have the adult check that
every claim has both sides or a reason.

## Exit check

On a slip of paper: "Willow Creek and Dry Fork are alike in ___ and
different in ___. One problem in the Willow Creek story is ___, and
one solution is ___." The adult checks that the solution is an action,
not a wish.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads "Two Streams" aloud first;
  the chart is built row by row, not all at once.
- **Alternate response:** the learner may sort sentence cards into
  "alike" and "different" piles, then dictate the chart.
- **Extension:** write a problem/solution paragraph about a real,
  small, safe problem the learner knows (a messy desk, a lost pencil)
  with a solution *and* a trade-off. A solution without a trade-off
  is usually too easy to be believed.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: a comparison that only describes one side, or
a "solution" that is just the opposite of the problem ("the field
should not flood"). Require an action and a cost for every solution.
Also watch the practice-count trap: learners may carry the fictional
12-vs.-4 counts into the project as if they were real data. The
project rubric requires the counts to be labeled "practice example"
if used at all — or left out. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 03.
