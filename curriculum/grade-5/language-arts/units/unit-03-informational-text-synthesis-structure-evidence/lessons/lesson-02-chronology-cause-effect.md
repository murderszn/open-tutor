# Lesson 02 — Text Structures: Chronology and Cause/Effect

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell when a text is organized by
time order and when it is organized by causes and effects, and I can
prove it with signal words."
**Time:** 40 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; Lesson 01's
passage "The Pond Builders"; pencil.
**Standards:** RI.5.3, RI.5.5, L.5.6.

## Readiness check (2 min)

The adult reads: "First I put on socks. Then I put on shoes." Ask:
"Could I wear the shoes first and the socks second?" (No — the order
matters.) Then: "I was late because my alarm did not ring." Ask:
"Which part is the cause, and which is the effect?" (Cause: alarm did
not ring. Effect: late.) Time order and cause/effect are different
jobs, and authors signal which job they are doing.

## Explicit teaching

A **text structure** is how an author organizes information. Two
structures in this lesson:

**Chronology (sequence)** — events in the order they happen. Ask:
*What happened first, next, and last?* Signal words: *first, then,
next, after, before, finally, in spring, later, when*.

**Cause/effect** — why something happened, and what resulted. A
**cause** is the reason; an **effect** is the result. Ask: *Why did
this happen? What happened because of it?* Signal words: *because,
since, as a result, therefore, so, consequently, because of this,
led to*.

The same passage can use both structures in different paragraphs.
Paragraph 2 of "The Pond Builders" is mostly chronology (the build
steps). Paragraph 3 is mostly cause/effect (the dam slowed the water,
so a pond formed and animals moved in). Naming the structure helps you
find the information faster — that is RI.5.5, and it is also how you
locate an answer quickly (RI.5.7).

**Signal words are clues, not proof.** The word *then* can appear in
a cause/effect paragraph too ("then the pond formed"). Always check:
is the author mainly listing steps in time, or mainly explaining why
something happened? The main job decides the structure.

### Modeled example 1 — a chronology map of the dam build

*Adult builds the map from paragraph 2, in order, with the signal
word that marks each step.*

1. **First:** beavers gnawed down small trees.
2. **Then:** they dragged branches into the stream and piled them
   across it.
3. **Next:** they packed mud and stones into the gaps.
4. **Finally:** the dam held back the water, and a pond began to form.

Test: scramble the steps and ask whether the dam could still form.
Step 4 cannot come first — a wall cannot hold water before it is
built. When order matters, the structure is chronology. A common
error: listing the steps but skipping the signal words. The signal
words are the evidence for the structure claim.

### Modeled example 2 — a cause/effect chain from paragraph 3

*Adult draws the chain left to right, one arrow per link, and quotes
the proof for each link.*

**Cause:** the dam slowed the water → **Effect 1:** the pond grew
deep and calm → **Effect 2:** animals that need still water moved in
(frogs laid eggs; a heron hunted) → **Effect 3 (a later, separate
effect):** in dry July the pond still held water while a nearby
stream shrank.

Proof words: "Because the dam slowed the water, the pond grew deep
and calm. As a result, animals that need still water moved in."
*Because* and *as a result* are the signal words. Note the chain
rule: an effect can become the next cause. The deep pond (effect) is
also the cause of animals moving in. Chains, not single pairs, are
how real explanations work.

### Modeled example 3 — telling the two structures apart

Read this new paragraph (original, written for this lesson):

> In April, the beavers repaired the dam. Because spring rain raised
> the creek, water spilled over the top. As a result, the pond grew
> wider, and the low field filled again.

Is it chronology or cause/effect? The paragraph has a time word
(*In April*), but its main job is explaining why the pond grew wider
and the field filled: rain raised the creek (cause) → water spilled
(effect) → pond wider and field filled (further effects). Signal
words *because* and *as a result* carry the argument. Answer:
cause/effect, with a time detail inside it. A learner who answers
"chronology because it says April" has used one word instead of the
paragraph's main job — correct that move explicitly.

## Guided practice

1. Re-read paragraph 2 of "The Pond Builders." Write the four build
   steps in order, each with its signal word.
2. Re-read paragraph 3. Draw the cause/effect chain with at least
   three links, quoting one signal word per link.
3. Read: "First the pond froze. Then the beavers stayed inside the
   lodge. Finally, in March, the ice melted." Chronology or
   cause/effect? Prove it with two signal words and the main-job
   test.
4. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "The heron caused the pond."
   Use paragraph 3 to explain why that reverses cause and effect.
   (The pond came first; the heron moved in *because* of the pond.)

## Independent practice

1. Write a 3-step chronology of your morning, using *first, then,
   finally*. Circle the signal words.
2. Write one cause/effect chain about the pond with at least three
   links, using *because* and *as a result*. Do not copy paragraph 3
   word for word.
3. Read this paragraph (original): "The farmer planted willows far
   from the field. As a result, the beavers cut those trees instead,
   and the field's edge stayed clear." Name the structure and quote
   the signal word that proves it.
4. Read this paragraph (original): "Before dawn, Ana walked to the
   pond. Next she counted frogs. Then she wrote the number in her
   notebook." Name the structure and explain why it is not
   cause/effect, even though counting follows walking.
5. Relationships (RI.5.3): Explain the relationship between the dam
   and the heron in one sentence, using a detail from the passage.
   (The heron depends on the pond the dam created; it did not create
   the pond.)
6. Vocabulary in context: what does *consequently* mean? Use it in a
   sentence about the dam and the pond, then check a dictionary.

## Application

Take the chronology map from Modeled example 1 and turn it into a
cause/effect chain: what did each build step *cause*? (Example:
packing mud into gaps caused the wall to hold water.) Notice that the
same facts can be organized two ways — the author chooses the
structure that fits the question being answered.

## Exit check

On a slip of paper: one sentence in chronology ("First … then …") and
one in cause/effect ("Because … as a result …"), both about Willow
Creek. The adult checks that the chronology sentence's order matters
and the cause/effect sentence's cause really produces its effect.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** signal words are underlined in a first pass
  before any structure is named; the learner may dictate the chain.
- **Alternate response:** build the chain with arrow cards on a
  table instead of writing it.
- **Extension:** find a paragraph in the passage that mixes both
  structures and explain, in two sentences, which structure does the
  main job and which is only a detail.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: any time word means chronology, any *because*
means cause/effect — without checking the main job. Teach the
main-job test ("What is the author mainly doing here?") every time.
Another trap: reversing cause and effect (heron → pond). Make the
learner point to the sentence order and the signal word before
accepting a chain. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 02.
