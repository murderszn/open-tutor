# Lesson 04 — Showing the Data: Tables, Graphs, and Plots

**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, colored
pencils, ruler, the learner's own measurement data (from Lessons 01–02 and
the project trials), graph-paper or lined paper.

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will turn your measurements into
pictures of data — a bar graph, a pictograph, and a line plot — and learn to
read patterns from them.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What three things does a careful data
table always have?" Labels, units, and every trial recorded (Lesson 03's
ritual).

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Data in a table is honest; data in a **picture** is honest *and* easy to
read. Every display needs the same four things: a **title**, **labels** on
each part, the **unit**, and the **data itself**.

- **Bar graph** — bars whose heights show amounts. Good for comparing
  groups (brand A vs. brand B vs. brand C).
- **Pictograph** — pictures (symbols) where each picture stands for a fixed
  amount. Good for counts a young reader can see at a glance.
- **Line plot** — marks (usually X's) stacked above a number line. Good for
  showing every single measurement, including repeats — scientists love
  line plots because repeated trials stay visible.

**Worked example 1 — bar graph from practice data**

> Adult: *"Question: which paper towel held the most water? Here is a
> **fictional practice dataset** (not our real test results): brand A held
> 40, 42, and 41 mL across 3 trials; brand B held 55, 53, and 56 mL; brand C
> held 38, 40, and 39 mL. First find each brand's middle value (about 41,
> 55, and 39 mL). Now draw the bars: title 'Water held by three paper-towel
> brands (mL)'; the bottom labels the brands; the side is marked in mL; each
> bar reaches its brand's value. The pattern jumps out: brand B's bar is
> tallest. 'How many more' milliliters did B hold than C? About 55 − 39 =
> 16 mL more."*

**Worked example 2 — line plot of repeated trials**

> Adult: *"Same fictional dataset, brand B only: 55, 53, 56 mL. Draw a
> number line from 50 to 60 mL. Put one X above 55, one above 53, one above
> 56. Title: 'Brand B: three trials (mL)'. The X's cluster near 55 — that
> cluster is the **pattern** of repeated trials. A fourth trial would
> probably land near 55 too, because patterns repeat. That is why
> scientists repeat trials instead of trusting one try."*

Key idea to say plainly: **A good display has a title, labels, units, and
the data. Bar graphs compare groups; pictographs show counts with pictures;
line plots keep every trial visible — and all of them reveal patterns.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner builds and reads)

The learner builds all three displays from a second **fictional practice
dataset** (clearly labeled "practice data — not our measurements"): three
brands, two trials each — A: 30, 32 mL; B: 44, 46 mL; C: 28, 29 mL.

1. Draw and label the bar graph (title, brand labels, mL scale). *(B
   tallest at ~45)*
2. "How many more milliliters did brand B hold than brand C?" *(~45 − 28.5
   ≈ 16–17 mL; accept a reasonable rounded answer with the subtraction
   shown)*
3. Draw the line plot for brand A's two trials. *(X's above 30 and 32)*
4. "The X's sit close together. What does that tell you?" *(the trials
   agree — a pattern; the result is trustworthy)*
5. Draw a pictograph where one water-drop symbol = 10 mL: how many full
   drops for brand B (~45 mL)? *(4 full drops + about half — symbols show
   approximate amounts)*
6. **Error analysis:** "A bar graph has no unit on the side and no title.
   What two questions can a reader NOT answer?" *(how much (unit missing);
   what the graph is about (title missing))*

## Independent practice

The learner chooses one display and rebuilds it from their **own** Lesson 02
measurements (e.g., a line plot of a repeated length measurement, or a bar
graph comparing three measured objects). Every display must carry the four
requirements (title, labels, unit, data). Then the learner writes two
"how many more / which is bigger" questions about their own display and
answers them.

## Application

Find a bar graph or pictograph in a book, newspaper, or on a food package.
Check it for the four requirements and report which one (if any) is missing.

## Exit check

Prompt: "You ran 3 trials and got 20, 21, and 60 mL. What does the line
plot show, and what should you do about the 60?" Accept: the X's show two
clustered and one far away — the 60 is likely a mistake (spill, misread);
check your notes, and if it was an error, redo that trial honestly rather
than hiding it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Pre-draw the axes and scales; the learner places the bars
  and X's. Sentence frame: "My graph shows ___. The pattern is ___."
- **Extension:** The learner explains which display fits which question —
  bar graph for comparing brands, line plot for showing repeat-trial
  spread — and converts the pictograph's symbols back into numbers.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** the tallest bar "proves" the winner forever.
Re-anchor: data supports a claim *for this test*; a new test with new
towels could differ. Also watch for learners who drop the unit on the axis
— insist on it every time. **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md)
(Lesson 04).
