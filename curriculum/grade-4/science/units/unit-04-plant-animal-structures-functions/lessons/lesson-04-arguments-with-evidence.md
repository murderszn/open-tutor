# Lesson 04 — Building Arguments With Evidence

**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** claim/evidence sorting
cards (adult-prepared from the examples below), science journal.

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will build a scientific
argument: a claim, evidence you observed, and a reason why the
evidence supports the claim — and you will compare structures across
two organisms.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "How do you know the sun is shining,
without looking at it?" Any answer that points to evidence (warmth,
bright light through a window, shadows) shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **claim** is a statement you say is true. **Evidence** is something
you actually observed that supports the claim. A **reason** explains
*why* the evidence supports the claim. An **argument** is all three
together. Scientists do not win arguments by speaking loudly or by
liking an idea — they win them with evidence.

The pattern for this unit is:

> **Claim:** This structure does this job, which helps the organism
> survive / grow / behave / reproduce.
> **Evidence:** I observed…
> **Reason:** This supports the claim because…

Two rules keep arguments honest. First, evidence must be observed, not
guessed or wished: "I saw bees visit the bright flowers many times"
is evidence; "bees probably like pretty things" is a guess. Second, an
opinion is not evidence: "the flower is pretty" tells us about taste,
not about the flower's job.

**Worked example 1 — thorns protect (fictional garden notes)**

> Adult thinks aloud, using the adult's fictional garden notes as
> practice data (these notes are made-up practice data, not the
> learner's observations): *"The notes say: on Monday, a goat nibbled
> the thornless stems but left the thorny stems alone. On Thursday,
> the same thing happened again. Claim: thorns protect the plant.
> Evidence: the goat ate thornless stems twice and avoided thorny
> stems twice. Reason: if thorns did nothing, the goat would eat both
> kinds equally — but it consistently avoided the thorny ones, so the
> thorns are doing the protecting. Claim, evidence, reason — and the
> evidence was observed twice, not once."*

**Worked example 2 — fixing a weak argument**

> Adult models: *"A learner says: 'Bright petals help flowers
> reproduce because flowers are pretty.' That is weak. 'Pretty' is an
> opinion, and it does not explain reproduction. Fix it with
> observation: 'Claim: bright petals help flowers reproduce.
> Evidence: I watched bees visit bright flowers ten times in an hour
> and dull flowers once. Reason: bees move pollen between flowers,
> and pollen is how flowers make seeds — so more bee visits mean more
> chances to make seeds.' Same claim, now with evidence and a reason
> that connects the evidence to reproduction."*

Comparing across two organisms uses the same pattern twice, then a
conclusion: a sunflower's bright petals attract pollinators (helps
reproduction), while a cactus's thorns discourage eaters (helps
survival). Different structures, different jobs — and both jobs help
their plant live and make more plants.

Key idea to say plainly: **An argument is claim + observed evidence +
reason. Opinions are not evidence. One observation is a start; repeated
observations are stronger.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner answers and does)

1. Sort these into Claim or Evidence: (a) "Roots hold plants
   steady." (b) "I pulled a plant gently and it stayed in the soil."
   (c) "Thorns protect plants." (d) "A goat avoided thorny stems
   twice."
2. Build an argument from this evidence (fictional practice data): a
   gardener watered two identical plants; one had its leaves covered
   for a week and grew less. Claim: leaves help plants grow. Supply
   the evidence and the reason.
3. A learner argues: "Feathers help birds survive because feathers
   are beautiful." What is wrong with this argument, and how would
   you fix it with one observation?
4. Compare a duck and a fish: name one structure each uses to move in
   water, and state the job in one sentence each.
5. Which is stronger evidence, and why: one bee visit observed once,
   or bee visits counted over five days? Explain in one sentence.

## Independent practice

1. Write a full argument (claim, evidence, reason) for this claim:
   "A stem helps a plant survive." Use the broken-stem reasoning from
   Lesson 01 as your evidence source, stated as an observation.
2. Write a full argument for: "A turtle's shell helps it survive."
3. Compare two organisms: a sunflower and a rabbit. Name one
   structure from each, give its job, and say whether it supports
   survival, growth, behavior, or reproduction.
4. Find the flaw: "The heart is the most important structure because
   I like hearts best." Rewrite it as a proper argument about the
   heart's job, or explain why it cannot be fixed without evidence.

## Applied task

With the adult, choose one structure from Lessons 01–03. Observe it
in a photo or a real specimen (adult-supervised). Write one argument
with all three parts, then read it aloud and check: is my evidence
something I actually observed? Does my reason connect the evidence to
the claim? Fix any part that fails the check, and note what you
changed.

## Exit check

Tell the adult, without looking at your journal: what are the three
parts of an argument, and why is "I think so" not evidence? Three
parts named, plus the reason opinions are not evidence, passes.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the three parts blur, use the sentence frames:
  "I claim…", "I observed…", "This supports my claim because…"
  Fill one frame at a time.
- **Extension:** take one of your arguments and strengthen it: add a
  second piece of observed evidence, or name a test that would check
  the claim (for example, covering leaves and measuring growth over a
  week, with the adult's help).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners often treat a vivid single observation
as proof, or restate the claim as its own evidence ("thorns protect
because they are protective"). Insist on the separation: the claim
says what is true, the evidence says what was seen, the reason says
why the seen thing supports the true thing. Do not let "because I saw
it on a video" stand as observation without naming what was seen.
