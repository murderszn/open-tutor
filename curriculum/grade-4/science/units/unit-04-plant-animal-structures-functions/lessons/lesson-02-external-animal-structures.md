# Lesson 02 — Outside Parts of an Animal and Their Jobs

**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** adult-chosen photos of a
duck, a turtle, a rabbit, and a fish; science journal; word cards.

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will name the outside parts
of animals — skin, fur, feathers, scales, beaks, claws, shells, legs,
wings, and fins — and say the job each part does.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Name an animal and one part of its
body." Any animal-part pair shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Animals have outside parts just as plants do, and each part has a job
that helps the animal survive, move, eat, or stay safe. The same kind
of job can be done by different parts in different animals: a duck
swims with webbed feet, a fish swims with fins, and a bird flies with
wings. The job — moving through water or air — is similar; the
structure is different.

- **Skin** covers an animal's body and protects what is inside. It is
  the animal's outer wrapping.
- **Fur** is soft hair that covers mammals like rabbits and dogs. It
  keeps them warm and protects their skin.
- **Feathers** cover birds. They keep birds warm, keep rain off, and —
  on the wings — let birds fly.
- **Scales** are small, flat, hard plates covering fish and reptiles.
  They protect the body and, on a fish, let it slip through water.
- **Beaks** are the hard mouthparts of birds. Their shape fits the
  food: a duck's flat beak scoops and strains food from water, while
  a seed-eating bird's short, strong beak cracks seeds.
- **Claws** are sharp, curved nails. They grip, scratch, dig, or catch
  food, depending on the animal.
- **Shells** are hard outer coverings, like a turtle's. A shell
  protects the soft body inside from predators and bumps.
- **Legs, wings, and fins** move the animal: legs for walking and
  running, wings for flying, fins for swimming. Webbed feet are legs
  with skin between the toes — built for paddling.

A structure's shape tells you about its job. That is the pattern to
look for in every photo today: *shape → job → survival.*

**Worked example 1 — reading a duck**

> Adult thinks aloud over the duck photo: *"Feathers cover the duck
> — they keep it warm and dry. Its beak is flat and wide — good for
> scooping food from shallow water. Its feet are webbed — skin
> between the toes, like paddles, for swimming. Three structures,
> three jobs: feathers protect, beak feeds, webbed feet move. If I
> swapped the duck's beak for an eagle's hooked beak, the duck would
> be worse at scooping — shape fits job."*

**Worked example 2 — the turtle's trade-off**

> Adult models: *"A turtle's shell is a hard covering. Its job is
> protection: when danger comes, the turtle pulls in and the shell
> takes the hit instead of the soft body. But a shell is heavy, so
> the turtle moves slowly. Every structure has a trade-off: what
> protects can also slow you down. The argument still holds — the
> shell supports survival, because a slow turtle that survives beats
> a fast turtle that gets eaten."*

Key idea to say plainly: **Skin, fur, feathers, scales, and shells
protect. Beaks and claws get food. Legs, wings, and fins move the
animal. Shape fits job — and the job helps the animal survive.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner answers and does)

1. Look at the rabbit photo. Name two outside structures you can see
   and give one job for each.
2. A fish has scales and fins. Which structure protects, and which
   one moves the fish? Explain how you can tell.
3. Two birds: one has a long, thin beak; the other has a short,
   strong beak. Which one is better at cracking seeds, and why does
   shape matter?
4. A turtle and a rabbit both need to escape a predator. The rabbit
   runs; the turtle pulls into its shell. Name the structure each
   animal relies on, and the job it does.
5. True or false, with a reason: "A duck's webbed feet are for
   flying." Correct the statement if it is false.

## Independent practice

1. Choose one animal from the photos. Draw it and label three outside
   structures.
2. Write one sentence per labeled structure giving its job.
3. A bird lives where it rains almost every day. Which covering —
   feathers or bare skin — protects it better, and why?
4. An animal has sharp claws and a hooked beak. What kind of food do
   you predict it eats? Give a reason based on shape.

## Applied task

With the adult, compare a duck and a turtle in a two-column table:
structure | duck | turtle. Fill in at least two rows: one for a covering
(feathers vs. shell) and one for movement (webbed feet vs. legs).
Then write one sentence answering: which animal is better protected,
and which is better at moving quickly — and what structure explains
each answer?

## Exit check

Tell the adult, without looking at your journal: name three outside
animal structures, give one job for each, and say whether each job
helps the animal survive, move, or eat. Three correct structure-job
pairs passes.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if naming from photos is hard, sort word cards into
  three piles — Protect, Eat, Move — and match each card to a photo
  before writing anything.
- **Extension:** compare beaks of three birds (duck, eagle,
  hummingbird) and write one sentence each explaining how beak shape
  fits food. Predict what would happen to each bird if its beak shape
  changed.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners often say every covering does the same
job ("fur keeps warm, so feathers must only keep warm too"). Feathers
also enable flight, and scales also streamline swimming — press for
the *specific* job in each animal, not the category. Do not teach
skeletons, muscles, or internal anatomy here; that is Lesson 03.
