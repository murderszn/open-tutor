# Lesson 03 — Inside Parts of an Animal and How They Work Together

**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** a simple outline drawing of
a dog (adult-drawn or printed, no anatomy detail beyond the five
structures); science journal; word cards.

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will name the inside parts
of an animal — heart, stomach, lungs, brain — plus skin, and say the
job each part does inside its system.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What happens to your breathing when
you run fast?" Any answer about breathing faster or harder shows
readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Some structures are **internal** — inside the body. You cannot see
them from outside, but their jobs keep the animal alive every minute.
The five structures in the standard's own list are heart, stomach,
lung, brain, and skin. Skin sits on the outside, but it belongs in
this lesson too: it is the covering that protects everything inside.

- **Heart** — pumps blood around the body. Blood carries what the
  body needs to every part, so the heart's job is delivery.
- **Stomach** — breaks down food. Food that is broken down can be
  used by the body for energy and growth.
- **Lungs** — take air into the body and let air out. Breathing in
  brings in the air the body needs; breathing out lets used air go.
- **Brain** — controls the body. It is the control center: it takes in
  information and directs what the body does next. (How the brain
  stores memories is not part of grade 4 — only that it controls.)
- **Skin** — covers and protects the body, keeping the inside in and
  much of the outside out.

These parts do not work alone. They work as a **system** — structures
working together for one larger job. Watch what happens when a dog
runs: its legs move, its heart beats faster to pump blood more
quickly, and its lungs breathe faster to take in more air. Heart and
lungs speed up *together* because the running body needs more of what
blood and air deliver. After eating, the stomach breaks food down so
the body can use it for growth and energy. The brain directs the whole
performance: it tells the legs to run and the body to rest afterward.

**Worked example 1 — the running dog as a system**

> Adult thinks aloud: *"The dog runs. Its heart pumps faster — more
> blood moving. Its lungs breathe faster — more air coming in. Why
> both at once? Because the muscles doing the running need more of
> what blood and air bring. If only the heart sped up but the lungs
> did not, the body would not get enough air. The claim is: heart and
> lungs work as a system. The evidence is: both speed up together
> when the body works hard. The job they share is keeping the running
> body supplied."*

**Worked example 2 — food's journey, in order**

> Adult models with the outline drawing: *"Food goes into the mouth
> and reaches the stomach. The stomach breaks it down. What is broken
> down can then be used by the body — for energy to move and play, and
> for growth. So the stomach's job is not 'holding food.' Its job is
> breaking food down so the body can use it. A learner who says the
> stomach 'stores food like a bag' has named a container, not a
> function. Ask: what changes about the food in the stomach? It gets
> broken down. That change is the function."*

Key idea to say plainly: **Heart pumps blood. Stomach breaks down
food. Lungs take air in and out. Brain controls the body. Skin covers
and protects. Together they work as systems that keep the animal
alive.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner answers and does)

1. Point to each structure on the outline drawing (heart, stomach,
   lungs, brain, skin) and say its job.
2. A dog has just finished running. What do its heart and lungs do,
   and why do they change together?
3. Which structure breaks down food, and why does breaking food down
   matter for growth?
4. A cut in the skin is covered with a bandage. Which job of the skin
   is the bandage helping with, and why does that job matter?
5. The brain is the control center. Give one example of something the
   brain directs during a game of tag.

## Independent practice

1. Draw a simple animal outline. Mark and label the heart, stomach,
   lungs, brain, and skin. (Position matters only roughly: heart in
   the chest, stomach in the belly, lungs in the chest, brain in the
   head, skin all around.)
2. Write one sentence per structure giving its job.
3. Explain in two sentences why the heart and lungs are a system, not
   two separate jobs. Use the running-dog example.
4. A friend says, "The stomach makes blood." Is that right? Correct
   the statement, naming which structure pumps blood and which breaks
   down food.

## Applied task

With the adult, act out (no running indoors if space is unsafe —
marching in place works) a "body at work" sequence: first at rest,
then working hard. After each stage, the learner reports: heart
(slower/faster), lungs (slower/faster), and one sentence about why
both changed together. Record the sequence in the journal as a
two-row table: Rest | Working hard.

## Exit check

Tell the adult, without looking at your journal: name the five
structures from this lesson and give one job for each. Five structures
with five correct jobs passes; four correct with one fix after a
prompt is a near pass — revisit Practice Set C.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if the five jobs blur together, use one-word job cards
  — pump, break down, breathe, control, protect — and match each card
  to a structure before writing sentences.
- **Extension:** explain what happens to the system if one structure
  cannot do its job (for example, lungs that cannot take in enough
  air), and why the other structures cannot fully cover for it. Keep
  the explanation at the system level — no disease or medical detail.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners often assign every job to the brain
("the brain pumps blood because it controls everything") or confuse
stomach and heart jobs. Control is not doing: the brain directs, the
heart pumps, the stomach breaks down. Re-run the job-card match
whenever jobs migrate to the wrong structure. Do not teach blood
vessels, intestines, or cell-level digestion; all are outside the
grade-4 boundary. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 03.
