# Lesson 05 — Structures Working Together, and Checking Your Journal

**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** the learner's
structure-function journal (Lessons 01–04 entries), science journal,
colored pencils.

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will explain how structures
work together as a system, find and fix errors in structure-function
claims, and checkpoint your journal before the project.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Name one plant structure and one
animal structure, and one job for each." Any correct pair shows
readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A single structure never does the whole job of living. A plant is a
system: roots take in water, the stem carries it up, the leaves use
sunlight and water to help make food, and the petals attract the
pollinators that help make seeds. Remove any one part and the system
weakens: no roots, no water; no stem, no delivery; no leaves, little
food-making; no petals, fewer pollinator visits. An animal is a
system too: skin protects, the stomach breaks down food, the heart
pumps blood, the lungs move air, and the brain directs — and the parts
adjust together, as the running dog showed in Lesson 03.

Because systems have many parts, errors creep in easily. The three
most common errors in this unit are:

1. **Wrong job:** "Roots make the food." (Food-making is helped by
   leaves catching sunlight, not by roots.)
2. **Wrong structure:** "The stomach pumps blood." (The heart pumps
   blood; the stomach breaks down food.)
3. **Claim without evidence:** "This is the best structure." (Best is
   an opinion; an argument needs observed evidence and a reason.)

Fixing an error is good science, not failure. A journal checkpoint
asks three questions of every entry: Is the structure named correctly?
Is the job the structure's real job? Is there evidence behind the
claim? Revise whatever fails, and note what changed — the revision
itself is evidence of learning.

**Worked example 1 — the plant as a system**

> Adult thinks aloud: *"A sunflower wilts on a hot day. Which
> structure do I check first? The roots — is there water in the soil
> for them to take in? Then the stem — is it unbroken, so water can
> travel up? Then the leaves — are they in sunlight? One wilted plant,
> three structures to check, because the plant is a system. If I only
> checked the flower, I would miss the real problem. Systems thinking
> means asking: which part of the chain broke?"*

**Worked example 2 — fixing a journal entry**

> Adult models with a sample entry: *"Old entry: 'The heart makes
> food because the body needs food.' Two errors: wrong job (the
> stomach breaks down food; the heart pumps blood) and no evidence.
> Revised entry: 'Claim: the heart pumps blood around the body.
> Evidence: when the dog ran, its heartbeat felt faster, and its
> breathing sped up too. Reason: a working body needs more blood
> moving, so the heart pumps faster.' Structure fixed, job fixed,
> evidence added. That is a checkpoint revision."*

Key idea to say plainly: **Structures work as systems. Check every
claim three ways: right structure, right job, real evidence. Revising
a claim with better evidence is good science.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner answers and does)

1. A plant has healthy leaves but dry soil. Which structure cannot do
   its job, and what breaks down in the system? Explain in two
   sentences.
2. Find the error: "Lungs break down food so the body can grow."
   Name the correct structure and its real job.
3. Find the error: "Feathers are the best covering." Rewrite it as a
   proper claim with evidence, or explain why it cannot stand as is.
4. Open your journal to your favorite entry. Check it three ways
   (structure, job, evidence) and report one thing that passes and
   one thing you would change.
5. Two learners argue: one says the heart is more important than the
   lungs; the other says the lungs are more important than the heart.
   Give the systems answer: why does "more important" miss the point?

## Independent practice

1. Draw a plant system diagram: roots, stem, leaves, flower, with an
   arrow showing what moves or happens at each step (water up,
   sunlight caught, pollinator visit). Label each structure and job.
2. Draw an animal system diagram: skin, heart, lungs, stomach, brain,
   with one job word beside each.
3. Write a paragraph (3–4 sentences) explaining why a plant with no
   roots cannot survive even if its leaves are in bright sunlight.
4. Revise one of your own journal entries: copy the old claim, name
   the error (if any), and write the revised claim with evidence.

## Applied task

Checkpoint your journal for the project: pick your strongest entry
and your weakest entry. For the strongest, add one more piece of
evidence or a second organism for comparison. For the weakest, rewrite
it fully (claim, evidence, reason). The adult initials both revisions
in the journal. These two entries become the models for your field
guide arguments.

## Exit check

Tell the adult, without looking at your journal: explain in two
sentences why structures must be understood as systems, and name the
three checks for a journal claim. Both parts correct passes.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if system diagrams are hard, build the chain with
  word cards in order (roots → stem → leaves → flower; skin → heart →
  lungs → stomach → brain as a web, not a chain) and say one job per
  card before drawing.
- **Extension:** write a "system failure" story: one structure stops
  working, and trace what happens to the rest of the system, step by
  step, for a plant and for an animal.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners often rank structures ("heart beats
lungs") instead of seeing interdependence. The running-dog example
from Lesson 03 is the cure: remove either partner and the system
fails. Also watch for journal entries that copy the lesson text
instead of recording observations — an entry counts as evidence only
when it reports something the learner saw, drew, or tested.
