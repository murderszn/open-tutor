# Lesson 05 — Putting It Together: Energy Stories and Fair Tests

**Goal (student-readable):** I can build an energy story that traces energy
from start to finish, and I can spot and fix an unfair test.
**Time:** about 35 minutes. **Standards:** 4-PS3-1, 4-PS3-2, 4-PS3-3.

**Materials:** the learner's notes and tables from Lessons 01–04, the posted
frame sentence and evidence rule cards, paper and pencils. No new equipment
— this is a synthesis and error-analysis lesson.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** "In one sentence each: what did the clay
dents prove? What did the drum and rice prove? What did the marbles prove?"
(1: faster = more energy; 2–3: energy moves by sound, light, heat, current;
4: in collisions energy moves from the hitter to the target and to the air
as sound.) "Today we connect all three into stories — and learn to catch a
bad test before it wastes our time."

## Teaching

An **energy story** traces one batch of energy from where it started to
everywhere it ended, naming the evidence at each step. Every story in this
unit uses the same skeleton:

> **Start → travel → arrival(s) → evidence at each arrival.**

Example — the full marble-collision story: "Energy started in my hand as I
lifted the marble (I did the lifting). It traveled down the ramp as the
marble sped up. At the collision it split three ways: into the target marble
(it started moving — evidence), into the air as sound (we heard the clack —
evidence), and a tiny bit into heat where they touched (we name it because
the sound proves energy left the marbles). Nothing vanished."

Now the misconception that ruins energy stories: **"the energy got used
up."** When the marbles stop rolling, or the drum stops ringing, it *looks*
like the energy is gone. But Lessons 02–04 showed it is not: it **moved and
changed** — into the air as sound, into the clay as a dent, into the paper
as warmth. In this unit's language: *energy moves from place to place; it
does not disappear.* (The adult notes: the full conservation law is beyond
grade 4 — we only establish "moved and changed, not vanished," which is all
4-PS3 asks.)

Second skill — **error analysis**: catching an unfair test *before* running
it. A test is unfair when it changes **more than one thing at a time**,
because then no one knows which change caused the result.

### Worked example 1 — An energy story, built step by step (modeled)

Nadia builds the story of Lesson 03's circuit:

1. **Start:** "Energy started in the battery cell." (Evidence: nothing
   glowed before the cell was connected.)
2. **Travel:** "It traveled as an electric current through the wires in a
   complete loop." (Evidence: breaking the loop stopped the light.)
3. **Arrival 1:** "It arrived at the bulb as light — the bulb glowed."
   (Evidence: glow only with a complete loop.)
4. **Arrival 2:** "Some arrived as heat — the bulb felt warm." (Evidence:
   touch, after the adult confirmed it was safe.)
5. **The anti-misconception check:** "The energy was not 'used up' by the
   bulb — I can point to where it went: light I saw, warmth I felt."

### Worked example 2 — Sam's unfair test (error analysis, modeled)

Sam wants to test whether a faster ball has more energy. His plan: "I will
roll a **big heavy ball** from the **top** of the ramp into clay, and a
**small light ball** from the **bottom** of the ramp into clay, and compare
the dents."

Walk through the fix:

1. **Spot the problem:** Sam changed **two** things — the ball (big/heavy
   vs. small/light) *and* the release height (top vs. bottom). If the dents
   differ, no one knows whether the ball or the speed caused it.
2. **Name the variable:** the question is about *speed*, so the variable
   must be release height only.
3. **Name a control:** the ball — use the **same** ball for both sets, just
   like Lesson 01.
4. **The fixed plan:** "Same ball, same ramp, same clay. Release from the
   top three times, then from halfway three times. Compare the dents."
5. **The rule to remember:** *one variable, everything else the same,
   three trials* — the Unit 01 routine, now applied to energy.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Story chain, learner-led:** the learner picks one Lesson 02 or 03
   station and builds the full energy story aloud using the skeleton
   (start → travel → arrivals → evidence), pointing at the posted frame
   card. The adult asks only: "And what is your evidence for *that* step?"
   until every step has evidence.
2. **Fix-the-test:** the adult reads a second flawed plan: "Mia wants to
   test whether louder drumming moves rice higher. She taps softly with her
   finger and hard with a spoon." The learner names the two changed things
   (loudness *and* the tapping tool) and rewrites the plan with one
   variable.

## Independent practice

1. Write a complete energy story for the Lesson 04 marble collision (start
   → travel → three arrivals → evidence for each). End with one sentence
   answering: "Was any energy 'used up'? How do you know?"
2. **Error analysis:** read Sam's plan again (above). Write: (a) the two
   things Sam changed, (b) the one variable his question actually needs,
   (c) one control he must keep the same, (d) how many trials and why.
3. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "A drum stops ringing, so its energy is
   gone." Agree or disagree — with evidence from Lesson 02.

## Applied task

Tell someone at home the full story of one station from this unit (clay
dents, drum + rice, lamp + paper, circuit, or marbles) using the skeleton:
start, travel, arrivals, evidence. Ask them: "What would you change to test
it fairly?" Write down their idea and whether it keeps one variable.

## Exit check

1. Build a two-arrival energy story for the drum-and-rice station (start,
   travel, two arrivals, one evidence each).
2. A test compares a fast big ball with a slow small ball. What is unfair
   about it, and what is the one-variable fix?

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** the story skeleton is posted as five numbered
  blanks; the learner may fill it as a list before writing sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** stories may be told aloud and recorded by the
  adult; the fix-the-test may be answered by circling the changed things on
  a printed plan.
- **Extension:** write a *prediction* for the Collision Challenge project
  question the learner will choose next session, in full predict-with-reason
  form; or build an energy story for something untested (a bouncing ball:
  where does the energy go on each bounce? — good project-question seed).
- **Likely misconception:** "used up" language is sticky because it matches
  everyday speech ("the battery is used up"). Distinguish carefully: the
  *battery* runs down (its stored energy moved elsewhere), but the *energy*
  moved — into light, heat, and sound. Never let "used up" stand unchallenged
  in an energy story.
- **Bridge to the project:** Session 12 launches the Collision Challenge —
  this lesson's error-analysis skill is exactly what the learner needs to
  write a fair project plan.
