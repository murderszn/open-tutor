# Lesson 04 — Collisions: Ask and Predict

**Goal (student-readable):** I can predict what happens to energy when
objects collide — with a reason — and test my prediction fairly.
**Time:** about 35 minutes. **Standard:** 4-PS3-3.

**Materials (adult gathers):** the ramp and one marble from Lesson 01; five
identical marbles for targets; a flat strip of cardboard or a ruler with a
groove as a "collision lane"; modeling clay to make small target stands;
tape to mark start lines. The adult sets up the ramp at table height and
keeps rolling marbles clear of stairs.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** "We know a faster ball has more energy
(deeper dent) and that energy travels by sound, light, heat, and current.
Today's question: **when two objects collide, where does the energy go?**
Before we test — make a guess: when a rolling marble hits a still marble,
what will each marble do?" Collect guesses without judging; every guess
becomes a prediction to test.

## Teaching

A **collision** is when two objects hit each other. The framework's question
for 4-PS3-3 is: *what happens to the energy?* In this unit the answer stays
qualitative and the emphasis stays on **the energy change, not on forces**
(we never talk about pushes in newtons — we talk about where the energy
went). The routine is **predict → observe → explain**:

1. **Predict** (in writing, with a reason — no peeking at the test first).
2. **Observe** (run the collision, three trials).
3. **Explain** (where did the energy go? Use evidence from what you saw
   *and heard*).

The core collision story: a rolling marble hits a still marble. Watch
closely and you will see three things happen to the energy:

- The **hitting marble slows down or stops** — it gave energy away, so it
  has less energy (and less speed) than before.
- The **still marble starts moving** — it received energy, so it now has
  more energy (and more speed) than before.
- You **hear a *clack*** — some energy traveled to the **air as sound**,
  exactly like the drum in Lesson 02. (A tiny bit also becomes heat where
  they touched, but we cannot feel it — we *name* it because the sound
  proves energy left the marbles.)

So the energy was not destroyed and did not vanish: it **moved** — from the
hitting marble, into the target marble, and out to the air as sound.

### Worked example 1 — Fast marble hits a still marble (modeled)

Sofia rolls a marble fast down the ramp into a still marble waiting in the
lane. *Before* the test she writes: "Prediction: the still marble will roll
away fast and the hitting marble will slow down, because the hitting marble
will give some of its energy to the still marble."

She runs three trials. Every time: the still marble shoots forward, the
hitting marble slows to a crawl or stops, and there is a *clack*.

- **Explain:** "My prediction was right. The hitting marble slowed down, so
  it lost energy. The still marble started moving, so it gained energy. We
  heard a clack, so some energy also moved to the air as sound — like the
  drum. The energy moved from the fast marble into the still marble and
  into the air."
- **The Lesson 01 connection:** "The fast marble had more energy to give
  (Lesson 01: faster = more energy), so the still marble rolled farther
  than it would have after a slow hit."

### Worked example 2 — Slow marble hits a still marble (modeled)

Marcus repeats Sofia's test but releases the hitting marble from **halfway**
down the ramp (slower). Prediction first: "The still marble will roll, but
not as far as in Sofia's test, because the slower marble has less energy to
give."

Three trials: the still marble rolls a short distance; the *clack* is
quieter; the hitting marble barely slows.

- **Explain:** "My prediction was right. The slower marble had less energy,
  so it gave less to the still marble (shorter roll) and less to the air
  (quieter clack). Same collision idea, smaller energy at the start —
  smaller changes everywhere, exactly the Lesson 01 rule again."
- **Asking the next question (4-PS3-3's "ask questions"):** "Marcus now
  asks: what would happen if *two* marbles rolled into the still marble at
  once? That question — written before any test — is the seed of the
  Collision Challenge project."

## Guided practice (with the adult)

1. **Predict-observe-explain, learner-led:** the learner sets a still marble
   in the lane, writes a prediction with a reason for a *medium-speed* hit
   (release from three-quarters down), runs three trials, and records for
   each trial: *hitting marble after* (kept rolling / slowed / stopped),
   *target marble* (did not move / moved a little / moved a lot), *sound*
   (silent / soft clack / loud clack).
2. Prompt: "Compare your three trials — did the same thing happen each
   time? Where did the energy go in *your* test? Point to each place." The
   learner traces: hitting marble → target marble → air (sound).

## Independent practice

1. Write a predict-observe-explain paragraph for a new test: a fast marble
   hits **two** still marbles lined up in a row. Predict first (with a
   reason), then — only after the adult approves the plan — test it and
   write what happened.
2. Draw the energy's trip in a collision as three labeled arrows: hitting
   marble → target marble; hitting marble → air (sound); and a short note
   saying which marble *lost* energy and which *gained* it.
3. **Explain-your-reasoning:** Ana says, "After the collision the energy is
   gone because both marbles eventually stop." Write three sentences
   explaining where the energy actually went — name the sound, and say what
   the sound proves.

## Applied task

With the adult, watch or set up one more collision: a playground ball
bouncing off a wall, a toy car hitting a block tower, a spoon tapping a pot.
Before it happens, write a prediction (what will move? what will you hear?).
Then observe and write where the energy went, using the three-part story:
*slowed/stopped giver → moved receiver → sound to the air.*

## Exit check

1. A fast marble hits a still marble. The still marble rolls away and you
   hear a loud clack. Name two places the fast marble's energy went, with
   the evidence for each.
2. Why do we write the prediction *before* running the collision, not after?

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** the predict-observe-explain steps are posted
  as 1-2-3; "collision" is defined with the marble demo itself, not just
  words.
- **Alternate response mode:** predictions and explanations may be dictated;
  the three-arrow drawing may replace the written paragraph.
- **Extension:** test Marcus's two-marble question as a fair test (number of
  hitting marbles is the variable; release height and target setup are
  controls); or test a head-on vs. glancing collision and describe how the
  energy split differs (qualitative only).
- **Likely misconception:** "the energy disappears when things stop." Every
  collision in this lesson produces a *clack* — train the learner to treat
  the sound as the proof that energy left the objects, exactly as the drum
  proved sound carries energy in Lesson 02.
- **Emphasis guardrail:** if the learner starts talking about "forces" or
  "push strength," redirect to the energy story: "What *lost* energy? What
  *gained* it? What did you hear?" — the framework's emphasis is the energy
  change, not the forces.
