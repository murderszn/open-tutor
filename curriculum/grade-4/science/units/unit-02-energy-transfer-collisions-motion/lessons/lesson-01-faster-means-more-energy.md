# Lesson 01 — Faster Means More Energy

**Goal (student-readable):** I can run a fair test that shows a faster-moving
object has more energy, and I can explain my claim with evidence.
**Time:** about 35 minutes. **Standard:** 4-PS3-1.

**Materials (adult gathers):** a smooth board about 60 cm long (a shelf board
or large hardcover book), a stack of books to make a ramp, two identical
marbles or bouncy balls, two same-size lumps of modeling clay (about the size
of a plum), a ruler, a shallow tray or baking pan to catch the ball, tape.
The adult builds the ramp at table height and checks it before the learner
touches it. Rolling objects stay clear of stairs.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** The adult asks, "What is energy?" Accept the
unit wording: *energy is what lets things move, make sound, give light, or
warm things up.* Then: "Where have you seen something fast hit something?"
(soccer ball, bowling ball, rain on a roof). Then the fair-test refresher:
"When we test fairly, what do we change?" (One thing — the variable.)
"What do we keep the same?" (Everything else — the controls.) "How many
times do we repeat?" (At least three trials.)

## Teaching

Today we answer one question: **does a faster-moving object have more
energy?** We cannot see energy, and in this unit we will never use speed
numbers. So how can we tell which object has more energy? We look at what
the moving object **does** — the change it makes.

Think of a ball rolling into soft clay. The ball's energy moves into the clay
and **changes the clay's shape** — it makes a dent. A deeper dent means a
bigger change, and a bigger change means the ball had **more energy**. That
is our evidence rule for this unit:

> **The bigger the change a moving object makes, the more energy it had.**

This is exactly the kind of evidence the science framework names for 4-PS3-1:
*change of shape on impact* shows the relationship between an object's speed
and its energy.

To test fairly, everything must be identical except the speed. We will roll
the **same ball** down the **same ramp** — but release it from two different
heights. Released from the top, the ball rolls **faster**; released from
halfway down, it rolls **slower**. The variable is the release height (which
changes the speed). The controls are the ball, the ramp, the clay lump, and
the ramp's steepness. The measurement is simple and non-numerical: which dent
is deeper?

### Worked example 1 — The clay-dent fair test (modeled)

Maya sets up her ramp exactly like ours. She flattens two clay lumps to the
same starting shape. She releases the **same ball** from the **top** of the
ramp; it rolls down and thumps into the first clay lump, leaving a deep dent.
She resets, then releases the **same ball** from **halfway** down the ramp;
it rolls more slowly into the second clay lump and leaves a shallow dent.

- **Claim:** The faster-moving ball (released from the top) has more energy.
- **Evidence:** The ball released from the top made a deeper dent in the
  clay than the same ball released from halfway.
- **Reasoning:** The ball's energy moved into the clay and changed the clay's
  shape. A deeper dent is a bigger change, so the faster ball had more
  energy to give. Because Maya used the same ball, the same ramp, and the
  same clay, the only difference was the speed — so the speed must explain
  the different dents.

Notice what Maya did *not* say: she did not say "the heavier ball" (same
ball), she did not give a speed number (we compare faster/slower), and she
did not claim this proves it for every object — it is evidence from this
test.

**Text-only alternative** (same information, no picture needed): *Imagine two
identical lumps of orange clay side by side. The left lump has a deep dent
with a red marble resting inside it. The right lump has a shallow dent with a
blue marble sitting beside it on the ground, outside the dent.*

Now reason it through, step by step:

1. **Observe:** The left dent is deeper; the right dent is shallower. Both
   clay lumps started the same.
2. **Recall the evidence rule:** the bigger the change, the more energy the
   moving object had.
3. **Conclude:** The **red marble** (left, deep dent) was moving **faster**
   and had **more energy**; the blue marble (right, shallow dent) was moving
   **slower** and had less energy.
4. **Check the alternative:** Could the red marble just be heavier? In a fair
   test we would use the *same* marble both times — that is why the lesson's
   real experiment uses one ball. The picture alone cannot prove the marbles
   are identical, which is exactly why scientists run fair tests instead of
   trusting a single picture.

## Guided practice (with the adult)

Run the real clay-dent test together, 3 trials per release height:

1. The adult sets and checks the ramp. The learner places a fresh clay lump
   (same size each time) at the ramp's foot inside the tray.
2. **Trial set 1 (fast):** release the ball from the **top** of the ramp,
   three times, reshaping the clay between trials. After each trial the
   learner records *deeper / shallower / same* compared with the other set.
3. **Trial set 2 (slow):** release the **same ball** from **halfway** down,
   three times. Record the same way.

Prompts the adult uses: "What are we changing on purpose?" (Release height
→ speed.) "What stays the same?" (Ball, ramp, clay, steepness.) "Why three
trials?" (One roll could be a lucky bounce; three shows a pattern.)

Recording table (learner copies and fills):

## Independent practice

1. Write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph for your test: *Claim* (which
   ball had more energy?), *Evidence* (what did the dents look like?),
   *Reasoning* (why do deeper dents mean more energy?).
2. Draw the two clay lumps *before* and *after* the fast-ball trials. Label
   which drawing shows the bigger change and write one sentence saying what
   the bigger change tells you.
3. **Explain-your-reasoning:** Jaden says, "The fast ball had more energy
   because it is heavier." What is wrong with Jaden's reasoning? (Hint: what
   did we keep the same on purpose?)

## Applied task

With the adult's supervision, find a fast/slow pair at home: for example,
drop the same soft ball (a rolled-up sock works) onto a pillow from knee
height and from shoulder height. Before you test, **predict** which drop will
make the deeper mark, and write your reason. Then test it twice per height
and check your prediction. Write one sentence: was your prediction right,
and what was your evidence?

## Exit check

1. A ball rolls slowly into clay and makes a shallow dent. The same ball
   rolls fast into fresh clay and makes a deep dent. Which ball had more
   energy, and what is your evidence?
2. Why did we use the *same* ball for both the fast and slow tests instead of
   a big ball for fast and a small ball for slow?

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** the evidence rule ("bigger change → more
  energy") is posted on a card the learner can point to; the adult reads the
  worked examples aloud on request.
- **Alternate response mode:** the claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph may be
  dictated to the adult, drawn as a three-panel comic (claim / evidence /
  reasoning), or built with the dent-comparison table plus one spoken
  sentence.
- **Extension:** predict what would happen if the ramp were *steeper* (faster
  ball → deeper dent) and test it as a second fair test with ramp steepness
  as the variable; or test whether a heavier ball at the *same* speed makes
  a deeper dent (introduces mass as a separate variable — keep it a second,
  separate test, never mixed into the first).
- **Likely misconception:** "faster" gets confused with "heavier" or
  "bigger." The lesson's same-ball control exists precisely to separate
  speed from size — return to it whenever the learner reaches for weight as
  the explanation.
