# Practice Sets — Unit 03

Short written work (8–12 tasks per set) that the adult reviews the same
day. Oral, drawn, or manipulative answers are always acceptable
alternatives; the adult records what the learner says. **No answers
appear in this file** — solutions are in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md)
(adult only).

## Set A — Waves are patterns (after Lesson 01)

1. In your own words, what is a wave?
2. A wave's crests are 5 finger-widths apart. Another wave's crests are
   2 finger-widths apart. Which wave has the longer wavelength?
3. Draw a wave and label: one crest, one trough, the amplitude, and
   one wavelength.
4. A cork floats in a tray. Ripples reach it. Does the cork sail to
   the far side? What does it actually do? What does that prove about
   waves?
5. Wave P is tall. Wave Q is low. Which has the bigger amplitude?
6. A friend draws a wave and labels the distance from a crest to the
   next trough as the wavelength. Is that right? What is the wavelength
   really?
7. Name two places you might see a wave pattern outside school.
8. True or false, with one reason: "A wave carries water across the
   whole tray."
9. Look at the wave diagram in
   [assets/wave-diagram.svg](assets/wave-diagram.svg). Which wave, A
   or B, has the taller amplitude? Which has the longer wavelength?
10. A rope wave is made with small shakes, then with big shakes. What
    changed — amplitude, wavelength, or both? Explain.

## Set B — Sound waves (after Lesson 02)

1. What starts every sound? Use the word *vibration* in your answer.
2. A drum is tapped gently, then struck hard. Which hit is louder, and
   why?
3. A rubber band is plucked loose, then stretched tight and plucked
   again (same band both times). What happens to the pitch? Is this a
   fair test? Why?
4. Sort each pair as "louder/softer" or "higher/lower": (a) whisper
   vs. shout; (b) a kitten's mew vs. a dog's deep bark; (c) a drum
   tapped gently vs. struck hard.
5. A learner says, "That drum is loud, so it must be high-pitched."
   Give one example that shows loud and high are different.
6. You hold a hand near a drum while it is hit. What do you feel, and
   what does that feeling tell you about how sound starts?
7. Two bands are compared: a thick loose band and a thin tight band.
   The thin tight band sounds higher. Can you conclude tightness
   caused it? Explain the fair-test problem.
8. A whistle is blown softly, then a drum is struck hard. Which sound
   is higher? Which is louder? (Answer both; they are different
   questions.)
9. Write one sentence explaining why sound can travel across a room
   even though the drum stays where it is.
10. A string telephone works when the string is tight but not when it
    hangs slack. Give one reason why tightness might matter for the
    pattern traveling.

## Set C — Light and seeing (after Lesson 03)

1. List the three links of the seeing chain, in order.
2. A lamp is switched off in a room. The chair is still there. Why can
   you no longer see it?
3. Draw arrows for: flashlight → mirror → wall. Does the light stop at
   the mirror? Explain.
4. Look into a sealed dark box through a peephole. What do you see?
   Open a flap that lets light in and look again. What changed, and
   what does it prove?
5. Which of these is a light source: a lamp, a mirror, the moon, a
   book? Give one reason for each choice.
6. A friend says, "Eyes shoot out light to find things." Use the
   dark-box test to explain why that is wrong.
7. A mirror is tilted and the bright spot moves from the wall to the
   ceiling. What does that show about reflected light?
8. Paper reflects light, but you cannot see a clear picture in paper
   the way you can in a mirror. Give one reason why the two surfaces
   behave differently.
9. Draw and label a seeing-chain model for: the sun, a tree, and a
   person looking at the tree.
10. In a dim room, which surface would make the best light-signal
    reflector — a mirror, white paper, or a dark cloth? Why?

## Set D — Codes and picture grids (after Lesson 04)

Use the agreed drum code from Lesson 04 (1 = come, 2 = stop, 3 = look,
4 = help) unless a question gives a different code.

1. The adult drums 3 beats. What message is that?
2. You need to send "help." How many beats do you drum?
3. Decode this grid. What letter is it?
   Row 1: 1 0 0 0 1; Row 2: 1 0 0 0 1; Row 3: 1 1 1 1 1; Row 4: 1 0 0 0 1.
4. Encode the letter E on a 4-row, 5-column grid of 1's and 0's.
5. A sender drums 2 beats but the receiver hears 3. What message was
   sent, what message was received, and what check would catch this
   error?
6. A grid message arrives with one number changed in row 1 (1 1 1 1 1
   becomes 1 1 0 1 1). What happens to the picture? Why does one
   changed number matter?
7. Explain, in your own words, why a code must be agreed before the
   message is sent.
8. Name one everyday pattern that carries information without words,
   and say what it means.
9. A team sends each message twice instead of once. Does that make
   delivery more or less reliable? Give one reason.
10. A friend says, "The paper carries the message." Correct the
    friend: what travels, and what stays put?

## Set E — Comparing solutions (after Lesson 05)

Practice data below are fictional — they are for judging practice,
not results you measured.

1. Solution P, 3 trials of 5 messages: 5, 4, 5 right. Solution Q:
   3, 5, 4 right. What is each total out of 15? Which is more accurate,
   and by how many messages?
2. A shouting solution gets 15 of 15 right but breaks the "no
   speaking" constraint. Does it win? Explain.
3. Write one criterion and one constraint for a message code that
   must work in a dark room.
4. Team A tests in a quiet room; Team B tests while music plays, and
   Team A wins. Name two things that changed besides the solution,
   and say why the comparison is unfair.
5. A team changes its code card halfway through testing. Why must all
   earlier trials be re-run?
6. A solution gets every message right but takes 3 minutes, against a
   criterion of "under 1 minute." Does it meet the criteria? What
   should the team do next?
7. Give one reason engineers compare more than one solution instead
   of building the first idea they have.
8. A solution fails trial 1 but passes trials 2 and 3. Should the
   failure be erased? Why does honest recording matter?
9. Add a new criterion — "the receiver can use it without seeing the
   sender" — to the drum-code vs. flashlight-code comparison. Does
   the winner change? Explain.
10. Write a one-sentence recommendation for the fictional table in
    Lesson 05 (drum 13 of 15, flashlight 11 of 15) that names the
    winner and the numbers behind it.
