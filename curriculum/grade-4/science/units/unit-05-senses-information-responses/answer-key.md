# Answer Key — Unit 05: Senses, Information Processing, and Responses

**Adult only.** Every lesson item, practice item, quiz item,
assessment task, and project criterion is solved or scored here.
Accept reasonable wording that states the same sense, kind of
information, step, or reasoning; the adult judges meaning, not exact
phrasing.

## Lesson 01 — Five senses, five kinds of information

**Guided practice.** 1. Eyes (sight), ears (hearing), nose (smell),
tongue (taste), skin (touch). 2. Ears; sound information — a bark. 3.
Eyes told the color (yellow); nose told the scent (sharp and fresh).
4. Skin (touch); no — ears cannot tell temperature, they hear sound.
5. Red bird → sight (eyes); song → hearing (ears); smell of rain →
smell (nose); sweet peach → taste (tongue); rough rock → touch (skin).
**Independent practice.** 1. Drawing check: five organs present and
labeled. 2. One correct kind of information per sense (eyes — light,
shape, color; ears — sound; nose — scents; tongue — flavors; skin —
touch, texture, temperature). 3. Not right: ears cannot taste. The
tongue tastes the salt; the ears only hear sound. 4. Accept any
correct pair, e.g., eyes tell color (a red bird) and ears tell sound
(a song).
**Applied task.** Check one journal line per sense (see, hear, smell,
feel); taste is not tested on the walk.
**Exit check.** Five senses with five correct kinds of information:
eyes — light/what things look like; ears — sound; nose — scents;
tongue — flavors; skin — touch.

## Lesson 02 — Fair observations with your senses

**Guided practice.** 1. The sweeter jar named; the nose gave that
information. 2. So the test measures smell only — otherwise the eyes
could answer from sight. 3. Smooth: glass marble, silk ribbon,
polished stone, plastic spoon. Rough: sandpaper square, tree bark,
brick piece, wool cloth. 4. 8 correct; trials 4 and 9 were wrong. 5.
One correct guess could be luck; one try does not prove the ears are
perfect.
**Independent practice.** 1. The learner's own result — check the
count is honest and the two hardest jars are named with a reason. 2.
Texture table: all eight objects sorted as in Guided practice 3. 3.
(a) 8 out of 10. (b) Shows: ears can usually tell which direction a
sound came from. Does not show: that ears are perfect, or that the
result holds for everyone. 4. Not fair: with eyes open, the learner
could see the jars and guess from sight, so the test would measure
sight, not smell.
**Applied task.** The learner's own 10-trial table — check every trial
is recorded, the match count is correct, and the one-sentence reading
is honest (e.g., "I got 7 of 10 right, so my ears usually tell
direction but not always").
**Exit check.** Two fairness features: one sense at a time; keep the
rest the same; repeat tries (any two).

## Lesson 03 — The path: sense → brain → response

**Guided practice.** 1. Senses pick up information; brain works out
what it means; body responds. 2. Eyes see the ball coming → brain
works out where it will be → hands catch it. 3. Ears; sound (the
doorbell); the brain works out someone is at the door and I should
answer; response: walk to the door and open it. 4. Order: a sense
picks up information → the brain works out what it means → the body
responds. 5. Wrong: ears do not decide. Fixed: "My ears heard the dog
bark, my brain worked out it might be danger, and then I ran."
**Independent practice.** 1. Drawing check: three boxes labeled with
their jobs, arrows in order. 2. Nose; scent of dinner cooking; the
brain works out food is near and it is time to eat; response: walk to
the kitchen. 3. (a) Ears. (b) Sound — the can opener. (c) The brain
worked out the sound means food. (d) The dog runs to the kitchen. 4.
Missing: the brain step. The model jumps from the ears to the treat;
add that the brain works out the whistle means a treat is coming.
**Applied task.** Check each acted path is traced in order: (a) ears
hear a name → brain works out someone is calling me → turn around /
answer; (b) eyes see a red light → brain works out stop → stop; (c)
skin feels rain → brain works out I will get wet → go inside.
**Exit check.** Hear a name: ears hear it → brain works out someone
is calling me → turn around and answer.

## Lesson 04 — Different animals, different responses

**Guided practice.** 1. Ears; sound (a twig snapping); the deer is
freezing. 2. Flee; fleeing puts distance between the rabbit and
possible danger. 3. Investigate; no, it is different from the
rabbit's flee. 4. Call out; the call may warn other birds. 5. Deer
stands still → freeze; mouse darts into its burrow → hide; dog walks
toward a new smell → investigate.
**Independent practice.** 1. Drawing check: three steps labeled in
order (ears hear the twig snap → brain works out possible danger →
deer freezes). 2. (a) 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 8 responses. (b) Deer freeze (3).
(c) Accept: freezing happened more than any other single response in
those five minutes. 3. Two sentences: freezing hides the deer in the
grass so danger may not notice it; fleeing puts distance between the
rabbit and danger — both help survival in different ways. 4. Accept
any correct example with a reason, e.g., the deer: running would make
it easier to see in the grass, so freezing is the safer choice.
**Applied task.** Check the tally is complete (or the fictional tally
is used and labeled as such) and both sentences are present: which
response was seen most, and why it might help that animal.
**Exit check.** Three responses with three matching animals, e.g.,
freeze — deer; flee — rabbit; investigate — squirrel.

## Lesson 05 — Building your complete model

**Guided practice.** 1. Animal + signal: dog, smell of dinner. Sense:
nose, scent. Brain: works out food is near. Response: walks to the
kitchen and waits. 2. Missing: the brain step — what the whistle
means. The fix adds the brain working out that the whistle means a
treat is coming. 3. Cat: ears hear the bird (sound); the brain works
out a bird is nearby; response: creeps closer / crouches and watches
(investigate) — accept freeze with a reason. 4. Not complete — the
brain step is missing. Corrected: "Rabbit sees a fox → brain works
out the fox may be danger → rabbit runs." 5. Evidence named: the
learner's own observation, or an adult-chosen video — either is
acceptable if stated.
**Independent practice.** 1. Animal + signal chosen and written. 2.
Four lines present: sense + kind of information | brain step |
response | survival sentence. 3. Missing: the brain step. Corrected:
"Fish smells food in the water → brain works out food is near → fish
swims toward the food." 4. Two sentences: a model is a tool for
thinking; new evidence may show a link is wrong or missing, so
revising makes the model match what really happens.
**Applied task.** Project plan draft complete with all links (animal,
signal, sense, kind of information, brain step, response, survival
sentence, evidence).
**Exit check.** Four parts: animal + signal; sense + kind of
information; brain step; response.

## Practice Set A — Five senses and kinds of information

1. (a) eyes — light, what things look like; (b) ears — sound; (c)
   nose — scents in the air; (d) tongue — flavors in food; (e) skin
   — touch: shape, texture, temperature.
2. (a) Eyes (sight). (b) Ears (hearing). (c) Tongue (taste). (d) Skin
   (touch). (e) Nose (smell).
3. (a) False — the nose smells scents; it cannot tell colors. The
   eyes tell color. (b) True — skin feels temperature.
4. Eyes: yellow and round (color and shape). Nose: sharp and fresh
   (scent).
5. Sight — eyes; hearing — ears; smell — nose; taste — tongue; touch
   — skin.
6. Two sentences: the tongue tastes flavors and the nose smells
   scents — different organs picking up different kinds of
   information. They work together when eating (a blocked nose makes
   food taste plain), but they are still two different senses.
7. Wrong: ears cannot taste. Corrected: "I tasted that the soup was
   salty" — the tongue tastes salt.

## Practice Set B — Fair observations

1. (a) 8 correct. (b) Trials 4 and 9 were wrong (the learner pointed
   right when the sound came from the left, twice).
2. (a) 8 out of 10. (b) Shows: ears can usually tell which direction
   a sound came from. Does not show: that ears are perfect, or that
   the result holds for everyone.
3. (a) Cinnamon and coffee are both brown, warm, spicy smells, so
   they are easy to confuse by scent alone. (b) Repeat the test with
   eyes closed, one jar at a time, and see whether the same mistake
   happens again.
4. Smooth: glass marble, silk ribbon, polished stone, plastic spoon.
   Rough: sandpaper square, tree bark, brick piece, wool cloth.
5. Change: one finger instead of the whole hand. Keep the same (any
   two): the objects, eyes closed, the number of tries, the quiet
   room.
6. False — one try could be luck; a fair conclusion needs repeated
   trials.
7. Not fair: with eyes open, the learner could see the jars and guess
   from sight, so the test would measure sight, not smell.

## Practice Set C — The sense → brain → response path

1. Order: eyes see the ball coming → the brain works out where the
   ball will be → hands catch the ball.
2. Ears; sound (the phone ringing); the brain works out someone is
   calling and I should answer; response: answer the phone.
3. The brain works out the rain is cold and I will get wet and cold,
   so I should go inside. (Accept any correct working-out.)
4. Missing: the brain step. The model jumps from smelling to eating;
   the brain must work out that the smell means food is near.
5. (a) Ears. (b) Sound — the can opener. (c) The brain worked out
   the sound means food. (d) The dog runs to the kitchen.
6. Two sentences: a sense only reports information; the brain is the
   part that works out what it means and decides what to do — so it
   sits between the sense and the response in every model.

## Practice Set D — Different responses and complete models

1. (a) 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 8 responses. (b) Deer freeze (3). (c) Accept:
   freezing happened more than any other single response in those
   five minutes.
2. (a) Flee (the rabbit) puts the most distance between the animal
   and the sound. (b) Freeze (the deer) keeps the animal hardest to
   see.
3. Two sentences: freezing hides the deer in the grass so danger may
   not notice it; fleeing puts distance between the rabbit and
   danger — both help survival in different ways.
4. Corrected: "Rabbit sees a fox → brain works out the fox may be
   danger → rabbit runs." (Accept equivalent wording.)
5. Four parts present and consistent, e.g., animal cat + signal bird
   call; sense ears + sound; brain works out a bird is nearby;
   response: creeps closer to investigate.
6. Missing: the brain step. Corrected: "A fish smells food in the
   water → its brain works out food is near → the fish swims toward
   the food."
7. Two sentences: new evidence may show a link is wrong or missing;
   revising makes the model match what really happens.
