# Lesson 05 — Building Your Complete Model

**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** the
[sense → brain → response diagram](../assets/sense-brain-response-diagram.svg),
science journal, the project plan sheet (see
[project.md](../project.md)), colored pencils.

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will build a complete
sense-to-response model for one animal and one signal — and check
every link in it against evidence.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Name the three steps of the
information path, and name three different responses." Three steps
in order plus one response shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **model** is a drawing, a table, or an acted-out version that shows
how something works, step by step. You have been using models all
unit: the three-box diagram, the tables, the acted-out paths. Today
you build your own complete model. A complete model must show four
things:

1. **The animal and the signal** — who, and what information arrived
   (a sound, a scent, a light, a touch, a flavor).
2. **The sense and the kind of information** — which sense picked it
   up, and what kind it was.
3. **The brain step** — what the brain worked out, in plain words.
4. **The response** — what the body did, and one sentence about how
   it helps the animal survive.

Then you check the model against **evidence**: what did you actually
observe (your sound-direction test, your scent match, your texture
sort), or what did you see in an adult-chosen video or photo? If the
evidence does not fit, you revise the model. Scientists do this all
the time — a model is a tool for thinking, not a finished picture.
When new evidence appears, a good model changes.

**Worked example 1 — a complete model: a dog smells dinner**

> Adult thinks aloud, building the model on paper: *"Animal: a dog.
> Signal: the smell of dinner cooking. Sense: the nose — smell
> information, a scent in the air. Brain: the brain works out that
> the smell means food is near, and that food means dinner time.
> Response: the dog walks to the kitchen and waits by the door.
> Evidence: I have watched a dog do exactly this — nose up, walking
> toward the kitchen, waiting. Four parts, all present, in order.
> Now the survival sentence: smelling food and walking toward it
> helps the dog find food, and finding food helps it survive."*

**Worked example 2 — finding and fixing a broken model**

> Adult models error analysis: *"Here is a model a learner wrote:
> 'Ears hear a whistle → dog gets a treat.' Read it again. What is
> missing? The brain step. The ears heard the whistle, and then —
> somehow — the dog gets a treat. But how did the dog know the
> whistle meant a treat? The brain is missing. Fixed model: 'Ears
> hear a whistle → brain works out the whistle means a treat is
> coming (the dog learned this) → dog runs to its owner.' Now every
> link is there: sense, brain, response. A model with a missing link
> is not wrong about everything — it is incomplete, and we fix it by
> adding the missing step, not by starting over."*

Key idea to say plainly: **A complete model shows the animal, the
signal, the sense, the brain step, and the response — and the evidence
that each link is real. If the evidence does not fit, revise the
model.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner answers and does)

1. Read the dog model in Worked example 1. Point to each of the four
   parts: animal + signal, sense, brain step, response.
2. In Worked example 2, what was missing from the whistle model, and
   what did the fix add?
3. Build a model for a cat hearing a bird: name the sense, the kind
   of information, the brain step, and one likely response.
4. A model says: "Rabbit sees a fox → rabbit runs." Is it complete?
   If not, name the missing step and add it.
5. Where does the evidence for your cat model come from — something
   you observed, or something from an adult-chosen video? Say which,
   in one sentence.

## Independent practice

1. Choose an animal and a signal for your project model (you will
   build it properly in the project). Write them down: animal | signal.
2. Write your model's four parts as four short lines: sense + kind of
   information | brain step | response | how it helps survival.
3. A learner's model says: "Fish smells food in the water → fish
   swims toward the food." Fix it: add the missing brain step in one
   sentence, then write the whole corrected path.
4. Explain in two sentences why a scientist would revise a model
   after seeing new evidence. (Hint: a model is a tool for thinking,
   not a finished picture.)

## Applied task

With the adult, draft your project model on the project plan sheet:
animal, signal, sense, kind of information, brain step, response,
survival sentence, and the evidence you will use (your observation
log, or an adult-chosen video). The adult checks that every link is
present before you build the poster in Session 13.

## Exit check

Tell the adult, without looking at your notes: name the four things
a complete model must show. Four parts named passes.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if writing four parts is hard, build the model with
  the three index cards first (SENSE, BRAIN, RESPONSE), then add the
  animal, the signal, and the survival sentence one at a time.
- **Extension:** build a second model for the *same* animal and
  signal, but with a different response — and explain in two
  sentences when each response would be the better choice.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "a model is a drawing" — correct it: a model
can be a drawing, a table, or acted out; what matters is that it
shows the steps in order and can be checked against evidence.
Another: "once I write a model, it is finished" — the revision habit
is the point of this lesson; praise a learner who changes a model
when the evidence says so. Watch for models that skip the brain step
— the most common gap — and send the learner back to Lesson 03's
ball-catch trace. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 05.
