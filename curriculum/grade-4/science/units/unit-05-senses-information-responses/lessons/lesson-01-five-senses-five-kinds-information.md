# Lesson 01 — Five Senses, Five Kinds of Information

**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** word cards (eye, ear, nose,
tongue, skin), science journal, pencils. The adult leads a short
sense walk at the end (see Applied task).

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will name the five senses
and say what kind of information each one picks up — what eyes, ears,
nose, tongue, and skin are each good at noticing.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Name a way you find out about the
world around you." Any answer naming seeing, hearing, smelling,
tasting, or touching shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

You find out about the world through your **senses**. A sense is one
of the five ways an animal picks up **information** — what is
happening around it. The five senses are sight, hearing, smell, taste,
and touch, and each one has its own organ and its own kind of
information:

| Sense | Organ | Kind of information it picks up |
|---|---|---|
| Sight | eyes | light — what things look like: shape, color, size, movement |
| Hearing | ears | sound — noises, voices, how loud, which direction |
| Smell | nose | scents in the air |
| Taste | tongue | flavors in food: sweet, sour, salty, bitter |
| Touch | skin | what things feel like: shape, texture, temperature |

Here is the big idea of this whole unit: **each sense is specialized
— built to do one kind of job well, and poor at the others.** Your
eyes are wonderful at shape and color and useless at smell. Your nose
is wonderful at scents and cannot tell you a color. Your ears can
tell you a sound came from the left, but they cannot tell you what
color the thing is that made it. The senses do not do each other's
jobs.

Your tongue tastes flavors, and your nose helps more than you might
think: when you have a cold and your nose is blocked, food tastes
plain, because the smell part of flavor is missing. Taste and smell
work together, but they are still two different senses — the tongue
tastes, the nose smells.

The brain is not a sense. The brain is the part that takes the
information the senses pick up and works out what it means and what
to do. That is Lesson 03. For today, keep the senses straight: five
senses, five kinds of information.

**Worked example 1 — a thunderstorm gives two kinds of information**

> Adult thinks aloud: *"A storm is coming. My eyes see a flash of
> lightning — that is light information: a bright zigzag shape in the
> sky. A moment later my ears hear thunder — that is sound
> information: a low rumble. The same storm gives my eyes one kind of
> information and my ears a different kind. My eyes cannot hear the
> thunder, and my ears cannot see the lightning. Two senses, two kinds
> of information, one storm."*

**Worked example 2 — cookies baking**

> Adult models with an everyday scene: *"Cookies are baking. Before I
> can see them, my nose smells them — a sweet scent drifting from the
> kitchen. That is smell information. When I walk in, my eyes see the
> cookies — round, golden brown. That is sight information: shape and
> color. My nose told me something was baking; my eyes told me what
> the cookies look like. If I asked my nose what color the cookies
> are, it could not answer. If I asked my eyes what the cookies smell
> like, they could not answer either. Each sense reports only its own
> kind of information."*

Key idea to say plainly: **Eyes see light. Ears hear sound. Nose
smells scents. Tongue tastes flavors. Skin feels touch. Each sense is
good at its own kind of information and poor at the others.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner answers and does)

1. Point to each sense organ on yourself — eyes, ears, nose, tongue,
   skin — and name the sense that goes with it.
2. A dog barks outside. Which sense picks that up, and what kind of
   information is it?
3. You hold a lemon. Your eyes see it is yellow and round. Your nose
   smells it is sharp and fresh. Which sense told you the color, and
   which told you the scent?
4. Which sense could tell you whether bath water is hot or cold?
   Could your ears tell you that? Explain in one sentence.
5. Sort these into the sense that picks them up: a red bird, a song,
   the smell of rain, a sweet peach, a rough rock.

## Independent practice

1. Draw five small pictures: an eye, an ear, a nose, a tongue, and a
   hand (skin). Label each with its sense.
2. Beside each picture, write one kind of information that sense
   picks up.
3. A friend says, "I can hear that the soup is salty." Is that
   right? Explain which sense actually tastes salt, and what the ears
   can and cannot do.
4. Name one thing your eyes can tell you that your ears cannot, and
   one thing your ears can tell you that your eyes cannot.

## Applied task

With the adult, take a short sense walk around the home or yard
(adult chooses a safe route). At each stop, name one thing for each
sense: one thing you see, one you hear, one you smell, one you feel.
Write one line in your journal for each sense. (Taste is not tested
on the walk: nothing is tasted outside, and nothing unknown is ever
tasted. Taste is discussed from familiar foods only.)

## Exit check

Tell the adult, without looking at your journal: name the five senses
and give one kind of information for each. Five senses with five
correct kinds of information passes.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if writing labels is hard, place word cards ("eyes,"
  "ears," "nose," "tongue," "skin") beside the matching body part
  instead of writing, and answer the exit check orally.
- **Extension (optional home taste test, adult-led):** the adult
  chooses two familiar, safe foods — for example, a lemon wedge and a
  cracker with honey. The learner tastes each with eyes closed and
  names the flavor (sour, sweet, salty). The adult checks allergies
  first, chooses only foods the learner has eaten before, and never
  offers anything unknown. Discuss: did closing your eyes change
  anything about the taste? (Sight does not do taste's job.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners often treat the brain as a sixth sense
("my brain senses danger"). Correct now and again in Lesson 03: the
brain is not a sense — it is the part that works out what the senses'
information means. Another common mix-up: taste and smell — "I taste
the smell of cookies" — correct with the blocked-nose example: the
nose smells, the tongue tastes, and they help each other without
being the same sense. Do not teach how receptors work inside, or how
the brain stores memories; both are outside the grade-4 boundary.
