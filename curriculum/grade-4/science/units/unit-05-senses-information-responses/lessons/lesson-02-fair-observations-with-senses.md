# Lesson 02 — Fair Observations With Your Senses

**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** six adult-prepared scent
jars with lids (lemon peel, cinnamon, mint leaves, vanilla extract on
cotton, ground coffee, orange peel — allergy-checked), eight texture
objects (glass marble, silk or satin ribbon, polished stone, plastic
spoon, sandpaper square, tree bark, brick piece, wool cloth),
blindfold or sleep mask, science journal. **Adult prepares the jars
and checks allergies before the lesson. Nothing is tasted.**

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will make fair observations
with your senses — testing one sense at a time, repeating your tries,
and writing down what you actually found.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Which sense tells you a lemon's
scent, and which sense tells you its color?" Naming nose for scent and
eyes for color shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Yesterday you learned that each sense picks up its own kind of
information. Today you will test your senses — and a test is only
useful if it is **fair**. A fair test changes one thing at a time,
keeps the rest the same, and repeats tries so one lucky guess does
not fool you. You already know this from Unit 01; here it is with
senses:

- **One sense at a time.** When you test smell, close your eyes.
  Otherwise your eyes see the lemon peel and guess the answer before
  your nose has done any work — and you would not know whether your
  nose or your eyes found it.
- **Keep the rest the same.** Same jars, same sniff time, same quiet
  room, same learner. If you change two things at once, you cannot
  tell which one made the difference.
- **Repeat your tries.** One correct guess could be luck. Ten tries
  show a pattern.

You will run three small fair observations today: scent matching,
texture sorting, and a sound-direction test. The scent jars are mild
and adult-prepared. You sniff; you never taste anything in class.

**Worked example 1 — why eyes closed matters for the scent test**

> Adult thinks aloud, holding up a jar: *"This jar holds lemon peel.
> If your eyes are open, you see yellow peel through the glass and
> you say 'lemon' — but your eyes answered, not your nose. Now close
> your eyes. Sniff. You smell something sharp and fresh. Now your
> nose is doing the work, and the answer belongs to your nose. Closing
> your eyes does not make your nose better — it stops your eyes from
> helping, so the test is fair."*

**Worked example 2 — reading a practice dataset: the sound-direction test**

> Adult models with a fictional practice dataset (labeled as made-up
> practice data, not the learner's own results): *"A learner did the
> sound-direction test: eyes closed, the adult clapped once from the
> left or the right, and the learner pointed where the sound came
> from. Ten trials. The sound came from: left, right, right, left,
> right, left, left, right, left, right. The learner pointed: left,
> right, right, right, right, left, left, right, right, right. Count
> the matches: trials 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 10 match — that is 8
> correct out of 10. Trials 4 and 9 do not match. What does 8 out of
> 10 show? That ears can usually tell which direction a sound came
> from. What does it not show? That ears are perfect — two misses
> say they are not. And one test with one learner does not prove it
> for everyone. That is how you read data honestly: say what it
> shows, and say what it does not show."*

Key idea to say plainly: **A fair sense test uses one sense at a
time, keeps everything else the same, and repeats tries. Then you
report what the results show — and what they do not show.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner answers and does)

1. The adult holds up two scent jars with your eyes closed. Sniff
   each. Which smells sweeter, and which sense gave you that
   information?
2. Why must your eyes be closed during the scent test? Answer in one
   sentence.
3. Sort the eight texture objects into smooth and rough by touch
   alone (eyes closed or looking away). Name two objects in each
   group.
4. In the fictional sound-direction data above, how many trials were
   correct, and which two trials were wrong?
5. A learner does the sound-direction test once, guesses right, and
   says, "My ears are perfect." What is wrong with that conclusion?

## Independent practice

1. Match the scent to its jar (eyes closed, adult hands you the jars
   one at a time): lemon, cinnamon, mint, vanilla, coffee, orange.
   Write how many of the six you matched. Which two were hardest to
   tell apart, and why do you think so?
2. Complete the texture table in your journal: object | smooth or
   rough | how you could tell by touch. Use all eight objects.
3. Look at the fictional sound-direction data again. (a) What fraction
   of the trials were correct? (b) Write one sentence about what the
   data show, and one sentence about what they do not show.
4. A friend tests smell with eyes open and gets all six scents right.
   Is that a fair test of smell? Explain why or why not in two
   sentences.

## Applied task

With the adult, run the real sound-direction test: eyes closed, the
adult claps once from the left or the right, you point. Do 10 trials
and record each one in a table: trial | side of the sound | your
point | match? Then count your matches. Write one sentence about what
your own results show. Keep the sound comfortable — a soft clap, not
a loud bang.

## Exit check

Tell the adult, without looking at your journal: name two things that
make a sense observation fair. (Accept: one sense at a time; keep the
rest the same; repeat tries.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** if six scents are too many, test three jars (lemon,
  mint, vanilla) and build up. Texture objects may be sorted with
  eyes open first, then re-sorted by touch alone.
- **Extension:** design a fair test for one more question — "Can I
  tell two textures apart with one finger instead of my whole hand?"
  Write what you would change (finger vs. hand) and what you would
  keep the same (objects, eyes closed, number of tries).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "more trials made my ears better" — correct it:
more trials do not sharpen a sense; they make the *conclusion*
stronger. Another: "I guessed right once, so I proved it" — one try
is luck, not evidence; that is why the lesson repeats ten times.
Watch for learners who peek during the scent test; treat it as a
fairness question, not a rule break: "Whose answer was that — your
nose's or your eyes'?" Do not teach how receptors work inside; the
boundary is the system, not the mechanism. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 02.
