# Grade 4 Mathematics

Independent, grade-specific materials in the public OpenTutor curriculum library.
This track is a **draft in progress**: the audit and scope-and-sequence are delivered;
the eight units are planned, not yet written. See [scope-and-sequence.md](scope-and-sequence.md).

**Do not refer a learner to another grade track.** This folder is self-contained for
one grade-4 learner at a time; a guiding adult adapts pacing, materials, and
supports. Keep completed learner work, names, photos, schedules, and grades in
private storage — never in this public repository.

See also: [Grade 4 track](../README.md), [curriculum index](../../README.md).

## What this track covers

A full-year (about 36 weeks) grade-4 math course organized around place value to
1,000,000 with rounding to any place, fluent multi-digit addition and subtraction
with the standard algorithm, multiplicative comparison and multistep word problems,
factors and multiples (prime/composite), multi-digit multiplication (up to
four-digit by one-digit, two two-digit numbers) and division with remainders,
fraction equivalence and comparison, fraction addition/subtraction with like
denominators and multiplication of fractions by whole numbers, decimals to
hundredths, measurement conversions and word problems, area and perimeter of
rectangles, line plots with fractional data, angle concepts with protractors, and
lines, angles, shapes, and symmetry. Sessions are about 35 minutes, four per week,
blending concrete models (base-ten blocks, area models, fraction strips and
circles, protractors, rulers, measuring tools) with independent written practice
that the adult reviews the same day.

## Track objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the year the learner will be able to:

1. Generalize place value: recognize that in a multi-digit whole number a digit
   in one place represents ten times what it represents in the place to its
   right; read, write, and compare whole numbers to 1,000,000 using base-ten
   numerals, number names, and expanded form.
2. Round multi-digit whole numbers to any place.
3. Fluently add and subtract multi-digit whole numbers using the standard
   algorithm.
4. Interpret a multiplication equation as a comparison (for example, 35 = 5 × 7
   as 35 being 5 times as many as 7); represent verbal multiplicative
   comparisons as multiplication equations; solve multiplicative-comparison word
   problems, distinguishing multiplicative comparison from additive comparison.
5. Solve multistep word problems with whole-number answers using the four
   operations, including problems where remainders must be interpreted; represent
   them with equations using a letter for the unknown; assess reasonableness
   with mental computation and estimation strategies including rounding.
6. Find all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1–100; recognize that
   a whole number is a multiple of each of its factors; determine whether a
   given whole number in 1–100 is prime or composite.
7. Generate a number or shape pattern that follows a given rule; identify
   apparent features of the pattern not explicit in the rule itself.
8. Multiply a whole number of up to four digits by a one-digit whole number,
   and multiply two two-digit numbers, using strategies based on place value
   and properties of operations; illustrate and explain with equations,
   rectangular arrays, and/or area models.
9. Find whole-number quotients and remainders with up to four-digit dividends
   and one-digit divisors using strategies based on place value, properties of
   operations, and/or the relationship between multiplication and division;
   illustrate and explain with equations, rectangular arrays, and/or area models.
10. Explain why a fraction a/b is equivalent to (n × a)/(n × b) using visual
    fraction models, attending to how the number and size of the parts differ
    even though the fractions are the same size; use this principle to recognize
    and generate equivalent fractions.
11. Compare two fractions with different numerators and different denominators
    (common denominators or numerators, benchmark fractions such as 1/2),
    recognizing comparisons are valid only for the same whole; record with
    `>`, `=`, `<` and justify with a visual fraction model.
12. Understand fraction addition and subtraction as joining and separating parts
    of the same whole; decompose a fraction into a sum of same-denominator
    fractions in more than one way; add and subtract mixed numbers with like
    denominators; solve word problems involving addition and subtraction of
    fractions with like denominators.
13. Multiply a fraction by a whole number: understand a/b as a multiple of 1/b
    (for example, 5/4 = 5 × (1/4)); multiply a fraction by a whole number
    (n × (a/b) = (n × a)/b); solve word problems involving fraction-by-whole-number
    multiplication.
14. Express a fraction with denominator 10 as an equivalent fraction with
    denominator 100 and add fractions with denominators 10 and 100; use decimal
    notation for fractions with denominators 10 or 100; locate decimals on a
    number line; compare two decimals to hundredths by reasoning about their size.
15. Know relative sizes of measurement units within one system (km, m, cm; kg,
    g; lb, oz.; l, ml; hr, min, sec); express measurements in a larger unit in
    terms of a smaller unit and record equivalents in a two-column table.
16. Use the four operations to solve word problems involving distances, time
    intervals, liquid volumes, masses, and money, including problems with simple
    fractions or decimals; represent measurement quantities with diagrams such
    as number-line scales.
17. Apply the area and perimeter formulas for rectangles in real-world and
    mathematical problems, including finding an unknown side length by viewing
    the area formula as a multiplication equation with an unknown factor.
18. Make a line plot to display a data set of measurements in fractions of a
    unit (1/2, 1/4, 1/8); solve addition and subtraction problems using
    information presented in line plots.
19. Recognize angles as geometric shapes formed wherever two rays share a common
    endpoint; understand that an angle turning through 1/360 of a circle is a
    one-degree angle; measure angles in whole-number degrees with a protractor;
    recognize angle measure as additive and solve for unknown angles on diagrams.
20. Draw points, lines, line segments, rays, right/acute/obtuse angles, and
    perpendicular and parallel lines; identify these in two-dimensional figures;
    classify two-dimensional figures by parallel/perpendicular lines or angles
    of a specified size (recognizing right triangles as a category); recognize
    lines of symmetry, identify line-symmetric figures, and draw lines of
    symmetry.

## For the guiding adult

Grade-4 math turns grade-3 fluency into multi-digit power: every new operation
first lives in concrete models and drawings (area models, arrays, fraction
circles) before it becomes a written method. Read the scope-and-sequence before
starting; it lists safe household materials, accessibility supports, and the
diagnostic, midyear, and review weeks that keep pacing humane. Multiplication
fluency is strategy-based — no timed speed tests. Each future unit will include
its own teacher guide and answer key; the legacy quizzes have no keys yet, so
always work solutions yourself before assigning. Decimals to hundredths and the
protractor are new in this grade — both deserve slow, hands-on introductions.
