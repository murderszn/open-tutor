# Lesson 03 — Benchmark 1/2 and Same Numerator or Denominator

**Session:** 5 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can compare two
fractions by checking them against the benchmark 1/2, by comparing
fractions with the same numerator, or by comparing fractions with the
same denominator — and I can say which strategy I used and why it works.

**Materials:** paper strips, a benchmark card showing 1/2 and its
equivalents (2/4, 3/6, 4/8, 5/10, 6/12, 50/100), paper and pencil.

## Readiness check (4 min)

Warm-up retrieval: "Is 3/8 more or less than 1/2? How do you know
without drawing?" (Less: half of 8 is 4, and 3 < 4 — fewer than half the
pieces are shaded. "Is 5/6 more or less than 1/2?" More: half of 6 is 3,
and 5 > 3.) The benchmark 1/2 is today's fastest tool — when it decides
the comparison, use it.

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

Comparing fractions means deciding which names the greater amount, and
recording it with `>`, `=`, or `<`. Three strategies, in the order to try
them:

**Strategy A — Benchmark 1/2.** If one fraction is greater than 1/2 and
the other is less than 1/2, the first is greater. To test against 1/2,
compare the numerator with half the denominator: for a/b, if a is more
than half of b, the fraction is more than 1/2; if a is less than half of
b, it is less than 1/2; if a is exactly half of b, it equals 1/2.

**Strategy B — Same denominator.** If the denominators match, the pieces
are the same size, so more pieces means a greater amount: 5/8 > 3/8
because 5 eighths cover more than 3 eighths.

**Strategy C — Same numerator.** If the numerators match, the counts
match, so bigger pieces mean a greater amount: 3/4 > 3/5 because fourths
are bigger than fifths. (Fewer pieces in the whole → each piece is
bigger. This is the one learners get backwards most often: a bigger
denominator means SMALLER pieces.)

If none of the three decides it — for example, 1/3 vs. 5/12, where both
are less than 1/2 — do not guess. Lesson 04's common denominators will
decide it.

One rule governs all three strategies: **the wholes must be the same
size.** 1/2 of a small sandwich and 1/2 of a giant sandwich are not the
same amount of food. Every comparison in this unit names one shared whole
unless it is explicitly testing that rule (Lesson 04).

## Worked example 1 — Benchmark decides: 3/8 vs. 5/6

Problem: "Compare 3/8 and 5/6. Record with >, =, or <, and justify."

1. Try the benchmark. Half of 8 is 4; 3 < 4, so 3/8 < 1/2.
2. Half of 6 is 3; 5 > 3, so 5/6 > 1/2.
3. One fraction is below the benchmark and the other is above it, so
   3/8 < 5/6. ✓
4. Model check: a bar with 3 of 8 parts shaded covers less than half the
   bar; a bar with 5 of 6 parts shaded covers more than half. The
   picture agrees with the benchmark reasoning.

## Worked example 2 — Same numerator, then same denominator

Problem: "Compare 3/4 and 3/5. Then compare 5/8 and 3/8."

1. 3/4 vs. 3/5 — same numerator (3). The counts match, so compare piece
   size: fourths are bigger than fifths (4 pieces per whole vs. 5), so
   3/4 > 3/5. ✓
2. 5/8 vs. 3/8 — same denominator (8). The pieces match, so compare
   counts: 5 pieces cover more than 3 pieces, so 5/8 > 3/8. ✓
3. Say the strategy out loud for each: "same numerator — bigger pieces
   win" and "same denominator — more pieces win." Naming the strategy
   is part of the answer.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Compare 2/3 and 3/8 with the benchmark. Record the symbol and justify
   each benchmark check.
   *(Hint: 2/3 > 1/2 because half of 3 is 1.5 and 2 > 1.5; 3/8 < 1/2
   because half of 8 is 4 and 3 < 4. So 2/3 > 3/8.)*
2. Compare 4/5 and 4/6. Which strategy applies? Record the symbol.
   *(Hint: same numerator — fifths are bigger than sixths, so
   4/5 > 4/6.)*
3. Compare 7/12 and 5/12. Which strategy applies? Record the symbol.
   *(Hint: same denominator — 7 pieces beat 5 pieces: 7/12 > 5/12.)*
4. Compare 1/3 and 5/12. Show that the benchmark does NOT decide this
   one, then decide it another way.
   *(Hint: both are less than 1/2 (1 < 1.5; 5 < 6), so the benchmark is
   silent. Rewrite 1/3 as 4/12 — then 4/12 < 5/12, so 1/3 < 5/12. The
   rewrite is Lesson 04's common-denominator move, previewed here.)*

## Independent practice

5. Compare 5/8 and 2/5 with the benchmark. Record the symbol and justify
   each check.
6. Compare 3/10 and 3/12. Which strategy applies? Record the symbol.
7. Compare 9/10 and 7/10. Which strategy applies? Record the symbol.
8. **Error analysis:** Mia says, "5/6 < 5/8, because 8 is bigger than 6."
   Explain why Mia is wrong, using piece size.

## Applied task

Two identical juice bottles start the day full. By dinner, one bottle is
3/4 full and the other is 2/3 full. Which bottle has more juice left?
Compare 3/4 and 2/3 and justify your answer. (Answer: 3/4 > 2/3. The
benchmark alone does not decide — both are above 1/2 — so rewrite with
a common denominator: 3/4 = 9/12 and 2/3 = 8/12, and 9/12 > 8/12. The
rewriting is Lesson 04's move — noticing that you need it is the point
of this task.)

## Exit check

Compare 4/5 and 3/8. Record the symbol and name your strategy.
(Answer: 4/5 > 3/8 — benchmark: 4/5 > 1/2 because half of 5 is 2.5 and
4 > 2.5; 3/8 < 1/2 because half of 8 is 4 and 3 < 4. If you compared
numerators or denominators alone, re-read Strategy C's warning.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** strategy cards on the desk — "Benchmark
  1/2", "Same denominator — more pieces win", "Same numerator — bigger
  pieces win". Learners point to a card before writing a symbol.
- **Alternate response mode:** shade two bars and point to the longer
  shaded length; the adult scribes the symbol and the strategy name.
- **Extension:** compare 5/12 and 4/8 without a common denominator:
  4/8 = 1/2, and 5/12 < 1/2 (5 < 6), so 5/12 < 4/8 — the benchmark plus
  an equivalence.
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** "bigger denominator means
  bigger fraction" (Mia's error) is the signature error of this lesson.
  Keep returning to the pieces: more pieces per whole means each piece
  is smaller. **Answer-key reference:** [../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
  Lesson 03.
