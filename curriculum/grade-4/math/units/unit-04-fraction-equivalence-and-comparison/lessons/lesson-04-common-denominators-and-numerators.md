# Lesson 04 — Common Denominators, Common Numerators, and the Same Whole

**Session:** 6 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can compare two
fractions with different numerators and denominators by rewriting them
with a common denominator or a common numerator, and I can tell when a
comparison is invalid because the wholes differ.

**Materials:** paper strips, colored pencils, paper and pencil.

## Readiness check (4 min)

Warm-up retrieval from Lesson 02: "Rewrite 2/3 with denominator 12."
(2/3 = 8/12 — multiply both by 4.) "Rewrite 3/4 with denominator 12."
(3/4 = 9/12 — multiply both by 3.) Today's lesson is what those two
rewrites are for: once the denominators match, the comparison is easy.

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

When the benchmark does not decide and neither numerator nor denominator
matches, rewrite one or both fractions so that something matches — then
use Lesson 03's rules.

**Common denominator.** Find a denominator that both fractions can be
rewritten with (a common multiple of the two denominators, kept inside
the grade-4 list where possible). Then the pieces are the same size, so
more pieces wins:

> 2/3 vs. 3/4 → 8/12 vs. 9/12 → 2/3 < 3/4

**Common numerator.** Sometimes rewriting to a shared numerator is
shorter. Then the counts match, so bigger pieces win:

> 2/3 vs. 3/5 → 6/9 is outside the grade-4 denominator list, so this
> pair is a poor common-numerator example — use a common denominator
> instead (2/3 = 10/15 is also outside the list). Pairs like this one
> are exactly why Lesson 03's benchmark comes first: 2/3 > 1/2 and
> 3/5 > 1/2, so the benchmark is silent too, and the honest grade-4 move
> is a strip model: draw both bars and compare shaded lengths directly.
> Not every pair has a tidy in-list common denominator, and a model is
> always a valid justification.

Keep common denominators inside the grade-4 list (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10,
12, 100) whenever the pair allows it. Pairs that work tidily include
thirds with quarters or sixths (denominator 12 or 6), quarters with
sixths (12), quarters with eighths (8), fifths with tenths (10), and
halves with anything (the other denominator).

**The same-whole rule.** A comparison is valid only when both fractions
refer to the same whole. 1/2 of a small loaf is less food than 1/2 of a
big loaf, even though the fractions are equal. So:

- If two fractions name parts of the *same* whole, compare them freely.
- If they name parts of *different-sized* wholes, the fractions alone
  cannot tell you which amount is greater. Say so, and refuse the
  comparison — that refusal is a correct answer.

## Worked example 1 — Common denominator: 2/3 vs. 3/4

Problem: "Compare 2/3 and 3/4. Record with >, =, or <, and justify."

1. Benchmark check: both are above 1/2 (2 > 1.5; 3 > 2), so the benchmark
   is silent. Numerators differ, denominators differ — rewrite.
2. Common denominator: 12 works for both (3 × 4 = 12; 4 × 3 = 12), and
   12 is in the grade-4 list.
3. Rewrite: 2/3 = 8/12 (multiply both by 4); 3/4 = 9/12 (multiply both
   by 3).
4. Same denominator — more pieces win: 8/12 < 9/12, so 2/3 < 3/4. ✓
5. Model check: two bars of the same length, one with 8 of 12 parts
   shaded, one with 9 — the second shaded length is longer by one
   twelfth. ✓

## Worked example 2 — A comparison that must be refused

Problem: "Mia ate 1/2 of her small sandwich. Jon ate 1/3 of his giant
sandwich. Jon says he ate more because his sandwich is bigger. Is the
comparison 1/2 vs. 1/3 valid here?"

1. The fractions refer to different wholes (a small sandwich and a
   giant sandwich).
2. 1/2 and 1/3 can only be compared as amounts when the wholes are the
   same size. Here they are not.
3. Jon might have eaten more food — or less. The fractions alone cannot
   say. The correct answer is: **the comparison is invalid as stated**;
   Jon's reasoning uses the sandwich sizes, not the fractions.
4. Contrast: if both sandwiches were identical, 1/2 > 1/3 would be
   valid (benchmark: 1/2 = 1/2, 1/3 < 1/2; or common denominator 6:
   3/6 > 2/6).

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Compare 5/6 and 7/12 with a common denominator. Record the symbol.
   *(Hint: 12 works for both: 5/6 = 10/12, and 10/12 > 7/12, so
   5/6 > 7/12.)*
2. Compare 3/4 and 5/6 with a common denominator. Record the symbol.
   *(Hint: 12 works for both: 3/4 = 9/12, 5/6 = 10/12, so 3/4 < 5/6.)*
3. Compare 2/3 and 5/6 with a common denominator. Record the symbol.
   *(Hint: 6 works for both: 2/3 = 4/6, and 4/6 < 5/6, so 2/3 < 5/6.)*
4. **Same-whole check:** "Lena ate 3/4 of her pizza. Omar ate 1/2 of his
   pizza. Lena ate more pizza than Omar." Is this conclusion valid from
   the fractions alone? Explain.
   *(Hint: no — the pizzas may be different sizes. 3/4 > 1/2 as
   fractions, but amounts can only be compared when the wholes are the
   same. If the pizzas were identical, Lena would have eaten more; as
   stated, the conclusion is not proven.)*

## Independent practice

5. Compare 5/8 and 3/4 with a common denominator. Record the symbol.
6. Compare 7/10 and 3/5 with a common denominator. Record the symbol.
7. Compare 4/5 and 8/10. Are they equal? Justify with a rewrite.
8. **Error analysis:** Lee says, "4/5 < 7/10, because 4 < 7 and 5 < 10."
   Show that Lee is wrong, with a common-denominator rewrite.

## Applied task

Two identical ribbon rolls are each cut for a class project. Priya uses
5/6 of one roll; Sam uses 7/12 of the other roll. Who used more ribbon?
Justify with a common denominator and a sketch of two same-length bars.
Then answer: would your comparison still be valid if Sam's roll had
been twice as long as Priya's? Explain. (Answers: 5/6 = 10/12 > 7/12, so
Priya used more. No — if the rolls differed, the fractions would refer
to different wholes and the comparison would be invalid.)

## Exit check

Compare 5/6 and 3/4 with a common denominator. Record the symbol and show
both rewrites. (Answer: 5/6 = 10/12, 3/4 = 9/12, so 5/6 > 3/4. If you
rewrote only one fraction, the denominators still do not match —
re-read step 3 of Worked example 1.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** a common-denominator card lists tidy
  pairs: (3, 4 → 12), (3, 6 → 6), (4, 6 → 12), (4, 8 → 8), (5, 10 → 10),
  (2, anything → the other denominator).
- **Alternate response mode:** build both fractions with strips of the
  same length and point to the longer shaded length; the adult scribes
  the rewrites.
- **Extension:** find a pair where the common numerator is the shorter
  route: compare 2/3 and 4/5 by rewriting 2/3 with numerator 4:
  2/3 = 4/6, and 4/5 stays 4/5. Same numerator — fifths are bigger than
  sixths, so 4/6 < 4/5, hence 2/3 < 4/5. Verify with a strip model
  (a denominator-15 rewrite would sit outside the grade-4 list, so the
  model is the grade-4 proof).
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** comparing numerators and
  denominators separately (Lee's error) survives all the way to this
  lesson. Insist on the rewrite: until a numerator or a denominator
  matches, no comparison has been made. **Answer-key reference:**
  [../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
