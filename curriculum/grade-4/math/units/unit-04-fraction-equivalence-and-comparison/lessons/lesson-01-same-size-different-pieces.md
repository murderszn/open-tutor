# Lesson 01 — Same Size, Different Pieces

**Session:** 1 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can explain why two
fractions with different numbers can name the same amount, using a visual
fraction model that shows how the number and size of the pieces change.

**Materials:** paper strips (two identical strips per learner), colored
pencils, paper and pencil.

## Readiness check (4 min)

Warm-up retrieval from grade 3: "Draw a bar. Shade 1/2 of it. What does the
2 mean? What does the 1 mean?" (The 2 means the whole is split into 2 equal
parts; the 1 means 1 of those parts is shaded. A fraction a/b means a parts
of size 1/b.) Then ask: "If I cut each half into 2 smaller pieces, how many
pieces will the whole have? How many will be shaded?" (4 pieces; 2 shaded.
Hold that thought — it is today's whole lesson.)

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

A **fraction** names a part of a whole. The **denominator** (bottom number)
says how many equal parts the whole is split into. The **numerator** (top
number) says how many of those parts you have.

**Equivalent fractions** are different fractions that name the same amount.
They happen when you cut each piece into smaller equal pieces — or join
small pieces into bigger ones — without changing the total amount.

Take a bar that is 1/2 shaded. Cut every piece in half. Now the whole has 4
equal pieces instead of 2, and the shaded part has 2 pieces instead of 1.
The shaded length has not moved at all. So:

> 1/2 = 2/4

Notice what changed and what did not:

- The **number of pieces** changed: 2 pieces became 4 pieces.
- The **size of each piece** changed: quarters are smaller than halves.
- The **amount** did not change: the shaded length is exactly the same.

That is the whole idea of 4.NF.A.1: when you multiply the numerator and
the denominator by the same number, you are cutting each piece into that
many smaller pieces. The amount stays the same.

Two habits for this unit:

1. **Draw the whole first, and keep it the same size.** A comparison or an
   equivalence claim means nothing if the two wholes are different sizes.
2. **Name the cut.** Before writing an equation, say what you did to the
   pieces: "I cut each half into 2 pieces" or "I joined pairs of eighths."

## Worked example 1 — 1/2 = 2/4, drawn two ways

Problem: "Show that 1/2 = 2/4 with fraction strips."

1. Take a strip for the whole. Fold it in half and shade 1 of the 2 equal
   parts. That is 1/2.
2. Take an identical strip. Fold it into 4 equal parts (fold in half, then
   in half again) and shade 2 of the 4 parts. That is 2/4.
3. Lay the strips side by side, lined up at the left edge. The shaded
   lengths match exactly. ✓
4. Say what changed: the number of pieces doubled (2 → 4) and each piece
   got smaller (halves → quarters). The shaded amount did not change.
5. Write the equation: 1/2 = 2/4. The equation is true because the models
   show the same shaded length, not because the numbers look alike.

## Worked example 2 — 3/4 = 6/8

Problem: "A bar is 3/4 shaded. Cut each quarter in half. What fraction
names the same amount now?"

1. Start: 4 equal parts, 3 shaded → 3/4.
2. Cut each quarter into 2 equal pieces. The whole now has 4 × 2 = 8 equal
   pieces, and the shaded part has 3 × 2 = 6 pieces.
3. The shaded length has not changed — only the cuts have. So
   3/4 = 6/8.
4. Check with the multiply-both rule: 3/4 = (2 × 3)/(2 × 4) = 6/8. ✓
   Multiplying numerator and denominator by 2 is exactly "cut each piece
   in 2."
5. Common error to avoid: 3/4 is NOT equal to 4/5. Adding 1 to the top and
   bottom is not cutting pieces — it changes the amount. Only multiplying
   or dividing both numbers by the same number preserves the amount.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Draw a bar split into thirds. Shade 1/3. Now cut each third in half.
   How many equal pieces does the whole have now? How many are shaded?
   What equation does that show?
   *(Hint: cutting each piece in 2 doubles both counts: 1/3 = 2/6.)*
2. A bar is 2/3 shaded. Cut each third in half. Write the equivalent
   fraction and explain what changed and what stayed the same.
   *(Hint: 2 parts become 4, 3 parts become 6. The shaded length does not
   move.)*
3. Shade 3/6 of a bar. Shade 1/2 of an identical bar. Lay them side by
   side. Are the amounts equal? Explain using the number and size of
   the pieces.
   *(Hint: sixths are smaller than halves, but there are more of them —
   3 sixths cover exactly the same length as 1 half.)*
4. **Error analysis:** Kim says, "4/8 is bigger than 1/2, because 4 and 8
   are bigger numbers than 1 and 2." Explain why Kim is wrong, with a
   model.
   *(Hint: draw both bars the same length. 4 eighths cover exactly half
   the bar. Bigger numbers do not mean a bigger amount when the pieces
   are smaller.)*

## Independent practice

5. Draw a bar split into quarters. Shade 1/4. Cut each quarter in half.
   What equivalent fraction do you get? Write the equation.
6. A bar is 3/5 shaded. Cut each fifth in half. What fraction names the
   same amount? Explain the cut in one sentence.
7. Draw two identical bars. Shade 2/3 of one and 4/6 of the other. Are
   they equal? Justify with the number and size of the pieces.
8. **Error analysis:** Alex says, "1/2 and 2/4 cannot be equal, because
   the pieces are different." Explain why Alex is wrong.

## Applied task

Make a "same amount, different pieces" strip pair from paper: one strip
showing 2/3, one showing 4/6, lined up at the left edge. Label each strip
with its fraction and write one sentence under the pair explaining what
changed (number and size of pieces) and what stayed the same (amount).
Trade with a partner or show the adult: can they point to the matching
shaded lengths without reading your sentence?

## Exit check

Fill in the blank and justify with a cut: 2/3 = ___/6. (Answer: 4/6 —
cut each third in half, so 2 shaded thirds become 4 shaded sixths and the
whole becomes 6 sixths. If you wrote 3/6, you added 1 to the top and
bottom instead of cutting — re-read Worked example 2, step 5.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** keep the sentence frame on a card: "I cut
  each ___ into ___ pieces, so ___ = ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** fold paper strips instead of drawing bars;
  point to the matching shaded lengths instead of writing the explanation,
  with the adult scribing the sentence.
- **Extension:** show 1/2 = 3/6 = 4/8 as three strips and explain both
  cuts (each half into 3 pieces; each half into 4 pieces).
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** learners who judge fractions by
  the size of the numbers alone (Kim's error) need the strips in their
  hands, not another rule. Require the lined-up model before the equation.
