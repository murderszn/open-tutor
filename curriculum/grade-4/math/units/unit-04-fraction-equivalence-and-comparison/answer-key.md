# Answer Key — Unit 04: Fraction Equivalence and Comparison

**Audience:** the guiding adult only. Every student question in this
unit — lessons, practice, project samples, quiz, and assessment — is
solved here independently. Open responses accept the reasoning shown or
any equivalent correct reasoning.

## Lesson 01 — Same size, different pieces

- **Guided 1:** 6 equal pieces; 2 shaded. Equation: 1/3 = 2/6.
- **Guided 2:** 2/3 = 4/6. Changed: number of pieces (3 → 6) and piece
  size (thirds → sixths, smaller). Stayed the same: the shaded length.
- **Guided 3:** Yes, equal. 3 sixths cover exactly the same length as
  1 half — more pieces, smaller pieces, same amount.
- **Guided 4 (Kim):** Wrong. Drawn to the same length, 4 eighths cover
  exactly half the bar, so 4/8 = 1/2. Bigger numbers do not mean a
  bigger amount when the pieces are smaller.
- **Independent 5:** 1/4 = 2/8 (each quarter cut in half).
- **Independent 6:** 3/5 = 6/10. Each fifth was cut in half, doubling
  both counts without moving the shaded length.
- **Independent 7:** Yes, equal. 2/3 = 4/6: cutting each third in half
  gives 4 shaded sixths covering the same length as 2 shaded thirds.
- **Independent 8 (Alex):** Wrong. Equal amounts can have different
  pieces: 1/2 and 2/4 cover the same length — the pieces differ in
  number (2 vs. 4) and size (halves vs. quarters), but the amount is
  the same.
- **Applied task:** strips labeled 2/3 and 4/6, lined up; sentence:
  "The number of pieces doubled and each piece got smaller, but the
  shaded length stayed the same."
- **Exit check:** 2/3 = 4/6.

## Lesson 02 — Generating equivalent fractions

- **Image activity:** (1) 1/5. (2) 2/5. (3) 10 pieces; 4 pieces;
  2/5 = 4/10. (4) 5/5 and 10/10. (5) The picture is a story prompt —
  it tells you the loaf is cut into 5 strips; exact work uses the
  learner's own equal strips or folded paper, where the cuts are
  controlled.
- **Guided 1:** 1/4 = 2/8 (×2) and 1/4 = 3/12 (×3); also 25/100 (×25).
- **Guided 2:** 8/12 = 4/6 (÷2) = 2/3 (÷4 in one step, or ÷2 again).
  Both roads reach 2/3.
- **Guided 3:** 5/10 = 1/2 (÷5). Five pairs of tenths join into halves.
- **Guided 4 (Dana):** Reasoning wrong, answer right by luck. Correct
  reasoning: 6/10 = 3/5 by DIVIDING both numbers by 2. Subtracting
  does not preserve amounts in general: 3/4 minus 1 from both gives
  2/3, which a model shows is a different amount from 3/4.
- **Independent 5:** 2/3 = 4/6 (×2) and 2/3 = 8/12 (×4).
- **Independent 6:** 9/12 = 3/4 (÷3).
- **Independent 7:** 10/100 = 1/10 (÷10).
- **Independent 8:** 6/10 is NOT equivalent to 3/4. Checks: 6/8 = 3/4
  (÷2); 9/12 = 3/4 (÷3); 75/100 = 3/4 (÷25); 6/10 = 3/5 (÷2), and
  3/5 ≠ 3/4 (fifths are smaller than quarters, same count).
- **Applied task:** 2/8 = 1/4, so 1 strip of the second loaf.
- **Exit check:** 4/6 = 2/3 (÷2); another equivalent: 4/6 = 8/12 (×2).

## Lesson 03 — Benchmark and same numerator/denominator

- **Guided 1:** 2/3 > 3/8. Benchmark: 2/3 > 1/2 (2 > 1.5); 3/8 < 1/2
  (3 < 4).
- **Guided 2:** 4/5 > 4/6. Strategy: same numerator — fifths are bigger
  than sixths.
- **Guided 3:** 7/12 > 5/12. Strategy: same denominator — more pieces win.
- **Guided 4:** Benchmark silent (both < 1/2). Rewrite 1/3 = 4/12, so
  1/3 < 5/12.
- **Independent 5:** 5/8 > 2/5. Benchmark: 5/8 > 1/2 (5 > 4);
  2/5 < 1/2 (2 < 2.5).
- **Independent 6:** 3/10 > 3/12. Strategy: same numerator — tenths are
  bigger than twelfths.
- **Independent 7:** 9/10 > 7/10. Strategy: same denominator.
- **Independent 8 (Mia):** Wrong. Same numerator means bigger pieces
  win: sixths are bigger than eighths, so 5/6 > 5/8. A bigger
  denominator means smaller pieces, not a bigger fraction.
- **Applied task:** 3/4 > 2/3. Benchmark silent (both > 1/2); rewrite
  3/4 = 9/12, 2/3 = 8/12, so 3/4 > 2/3.
- **Exit check:** 4/5 > 3/8. Strategy: benchmark (4/5 > 1/2; 3/8 < 1/2).

## Lesson 04 — Common denominators and the same whole

- **Guided 1:** 5/6 = 10/12 > 7/12, so 5/6 > 7/12.
- **Guided 2:** 3/4 = 9/12 < 10/12 = 5/6, so 3/4 < 5/6.
- **Guided 3:** 2/3 = 4/6 < 5/6, so 2/3 < 5/6.
- **Guided 4:** Not valid from the fractions alone. The pizzas may
  differ in size; 3/4 > 1/2 as fractions, but amounts need the same
  whole. If the pizzas were identical, Lena would have eaten more.
- **Independent 5:** 5/8 < 3/4. Rewrite 3/4 = 6/8; 5/8 < 6/8.
- **Independent 6:** 7/10 > 3/5. Rewrite 3/5 = 6/10; 7/10 > 6/10.
- **Independent 7:** Yes, equal. 8/10 = 4/5 (÷2).
- **Independent 8 (Lee):** Wrong. Rewrite 4/5 = 8/10, and 8/10 > 7/10,
  so 4/5 > 7/10. Comparing numerators and denominators separately
  proves nothing.
- **Applied task:** Priya: 5/6 = 10/12 > 7/12, so Priya used more.
  If Sam's roll were twice as long, the comparison would be invalid —
  different wholes.
- **Exit check:** 5/6 = 10/12, 3/4 = 9/12, so 5/6 > 3/4.

## Lesson 05 — Ordering sets and equivalence chains

- **Guided 1:** 2/5 = 4/10, 1/2 = 5/10, 7/10 stays → 2/5 < 1/2 < 7/10.
- **Guided 2:** 5/6 = 10/12, 3/4 = 9/12, 2/3 = 8/12 →
  2/3 < 3/4 < 5/6.
- **Guided 3:** 2/5 = 4/10 = 40/100. Links: ×2, then ×10; both checked.
- **Guided 4:** Same whole (the track, 1 km). 3/4 = 9/12, 5/6 = 10/12,
  2/3 = 8/12 → Cid (2/3) < Ana (3/4) < Ben (5/6). Ben farthest; Cid least.
- **Independent 5:** 1/4 = 2/8, 3/8 stays, 1/2 = 4/8 →
  1/4 < 3/8 < 1/2.
- **Independent 6:** 3/5 = 6/10 = 60/100. Links: ×2, then ×10.
- **Independent 7:** 5/8 stays, 3/4 = 6/8, 1/2 = 4/8 → Ria (1/2) <
  Pia (5/8) < Quinn (3/4).
- **Independent 8 (Sam):** Wrong. Rewrite: 2/5 = 4/10 and 1/2 = 5/10,
  so 2/5 < 1/2 even though 2 > 1. Numerator order alone proves nothing.
- **Applied task:** strips labeled 2/3, 3/4, 5/6 in that order;
  sentence: the strips are the wholes, so they must be the same
  length or the shaded lengths are not comparable.
- **Exit check:** 1/2 = 5/10, 3/5 = 6/10, 4/5 = 8/10 →
  1/2 < 3/5 < 4/5.

## Practice (Session 11)

1. Equal — 2 quarters cover exactly the same length as 1 half.
2. No in-list equivalent exists by multiplying: ×2 gives 6/16, ×3
   gives 9/24, ×12 gives 36/96, ×25 gives 75/200 — all outside the
   grade-4 denominator list. (3/8 is compared via the benchmark or a
   model, not via an equivalent.)
3. 3/4 = 6/8 (×2) and 3/4 = 9/12 (×3); also 75/100 (×25).
4. 12/12 = 1 (÷12); 6/10 = 3/5 (÷2); 9/12 = 3/4 (÷3).
5. False. 4/6 simplifies to 2/3 and 6/10 to 3/5; strip models show
   2/3 covers more than 3/5, so 4/6 ≠ 6/10.
6. Error: subtracting 3 from both numbers is not a cut. Correct test:
   multiply or divide both by the same number — 5/8 cannot become
   2/5 that way, and the benchmark already separates them
   (5/8 > 1/2 > 2/5), so 5/8 ≠ 2/5.
7. 3/4 > 5/8. Strategy: common denominator — 3/4 = 6/8 > 5/8.
8. 7/12 < 5/6. Rewrite 5/6 = 10/12; 7/12 < 10/12.
9. 3/10 < 3/4. Strategy: same numerator — tenths are smaller than
   quarters.
10. Yes, equal. 9/12 = 3/4 (÷3).
11. Not valid from the fractions alone. The pizzas may be different
    sizes; equal fractions of different wholes are different amounts.
12. Wrong. 8/10 = 4/5 (÷2), so the fractions are equal, not greater.
    Comparing parts separately proves nothing.
13. 1/3 = 2/6, 1/2 = 3/6, 5/6 stays → 1/3 < 1/2 < 5/6.
14. 3/8 stays, 1/4 = 2/8, 3/4 = 6/8 → 1/4 < 3/8 < 3/4.
15. 4/5 = 8/10 = 80/100. Links: ×2, then ×10; both checked.
16. 3/5 = 6/10 < 7/10, so the first jug has more water. If the jugs
    had started at different sizes, the wholes would differ and the
    comparison would be invalid.
17. Sample: pairs 1/2 = 2/4, 2/3 = 4/6, 3/4 = 6/8, each with two
    same-length bars and the equation. Any three correct pairs with
    models score full marks.
18. Rewrites (denominator 12): 2/3 = 8/12, 1/2 = 6/12, 5/6 = 10/12,
    1/4 = 3/12, 3/4 = 9/12 → 1/4 < 1/2 < 2/3 < 3/4 < 5/6.
19. Sample: "Two identical sandwiches. Sara eats 3/4 of hers; Tom eats
    5/8 of his. Who eats more?" Answer: 3/4 = 6/8 > 5/8, so Sara.
    Any correct story, solution, and model/rewrite justification
    scores full marks.
20. 36 × 14 = 504 (30 × 10 = 300, 30 × 4 = 120, 6 × 10 = 60,
    6 × 4 = 24; sum 504). Sample sentence: "Splitting 36 into 30 + 6
    is like cutting a fraction bar into smaller pieces — the parts
    change, but the total stays the same."
