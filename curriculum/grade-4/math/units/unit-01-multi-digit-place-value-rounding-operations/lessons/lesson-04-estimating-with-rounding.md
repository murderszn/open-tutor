# Lesson 04 — Estimating with Rounding: The Market Day Check

**Session:** 7 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can round to estimate a sum
or difference, compute the exact answer, and judge whether the exact answer is
reasonable.

**Materials:** the generated illustration
[`assets/estimation-market-scene.png`](../assets/estimation-market-scene.png)
(display it), paper and pencil.

> **Text-only alternative to the illustration:** the market stall shows four
> signs with fictional practice amounts — 4,786 APPLES, 2,395 PEARS, 9,965
> VISITORS, 12,438 SEEDS. The panel at the bottom rounds each to the nearest
> thousand (5,000; 2,000; 10,000; 12,000) and gives an estimated total of
> 29,000. Every question below can be answered from these numbers alone.

## Readiness check (4 min)

"Estimate 5,204 + 1,876 by rounding each to the nearest thousand." (5,000 +
2,000 = 7,000; exact is 7,080 — close.) If estimating feels new, do one more
small pair before the market scene.

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

An **estimate** is a close-enough answer we find fast by rounding first. The
routine is always the same:

1. **Round** each number to a sensible place (thousands for four-digit numbers,
   ten-thousands for five- and six-digit numbers).
2. **Compute** with the friendly rounded numbers.
3. **Compute exactly**, then compare: is the exact answer near the estimate?
   If yes, the exact answer is **reasonable**. If it is far away, something
   went wrong — recheck the computation, not the estimate.

Rounding to a bigger place gives a rougher, faster estimate; rounding to a
smaller place gives a closer one. For checking work, the rougher estimate is
usually enough: it catches disasters (a dropped regroup, a misaligned column)
while staying easy to do mentally.

## Worked example 1 — Verify the market's estimate (with the illustration)

Look at the illustration's bottom panel: 4,786 → 5,000; 2,395 → 2,000;
9,965 → 10,000; 12,438 → 12,000; estimated total 29,000.

1. Check each rounding: 4,786 → 5,000 ✓ (786 ≥ 500); 2,395 → 2,000 ✓;
   9,965 → 10,000 ✓; 12,438 → 12,000 ✓ (438 < 500).
2. Check the estimated total: 5,000 + 2,000 + 10,000 + 12,000 = 29,000. ✓
3. Now compute exactly: 4,786 + 2,395 = 7,181; 7,181 + 9,965 = 17,146;
   17,146 + 12,438 = 29,584.
4. Compare: 29,584 is 584 away from 29,000 — in the same ballpark. The
   illustration's estimate is reasonable, and our exact total is trustworthy.

## Worked example 2 — Estimate, then catch a mistake

Problem: is 52,010 − 28,476 = 33,534 correct?

1. Estimate to the nearest thousand: 52,000 − 28,000 = 24,000.
2. 33,534 is nowhere near 24,000 — not reasonable. Recheck the subtraction.
3. Exact: 52,010 − 28,476 = 23,534 (borrow-chain across the tens and hundreds).
4. 23,534 sits right by the estimate. ✓ The estimate caught a 10,000-sized
   error before it could hide.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Estimate **7,205 − 3,468** to the nearest thousand, then compute exactly.
   Is the exact answer reasonable? *(Hint: 7,000 − 3,000 first.)*
2. Round each addend of **46,207 + 38,865** to the nearest thousand, estimate,
   then compute exactly. How far apart are the estimate and the exact answer?
   *(Hint: 46,000 + 39,000.)*
3. Mia estimates 712,546 + 288,354 as 700,000 + 300,000 = 1,000,000. Compute
   the exact sum. Was her estimate reasonable? *(Hint: add carefully — watch
   the hundred-thousands column.)*

## Independent practice

4. Estimate **905,318 + 76,295** to the nearest ten thousand, then compute
   exactly. Reasonable?
5. Estimate **600,000 − 47,385** to the nearest ten thousand, then compute
   exactly. Reasonable?
6. **Error analysis:** Leo rounded 34,872 to 34,000 "to the nearest thousand."
   Is Leo right? Explain which digit decides and give the correct rounding.
7. Write your own estimate-first check for 81,436 − 57,289: show the estimate,
   the exact computation, and a one-sentence verdict.

## Application

Market reporter: using the illustration's four amounts, write a two-sentence
news flash: one sentence with the rounded headline total ("about 29,000 items
and visitors"), one with the exact total. Which sentence would a busy reader
prefer, and why? (There is no single right answer — defend your choice.)

## Exit check

A classmate claims 28,416 + 35,902 = 54,318. Estimate to the nearest thousand
and write a verdict: reasonable or not? (Then compute exactly to confirm.)

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** keep the three-step routine (round → estimate →
  compare) posted; read word problems aloud together.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the estimate reasoning
  while the adult scribes the exact computation, then the learner checks it.
- **Extension:** which rounding place — hundreds or thousands — gives the
  closer estimate for 4,786 + 2,395? Compute both estimates and both errors.
  When would the rougher one still be the better choice?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: treating the estimate as "the answer" and skipping the
exact computation, or rounding each number to a *different* place and adding
incompatible magnitudes. Reteach: the estimate is a guardrail, not the
destination. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
