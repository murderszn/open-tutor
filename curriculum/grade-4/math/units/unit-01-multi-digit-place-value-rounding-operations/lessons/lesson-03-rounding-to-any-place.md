# Lesson 03 — Rounding to Any Place

**Session:** 5 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can round a multi-digit
whole number to any named place, and I can justify the decision with benchmarks
on a number line.

**Materials:** number-line strip, the reproducible SVG
[`assets/rounding-number-line-hundreds.svg`](../assets/rounding-number-line-hundreds.svg)
(print it), paper and pencil.

## Readiness check (4 min)

"Round 486 to the nearest hundred." (500.) "How did you decide?" (486 is past
the halfway point 450, closer to 500.) If the halfway idea is gone, rebuild it
on a 400–500 number line before today's multi-digit work.

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

**Rounding** replaces a number with a nearby round number that is easier to
use. To round to a place:

1. **Find the digit in the rounding place.** Everything to its right becomes
   zero; the digits to the left either stay or tick up by one.
2. **Look at the digit just to the right** of the rounding place.
   - 5 or more → the rounding digit goes **up**.
   - 4 or less → the rounding digit **stays**.
3. **Exactly halfway rounds up** (round half up): 67,500 → 68,000 to the
   nearest thousand.

Why does the digit rule work? Rounding to the nearest thousand asks "which
thousand is closer — 12,000 or 13,000?" The hundreds digit tells us how far past
12,000 we are: 500 or more means past halfway, so we go up. The number line
makes this visible: benchmarks on either side, a halfway mark, and the number's
position deciding the winner.

Bigger places follow the same idea. Rounding 46,207 to the nearest ten
thousand: the benchmarks are 40,000 and 50,000, halfway is 45,000, and 46,207
sits past halfway → 50,000. The thousands digit (6 ≥ 5) says the same thing.

## Worked example 1 — Round 4,786 to the nearest hundred

1. Rounding place: hundreds → the 7 (worth 700).
2. Digit to the right: 8 (tens) ≥ 5 → round the hundreds digit up: 7 → 8.
3. Everything right of the hundreds becomes zero: 4,800.
4. Number-line check (see the SVG): benchmarks 4,700 and 4,800, halfway 4,750.
   4,786 sits past halfway, nearer 4,800. ✓

## Worked example 2 — Round 46,207 to the nearest ten thousand

1. Rounding place: ten-thousands → the 4 (worth 40,000).
2. Digit to the right: 6 (thousands) ≥ 5 → round up: 4 → 5.
3. Result: 50,000.
4. Sanity check: 46,207 is between 40,000 and 50,000 and past the halfway mark
   45,000. ✓
5. Common slip: writing 46,000 (that rounds to the nearest thousand, not ten
   thousand) or 40,000 (rounding down despite 6 ≥ 5).

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Round **12,438** to the nearest thousand. *(Hint: what are the two thousand
   benchmarks? Where is 438 relative to halfway 500?)*
2. Round **9,965** to the nearest thousand. *(Hint: 965 — above or below 500?)*
3. Round **356,209** to the nearest hundred thousand. *(Hint: benchmarks
   300,000 and 400,000; halfway is 350,000.)*
4. Round **83,421** to the nearest ten. *(Hint: which digit decides?)*
5. I rounded 46,207 and got 46,000. Which place did I round to? *(Hint: what
   stayed the same?)*

## Independent practice

6. Round **2,395** to the nearest thousand.
7. Round **712,546** to the nearest ten thousand.
8. Round **905,318** to the nearest hundred thousand.
9. Round **34,872** to the nearest thousand.
10. Round **67,500** to the nearest ten thousand AND to the nearest thousand.
    Explain the halfway case in your own words.

## Application

Benchmark detective: the adult writes a rounded result (e.g., "≈ 400,000")
without naming the place. The learner finds a number from today's practice that
rounds to it and names the place. Swap roles twice.

## Exit check

Round 126,314 to the nearest thousand and to the nearest ten thousand. For one
of them, sketch a mini number line with benchmarks and the halfway mark.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** say "round 4,786 to the nearest hundred" while
  pointing at the hundreds digit; keep a place-name anchor chart visible.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may place a counter on the printed
  number line instead of writing the justification.
- **Extension:** round 999,750 to the nearest thousand. What happens?
  (1,000,000 — rounding can cross a period boundary.) Explain why that is
  correct, not a mistake.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: applying the digit rule to the wrong digit (e.g., using
the ones digit when rounding to thousands). Reteach: circle the rounding-place
digit first, then look exactly one step right — the rehoused scaffolds in the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#rounding-scaffolds) ("which two hundreds is
this number between?") help here. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
