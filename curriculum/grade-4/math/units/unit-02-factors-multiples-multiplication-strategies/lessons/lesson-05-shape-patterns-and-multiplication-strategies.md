# Lesson 05 — Shape Patterns and Multiplication Strategies

**Session:** 10 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can describe a growing
shape pattern with a rule, turn factor pairs into multiplication strategies
(doubling/halving, breaking into known facts), and read a 24 × 13 area model
as the preview of Unit 03.

**Materials:** dot paper or graph paper, tiles, crayons, ruler, paper and
pencil.

## Readiness check (4 min)

Retrieval warm-up from Unit 01: round 99,750 to the nearest thousand (100,000
— the halfway case; name the rule). Then ask: "List all factor pairs of 12."
((1, 12), (2, 6), (3, 4).) If the hunt is slow, rebuild with the Lesson 01
routine before new content.

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

**Shape patterns.** Draw the L-shape sequence — each new figure adds one dot
to each arm:

- Figure 1: 1 dot. Figure 2: 3 dots. Figure 3: 5 dots. Figure 4: 7 dots.

Counts: 1, 3, 5, 7, … The rule: "add 2 dots each time" — or "figure n has
2n − 1 dots." Hidden feature: every count is odd (rule never said so).
Why: start odd, add 2 (even) — odd + even stays odd forever.

Now the **square** sequence — each figure is a bigger filled square:

- Figure 1: 1 × 1 = 1 dot. Figure 2: 2 × 2 = 4. Figure 3: 3 × 3 = 9.
  Figure 4: 4 × 4 = 16.

Counts: 1, 4, 9, 16, … The rule: "figure n is an n-by-n square." Hidden
feature: the *gaps* between terms grow: +3, +5, +7, +9 — the odd numbers in
order! Why: each new square adds an L-shaped border one dot thicker, and that
border holds the next odd number of dots.

**Multiplication strategies from factor pairs.** Factors are not just labels
— they are tools. Watch:

- **Breaking into known facts:** 12 × 6. You know 6 × 6 = 36. Split 12 as
  6 + 6: 12 × 6 = (6 × 6) + (6 × 6) = 36 + 36 = 72.
- **Doubling/halving:** 8 × 15. Halve the 8, double the 15 — the product
  stays the same: 8 × 15 = 4 × 30 = 120. (Why: you moved one factor-2 from
  the 8 to the 15.)
- The array proves it: cut a 12-by-6 tile rectangle into two 6-by-6 squares,
  or restack 8 rows of 15 into 4 rows of 30.

**Area-model preview (Unit 03 groundwork).** Draw a rectangle labeled
**24 × 13** — 24 units wide, 13 tall. We do not need the whole answer today;
just see the structure: splitting the sides splits the rectangle, and each
piece is a small multiplication you already know. Next unit, this becomes the
standard route to multi-digit multiplication.

## Worked example 1 — The square pattern's hidden gap feature

Figures hold 1, 4, 9, 16 dots.

1. Rule check: figure 5 is 5 × 5 = 25 dots. Sketch it (draw the 4 × 4 and add
   the border: 4 dots across the bottom + 4 up the side + 1 corner = 9 new
   dots — the next odd number).
2. Gaps: 4 − 1 = 3, 9 − 4 = 5, 16 − 9 = 7, 25 − 16 = 9. Gaps are 3, 5, 7, 9.
3. Predict figure 6: 25 + 11 = 36 = 6 × 6. ✓ (The rule "n-by-n square"
   agrees.)
4. Why the gaps are odd: each new border is an L with equal arms — arm +
   arm + 1 corner = even + 1 = odd. The rule never said "odd gaps" — we found
   it.

## Worked example 2 — 6 × 14 by breaking into known facts

1. Known fact: 6 × 10 = 60. Split 14 = 10 + 4.
2. 6 × 14 = (6 × 10) + (6 × 4) = 60 + 24 = 84.
3. Tile check: build a 6-by-14 rectangle, cut it after 10 columns — a 6-by-10
   piece (60 tiles) and a 6-by-4 piece (24 tiles). 60 + 24 = 84. ✓
4. Alternative: doubling — 6 × 14 = 3 × 28 = 84 (halve 6, double 14).

Common slip: breaking 6 × 14 as (6 × 10) × (6 × 4) — multiplying the parts
instead of adding them. The rectangle is *cut*, not stacked: pieces add.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner does the
writing.

1. L-shape pattern counts: 1, 3, 5, 7 — what are the next two counts, and
   what is the rule in words? *(Hint: look at the arms — how many dots does
   each arm gain?)*
2. Compute 12 × 6 two ways: breaking into known facts AND doubling/halving.
   *(Hint: for doubling/halving — halve which factor to land on a known
   fact?)*
3. Compute 8 × 15 by doubling/halving. Show the tile-restack reasoning.
   *(Hint: 8 → 4, so 15 → ?)*
4. Sketch the 24 × 13 area model as two stacked rectangles (24 × 10 on top,
   24 × 3 below). Label all four dimensions. *(Hint: which two small
   multiplications do you get? Hold the answers for Session 16.)*

## Independent practice

5. Square pattern: figure 5 has 25 dots. How many dots does figure 7 have?
   Show the gap reasoning.
6. Compute 9 × 16 by breaking into known facts two different ways.
7. Compute 14 × 5 by doubling/halving. Then check with a known fact.
8. Draw the triangle-dot pattern: 1, 3, 6, 10, … (figure n adds n dots).
   Name one hidden feature and explain it.

## Application

Strategy showdown: compute **16 × 12** three ways — (a) breaking into known
facts, (b) doubling/halving, (c) the long way (skip counting or repeated
addition). All three must give the same answer (prove it). Which way was
fastest for you, and why?

## Exit check

Compute **7 × 18** by breaking into known facts, and **12 × 15** by
doubling/halving. Sketch the array cut that proves one of them.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** read "doubling/halving" as "move a 2 from one
  factor to the other"; read 24 × 13 as "a rectangle 24 wide and 13 tall."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build every strategy with
  tiles and narrate the cut/restack instead of writing the equations.
- **Extension:** prove doubling/halving always works with a 6-by-10 tile
  rectangle: restack it as 3-by-20 and as 12-by-5 — count every tile. Then: does 7 × 18 = (7 × 9) × 2 work? (Yes — 126 both
  ways. Because 18 = 9 × 2, this is a *product* split, so the parts multiply.
  Contrast with a *sum* split: 7 × 18 = 7 × (9 + 9) = 63 + 63 = 126, where the
  parts add. The rule: split a factor into a sum → add the parts; split it
  into a product → multiply the parts. Never mix them: (7 × 9) + 2 = 65 is
  wrong, and so is (7 × 10) × (7 × 8).)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: add-vs-multiply confusion when splitting
(6 × 14 = (6 × 10) × (6 × 4)). Reteach with the tile cut: the two pieces sit
*side by side* — side-by-side means add. Drill: "sum-split → add the parts;
product-split → multiply the parts." Another: thinking doubling/halving
"changes the answer" — the restack keeps every tile; count before and after
once, then trust the shortcut.
