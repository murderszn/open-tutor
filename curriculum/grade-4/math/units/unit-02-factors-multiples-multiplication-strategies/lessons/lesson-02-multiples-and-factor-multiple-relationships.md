# Lesson 02 — Multiples and the Factor↔Multiple Relationship

**Session:** 3 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can explain that a whole
number is a **multiple of each of its factors**, list multiples of a number by
skip counting, and decide whether a number 1–100 is a multiple of a given
one-digit number.

**Materials:** 1–100 chart, crayons (two colors), tiles from Lesson 01, paper
and pencil.

## Readiness check (4 min)

Retrieval warm-up from Unit 01: estimate 3,847 + 2,965 to the nearest thousand
(4,000 + 3,000 = 7,000). Then skip-count aloud: by 4s to 40 (4, 8, 12, 16,
20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40), by 7s to 70. If the learner stumbles, chant slowly
with clapping before new content.

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

A **multiple** of a number is what you get when you multiply that number by a
whole number. The multiples of 6 are:

6 × 1 = 6, 6 × 2 = 12, 6 × 3 = 18, 6 × 4 = 24, …

so 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, … are all multiples of 6. There is no last multiple
— you can always multiply by one more. Factors are **finite** (a number has
only so many); multiples are **infinite**.

Now the relationship that ties the whole unit together. Look at 18 and its
factor pairs: (1, 18), (2, 9), (3, 6). Since 2 × 9 = 18, 18 is what you get
when you multiply 2 — so **18 is a multiple of 2**. Since 3 × 6 = 18, **18 is
a multiple of 3**, and of 6, and of 9, and of 1, and of 18. Every factor pair
is a certificate:

> **A whole number is a multiple of each of its factors.**

Test a one-digit number the same way: to decide whether 56 is a multiple of 7,
ask "does 7 have a partner that multiplies to 56?" 7 × 8 = 56 — yes. Is 56 a
multiple of 6? 6 × 9 = 54, 6 × 10 = 60 — no partner — no.

## Worked example 1 — Is 48 a multiple of 4? Of 5? Of 6?

- Of 4: hunt a partner. 4 × 10 = 40 (too small), 4 × 12 = 48. Partner 12 →
  yes, 48 is a multiple of 4. (Also: 4 is a factor of 48, so by the rule 48
  is automatically a multiple of 4.)
- Of 5: 5 × 9 = 45, 5 × 10 = 50 — no partner → no.
- Of 6: 6 × 8 = 48 → yes.
- So 48 is a multiple of 4 and 6, but not 5. Check with factors: (1, 48),
  (2, 24), (3, 16), (4, 12), (6, 8) — 4 and 6 appear, 5 does not. The factor
  list and the multiple test always agree. ✓

## Worked example 2 — Coloring the multiples on the 100 chart

Color every multiple of 3 in blue and every multiple of 4 in red.

- Multiples of 3: skip-count 3, 6, 9, 12, …, 99. They land on a diagonal
  pattern down the chart.
- Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, …, 100.
- Numbers colored **both** (12, 24, 36, …) are multiples of 3 *and* 4 —
  because 12's factor pairs include (3, 4): 12 is a multiple of each of its
  factors.

Common slip: calling 0 a multiple you should list (in grade 4 we list positive
multiples: 3, 6, 9, … — 0 is technically a multiple of everything, but it
never helps here).

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner does the
writing.

1. List the first six multiples of **7**. *(Hint: skip-count — what is the
   4th one? Check: 7 × 4 = ?)*
2. Is 72 a multiple of 8? Of 9? *(Hint: hunt the partner for each.)*
3. Fill in: "36 is a multiple of each of its factors, so 36 is a multiple of
   1, 2, 3, ___, ___, ___, ___, ___, 36." *(Hint: use the Lesson 01 pair list
   for 36.)*
4. True or false: "If 9 is a factor of 45, then 45 is a multiple of 9."
   *(Hint: say the rule sentence aloud with these numbers.)*

## Independent practice

5. List the first eight multiples of **5**. Which of them are also multiples
   of 2? How do you know?
6. Determine: is 84 a multiple of 6? Is 84 a multiple of 5? Show the partner
   hunt for each.
7. A number is a multiple of 3 and of 4, and it is less than 30. List all
   numbers it could be. Explain with factor pairs.
8. Write the factor pairs of **20**, then list every one-digit number that 20
   is a multiple of.

## Application

Chart detective: on your 100 chart, color the multiples of 5 in one color.
Write one sentence describing the visual pattern you see, and one sentence
explaining *why* it looks that way using the rule "a number is a multiple of
each of its factors." (Why does every multiple of 10 also end up colored?)

## Exit check

In one sentence, state the factor↔multiple rule. Then decide: is 96 a multiple
of 12? Show the check.

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** chant multiples aloud before writing them;
  read "is a multiple of" as "shows up in the ___ times table."
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may point to the skip-counted
  positions on the 100 chart and circle partners instead of writing lists.
- **Extension:** the numbers colored both blue and red in the worked example
  are multiples of 12 — why? (They are multiples of 3 and 4; 3 × 4 = 12, and
  the pattern continues: 12, 24, 36, ….) Predict the overlap of multiples of
  4 and 6, then check the chart.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "multiples are the same as factors" or "multiples are
smaller than the number." Reteach: factors are the *ingredients* (bounded —
never bigger than the number); multiples are the *products* (unbounded — never
smaller than the number, in grade 4). The 100 chart makes it visible: factors
sit left of/inside the number; multiples march away from it. Another:
deciding multiples by "it ends in…" rules memorized without understanding —
insist on the partner hunt or the factor-list check every time.
