# Lesson 01 — Multiplication as Comparison

**Session:** 1 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can read a
multiplication equation as a comparison, write equations for "times as many"
problems, and tell multiplicative comparison apart from additive comparison.

**Materials:** paper and pencil, multiplication/division fact chart (allowed
for strategy lessons).

## Readiness check (4 min)

Warm-up retrieval from Unit 02: "Name all the factor pairs of 36."
((1, 36), (2, 18), (3, 12), (4, 9), (6, 6).) Then ask aloud: "Is 36 more than
6, or times as many as 6?" (Both are true in plain English — 36 is 30 more
than 6, and 36 is 6 times as many as 6. Today's lesson is about saying
*which* kind of comparison you mean, because the two kinds give different
equations.)

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

A **multiplication equation** can be read as a comparison. The equation

> 35 = 5 × 7

says two things at once: **35 is 5 times as many as 7**, and **35 is 7 times
as many as 5**. The two factors are the two ways of seeing the same
comparison. (This is the official example from 4.OA.A.1.)

**Multiplicative comparison** asks "how many times as many?" It always means
multiply. **Additive comparison** asks "how many more?" It always means add
or subtract. Mixing them up is the most common error in this unit, so learn
the two sentence frames:

- Multiplicative: "__ is __ times as many as __." → multiply.
- Additive: "__ is __ more than __." → add (or subtract, to find the
  difference).

When the unknown is not the total, write a **letter for the unknown** and
solve by the inverse operation: if 8 × b = 96, then b = 96 ÷ 8.

Two habits for every comparison problem:

1. **Name the comparison first.** Say out loud whether it is "times as
   many" or "more than" before you touch a number.
2. **Check the size.** A "times as many" answer must be bigger than both
   starting numbers (for whole numbers above 1); a "more than" answer is
   only a little bigger.

## Worked example 1 — Reading 35 = 5 × 7 two ways

Problem: "A red shelf holds 35 books. A blue shelf holds 7 books. Compare
them."

1. Write the equation that fits: 35 = 5 × 7.
2. Read it as a comparison, first way: 35 is **5 times as many as** 7.
   Check: 5 × 7 = 35. ✓
3. Read it the other way: 35 is **7 times as many as** 5. Check: 7 × 5 =
   35. ✓
4. Now the additive comparison, for contrast: 35 is **28 more than** 7,
   because 35 − 7 = 28. Same shelves, different question, different
   equation. If a problem asks "how many times as many," 28 is wrong; if it
   asks "how many more," 35 is wrong.

## Worked example 2 — An unknown factor

Problem: "A display shelf holds 8 times as many cups as one box. The shelf
holds 96 cups. How many cups are in one box?"

1. Name the comparison: "8 times as many" → multiplicative. Let b stand for
   the cups in one box.
2. Write the equation: 8 × b = 96.
3. Solve with the inverse operation: b = 96 ÷ 8 = 12. (Check: 8 × 12 = 96. ✓)
4. Answer in a sentence: one box holds **12 cups**.
5. Sanity check: 12 is smaller than 96, which is right — the box is the
   smaller amount, and 96 is 8 times as big as it.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Write both comparison readings of **42 = 6 × 7**.
   *(Hint: start "42 is ___ times as many as ___" twice, swapping the
   factors.)*
2. Sara has 6 stickers. Tom has 4 times as many stickers as Sara. How many
   stickers does Tom have? Then answer the additive version: how many would
   Tom have if he had 4 *more* than Sara?
   *(Hint: "times as many" → multiply; "more" → add. The two answers are
   far apart on purpose.)*
3. A garden bed has 3 times as many carrots as beets. There are 15 beets.
   How many carrots are there? Write the equation first.
   *(Hint: let c stand for carrots; c = 3 × 15.)*
4. A rope is 7 times as long as a 9-cm string. How long is the rope?
   *(Hint: 7 × 9 — a fact from Unit 02's factor work.)*

## Independent practice

5. Write both comparison readings of **72 = 8 × 9**.
6. A bookshelf holds 5 times as many books as a backpack. The backpack holds
   18 books. How many books are on the shelf? Write the equation first.
7. Solve for the unknown: 5 × n = 85. Write a one-sentence comparison story
   that fits this equation.
8. **Error analysis:** Jae says, "48 = 6 × 8 means 48 is 6 more than 8."
   Explain why Jae is wrong, and give the two correct comparison readings.

## Applied task

Write your own pair of problems about the same two amounts — one
multiplicative ("times as many") and one additive ("more than") — using
numbers from your own classroom (for example, crayons in two boxes). Solve
both, and explain in one sentence why the answers are different even though
the starting amounts are the same.

## Exit check

"A farmer has 6 baskets. A truck holds 9 times as many baskets as the
farmer has. The truck holds ___ baskets." Fill in the blank and write the
equation. (Answer: 54 baskets; 9 × 6 = 54. The additive trap answer would be
15 — if you wrote 15, re-read the teaching section's two sentence frames.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** keep the two sentence frames on a card;
  underline "times as many" or "more" in every problem before solving.
- **Alternate response mode:** build both comparisons with tiles (6 rows of
  7 vs. a row of 7 with 6 extra tiles) and point to the one that matches
  the words.
- **Extension:** write a comparison problem where the *smaller* amount is
  unknown (like Worked example 2) and one where the *multiplier* is unknown
  (for example, 96 is how many times as many as 12? Answer: 8).
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** learners who answer "times as
  many" problems by adding have usually read only the numbers, not the
  comparison words. Require the spoken sentence frame before any
  computation. **Answer-key reference:** [../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
  Lesson 01.
