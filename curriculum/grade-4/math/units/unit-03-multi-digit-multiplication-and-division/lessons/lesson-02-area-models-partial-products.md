# Lesson 02 — Area Models and Partial Products

**Session:** 2 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can multiply a
two-digit number by a two-digit number, and a number of up to four digits by
a one-digit number, with an area model and partial products — and I can
explain why the parts add up to the whole product.

**Materials:** the generated illustration
[`assets/market-stall-crates.png`](../assets/market-stall-crates.png)
(display it), grid paper, paper and pencil, the reproducible SVG
[`assets/area-model-36-by-14.svg`](../assets/area-model-36-by-14.svg).

> **Text-only alternative to the illustration:** the market stall shows two
> large signs — **14 CRATES** and **36 APPLES IN EACH CRATE**. The picture
> shows only a few crates on the table as an illustration; it is not a
> counting picture. Every question below can be answered from the two signs
> alone.

## Readiness check (4 min)

"Estimate 36 × 14 before we compute it." (Round: 40 × 10 = 400. So the exact
answer should be near 400, and a little above it, since we rounded both
factors down.) Then: "Split 36 into tens and ones, and 14 into tens and
ones." (36 = 30 + 6; 14 = 10 + 4.) Those four splits are today's whole
lesson.

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

An **area model** turns multiplication into a rectangle. For 36 × 14, draw a
rectangle 36 long and 14 wide, then split each side into tens and ones. The
rectangle breaks into four smaller rectangles — the **partial products** —
and the whole product is their sum. This is the **distributive property** in
picture form: multiplying by a sum means multiplying by each part and adding.

The routine is always the same:

1. **Estimate first** (round both factors).
2. **Split** each factor into place-value parts.
3. **Multiply** every part by every part (four partial products for two
   two-digit factors; four for a four-digit factor times a one-digit
   factor, one per place).
4. **Add** the partial products.
5. **Check** against the estimate, and by a second method if the check
   fails.

A common error is to multiply only the matching parts (tens × tens and
ones × ones) and skip the two cross parts. The area model prevents it: a
rectangle has four pieces, not two, and an empty piece means a missing
partial product.

## Worked example 1 — The market stall: 36 × 14 (image activity)

Look at the illustration's signs: **14 crates, 36 apples in each crate.**
How many apples are there in all?

1. Estimate: 40 × 10 = 400.
2. Split: 36 = 30 + 6; 14 = 10 + 4.
3. Area model (see also the SVG
   [`area-model-36-by-14.svg`](../assets/area-model-36-by-14.svg)):

   |  | 10 | 4 |
   |---|---|---|
   | **30** | 30 × 10 = 300 | 30 × 4 = 120 |
   | **6** | 6 × 10 = 60 | 6 × 4 = 24 |

4. Add the partial products: 300 + 120 + 60 + 24 = **504**.
5. Check: 504 is near the estimate of 400 and a little above it, as
   predicted. A second method confirms it: 36 × 14 = 36 × 10 + 36 × 4 =
   360 + 144 = 504. ✓

**Answer: 504 apples.** The stall's signs, the area model, and the
two-part check all agree.

## Worked example 2 — Four digits by one digit: 2,438 × 5

1. Estimate: 2,400 × 5 = 12,000.
2. Split by place: 2,438 = 2,000 + 400 + 30 + 8.
3. Partial products: 2,000 × 5 = 10,000; 400 × 5 = 2,000; 30 × 5 = 150;
   8 × 5 = 40.
4. Add: 10,000 + 2,000 + 150 + 40 = **12,190**.
5. Check: 12,190 sits right beside the estimate of 12,000. ✓ (A second
   method: 2,438 × 5 = 2,438 × 10 ÷ 2 = 24,380 ÷ 2 = 12,190. ✓)

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Compute **72 × 8** with partial products. *(Hint: split 72 into 70 + 2
   — only two partial products this time, because 8 is one digit.)*
2. Compute **125 × 6** with partial products. *(Hint: 100 + 20 + 5, each
   times 6.)*
3. Draw the area model for **48 × 23** and find the product.
   *(Hint: four partial products — watch the two cross parts, 40 × 3 and
   8 × 20.)*
4. Compute **1,275 × 4** with partial products, then check with an
   estimate. *(Hint: estimate with 1,300 × 4.)*

## Independent practice

5. Compute **27 × 35** with an area model. (Estimate first.)
6. Compute **64 × 18** with partial products. (Estimate first.)
7. Compute **3,482 × 6** with partial products. (Estimate first: about
   3,500 × 6.)
8. **Error analysis:** Leo computed 46 × 32 as (40 × 30) + (6 × 2) = 1,200 +
   12 = 1,212. Show the full area model, find the correct product, and
   explain exactly which partial products Leo missed.

## Applied task

A school garden sells seed packets in trays. One tray holds 24 packets, and
the garden has 17 trays. Use an area model to find how many packets that
is, estimate first, and write one sentence explaining why your answer is
reasonable. (Extension: if 6 more trays arrive, how many packets are there
then? Compute 24 × 23 two ways.)

## Exit check

"Write the four partial products of 53 × 26, then add them." (Answer: 50 ×
20 = 1,000; 50 × 6 = 300; 3 × 20 = 60; 3 × 6 = 18; total 1,378. If you
missed a cross part, redraw the rectangle — four pieces, not two.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** label each area-model cell with both the
  multiplication and its product ("30 × 10 = 300") before adding; never
  add bare numbers.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the area model with base-ten blocks
  on grid paper and count the block values instead of writing the table.
- **Extension:** find a missing partial product — "A class computed 58 ×
  34 as 1,500 + 200 + 240 + ___ = 1,972. What is the missing partial
  product, and which two parts was it made from?" (Answer: 32, from
  8 × 4.)
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** the "two-piece" error (tens ×
  tens plus ones × ones) is the signature mistake of this lesson. Insist on
  the drawn rectangle with four labeled cells until the learner produces
  all four partial products unprompted. **Answer-key reference:**
  [../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 02.
