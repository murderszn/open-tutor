# Lesson 04 — Multistep Problems and Interpreting Remainders

**Session:** 9 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can solve multistep
word problems with a letter for the unknown, decide what a remainder means
in the situation, and judge whether my answer is reasonable with an
estimate.

**Materials:** paper and pencil, fact chart (allowed for strategy lessons).

## Readiness check (4 min)

"347 students are going on a trip. Each van holds 8 students. Estimate how
many vans are needed." (About 320 ÷ 8 = 40 vans, or 400 ÷ 8 = 50 — the answer
is in the 40s.) Then ask: "If the division leaves 3 students without a
seat, can we order the estimated number of vans and leave 3 students
behind?" (No — and that is today's lesson: the remainder is not just a
number, it is people who still need a seat.)

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

**Multistep problems** need more than one operation, in the right order.
The routine:

1. **Read twice.** Underline each quantity and what is asked.
2. **Plan the steps** in words before computing ("first find the total,
   then share it").
3. **Use a letter for the unknown** in at least one step (for example,
   let v stand for the number of vans).
4. **Estimate first** for each step, compute, then judge: is the answer
   reasonable?

**Remainders mean different things in different situations.** The same
remainder can lead to four different answers:

- **Round up:** when everyone or everything must fit (vans, boxes to pack
  *all* the items). The remainder forces one more group.
- **Drop (full groups only):** when the question asks how many *full*
  groups there are. The remainder is ignored for the count.
- **Leftover:** when the question asks what is left over. The remainder
  *is* the answer.
- **Share the remainder:** when the leftover itself is shared or counted
  (for example, "how many seats are in the last, partly full row?"). The
  remainder becomes the size of the last partial group.

Never write a remainder down without saying which of these it is. A bare
"43 remainder 3" is an unfinished answer in this unit.

## Worked example 1 — Round up: the vans

Problem: "347 students are going on a trip. Each van holds 8 students. How
many vans are needed?"

1. Plan: divide 347 by 8. Let v stand for the number of vans.
2. Estimate: about 320 ÷ 8 = 40 vans.
3. Compute: 347 ÷ 8 = 43 remainder 3 (40 × 8 = 320, remainder 27; 3 × 8 =
   24, remainder 3; 40 + 3 = 43). Check: 43 × 8 + 3 = 344 + 3 = 347. ✓
4. Interpret: 43 vans carry 344 students, and 3 students would have no
   seat. They still need to travel, so one more van is needed.
5. **Answer: 44 vans.** The remainder rounded *up* — it added a van.

## Worked example 2 — Drop, and leftover: the pencil boxes

Problem: "A shop has 520 pencils. Each box holds 6 pencils. (a) How many
*full* boxes can the shop fill? (b) How many pencils are left over?"

1. Plan: divide 520 by 6.
2. Estimate: about 540 ÷ 6 = 90 boxes.
3. Compute: 520 ÷ 6 = 86 remainder 4 (80 × 6 = 480, remainder 40; 6 × 6 =
   36, remainder 4; 80 + 6 = 86). Check: 86 × 6 + 4 = 516 + 4 = 520. ✓
4. Interpret: (a) **86 full boxes** — the remainder is dropped for this
   question, because a box with 4 pencils is not full. (b) **4 pencils
   left over** — here the remainder *is* the answer.
5. Same division, two different answers. The question, not the arithmetic,
   decides.

## Worked example 3 — Two steps with a letter

Problem: "A school orders 14 boxes with 36 apples in each box, and 5 packs
with 48 juice boxes in each pack. Each of 6 classes gets an equal share of
the juice boxes. How many juice boxes does each class get, and how many
apples are there in all?"

1. Plan, step 1: total apples = 14 × 36. From Lesson 02: 504 apples.
2. Plan, step 2: total juice boxes = 5 × 48 = 240. (50 × 5 = 250 estimate;
   exact: 5 × 48 = 240.)
3. Plan, step 3: let j stand for juice boxes per class. j = 240 ÷ 6 = 40.
   Check: 6 × 40 = 240. ✓
4. Answers: **each class gets 40 juice boxes; there are 504 apples in
   all.** (Note what was *not* asked: nobody asked for apples plus juice
   boxes together, so 504 + 240 = 744 is not an answer here — a good
   reminder to answer the question that was actually asked.)

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Cupcakes: 258 cupcakes are displayed on trays that hold 9 each. (a)
   How many *full* trays are there? (b) If every cupcake must be displayed,
   how many trays are needed in all? *(Hint: one division, two
   interpretations — drop, then round up.)*
2. Two steps: a baker makes 3 batches of 48 muffins, then packs them into
   boxes of 8. How many boxes does she fill? *(Hint: step 1 is a
   multiplication; step 2 is a division.)*
3. Sharing: 1,250 stickers are shared equally among 7 classes, as far as
   possible. How many does each class get, and how many are left for the
   teacher? *(Hint: estimate with 1,400 ÷ 7 = 200 first.)*
4. Letter unknown: "n boxes hold 24 books each, plus 18 books on a shelf,
   make 234 books in all." Write the equation, solve for n, and check.
   *(Hint: subtract the shelf books first, then divide.)*

## Independent practice

5. Rows: a hall has 436 seats in rows of 9. How many *full* rows are
   there, and how many seats are in the last, partly full row?
6. Two steps: Maya buys 23 packs with 15 pencils in each pack, then gives
   away 128 pencils. How many pencils does she have left?
7. Letter unknown: "A class collects cans in bags of 32. After adding 17
   loose cans, the class has 305 cans in all." Let b stand for the number
   of bags. Write the equation, solve for b, and check.
8. **Error analysis:** Sam solved the van problem (347 students, 8 per
   van) and answered "43 vans." His division was correct. Explain what Sam
   forgot, and give the correct answer with a reason from the situation.

## Applied task

Write a remainder problem of your own about your classroom (books, chairs,
snack packs — any real classroom amount), where the remainder must round
*up*. Trade with a partner: solve each other's problem, and check that the
partner's answer rounds up for a reason from the situation, not from a
rule. If your partner's remainder could also be read as "leftover," say so
— and say which reading your question actually asks for.

## Exit check

"An after-school club has 200 snacks to pack into bags of 7. (a) How many
full bags? (b) How many snacks are left over? (c) If every snack must be
packed, how many bags are needed?" (Answers: (a) 28 full bags, (b) 4 left
over, (c) 29 bags. Check: 28 × 7 + 4 = 196 + 4 = 200. ✓)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** a four-choice remainder card (round up /
  drop / leftover / last partial group) the learner points to before
  writing the answer.
- **Alternate response mode:** act the situation with counters (vans as
  cups, students as counters) and physically place the leftover counters
  before deciding.
- **Extension:** the "remainder museum" — write five short situations with
  the *same* division (for example, 100 ÷ 7 = 14 remainder 2) where the
  final answers are 15, 14, 2, "14 full groups and a group of 2," and a
  fifth reading of your own, each justified by its situation.
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** learners who always round up
  or always drop are following a rule instead of reading the situation.
  Require the sentence "The remainder means ___ because ___" on every
  remainder item until the habit is automatic. **Answer-key reference:**
  [../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 04.
