# Lesson 05 — Choosing a Strategy: Split, Adjust, or Estimate?

**Session:** 10 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can choose an
efficient strategy for a multiplication or division problem, justify my
choice, and verify my answer by a second method.

**Materials:** paper and pencil, fact chart (allowed for strategy lessons).

## Readiness check (4 min)

"How would you compute 198 × 4?" (Many learners split 198 into 100 + 90 +
8 and grind through three partial products. The faster route is to notice
198 is 2 away from 200: 200 × 4 − 2 × 4 = 800 − 8 = 792. Today's lesson is
about noticing the friendly numbers *before* you start grinding.)

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

Every problem in this unit can be solved more than one way. A good
mathematician picks the way that fits the numbers, then proves the answer
with a different way. The three strategies in this unit:

1. **Split (partial products / partial quotients).** The reliable
   default. Split by place value, multiply or divide each part, add the
   parts. Use it whenever nothing friendlier jumps out — and whenever
   you are unsure.
2. **Adjust (near a round number).** When a factor is close to a round
   number (198 near 200, 96 near 100, 29 near 30), compute with the round
   number and adjust for the difference. For multiplication, adjust by
   subtracting or adding the extra part; for division, adjust only by
   checking against a nearby known quotient.
3. **Estimate, then verify.** For every problem: estimate first (so a
   wild answer gets caught), compute, then verify — by the inverse
   operation (division checks multiplication, multiplication checks
   division), by a second strategy, or by re-splitting.

Strategy choice is not about speed for its own sake. It is about
*reliability*: the best strategy is the one you can explain and check. A
fast answer you cannot check is worse than a slow answer you can prove.

Two warnings:

- **Adjusting division is tricky.** 1,200 ÷ 6 = 200 is a place-value split
  (12 hundreds ÷ 6), not an adjustment. Do not "adjust" a dividend and
  expect the quotient to adjust by the same amount — division does not
  work that way. For division, split or use partial quotients, and check
  with multiplication.
- **A strategy is only finished when it is checked.** An unchecked answer
  is a guess with extra steps.

## Worked example 1 — Adjusting: 96 × 7

1. Notice: 96 is 4 away from 100. Adjusting fits.
2. Compute with the round number: 100 × 7 = 700.
3. Adjust: we counted 4 too many, 7 times over, so subtract 4 × 7 = 28.
   700 − 28 = **672**.
4. Verify by splitting: 90 × 7 + 6 × 7 = 630 + 42 = 672. ✓ Both methods
   agree.
5. Why not split first? Splitting works too (630 + 42) — adjusting was
   chosen because 100 × 7 is instant, and the adjustment is a single
   small product.

## Worked example 2 — Splitting beats adjusting: 29 × 41

1. Notice: 29 is near 30 and 41 is near 40, so the estimate is 30 × 40 =
   1,200. But adjusting *both* factors at once is easy to get wrong, so
   split instead.
2. Split: 29 = 20 + 9; 41 = 40 + 1.
3. Partial products: 20 × 40 = 800; 20 × 1 = 20; 9 × 40 = 360; 9 × 1 = 9.
4. Add: 800 + 20 + 360 + 9 = **1,189**.
5. Verify against the estimate: 1,189 is near 1,200. ✓ Verify by a second
   method: 29 × 41 = 29 × 40 + 29 × 1 = 1,160 + 29 = 1,189. ✓

## Worked example 3 — Place-value division: 936 ÷ 9

1. Notice: 936 = 900 + 36, and both parts divide cleanly by 9.
2. Split the dividend: 900 ÷ 9 = 100; 36 ÷ 9 = 4.
3. Quotient: 100 + 4 = **104**, remainder 0.
4. Verify: 104 × 9 = 936. ✓ (100 × 9 = 900; 4 × 9 = 36.)
5. Why this strategy? The dividend's place-value parts were already
   multiples of the divisor — partial quotients would have found the same
   chunks, in more steps.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Compute **198 × 4** by adjusting, then verify by splitting.
   *(Hint: 200 × 4, minus what?)*
2. Compute **52 × 8** two ways (split and adjust from 50), and say which
   was easier for you and why. *(Hint: 50 × 8 + 2 × 8.)*
3. Compute **1,200 ÷ 6** by splitting the dividend by place value, then
   verify with multiplication. *(Hint: 12 hundreds ÷ 6.)*
4. Choose a strategy for **84 × 6**, justify your choice in one sentence,
   compute, and verify by a second method. *(Hint: 80 + 4 splits cleanly;
   84 is not especially near a round number.)*

## Independent practice

5. Compute **203 × 7** by adjusting, then verify by splitting.
6. Compute **47 × 9** two ways and state which you prefer.
7. Compute **2,408 ÷ 8** with partial quotients, then verify with
   multiplication. (Estimate first: about 2,400 ÷ 8 = 300.)
8. **Error analysis:** Dana computed 197 × 5 as 200 × 5 = 1,000 and wrote
   1,000 as her answer. Explain the missing step, give the correct answer,
   and verify it.

## Applied task

You are buying supplies for the Market Day project (see
[project.md](../project.md)). One supplier sells packs of 48 pencils for a
class stall, and you need 7 packs. Another sells single pencils at a price
that makes 336 pencils cost the same. First compute 48 × 7 two ways and
verify; then explain in two sentences why a buyer who checks with a second
method is less likely to over-order. (The point is the habit, not the
shopping: every project computation in this unit must be verified.)

## Exit check

"Compute 67 × 8 by adjusting from 70, then verify by splitting. Which
method did you find more reliable, and why?" (Answer: 70 × 8 − 3 × 8 = 560
− 24 = 536; split check: 60 × 8 + 7 × 8 = 480 + 56 = 536. Either preference
is acceptable if the justification names something real — fewer steps,
easier numbers, or a cleaner check.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** a strategy-choice card — "Is a factor
  near a round number? → Adjust. Does the dividend split into multiples
  of the divisor? → Split the dividend. Otherwise? → Partial products /
  partial quotients." — kept on the desk through Week 13.
- **Alternate response mode:** explain the strategy choice aloud before
  writing any numbers; the adult records the justification.
- **Extension:** find the most efficient strategy for 999 × 8 (adjust:
  1,000 × 8 − 1 × 8 = 8,000 − 8 = 7,992) and for 1,005 × 6 (adjust: 1,000 ×
  6 + 5 × 6 = 6,000 + 30 = 6,030); write a rule for when adjusting *up*
  vs. *down* is easier.
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** learners who adjust a division
  dividend and adjust the quotient by the same amount (for example,
  "1,201 ÷ 6 ≈ 200, so 1,200 ÷ 6 = 199") need the place-value split
  re-taught, not more adjusting practice. **Answer-key reference:**
  [../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 05.
