# Lesson 03 — Decomposing a Fraction in More Than One Way

**Session:** 5 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can break a fraction
into a sum of same-denominator fractions in more than one way, and prove
each way with an equation and a model.

**Materials:** paper strips (three identical strips per learner), colored
pencils, paper and pencil.

## Readiness check (4 min)

Warm-up retrieval from Lessons 01–02: "Compute 1/8 + 2/8 and 3/8 − 1/8.
What stayed the same in both?" (3/8 and 2/8. The denominator stayed 8;
only the numerator count changed.) Then ask: "3/8 is the answer to both
problems. Can you write 3/8 as a sum in a different way?" (1/8 + 1/8 +
1/8. Hold that thought — today every fraction gets taken apart.)

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

To **decompose** a fraction is to break it into a sum of smaller pieces
with the **same denominator**. Because addition with like denominators
only changes the numerator count (Lesson 01), any split of the numerator
into a sum gives a valid decomposition — as long as the parts add back to
the original numerator.

> 3/8 = 1/8 + 1/8 + 1/8 and 3/8 = 1/8 + 2/8 — two different ways to take
> 3/8 apart. Both are correct, because 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 and 1 + 2 = 3.

Two habits for this lesson:

1. **Check the parts add back.** After writing a decomposition, add the
   numerators of the parts. If they do not equal the original numerator,
   the decomposition is wrong — no matter how neat it looks.
2. **Justify with a model.** Draw the whole bar once, then mark the split
   points. Each part of the sum is a section of the same bar.

Decomposition is the reverse of Lesson 01's joining, and it is also how
mixed numbers come apart: a mixed number is a whole (or wholes) plus a
fraction part, and each whole can itself be written as same-denominator
pieces.

## Worked example 1 — two decompositions of 5/6

Problem: "Write 5/6 as a sum of same-denominator fractions in two
different ways. Justify each with a model."

1. Draw a bar split into 6 equal parts; shade 5 of them. That is 5/6.
2. **Way 1 — all unit fractions:** split the 5 shaded sixths into five
   single sixths: 5/6 = 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6 + 1/6. Check: 1 + 1 + 1 +
   1 + 1 = 5. ✓
3. **Way 2 — two chunks:** split the 5 shaded sixths into a group of 2
   and a group of 3: 5/6 = 2/6 + 3/6. Check: 2 + 3 = 5. ✓
4. A third way also works: 5/6 = 1/6 + 4/6 (1 + 4 = 5). ✓
5. All three ways name the same amount, because each one counts the same
   5 sixths — only the grouping changed.

## Worked example 2 — decomposing a mixed number: 2 1/8

Problem: "Write 2 1/8 as a sum in two different ways."

1. First, read the mixed number: 2 wholes and 1/8. Each whole is 8/8.
2. **Way 1 — wholes kept whole:** 2 1/8 = 1 + 1 + 1/8. Check: 1 + 1 +
   1/8 = 2 1/8. ✓
3. **Way 2 — wholes written as eighths:** 1 = 8/8, so
   2 1/8 = 8/8 + 8/8 + 1/8. Check: 8 + 8 + 1 = 17 eighths, and
   2 1/8 = 17/8. ✓ (This is the standard's own example, 4.NF.B.3.b.)
4. A third way: 2 1/8 = 1 1/8 + 1 = 9/8 + 8/8. Check: 9 + 8 = 17. ✓
5. Common error to avoid: writing 2 1/8 = 2 + 1/8 is a decomposition,
   but writing 2 1/8 = 2/8 + 1/8 is not — 2/8 + 1/8 = 3/8, which is a
   different amount. The wholes must be counted as wholes (or as 8/8),
   not as 2 eighths.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Write 3/8 as a sum in two ways: all unit fractions, and one chunk of
   1/8 plus one chunk of 2/8. Draw one bar and mark both splits.
   *(Hint: 3/8 = 1/8 + 1/8 + 1/8 and 3/8 = 1/8 + 2/8.)*
2. Write 7/10 as a sum in two ways. Check each by adding the numerators.
   *(Hint: 7 = 3 + 4, and 7 = 1 + 6.)*
3. Write 4/5 as a sum of two fractions, then as a sum of four unit
   fractions.
   *(Hint: 4/5 = 2/5 + 2/5 = 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5.)*
4. **Error analysis:** Noah says, "5/6 = 2/6 + 2/6, because 2 + 2 is
   almost 5." Explain why Noah is wrong, and give a correct
   decomposition.
   *(Hint: add the numerators of the parts: 2 + 2 = 4, not 5. The parts
   must add back to 5. 5/6 = 2/6 + 3/6 works.)*

## Independent practice

5. Write 6/8 as a sum in two different ways. Draw the bar for one of
   them.
6. Write 1 2/3 as a sum in two ways: one keeping the whole as 1, one
   writing the whole as thirds.
7. Write 9/12 as a sum of two fractions, then as a sum of three equal
   fractions.
8. **Error analysis:** Maya says, "7/10 = 5/10 + 5/10." Explain Maya's
   error and give a correct decomposition.

## Applied task

Decompose 4/6 in three ways on one strip drawing: (a) all unit
fractions, (b) two chunks, (c) a whole chunk plus a remainder — for
example, 4/6 = 3/6 + 1/6. Label each way with its equation and check
each by adding the numerators. Show the adult: can they verify all three
checks without your help?

## Exit check

Write 3/4 as a sum in two ways, and check each by adding the numerators.
(Answer: 3/4 = 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 and 3/4 = 1/4 + 2/4. Checks: 1 + 1 + 1 =
3 and 1 + 2 = 3. If one of your ways was 2/4 + 2/4, its numerators add
to 4, not 3 — it names 4/4, a whole, not 3/4.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** keep the sentence frame on a card: "___
  = ___ + ___ . Check: ___ + ___ = ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** fold paper strips instead of drawing bars;
  point to each chunk of the split bar instead of writing the equation,
  with the adult scribing it.
- **Extension:** decompose 11/8 in three ways, including one way that
  uses a whole: 11/8 = 8/8 + 3/8 = 1 + 3/8, and 11/8 = 5/8 + 6/8, and
  11/8 = 1/8 + 10/8.
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** learners who write parts that
  do not add back (Noah's and Maya's error) are guessing at splits
  instead of splitting the numerator. Make the numerator-add-back check
  a non-negotiable step before the model is drawn. **Answer-key
  reference:** [../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 03.
