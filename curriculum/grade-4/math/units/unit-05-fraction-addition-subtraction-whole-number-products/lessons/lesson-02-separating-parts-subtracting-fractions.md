# Lesson 02 — Separating Parts: Subtracting Fractions with Like Denominators

**Session:** 2 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can subtract fractions
with the same denominator by counting the parts that remain, and explain
how to rewrite a whole number with the same denominator first.

**Materials:** paper strips (two identical strips per learner), colored
pencils, paper and pencil.

## Readiness check (4 min)

Warm-up retrieval from Lesson 01: "Compute 3/10 + 4/10. What stayed the
same? What changed?" (7/10. The denominator stayed 10; the numerator
changed, 3 + 4 = 7.) Then ask: "If a bar is 7/10 shaded and I erase 4/10
of the shading, how many tenths are shaded now?" (3 tenths — 7/10 − 4/10
= 3/10. Subtraction is the reverse of yesterday's joining.)

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

Subtracting fractions with the **same denominator** is separating parts
of the same whole. The denominator names the **size** of the pieces, and
taking pieces away does not change their size. So the denominator stays
the same. Only the numerator changes: it counts how many pieces remain.

> When the denominators match, subtract the numerators and keep the denominator.

> 7/8 − 3/8 = 4/8 — seven eighths take away three eighths leaves four eighths.

Two habits for this lesson:

1. **Rewrite a whole before you subtract.** To compute 1 − 5/8, first
   write 1 as 8/8 — one whole cut into 8 equal pieces. Then subtract the
   numerators: 8/8 − 5/8 = 3/8. A whole number is not "0 eighths"; it is
   all of the pieces.
2. **Check with addition.** Every subtraction can be checked by adding
   the answer back: 4/8 + 3/8 should equal 7/8. If it does not, the
   subtraction is wrong.

The sandwich-tray picture from Lesson 01 works in reverse, too: the
tray holds 8/10 and the plate holds 2/10. The whole flatbread minus the
plate is 10/10 − 2/10 = 8/10 — exactly what is left in the tray.

## Worked example 1 — 7/8 − 3/8

Problem: "A ribbon is 7/8 red. 3/8 of it is cut off. What fraction of
the ribbon remains red?"

1. Draw a bar split into 8 equal parts (the whole ribbon).
2. Shade 7 parts. Cross out 3 of the shaded parts. Count the shaded parts
   that remain: 7 − 3 = 4.
3. The remaining parts are still eighths — the pieces did not change size.
4. Write the equation: 7/8 − 3/8 = 4/8. ✓
5. Simplify: 4/8 = 1/2. So half the ribbon remains red.
6. Check by adding: 4/8 + 3/8 = 7/8. ✓ The subtraction undoes correctly.

## Worked example 2 — subtracting from a whole: 1 − 5/8

Problem: "One whole sandwich is cut into 8 equal strips. You eat 5
strips. What fraction of the sandwich is left?"

1. The whole sandwich must be written with the same denominator first:
   1 = 8/8. (Eight eighths make one whole.)
2. Now subtract the numerators: 8/8 − 5/8 = 3/8.
3. Answer: **3/8 of the sandwich is left**. ✓
4. Check: 3/8 + 5/8 = 8/8 = 1 whole. ✓
5. Common error to avoid: 1 − 5/8 is NOT 4/8 or 1/8. Writing 1 as 1/8
   confuses "one whole" with "one eighth." Always rewrite the whole as
   eighths (or whatever denominator the problem uses) before subtracting.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Draw a bar split into sixths. Shade 5/6. Cross out 1/6. What fraction
   is shaded now? Write the equation and simplify.
   *(Hint: 5 − 1 = 4 sixths. 4/6 = 2/3.)*
2. Compute 4/5 − 2/5. Draw the model and check with addition.
   *(Hint: four fifths take away two fifths leaves two fifths.)*
3. Compute 2 − 5/6. Rewrite 2 with denominator 6 first.
   *(Hint: 2 = 12/6. 12/6 − 5/6 = 7/6 = 1 1/6.)*
4. **Error analysis:** Jay says, "7/10 − 3/10 = 4/0, because 10 − 10
   is 0." Explain why Jay is wrong, with a model.
   *(Hint: subtracting does not change the piece size. Four tenths
   remain, so the answer is 4/10 = 2/5. A denominator of 0 is
   impossible — it would mean the whole was cut into zero pieces.)*

## Independent practice

5. Draw a bar split into twelfths. Shade 9/12, then cross out 5/12.
   Write the subtraction equation and simplify the answer.
6. Compute 1 − 3/4. Show the rewrite of 1 first.
7. Compute 11/10 − 6/10. Is the answer greater than or less than 1?
   Write it as a mixed number if it is greater than 1.
8. **Error analysis:** Priya says, "5/8 − 2/8 = 3/0." Explain Priya's
   error and give the correct answer.

## Applied task

Make a "separating" strip pair from paper: one strip showing 6/8 shaded.
Cross out 2 of the shaded eighths with a different color. Draw the
remaining bar underneath and label it 4/8 = 1/2. Write the equation
6/8 − 2/8 = 4/8 underneath, and the addition check 4/8 + 2/8 = 6/8
beside it. Show the adult: can they see the 4 remaining eighths without
reading your equation?

## Exit check

Fill in the blank, simplify, and check with addition: 5/6 − 2/6 = ___.
(Answer: 3/6 = 1/2 — five sixths take away two sixths leaves three
sixths, and 3/6 simplifies to 1/2. Check: 1/2 + 2/6 = 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.
If you wrote 3/0, you subtracted the denominators — re-read Worked
example 1, step 3.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** keep the sentence frame on a card: "___
  take away ___ leaves ___ — the denominator stays ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** fold paper strips instead of drawing bars;
  point to the remaining shaded length instead of writing the
  explanation, with the adult scribing the sentence.
- **Extension:** subtract from 2 wholes: 2 − 7/10 = 13/10 = 1 3/10.
  (Rewrite 2 as 20/10, then subtract.)
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** learners who subtract the
  denominators (Jay's and Priya's error) are treating the denominator as
  a count instead of a size name. Ask: "If you take slices off a pizza,
  do the remaining slices get smaller?" No — the size stays; only the
  count changes. **Answer-key reference:**
