# Lesson 05 — Multiplying a Fraction by a Whole Number

**Session:** 9 · **Time:** 35 minutes · **Goal:** I can explain a fraction
as a multiple of a unit fraction, multiply a fraction by a whole number,
and solve word problems with that multiplication.

**Materials:** paper strips (three identical strips per learner), colored
pencils, paper and pencil, the reproducible SVG
[`../assets/fraction-strip-addition.svg`](../assets/fraction-strip-addition.svg)
for the addition model this lesson builds on.

## Readiness check (4 min)

Warm-up retrieval from Lesson 01: "Compute 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4.
How many quarters is that? What whole number does it equal?" (5/4 = 1 1/4.
Five quarters.) Then ask: "Five quarters is also 5 groups of 1/4. How
would you write that as a multiplication?" (5 × 1/4. Hold that thought
— today repeated addition of a fraction becomes multiplication.)

## Explicit teaching (10 min)

A fraction is a **multiple of a unit fraction**: 5/4 is 5 copies of 1/4,
so 5/4 = 5 × (1/4). Multiplying a fraction by a whole number is repeated
addition of that fraction — and repeated addition of a fraction only adds
the numerators (Lesson 01), because the denominator names the piece size
and never changes.

> n × (a/b) = (n × a)/b — multiply the numerator by the whole number;
> keep the denominator.

> 3 × (2/5) = 6/5 — three groups of two fifths is six fifths. As a mixed
> number, 6/5 = 1 1/5.

Two habits for this lesson:

1. **Count unit fractions first.** Before computing 4 × (3/8), say it as
   unit fractions: 4 groups of 3 eighths is 12 eighths, so 4 × (3/8) =
   12/8. The unit-fraction count is the numerator.
2. **Name where the answer sits.** A product like 15/8 is hard to picture
   as "fifteen eighths," so also say it as a mixed number and name the
   two whole numbers it lies between: 15/8 = 1 7/8, between 1 and 2.

## Worked example 1 — a fraction as a multiple of 1/b: 5/4 = 5 × (1/4)

Problem: "Show that 5/4 is 5 × (1/4) with a fraction model."

1. Draw a bar split into 4 equal parts (one whole). Shade all 4 parts:
   that is 4/4 = 1.
2. Draw a second identical bar and shade 1 of its 4 parts: that is 1/4.
3. Count the shaded quarters in all: 4 + 1 = 5 quarters. So 5/4 means
   5 copies of 1/4.
4. Write the equation: 5/4 = 5 × (1/4). ✓
5. As a mixed number: 5/4 = 4/4 + 1/4 = 1 1/4. The multiplication and
   the mixed number say the same thing.

## Worked example 2 — 3 × (2/5)

Problem: "Compute 3 × (2/5). Write the answer as an improper fraction
and as a mixed number."

1. Read it as unit fractions: 2/5 is 2 copies of 1/5, and there are 3
   groups of it — so the product is 3 × 2 = 6 copies of 1/5.
2. Write the equation: 3 × (2/5) = 6/5. (In general,
   n × (a/b) = (n × a)/b.) ✓
3. As a mixed number: 6/5 = 5/5 + 1/5 = 1 1/5.
4. Check by repeated addition: 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5 = 6/5. ✓ Multiplication
   and repeated addition agree.
5. Common error to avoid: 3 × (2/5) is NOT 6/15. Multiplying by a whole
   number multiplies the numerator only — the denominator stays 5,
   because the pieces stay fifths.

## Worked example 3 — a word problem (roast-beef style)

Problem: "If each person at a party will eat 3/8 of a pound of roast
beef, and there will be 5 people at the party, how many pounds of roast
beef will be needed? Between what two whole numbers does your answer
lie?" (This is the standard's own example, 4.NF.B.4.c.)

1. Each person eats 3/8 pound; 5 people means 5 groups of 3/8:
   5 × (3/8).
2. Count unit fractions: 5 groups of 3 eighths is 15 eighths:
   5 × (3/8) = 15/8.
3. As a mixed number: 15/8 = 8/8 + 7/8 = 1 7/8 pounds.
4. The answer lies between the whole numbers **1 and 2**. ✓
5. Check by estimating: 3/8 is a little less than 1/2, and 5 halves
   would be 2 1/2, so the answer should be a little less than 2 1/2 —
   and a little more than 1 1/2. 1 7/8 is. ✓

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Write 4/3 as a multiple of 1/3, and as a mixed number.
   *(Hint: 4/3 = 4 × (1/3) = 1 1/3.)*
2. Compute 2 × (3/4). Write the answer as an improper fraction and as
   a mixed number.
   *(Hint: 2 groups of 3 quarters is 6 quarters: 6/4 = 1 2/4 = 1 1/2.)*
3. Compute 6 × (2/8). Simplify the answer.
   *(Hint: 6 × 2 = 12 eighths: 12/8 = 1 4/8 = 1 1/2.)*
4. **Error analysis:** Sam says, "3 × (2/5) = 6/15, because I multiplied
   the top and the bottom by 3." Explain why Sam is wrong, with a model.
   *(Hint: multiplying by a whole number makes more pieces, not smaller
   pieces. Three groups of two fifths is six fifths: 6/5 = 1 1/5. The
   denominator stays 5.)*

## Independent practice

5. Compute 5 × (2/3). Write the answer as a mixed number.
6. Compute 4 × (3/10). Write the answer as a mixed number and simplify
   the fraction part.
7. Word problem: "Seven ribbons are each 5/6 of a meter long. How many
   meters of ribbon is that in all? Between what two whole numbers does
   your answer lie?"
8. **Error analysis:** Zoe says, "2 × (3/4) = 6/8, because I multiplied
   both numbers by 2." Explain Zoe's error and give the correct answer.

## Applied task

Four friends each eat 2/3 of a sandwich. Draw the four sandwiches as
bars (each split into thirds, 2 thirds shaded), count the shaded thirds
in all, and write the multiplication 4 × (2/3) = 8/3 = 2 2/3. Then answer:
how many whole sandwiches is that, and how much is left over? Show the
adult: can they count 8 shaded thirds in your drawing without reading
your equation?

## Exit check

Compute 3 × (1/6) and simplify. Then explain in one sentence why the
denominator did not change.
(Answer: 3/6 = 1/2 — three groups of one sixth is three sixths, and
three sixths simplifies to one half. The denominator stays 6 because
multiplying by a whole number makes more pieces of the same size, not
smaller pieces. If you wrote 3/18, you multiplied the denominator too —
re-read Worked example 2, step 5.)

## Supports, extensions, teacher note

- **Reading/language support:** keep the sentence frame on a card: "___
  groups of ___ is ___ — multiply the numerator, keep the denominator."
- **Alternate response mode:** fold paper strips instead of drawing bars;
  point to the counted unit fractions instead of writing the
  explanation, with the adult scribing the sentence.
- **Extension:** compute 8 × (3/4) = 24/4 = 6 (a whole-number answer),
  and explain why the product came out whole. Then try 7 × (5/12) =
  35/12 = 2 11/12, and name the two whole numbers it lies between.
- **Teacher note — likely misconception:** learners who multiply the
  denominator too (Sam's and Zoe's error) are treating "multiply a
  fraction" like "make an equivalent fraction" from Unit 04. Contrast
  them directly: multiplying numerator AND denominator by 3 keeps the
  amount the same (equivalence); multiplying ONLY the numerator by 3
  makes three times as much (this lesson). **Answer-key reference:**
  [../answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 05.
