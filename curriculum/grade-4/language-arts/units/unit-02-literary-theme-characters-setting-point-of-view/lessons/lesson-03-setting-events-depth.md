# Lesson 03 — Setting and Events in Depth

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can describe a story's setting and its key
events from exact details — and compare what the words say with what a
picture shows."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; Story Parts
notebook; the illustration below.
**Standards:** RL.4.3 (setting, event in depth); RL.4.7 (text vs.
visual presentation).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Characters are the *who*. What two story parts answer
*where/when* and *what happened*?" (Setting; events/plot.) "Today's
rule: **a setting description needs four details — place, time, weather
or light, and one thing you can hear, smell, or feel.**"

## Explicit teaching

- **Setting** = where and when the story happens. Strong settings are
  built from *sensory* details: what you would see, hear, smell, and
  feel standing there.
- **Events** = what happens, in order. The **plot** is the chain of
  important events: problem → attempts → resolution.
- In "The Lantern on Gray Hill," collect setting details like a
  detective: *place* — "the round stone tower above Miller's Ford,"
  "the stone path as it climbed the hill in lazy S-curves"; *time* —
  "every evening at dusk," "the sun was already sinking"; *light/
  weather* — "the sky deepened to evening blue," "a thin crescent moon
  hung like a fingernail clipping"; *sound/touch/smell* — "tall grass
  whispered," "the tower door groaned," "the narrow stair smelled of oil
  and dust."
- RL.4.7 skill — **text vs. visual:** a picture of a scene and the
  words of a scene are two *presentations* of the same story. Ask both
  directions: (1) What do the **words** tell you that the picture
  cannot show? (2) What does the **picture** add that the words never
  say?

### Modeled example 1 — setting in depth from the text

*Adult models aloud:* "Place: 'the round stone tower above Miller's
Ford.' Time: 'every evening at dusk.' Light: 'the sky deepened to
evening blue.' Sound: 'tall grass whispered.' That's four details, one
from each box — place, time, light, sound. My setting sentence: 'The
story happens at dusk on Gray Hill above Miller's Ford, where a stone
path winds up to a round tower; the sky turns evening blue as grass
whispers around the climber.'"

### Modeled example 2 — text vs. picture (RL.4.7)

*Adult models aloud:* "Direction 1 — words the picture cannot show:
'the tower door groaned on its hinges — it had swollen in the damp.'
You can't *hear* a groan in a still picture, and you can't see 'swollen
in the damp.' Also 'the narrow stair smelled of oil and dust' — no
smell in a picture. Direction 2 — what the picture adds: the girl's
braid, her blue coat and brown boots — the story never describes her
clothes. And the *exact* S-shape of the path — the words say 'lazy
S-curves,' but the picture lets my eyes trace them. Both directions
count."

## Guided practice (together)

1. Name the story's **problem** (what goes wrong) and its
   **resolution** (how it ends) in one sentence each.
2. Put these events in order: Nora lights the wick / Grandmother's
   fever comes / the boat's horn answers / Nora pushes through the
   tower door.
3. Words → picture: name one setting detail from the **text** that does
   **not** appear in the illustration.
4. Picture → words: name one detail **visible** in the illustration
   that the story's words never mention.

## Independent practice

1. Write a 4–6 sentence setting description of Gray Hill at dusk using
   at least one detail from each box: place, time, light/weather, and
   sound/smell/touch. Quote or closely follow the text.
2. List the story's five key events in order (problem → climb → door →
   lighting → horn).
3. **Application:** Draw or describe the setting of a story you know in
   four details (one per box). Then answer: what would an illustrator
   add that your words don't say?

## Exit check

"Give me two details from the text that the illustration cannot show,
and one detail from the illustration that the text never says."
(Accept: groaning/swollen door, smell of oil and dust, fogged glass,
boat's horn, matchbox, 'stomach knotted'; picture adds: braid, blue
coat, boots, exact path shape, star count.)

## Supports, alternatives, extensions

- **Support:** the adult supplies the four boxes as a chart with one
  example filled in; the learner finds the other three.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the setting as a labeled drawing
  first, then dictate the four sentences; use the text-only alternative
  instead of the image throughout.
- **Extension:** write the *next* paragraph of the story — Nora's walk
  home — keeping every setting detail consistent (dusk, hill, path);
  explain which two details an illustrator would most want to draw.

## Teacher note

**Watch for:** describing the illustration when asked for text details
(redirect: "finger on the sentence"); listing random details instead of
one per box; treating the picture as *proof* of story facts rather than
a second presentation.
