# Lesson 06 — Same Themes, Different Cultures

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can compare how stories from different
cultures treat the same theme."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; Story Parts
notebook.
**Standards:** RL.4.9 (themes across cultures); RL.4.10 (text
complexity).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "A **fable** is a short tale, often with animal
characters, that teaches a lesson — the **moral**. A **folktale** is a
traditional story passed down in a culture. Quick check: are fables and
folktales new inventions or old ones?" (Old — some are thousands of
years old, which is why they belong to everyone.) "Today we read two —
one from ancient Greece, one from Japan — and hunt for the *same*
theme wearing different clothes."

> **Source note (adult reads):** Both tales below are the curriculum's
> **original retellings** of public-domain traditional stories, written
> for this unit — not quoted translations. "The Tortoise and the Hare"
> is attributed to Aesop, a storyteller of ancient Greece (c. 6th
> century BCE). "Momotaro" is a Japanese folktale centuries old. The
> retellings keep each tale's well-known plot shape in the curriculum's
> own words.

### "The Tortoise and the Hare" (original retelling of Aesop's fable)

> Hare laughed at Tortoise's short legs. "Race me to the river," Hare
> bragged, "and try not to fall asleep on the way."
>
> At the starting line, Hare shot ahead like an arrow. By the first
> bend, he was so far ahead that he lay down in the clover for a nap.
> "Plenty of time," he yawned.
>
> Tortoise never stopped. Step, step, step — slow and steady, all the
> way to the riverbank. Hare woke to the sound of the other animals
> cheering. Tortoise had won.
>
> *Moral: slow and steady wins the race.*

### "Momotaro, the Peach Boy" (original retelling of the Japanese folktale)

> Long ago in Japan, a childless old couple found a giant peach floating
> down the river. Inside was a boy, whom they named Momotaro — Peach
> Boy. He grew strong and kind.
>
> When ogres from Ogre Island began stealing from the village, Momotaro
> said, "I will go and stop them." The old woman packed him millet
> dumplings for the journey. On the road, a dog, a monkey, and a
> pheasant each asked for a dumpling — and each joined him in return.
>
> At Ogre Island the ogres were huge and frightening, but Momotaro and
> his small companions fought together with courage and cleverness, and
> the ogres surrendered their stolen treasure. Momotaro shared it with
> the whole village.
>
> *Theme often told with it: courage and kindness overcome even the
> strongest fear.*

## Explicit teaching

- RL.4.9 asks you to compare **the treatment of similar themes** in
  stories from **different cultures** — and **patterns of events** like
  **the quest** (a hero journeys out, faces danger, and returns
  changed).
- Comparison routine: (1) name each story's theme in a full sentence;
  (2) find the **shared** theme underneath; (3) name one way each
  culture tells it **differently** (characters, setting, events).

### Modeled example 1 — shared theme

*Adult models aloud:* "Tortoise and Hare theme: 'Steady effort beats
showing off.' Momotaro theme: 'Courage and kindness can defeat even
giant fears.' The shared idea underneath: **'What looks weak can win
when it keeps going with a brave heart.'** Tortoise looks weak next to
Hare; Momotaro looks small next to ogres. Different clothes, same
theme."

### Modeled example 2 — told differently

*Adult models aloud:* "Greece tells it with **talking animals** in a
one-afternoon race — a fable with a stated moral. Japan tells it with a
**human boy and animal helpers** on a long **quest** to an island — a
folktale with feasting and treasure at the end. Same theme family;
different patterns of events: a race vs. a quest."

## Guided practice (together)

1. State the theme of "The Tortoise and the Hare" as a full sentence.
   (Not "slow and steady" — that's the topic.)
2. State the theme of "Momotaro" as a full sentence.
3. What is the **quest pattern** in Momotaro? (Leaves home → faces
   danger → returns with treasure.) Does the Tortoise story follow a
   quest, or a different pattern?
4. Name one **difference** in how the two cultures tell their story
   (characters, setting, or events).

## Independent practice

1. Write the **shared theme** of the two tales in one full sentence,
   with one detail from each tale supporting it.
2. Complete a two-column chart: "Told the same" (2 rows) / "Told
   differently" (2 rows), each row with a text detail.
3. **Application:** "The Lantern on Gray Hill" also follows a quest
   pattern (leaves home → climbs → lights the lantern → returns
   changed). Write two sentences comparing Nora's quest with
   Momotaro's: one similarity, one difference.

## Exit check

"Name the shared theme of today's two tales and one way the Greek and
Japanese tellings differ." (Accept: steady/brave effort beats
arrogance-or-fear; differs in characters — animals vs. boy + animal
helpers — or events — race vs. island quest.)

## Supports, alternatives, extensions

- **Support:** the adult supplies the shared-theme sentence frame:
  "Both stories show that ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** dictate the chart; draw the two quests
  as side-by-side paths with three labeled stops each.
- **Extension:** find the theme connection to "The Lantern on Gray
  Hill" — all three stories reward the character who keeps going;
  write a paragraph defending "persistence" as the unit's through-line
  theme, citing one detail per story.

## Teacher note

**Watch for:** restating the moral word-for-word instead of building a
theme sentence; claiming the stories are "the same" (they share a
theme, not a plot); treating the retellings as quoted ancient texts —
remind the learner these are the curriculum's own retellings of
traditional plots.
