# Lesson 04 — Poems, Drama, and Prose: Three Shapes of Story

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can tell a poem from a drama from prose by
their structural parts — and I can crack words that allude to
mythology."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; Story Parts
notebook.
**Standards:** RL.4.5 (poem/drama/prose structures); RL.4.4 (mythology
allusions).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "'The Lantern on Gray Hill' is written in sentences and
paragraphs. What is that shape called?" (Prose.) "Today we meet its two
cousins — and learn why calling a huge job 'Herculean' is a secret
handshake with an ancient myth."

## Explicit teaching

- **Prose** = ordinary sentences grouped into paragraphs. Stories and
  novels are prose. Clue: sentences run margin to margin.
- **Poetry** = words arranged in **verses** (lines) and **stanzas**
  (groups of lines, like paragraphs). Poems use **rhythm** (a beat
  pattern you can tap), **meter** (the measured pattern of stressed and
  unstressed syllables), and often **rhyme** (same ending sounds).
- **Drama** = a story written to be *performed*. Structural parts: a
  **cast of characters** (who's in it), a **setting description**
  (where/when the scene happens), **dialogue** (the spoken lines, with
  the speaker's name first), and **stage directions** (italic or
  bracketed notes telling actors what to do: *[she lifts the lantern]*).

### "October Wind" (original poem, written for this unit)

> October wind, you whistle low, *(verse 1)*
> You chase the dry leaves to and fro. *(verse 2)*
> You tug the squirrels' bushy tails, *(verse 3)*
> You rattle windows, fences, rails. *(verse 4)*
>
> October wind, you hum and moan, *(verse 5)*
> You will not leave my hat alone! *(verse 6)*
> You smell of apples, smoke, and rain — *(verse 7)*
> Come back and whistle low again. *(verse 8)*

Two stanzas, four verses each. Rhythm: tap it — *oc-TO-ber WIND, you
WHIS-tle LOW* — a bounce you can feel. Rhyme: low/fro (slant), tails/
rails, moan/alone, rain/again.

### "The Lost Compass" (original drama scene, written for this unit)

> **Cast of characters:**
> NORA, 10, determined and a little nervous
> SAM, 8, her brother, full of questions
>
> **Setting:** The attic of an old farmhouse. Evening. A trunk sits open,
> spilling scarves and a brass compass.
>
> NORA: *(lifting the compass)* It still points north! Grandpa's compass
> works!
>
> SAM: *(backing away)* Don't touch the trunk. Mom said the attic is
> off-limits.
>
> NORA: *(grinning)* Then it's a good thing we're explorers. Explorers
> don't read "off-limits" signs — they read compasses.
>
> SAM: *(peeking)* Which way is the treasure?

### Mythology allusions (RL.4.4)

An **allusion** is a word or phrase that points to a famous story
without retelling it — usually a **myth** (an ancient traditional tale).
Mythology allusions you will meet:

- **Herculean** (her-kyoo-LEE-un): like Hercules, the Greek hero famous
  for twelve impossible labors → an extremely hard task. *"Cleaning the
  garage was a Herculean job."*
- **Titanic**: like the Titans, the giants of Greek myth → enormous.
  *"They faced a titanic wave."*
- **Achilles' heel**: Achilles was a Greek hero invulnerable except his
  heel → someone's one weak spot. *"Spelling is his Achilles' heel."*
- **Pandora's box**: Pandora opened a forbidden jar and released
  troubles → an action that causes many unexpected problems. *"Opening
  that old argument was opening Pandora's box."*
- **Midas touch**: King Midas turned all he touched to gold → making
  everything succeed. *"Every garden she plants shows her Midas touch."*

You don't need the whole myth — you need the *meaning the word carries*,
found from context.

### Modeled example 1 — naming the form by its parts

*Adult models aloud:* "'October Wind' — I see verses grouped into two
stanzas, a tappable rhythm, and rhyming endings. That's a **poem**;
the structural clues are verse, stanza, rhythm, rhyme. 'The Lost
Compass' — I see a cast list, a setting description, names before spoken
lines, and bracketed notes like *(grinning)*. That's **drama**; the
clues are cast, setting description, dialogue, stage directions. The
Lantern story — paragraphs of sentences. **Prose.**"

### Modeled example 2 — cracking an allusion from context

*Adult models aloud:* "'Moving the piano upstairs was a Herculean
task.' I don't know Hercules' whole story, but the sentence is about an
extremely hard physical job, and I know Herculean points to a hero
famous for impossible labors. So Herculean ≈ 'requiring enormous
strength.' The context + the myth clue together unlock it."

## Guided practice (together)

1. Is "The Lost Compass" poem, drama, or prose? Name two structural
   parts that prove it.
2. Which structural part tells the actors what to *do* (not say)?
   Quote one from the scene.
3. "The test was a Herculean challenge, and we studied for weeks."
   What does *Herculean* mean here? What two clues did you use?
4. Tap the rhythm of verses 1–2 of "October Wind." Where do the
   stresses fall?

## Independent practice

1. Sort these into poem / drama / prose, naming the structural clue
   for each: (a) a recipe's steps, (b) "The Lost Compass," (c)
   "October Wind," (d) "The Lantern on Gray Hill."
2. Use each allusion in an original sentence that shows its meaning:
   *titanic, Achilles' heel, Pandora's box*.
3. **Application:** Turn one paragraph of "The Lantern on Gray Hill"
   (the tower-door paragraph) into three lines of stage directions for
   actors. Keep every story fact.

## Exit check

"Name one structural part found in drama but never in prose, and tell
me what *Herculean* means and which myth it points to." (Stage
directions — or cast list / setting description / dialogue labels;
Herculean = extremely difficult, from Hercules' labors.)

## Supports, alternatives, extensions

- **Support:** form-sorting cards with the structural parts printed;
  allusion word bank with meanings for matching.
- **Alternate response mode:** perform the drama scene with the adult
  (reader's theater) instead of writing about it; dictate allusion
  sentences.
- **Extension:** write a third stanza for "October Wind" keeping the
  4-verse shape and AABB rhyme; research (adult-guided) one more myth
  allusion (e.g., *odyssey*, *narcissistic*) and teach it to the adult.

## Teacher note

**Watch for:** calling any rhyming text a poem (songs and chants rhyme
too — check for verse/stanza structure); memorizing allusion
*definitions* without using context; confusing stage directions with
dialogue.
