# Lesson 06 — Spelling, Precise Words, Punctuation for Effect, and Formal vs. Informal English

**Goal:** I can spell grade-appropriate words by consulting a
reference, choose precise words, punctuate for effect, and rewrite
informal English formally — then revise and edit a draft with
guidance.
**Time:** about 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil,
dictionary or glossary (print or digital, adult-selected)
**Standards:** L.4.2.d, L.4.3.a–c, SL.4.6, W.4.5

## Readiness check

Spell *friend* aloud, then check it in the dictionary or glossary.
Did the spelling match? Good spellers do not guess and hope — they
check, especially for words that do not sound the way they look.

## Teach

### Spelling with references

Grade-4 spelling words in this unit, with a pattern or memory aid for
each:

| Word | Pattern / aid |
|---|---|
| because | *be-cause*: it names a cause |
| friend | *fri-end*: a friend to the end; the *i* comes before *e* here — check it, do not guess |
| different | *differ* + *-ent* |
| beautiful | *beau-ti-ful*: full of beauty |
| bicycle | *bi-* (two) + *cycle* (wheels) |
| library | *li-brar-y*: say it in three parts, not "liberry" |
| tomorrow | *to-* + *morrow* (the next day) |
| together | *to-gether*: gathered |
| carefully | *care* + *-ful* + *-ly* |
| favorite | *favor* + *-ite* |
| important | *import* + *-ant* |
| usually | *usual* + *-ly* |

When you are unsure, **consult a reference**: look the word up in a
dictionary or glossary, check its syllables, and copy it letter by
letter. Sounding it out helps, but English spelling is not always
sound-by-sound — *friend* and *beautiful* prove it.

### Precise words

A **precise word** says exactly what you mean. Vague words make the
reader guess:

- *went* → **pedaled** (how did she go? on a bicycle)
- *big* → **enormous** (how big? very big indeed)
- *said happily* → **cheered** (one word carries the feeling)
- *nice* → **kind** (nice about what? kind names the quality)

Test: if a reader could picture two different things from your word,
find a more precise one.

### Punctuation for effect

Punctuation is not only correct or incorrect — it changes how a
sentence feels:

- A period is calm and finished: "The kite flew high."
- An exclamation mark shows excitement or strong feeling: "The kite
  flew so high!"
- A question mark asks and invites an answer: "How high will the
  kite fly?"
- A comma creates a pause or separates items: "Maya, Jonah, and Sam
  watched the kite." (And in a compound sentence, the comma before
  *and, but, or, so* tells the reader where one thought ends and the
  next begins.)

Choose the mark that matches the feeling you want the reader to have
— then check that the sentence still follows the rules from
Lessons 04–05.

### Formal vs. informal English

**Formal English** is for presentations, letters to a teacher, and
reports: full sentences, polite requests, no slang. **Informal
English** is for friends and family talk: short sentences, casual
words, exclamations.

| Informal (talking with a friend) | Formal (presenting to the class) |
|---|---|
| "Hey! Can I grab that book?" | "Good morning. May I borrow that book, please?" |
| "That kite thing was awesome!" | "The kite flew remarkably high in the strong wind." |
| "Gotta go — see ya!" | "Thank you for listening. I must leave now." |

The situation decides, not the speaker's mood. A class presentation
calls for formal English even if you feel excited; a chat with a
friend allows informal English even if the topic is serious.

### Revising and editing with guidance (W.4.5)

**Revising** changes ideas and words (add a detail, swap a vague word
for a precise one). **Editing** fixes conventions (spelling,
capitals, punctuation). Do them in that order: revise first, so you
do not polish a sentence you are about to delete.

## Worked example 1 — editing a draft

Draft sentence: "yesterday me and jonah went to the libary and it was
fun."

Edits: capital *Y* for the sentence start; *Jonah and I* is the
formal order for people (and *I* is always capital); *went* is vague
→ **rode our bikes** (precise, if that is what happened);
*libary* → check the dictionary → **library**; add the comma
before *and* in the compound sentence; *fun* is vague → **exciting**
or a precise detail.

Revised: "Yesterday, Jonah and I rode our bikes to the library, and
it was exciting." Better still with a detail: "Yesterday, Jonah and I
rode our bikes to the library, and we found a book about kites."

## Worked example 2 — formal rewrite

Informal: "Hey Ms. Reed, can I grab the kite book? It's super cool!"

Formal, for a class request: "Good morning, Ms. Reed. May I borrow
the book about kites, please? I found it very interesting."

What changed: a greeting, *may* for polite permission, *borrow*
instead of *grab*, *very interesting* instead of *super cool*, and a
period instead of an exclamation mark. The meaning is the same; the
register fits the situation.

## Guided practice

Work together, dictionary or glossary open.

1. Spell-check: which word is misspelled — *because, freind,
   different*? Correct it and name its pattern.
2. Spell-check: write *beautiful* from memory, then check it letter
   by letter. What part is easiest to get wrong?
3. Precise word: replace *went* in "She went home" with a verb that
   shows how (walked, pedaled, hurried — pick one that fits a
   bicycle trip).
4. Punctuation for effect: rewrite "The dog caught the ball" as an
   excited sentence, then as a question.
5. Formal rewrite: rewrite "Hey! That's my ball, give it back!" as a
   polite formal request.
6. Revise or edit? "Swap *big* for *enormous*" — is that revising or
   editing? "Fix the capital at the start of a sentence" — revising
   or editing?

## Independent practice

1. Copy these words correctly after checking them: *tomorrow,
   together, carefully, favorite*. (Adult: check against the list.)
2. Replace the vague word in each sentence: (a) "The dog was big."
   (b) "Maya said the news." (Use a precise verb: *whispered,
   announced, explained* — pick the one that fits good news told
   quietly, or explain your choice.)
3. Punctuate for effect: write the sentence "The kite flew over the
   trees" three ways — calm (period), excited (exclamation), curious
   (question).
4. Formal or informal? Label each: (a) "Good afternoon, class. Today
   I will describe our park visit." (b) "Wanna come to the park
   after school?"
5. Edit this sentence (3 errors): "last friday, we went to the
   libary and read books."
6. Revise this sentence (vague words): "It was a nice day and we did
   fun stuff." (Add at least one precise verb and one precise
   adjective, and name what you changed.)

## Applied task

Edit and revise the "My Park Visit" draft below. Find at least 3
spelling or capitalization errors, swap at least 2 vague words for
precise ones, and rewrite the last sentence in formal English, as if
presenting it to the class.

> "yesterday, me and sam went to the park. it was a nice day. we saw
> a big dog and it went fast. we had fun. hey class, the park was
> cool!"

## Exit check

Rewrite this informal sentence formally, and name one spelling
strategy you would use for the word *beautiful*:

> "Hey, can I grab that awesome book?"

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the 12-word spelling list open during every
  writing task; checking is the skill, not memorizing under
  pressure. For formal rewrites, start from a frame: "Good morning.
  May I …, please?"
- **Extension:** revise a sentence twice — once for a friend
  (informal, warm, short) and once for a presentation (formal,
  precise, full sentences) — and explain, word by word, what changed
  and why the situation required it.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: thinking revising and editing are the same
job; thinking formal English means "longer words" rather than "right
for the situation"; guessing a spelling instead of checking it.
Reteach with the revise-first order and the situation test. Full
solutions are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md) under Lesson 06.
