# Lesson 04 — Fragments, Run-Ons, Sentence Combining, and Confused Words

**Goal:** I can fix fragments and run-ons, combine short sentences
into a compound sentence with a comma and a conjunction, and choose
the right confused word.
**Time:** about 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil,
[park-sentence-scene.png](../assets/park-sentence-scene.png)
**Standards:** L.4.1.f, L.4.1.g, L.4.2.c

## Readiness check

Read these aloud. Which one is a complete sentence?

- "Running to school."
- "Maya ran to school."

The second has a subject (*Maya*) and a verb (*ran*) and makes sense
alone. The first is missing both — it is a fragment.

## Teach

### Complete sentences, fragments, and run-ons

A **complete sentence** has a subject and a predicate (a verb plus
what goes with it), starts with a capital, and ends with a period,
question mark, or exclamation mark.

A **fragment** is a group of words that looks like a sentence but is
missing a subject, a verb, or a complete thought:

- Missing a subject: "Ran to the park." (Who ran?)
- Missing a verb: "The dog in the park." (What did the dog do?)
- A dependent clause alone: "Because it rained." (Because it rained…
  what happened?)

Fix a fragment by adding what is missing, or by joining it to a
sentence it belongs with: "Because it rained, we stayed inside."

A **run-on** squeezes two complete sentences together with no
punctuation or joining word:

> "Maya rode her bike Jonah flew his kite."

Fix a run-on in one of two grade-4 ways: split it into two sentences
("Maya rode her bike. Jonah flew his kite."), or join it with a comma
and a **coordinating conjunction** — *and, but, or, so* — to make a
**compound sentence**:

> "Maya rode her bike**, and** Jonah flew his kite."

The comma goes **before** the conjunction, not after it. A comma
alone, with no conjunction ("Maya rode her bike, Jonah flew his
kite"), does not fix a run-on.

### Frequently confused words

| Words | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *to* | direction, or part of a verb (*to ride*) | We walked **to** the park. |
| *too* | also, or more than enough | I want to come, **too**. / That bag is **too** heavy. |
| *two* | the number 2 | She has **two** kites. |
| *there* | a place | The ball is over **there**. |
| *their* | belonging to them | That is **their** dog. |

Test: if you can swap in *also* or *very*, the word is *too*. If you
can swap in *here*, the word is *there*. If you can swap in *our*,
the word is *their*.

## Worked example 1 — fixing a fragment and a run-on

Fragment: "Running through the park."

Ask: who is running? Add a subject and finish the thought: "Maya
**was running through the park**." Now it has a subject (*Maya*) and
a verb (*was running*).

Run-on: "The dog chased the ball the ducks swam away."

Two complete thoughts, no join. Split them: "The dog chased the ball.
The ducks swam away." Or combine them: "The dog chased the ball**,
and** the ducks swam away." Both fixes are correct; the combined
version shows the two events happening at the same time.

## Worked example 2 — combining with *but* and *so*

Short sentences: "Jonah wanted to fly his kite. The wind was too
weak."

The second sentence limits the first, so *but* fits: "Jonah wanted
to fly his kite**, but** the wind was too weak."

Short sentences: "It started to rain. We packed up our picnic."

The second sentence is the result of the first, so *so* fits: "It
started to rain**, so** we packed up our picnic."

Choosing the conjunction is choosing the relationship: *and* adds,
*but* contrasts, *or* offers a choice, *so* shows a result.

A brown dog chases a yellow ball across the grass. Two ducks swim in a small pond behind them.](../assets/park-sentence-scene.png)

of a real park visit."*

**Text-only alternative** (carries every fact the activities need):
"A girl in a red jacket rides a blue bicycle along a winding path. A
boy in a yellow shirt flies a red kite in the sky. An adult sits on a
wooden bench under a large leafy tree, reading a book. A brown dog
chases a yellow ball across the grass. Two ducks swim in a small pond
in the background, with houses and trees behind them."

Picture sentences (model): "The girl rides her blue bicycle." and
"The brown dog chases the yellow ball." Combined: "The girl rides her
blue bicycle, and the brown dog chases the yellow ball." Notice the
comma before *and*, and that each half could stand alone — that is
what makes it a compound sentence, not a run-on.

## Guided practice

Work together.

1. Fragment or complete? "After lunch in the park." Fix it or explain
   why it is complete.
2. Fragment or complete? "The ducks swam in the pond." Explain your
   answer.
3. Fix this run-on two ways (split, then combine): "Maya read her
   book Jonah flew his kite."
4. Combine with the best conjunction: "We can walk to the park. We
   can ride our bikes." (Choice — which conjunction?)
5. Choose: "We are going ___ the library, and Sam is coming, ___."
   (*to / too / two*)
6. Choose: "___ dog is over ___, by the bench." (*There / Their*)

## Independent practice

1. Fix the fragment: "Because the wind was strong."
2. Fix the fragment: "The boy with the red kite."
3. Fix the run-on: "The sun shone the children played all afternoon."
4. Combine: "Maya packed a lunch. She forgot her water bottle." Use
   *but*.
5. Combine: "It was hot. We drank cold water." Use *so*.
6. Choose the right words: "I have ___ tickets, and I want to go,
   ___." (*to / too / two* — two blanks, in order)

## Applied task

Look at the park picture (or read the text-only alternative). Write
four short sentences about four different things happening in the
picture. Then combine two of the pairs into compound sentences with a
comma before *and, but, or,* or *so*. Read the result aloud and check
that each half could stand alone.

## Exit check

Fix this run-on, then explain the fix in one sentence:

> "The dog chased the ball the girl rode her bike."

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** test every group of words with two questions: "Who or
  what?" and "Did what?" If either has no answer, it is a fragment.
  For run-ons, cover the second half: if the first half is already a
  sentence, you need a period or a comma plus a conjunction.
- **Extension:** combine three short sentences into one sentence
  with a relative clause and a compound join, for example: "The girl
  who rides the blue bicycle waved, and the dog chased the ball."
  Then explain each join.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: fixing a run-on with a comma alone; calling a
long sentence a run-on (length is not the test — missing joins are);
mixing up *their* and *there* by sound alone. Reteach with the
stand-alone test and the swap tests (*also / here / our*). Full
solutions are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md) under Lesson 04.
