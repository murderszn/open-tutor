# Lesson 04 — Context Clues: The Words Around the Word

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can figure out an unknown word by using the
words around it — and name the kind of clue I used."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; the unit word
sets; pencil and paper.
**Standards:** L.4.4.a.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult says: "The *weary* traveler sat down." "You may not know
*weary*. What nearby words help?" (*traveler, sat down* — someone who
sat down after traveling is probably tired.) "Today we name the three
clue types authors hide in sentences."

## Explicit teaching

A **context clue** is a hint in the surrounding words. Authors leave
three favorite kinds:

1. **Definition clue** — the sentence defines the word, often after a
   comma, dash, or the words *means* / *is*. Signal: "The *murmur*,
   a soft low sound, filled the hall."
2. **Example clue** — the sentence gives examples. Signal words:
   *such as, like, including, for example.* "Bring *sturdy* shoes,
   such as boots or sneakers, for the hike."
3. **Restatement clue** — the sentence says it again in simpler words.
   Signal words: *or, in other words, that is.* "The room was *vacant*,
   or empty, and silent."

The detective routine: **read the whole sentence → find the signal →
name the clue type → guess the meaning → re-read with your guess in
place** (does the sentence still make sense?).

### Modeled example 1 — definition clue

"The *triumph*, a great victory, made the whole town cheer."
*Adult models:* "Signal: the comma phrase 'a great victory' defines
*triumph* right in the sentence — definition clue. Guess: a great
victory. Re-read: 'The great victory made the whole town cheer.'
Makes sense. Arrested."

### Modeled example 2 — example clue

"Desert animals, such as lizards, snakes, and roadrunners, are *cautious*
around water holes."
*Adult models:* "Signal: 'such as' with examples — example clue. The
examples are careful animals, so *cautious* means careful, watchful.
Re-read: '…are careful around water holes.' Fits."

### Modeled example 3 — restatement clue, plus a miss

"The *brisk* wind, cold and sharp, cut through our jackets."
*Adult models:* "Signal: 'or'-style restatement — 'cold and sharp'
restates *brisk* — restatement clue. *Brisk* means cold and sharp, quick
and biting. Re-read to confirm." Then the adult models a miss: "At
first I guessed *brisk* meant 'gentle' — but 'cut through our jackets'
contradicts gentle, so I dropped it. Clues can correct your first
guess; let them."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 6 tasks)

1. "The *shimmer* of the lake, the sparkling light on the water, hurt
   her eyes." (Restatement — "the sparkling light on the water";
   *shimmer* = sparkling light.)
2. "Pack *sturdy* bags, like backpacks and duffels, for camp."
   (Example — "like backpacks and duffels"; *sturdy* = strong, tough.)
3. "A *vacant* lot, an empty piece of land, sat behind the store."
   (Definition — "an empty piece of land"; *vacant* = empty.)
4. "The *weary*, exhausted climbers rested at the base." (Restatement —
   "exhausted"; *weary* = very tired.)
5. **Which clue?** "She gave a *generous*, or giving, portion to each
   guest." (Restatement — "or giving"; *generous* = giving freely.)
6. **Error-analysis:** the adult claims, "In 'The *enormous*,
   mountain-sized cake fed fifty people,' the clue is an example
   clue." The learner corrects: (It is a definition/restatement clue —
   "mountain-sized" defines *enormous*; no "such as" examples appear.
   *Enormous* = very large.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 6 tasks)

1. "The *murmur* of the crowd, a low buzzing sound, grew louder."
   (Definition; *murmur* = low buzzing sound.)
2. "Nocturnal animals, for example owls and bats, sleep during the
   day." (Example; *nocturnal* = active at night.)
3. "His *brisk* pace, a quick energetic walk, got us there early."
   (Restatement; *brisk* = quick and energetic.)
4. "The hikers felt *weary*, or worn out, after the long climb."
   (Restatement/definition; *weary* = worn out, very tired.)
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why do you re-read the sentence with
   your guess in place?" (To check the guess — if the sentence stops
   making sense, the guess is wrong.)
6. **Detective's choice:** the learner picks any 3 unit context-clue
   words not yet used, writes one sentence per word containing a
   labeled clue, and solves a partner's (the adult's) three in return.

## Application

"Clue case cards." The learner makes 6 cards: unknown word on front;
on back, a sentence with a labeled clue type and the solved meaning.
Cards go in the Evidence Case File and are used for the session-15
review game. At least two cards must use each of two different clue
types.

## Exit check

The adult dictates two new sentences with unknown words (e.g., "The
*ancient*, very old map crumbled at the edges." / "Desert plants, such
as cacti and sagebrush, store water."). The learner names the clue
type and the meaning for each. Success: both correct with the signal
words pointed out.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads each sentence aloud;
  signal words may be highlighted; the learner may answer orally.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort pre-written clue
  sentences into three labeled boxes (definition / example /
  restatement) instead of writing sentences.
- **Extension:** hunt this week's reading for one real example of each
  clue type "in the wild"; log the sentence, the signal, and the
  solved meaning.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** naming a meaning without naming the clue, or
calling every comma phrase a definition. Require the signal words to be
pointed at. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-04). If the learner leans on
one clue type, deal three mixed cards and require the label before the
meaning.
