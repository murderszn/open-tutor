# Lesson 01 — Say It with the Text: Explicit Meaning and Quoting Evidence

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find the exact words in a text that answer
a question — and quote them as my evidence."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; a pencil and paper
(or the Evidence Case File notebook, started today).
**Standards:** RL.4.1, RI.4.1 (explicit meaning).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If I asked you what color the sky was in a story, where
would you look for the answer — in your head, or in the story's words?"
(In the story's words.) "Today we practice a detective rule: **every
answer must point to the exact words that prove it.** Those words are
called **evidence**, and what the words say right out loud — no guessing
needed — is called the **explicit** meaning."

## Explicit teaching

**Explicit** means stated right in the text — you can put your finger on
the sentence. **Evidence** is the exact word or words you quote to prove
your answer. Detectives never say "just trust me"; they show the clue.

The quoting routine (teach it as three steps, and use it all unit):

1. **Answer** the question in your own words.
2. **Point** to the sentence that says it — touch it with your finger.
3. **Quote** the exact words: "The text says…" and copy them word for
   word.

Quoting word-for-word matters: changing even one word can change the
meaning, and in later units your evidence has to survive a skeptic.

### The unit's two anchor passages

Read each passage aloud together first. They return in later sessions,
so read them like you will meet them again.

**"The Empty Bowl" (original story, written for this unit)**

> Maya set her backpack by the door and called, "Biscuit! Dinner!"
> No barking answered. She found the dog's bowl in the kitchen, empty
> and dry. A note in her brother's handwriting leaned against it: *Took
> Biscuit to the park. Back by six. — Leo.* The clock on the microwave
> said 5:40. Maya sighed, filled the bowl with kibble, and poured fresh
> water into the second bowl. "He'll be starving," she muttered, "and
> Leo never remembers the water." She set both bowls by the back door
> and sat on the steps to wait.

**"Beaver Builders" (original informational paragraph, written for this unit)**

> Beavers are rodents that reshape the streams where they live. Using
> their strong front teeth, they chew through aspen and willow trunks,
> then drag the branches into the water. They pile mud, stones, and
> sticks to build dams, which slow the stream and form ponds. The pond
> protects the beaver lodge — a dome of branches with underwater
> entrances — from predators such as coyotes. A beaver's teeth never
> stop growing, so constant chewing keeps them worn to a useful length.

### Modeled example 1 — explicit answer from the story

*Question:* Where did Maya find Biscuit's bowl?
*Adult models the routine aloud:* "My answer: in the kitchen. Now I
point — my finger is on this sentence: 'She found the dog's bowl in the
kitchen, empty and dry.' And I quote it: the text says, 'She found the
dog's bowl in the kitchen, empty and dry.' See how I did not say 'the
hallway' or 'near the door'? The evidence decides."

### Modeled example 2 — explicit answer from the informational text

*Question:* What do beavers use to build their dams?
*Adult models:* "My answer: mud, stones, and sticks. Pointing: 'They
pile mud, stones, and sticks to build dams.' Quote: the text says,
'They pile mud, stones, and sticks to build dams.' Notice the sentence
also tells *why* — 'which slow the stream and form ponds' — but the
question only asked *what*, so I quote only the what. Quote what answers
the question; leave the rest."

### A common trap (model it)

*Question:* Why did Maya fill the water bowl?
A careless answer: "Because dogs need water." That is true in real life,
but it is not what the passage says. *Adult models the fix:* "The text
says, 'Leo never remembers the water.' So my evidence-based answer is:
because Leo never remembers the water. Real-life facts are not evidence
— only the text's words are."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 6 tasks)

1. "What time did the microwave clock say?" (Answer: 5:40. Evidence:
   "The clock on the microwave said 5:40.")
2. "Who took Biscuit to the park?" (Leo. Evidence: "Took Biscuit to
   the park. Back by six. — Leo.")
3. **Find-the-finger:** the adult asks, "What did Maya put in the first
   bowl?" The learner must touch the sentence before answering. ("filled
   the bowl with kibble.")
4. "What kind of teeth do beavers use to chew trunks?" (Strong front
   teeth. Evidence: "Using their strong front teeth, they chew through
   aspen and willow trunks.")
5. "Why does the pond protect the lodge?" (It protects it from
   predators such as coyotes. Evidence: "The pond protects the beaver
   lodge … from predators such as coyotes.")
6. **Error-analysis:** the adult "answers" — "Beavers build dams with
   leaves and grass." "Am I right? Check me." (Wrong. The text lists
   mud, stones, and sticks — no leaves or grass. The learner quotes the
   real sentence to correct the adult.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 6 tasks)

1. "What did Maya set by the back door?" (Both bowls / the food bowl
   and the water bowl. Evidence: "She set both bowls by the back door.")
2. "Where did Maya sit to wait?" (On the steps. Evidence: "sat on the
   steps to wait.")
3. "What two kinds of trees does the passage name?" (Aspen and willow.
   Evidence: "chew through aspen and willow trunks.")
4. "What is the lodge made of, and how do beavers get in?" (A dome of
   branches; underwater entrances. Evidence: "a dome of branches with
   underwater entrances.")
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why must you quote the exact words
   instead of just giving the answer?" (Because the exact words prove
   the answer came from the text, not from memory or guessing; anyone
   can check.)
6. Speed round: the adult asks 4 new explicit questions across the two
   passages; the learner answers with a quote each time. Record the
   count correct.

## Application

"Evidence arrest." The learner re-reads "The Empty Bowl" and "arrests"
three details — writes each quoted detail on a slip of paper, labels
what question it answers, and files the slips in the Evidence Case File
notebook (the project). One slip must come from "Beaver Builders."

## Exit check

The adult asks two unpracticed explicit questions (e.g., "What did the
note say about when Leo would return?" — "Back by six"; "What keeps a
beaver's teeth a useful length?" — "constant chewing keeps them worn").
The learner answers each with an exact quote. Success: both answers
correct with quotes that match the text word for word.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads each passage aloud first;
  the learner may answer orally and dictate quotes for the adult to
  scribe, then check the copy against the text together.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may underline or highlight
  the evidence sentence instead of copying it, then read the
  highlighting aloud.
- **Extension:** the learner writes one *new* explicit question per
  passage that a classmate could answer only by quoting — and answers
  it themselves with the quote.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** answering from background knowledge ("dogs
need water") instead of the text. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-01). If the learner paraphrases
instead of quoting, accept the answer but require the finger-point and
one exact quoted phrase before moving on.
