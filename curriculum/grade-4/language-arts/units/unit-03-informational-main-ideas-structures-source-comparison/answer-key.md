# Answer Key — Unit 03

**For the guiding adult only.** Wording may differ from the learner's;
credit any answer that matches the key's reasoning. Main-idea items
accept any full sentence covering what most of the passage does;
reject topic words, opinions, and single details.

## Lesson 01 — Main idea, key details, summary

**Guided practice**

1. Topic: leaves in autumn. Main idea (accept variants): "In autumn,
   leaves change color because chlorophyll fades and hidden colors
   appear."
2. Key detail with quote: "The tree stops making chlorophyll, and
   the green color fades away." Interesting but not key: the list
   "sunlight, water, and air" — the main idea still stands without
   that list.
3. "Some leaves turn bright red" is a key detail, not a main idea:
   it covers one part of the passage, not the whole change the
   passage explains.
4. (b) is the best one-sentence summary. (a) covers only food-making;
   (c) covers only the ending.

**Independent practice**

1. Accept any one-sentence version of the Lesson 01 main idea, in
   the learner's own words.
2. Any three of: tree stops making chlorophyll / green fades;
   yellow and orange colors hidden all along show; some trees make
   red pigment in autumn; shorter days and cooler air trigger the
   change — each with its proving sentence.
3. Summary must contain the main idea + at least two key details,
   in the learner's own words, no copied sentences.
4. "Leaves are pretty" is an opinion about the topic, not a point
   the passage explains; a better main idea is the Lesson 01 main
   idea above.

**Exit check:** Topic = what a passage is about in a word or two
("leaves in autumn"); main idea = the full-sentence point about
that topic ("in autumn, leaves change color because chlorophyll
fades and hidden colors appear").

## Lesson 02 — What happened and why

**Guided practice**

1. In order: (1) plant seeds in warm soil after the last frost;
   (2) seeds germinate — root down, shoot up; (3) shoot becomes a
   seedling with two small leaves; (4) vine grows with big leaves
   and yellow flowers; (5) bees pollinate the flowers; (6) a small
   pumpkin grows and ripens from green to orange; (7) farmers
   harvest in autumn.
2. Bees must visit so flowers are pollinated: "After a flower is
   pollinated, a small green pumpkin begins to grow behind it."
   Without pollination, no pumpkin begins.
3. *Germinate:* "the seeds germinate: a tiny root grows down and a
   shoot pushes up through the soil." *Seedling:* "The shoot
   becomes a seedling with two small leaves."
4. Plant after the last frost because seeds need warm soil; the
   passage states "warm soil," and the learner may infer frost
   would leave soil too cold — that part is inference, not stated.

**Independent practice**

1. Seedling → vine with leaves and flowers → bees pollinate →
   small green pumpkin grows → ripens orange → harvest; at least
   one quoted sentence required.
2. Cause: the pumpkin stores food and ripens in the sun. Effect: it
   turns from green to orange. Quote: "All summer the pumpkin grows
   bigger and turns from green to orange, because it is storing
   food and ripening in the sun."
3. Correction: pumpkins are not painted; they turn orange because
   they store food and ripen in the sun (quote as in item 2).
4. *Vine:* a long, trailing stem that grows along the ground.
   *Pollinate:* when pollen moves to a flower so it can make seeds
   or fruit (bees carry pollen flower to flower). *Harvest:* to
   pick or gather a crop when it is ready. Dictionary checks may
   add wording; accept if the core meaning matches.

**Exit check:** First step: plant seeds in warm soil after the last
frost. Last step: harvest in autumn when the skin is hard and deep
orange. Orange because the pumpkin stores food and ripens in the sun.

## Lesson 03 — Structures, accounts, picture

**Guided practice**

1. A: chronology — "first," "next," "then," "finally." B:
   comparison — "both," "on the other hand," "while." C:
   cause/effect — "because," "as a result." D: problem/solution —
   "the problem was," "to solve this."
2. Similarity: a bridge and a ferry both carry people across a
   river. Difference: a bridge stays in place and works at any hour;
   a ferry moves back and forth and riders must wait. Without the
   similarity sentence, the comparison loses its "alike" half.
3. Firsthand: Maya's diary. Proof: "I knelt in the dirt and pressed
   the soil around my little seedling with both hands."
4. Picture details matching the diary: a girl pressing soil around
   a seedling; a boy watering a row; seedlings in a raised bed. The
   picture cannot show: the watering schedule (twice a week until
   summer), the food-bank donation, or the total of four beds.

**Independent practice**

1. D is problem/solution: it names a problem (noisy reading corner)
   and a solution ("To solve this, the librarian moved the reading
   corner to the quiet back room and put a thick rug on the
   floor"). The "made it hard to concentrate" clause sits inside
   the problem, but the passage's job is problem → solution.
2. Only the diary: the soil smelled like rain; Maya felt proud; her
   row was the straightest. Only the report: four beds; watering
   twice a week until summer; harvest donated to the town food bank.
3. How many beds → the report (four raised beds). How planting felt
   → the diary (cool soil, pride, laughter).
4. Any three picture facts: girl in a red cap kneeling; boy in a
   blue shirt watering with a green can; tray of potted seedlings
   on the grass; raised bed with seedling rows; wooden fence, leafy
   tree, blue sky with white clouds.

**Exit check:** Chronology — "then"; comparison — "both" /
"however"; cause/effect — "because"; problem/solution — "to solve
this." Test: was the writer in the event, or learning about it
from others?

## Lesson 04 — Charts, reasons, evidence

**Guided practice**

1. Point: every school should have a garden. Reasons: (1) gardens
   teach where food comes from; (2) gardens give fresh food to
   taste; (3) gardens get children outside and moving (exercise).
2. Evidence for reason 2: "At Riverside School, students harvested
   59 pounds of tomatoes and 48 pounds of carrots in one summer,
   and the school cook used them in lunch." The numbers are about
   food amounts, not about exercise, so they do not support
   reason 3.
3. Most tomatoes: July, 20 pounds. Most carrots: September, 16 pounds.
4. Carrots rise across the four months: 8 → 10 → 14 → 16 pounds.
   Tomatoes peak in July (20) and fall to 9 in September.

**Independent practice**

1. September total: 9 + 16 = 25 pounds. It means the combined
   tomato + carrot harvest in September only; it says nothing about
   other months or the summer total.
2. The chart shows harvest counts, not health or intelligence
   outcomes. Evidence for "smarter" would need, for example, a
   comparison of test or focus measures (practice example).
3. Accept any sensible reason + supporting evidence; invented
   evidence must be labeled "practice example, not a real fact."
4. The picture adds visual facts the report's words do not give:
   what the gardeners are doing (kneeling, pressing soil, watering),
   the tray of potted seedlings, the fence and tree setting.

**Exit check:** Reason = why the author thinks so ("gardens teach
where food comes from"). Evidence = the fact backing it ("a child
who plants, waters, and watches a seed grow understands food in a
way no textbook can teach" — or the 59/48-pound harvest for the
fresh-food reason).

## Lesson 05 — Two texts, notes, sources, listening

**Guided practice**

1. How to compost: "turn weekly with fork," "bin with air holes."
   Why compost helps: "holds water in soil," "landfill scraps make
   warming gas."
2. Text A: "After a few months, the pile becomes dark, crumbly
   compost that smells like earth." How long: a few months.
3. Talk reason 1: trees clean the air. Evidence: one large tree can
   absorb about 48 pounds of carbon dioxide in a year.
4. Accept any sentence combining one Text A fact and one Text B
   fact, e.g., "Composting helps because the pile turns scraps into
   soil instead of landfill gas."

**Independent practice**

1. Notes: at least 3 phrases per category (How: collect peels /
   eggshells / leaves; bin with air holes; keep damp; turn weekly;
   months later dark and crumbly. Why: landfill scraps make warming
   gas; compost pile lets air in; holds water in soil; feeds plants
   without fertilizer; one less peel in a landfill). No copied
   sentences. Source list names both texts.
2. Integrated paragraph: 4–6 sentences using both texts, marked by
   source, ending with the learner's own conclusion sentence.
3. Listening: point — plant one hundred new trees. Reasons:
   (1) clean air — about 48 pounds of carbon dioxide per large tree
   per year; (2) cool streets — shaded streets several degrees
   cooler, less air conditioning; (3) wildlife homes — no number or
   example given, so weakest evidence.
4. Copying three sentences is not note-taking, and two side-by-side
   summaries are not integration. Fix: phrase notes from memory,
   weave both texts into one paragraph, end with an own conclusion.

**Exit check:** Two summaries sit side by side; an integrated
paragraph weaves facts from both texts and ends with a conclusion
needing both. A source list names each text used so a reader can
find and check it.

## Practice A — Fireflies

1. Topic: fireflies (how they glow). Main idea (accept variants):
   "Fireflies make cool light in their abdomens through a chemical
   reaction, and flash in patterns that help them find mates."
2. Any two key details with quotes: chemicals mix with oxygen in
   the abdomen and give off light; the light makes almost no heat;
   each kind has its own flash pattern; flashes help fireflies find
   mates of their own kind.
3. Interesting but not key: "also called lightning bugs" (a name) —
   the main idea survives without it.
4. (b) is the best summary. (a) is too narrow (just "insects");
   (c) is an opinion, not a summary.
5. "Fireflies are bugs" is a topic, not a main idea: it names what
   the passage is about without stating its point. Better: the
   Practice A item 1 main idea.
6. Accept any 2-sentence own-words summary containing the main idea
   and at least one key detail, with no copied sentences.

## Practice B — Thermometer

1. In order: bulb warms → liquid expands → liquid rises up the
   tube; bulb cools → liquid contracts → liquid falls; read the
   temperature from the scale.
2. The liquid rises because it expands (takes up more room). Quote:
   "When the bulb gets warm, the liquid expands — it takes up more
   room — and rises up a thin tube."
3. The scale marks numbers in degrees along the tube. Without it,
   the liquid's height would show warmer/cooler but no temperature
   number.
4. *Expand:* take up more room. *Contract:* take up less room.
   Dictionary checks may refine wording; accept if core meaning
   matches.
5. Cause: the bulb gets cold. Effect: the liquid contracts and falls
   back down the tube.
6. Correction: numbers do not push the liquid; warmth makes the
   liquid expand and rise (quote as in item 2).

## Practice C — Structures, accounts, picture

1. (a) chronology — "first," "next," "then," "finally." (b)
   comparison — "both," "while." (c) cause/effect — "so," "as a
   result." (d) problem/solution — "too dark to read in" (problem),
   "to solve this" (solution).
2. Similarity: both bicycles and scooters have two wheels.
   Difference: a bicycle has pedals; a scooter is pushed with one
   foot.
3. Matching the diary: a girl pressing soil around a seedling; a
   boy watering; seedlings in a raised bed. Picture detail neither
   account names: the tray of potted seedlings on the grass (also
   acceptable: the wooden fence, the leafy tree, the blue sky).
4. Diary = firsthand ("my hands shook" — the writer felt it).
   Report = secondhand (counts from the event). "How did it feel?"
   → diary. "How many contestants?" → report.
5. Accept any two-sentence problem/solution rewrite keeping the
   facts (icy road, slow bus, late start) but organized as problem
   then solution. What changed: organization (problem first, then
   fix). What stayed true: the ice, the slow bus, the late start.
6. A picture shows one moment: it cannot show a schedule, a
   donation, a count, or a before/after change — all of which the
   report states in words.

## Practice D — Walking to school

1. Point: children should walk to school when it is safe. Reasons:
   (1) healthy exercise; (2) time with friends and noticing the
   neighborhood; (3) fewer car trips mean cleaner air.
2. No reason has a specific number or example; the chart shows how
   many children walked, not health, friendship, or air effects.
   Strengthen with an example or measurement for one reason, e.g.,
   a step count or an air-quality reading (label invented evidence
   as practice).
3. Most: Thursday, 35 children. Total check: 24 + 31 + 28 + 35 + 30
   = 148. (24 + 31 = 55; + 28 = 83; + 35 = 118; + 30 = 148.)
4. The chart adds the day-by-day pattern: Thursday highest (35),
   Monday lowest (24), total 148 — information the passage's words
   never give.
5. The chart shows counts of walkers, not health outcomes. Evidence
   for health would need, for example, fitness or activity measures
   before and after walking (practice example).
6. Accept any sensible reason + supporting evidence; invented
   evidence must be labeled "practice example, not a real fact."

## Practice E — Saving water

1. How to save (Text A): "turn off tap while brushing," "fix
   dripping faucets." Why it matters (Text B): "treatment plants
   use less energy," "more water stays in rivers."
2. Text A: cutting two minutes off a shower saves several gallons.
3. Notes: at least 3 phrases per category (How: turn off tap while
   brushing; shorter showers; fix drips; water plants morning or
   evening. Why: fresh water is limited; cleaning water takes
   energy; more water stays in rivers/lakes; lower water bill;
   town-wide savings add up). No copied sentences.
4. Source list names both texts: "Saving Water at Home" and "Why
   Saving Water Matters," both written for this unit.
5. Integrated paragraph: 4–6 sentences using both texts, marked by
   source, ending with the learner's own conclusion sentence.
6. Two side-by-side summaries are not integration. A weaving
   opening sentence, e.g.: "Saving water at home is easy, and it
   matters because every gallon saved keeps water in rivers and
   lowers the energy used to clean it."

## Week-14 quiz

1. Main idea (accept variants): "Water travels in a cycle:
   evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and collection, over
   and over."
2. In order: (1) sun warms water, water evaporates and rises;
   (2) gas cools and condenses into droplets, forming clouds;
   (3) droplets join and fall as rain; (4) rain collects in lakes
   and oceans, cycle restarts.
3. Chronology. Signal words (any two): "first," "next," "then,"
   "finally."
4. Droplets fall because they join together until heavy enough.
   Quote: "Then the droplets join together until they are heavy
   enough to fall as rain."
5. Any three picture facts (see Lesson 03 key, item 4).
6. Any one report-only fact: watering twice a week until summer;
   harvest donated to the town food bank; four raised beds; annual
   garden day at Riverside School.
7. Account A is firsthand ("I stood," "my heart pounding," "I
   cheered"). Account B is secondhand (reporter-style facts and
   totals from the event).
8. How many runners → B (eighty runners). How cheering felt → A
   (heart pounding, throat hurt). Each choice follows the source
   that was there (feelings) or that gathered totals (counts).
9. Point: schools should protect recess. Reasons: (1) exercise —
   running and playing build strong bodies; (2) a break, so
   children return ready to focus.
10. Reason 2 has evidence: "teachers reported that students
    concentrated better after recess than after a long stretch of
    lessons." Reason 1 has no specific evidence (no example or
    number). Evidence is recognizable as a specific fact, example,
    or number tied to its reason.

## Unit assessment

1. Main idea (accept variants): "The Moon seems to change shape
   because sunlight bounces off it and the part of its lit half we
   can see changes as it orbits Earth." Key details (any two):
   the Moon makes no light of its own; sunlight bounces off its
   surface; the visible lit part changes; the cycle takes about 29
   days; people used the full moon to mark time.
2. The Moon seems to change shape because, as it travels around
   Earth, the amount of its lit half we can see changes. Quote:
   "As the Moon travels around Earth, the amount of its lit half
   that we can see changes, and that is why the Moon seems to
   change shape."
3. *Cycle* here: a pattern that repeats in the same order and then
   starts again — new, quarter, full, quarter, new — over about 29
   days.
4. New moon to full moon: 14 days. Pattern: about 7 days between
   phases (0, 7, 14, 22, 29 — the last step is 7 days to the restart
   at 29), then the cycle restarts.
5. The explanatory paragraph is cause/effect: the cause is the
   changing visible lit half as the Moon orbits; the effect is the
   seeming shape change; signal phrase "that is why." (The table
   itself is organized chronologically by day — accept that
   observation as partial credit.)
6. Summary: 2–3 own-word sentences with the main idea + at least
   two key details, no copied sentences.
7. Text A is firsthand (Sam was there: "I was at the new playground
   on opening day, and I tried the tall slide three times").
   Text B is secondhand (reporter facts, parks-department source).
   Only A: feeling proud, third try, favorite climbing wall. Only
   B: $40,000 cost, more than two hundred children, Elm Street
   location, sandbox.
8. Sam's point: build more climbing walls. Reason: the climbing wall
   was Sam's favorite part. Evidence: Sam reached the top on the
   third try and felt proud. Strength: weak for a town-wide decision
   — one child's feeling supports Sam's own preference, but one
   experience alone does not show all children want more walls.
   Accept "strong for Sam's own feeling, weak as town-wide
   evidence" with that reasoning.
9. Cost → Text B ($40,000). Feeling → Text A (proud on the third
   try). The cost is a gathered fact; the feeling is firsthand
   experience.
10. Integrated paragraph: 4–6 sentences using facts from both texts
    (marked), ending with the learner's own conclusion sentence
    (e.g., "Opening day showed both a happy crowd and one child's
    proud climb — a playground can serve a whole town and still
    matter most to one climber").
11. (a) chronology; *sprouts* = begins to grow (a shoot comes up).
    (b) comparison; *groom* = clean themselves. (c) cause/effect;
    *damaged* = harmed or broken by the storm. (d) problem/solution;
    *soften* (here) = make quieter, less harsh (of sound).
12. Source list: "How the Moon Changes Shape" — informational
    passage — written for this unit; "Two Texts: The New
    Playground" — firsthand letter + secondhand report — written
    for this unit. Notes (phrases, not copies), e.g., *What the Moon
    does:* travels around Earth; lit half faces different ways.
    *Why it looks that way:* sunlight bounces off surface; visible
    lit part changes.
