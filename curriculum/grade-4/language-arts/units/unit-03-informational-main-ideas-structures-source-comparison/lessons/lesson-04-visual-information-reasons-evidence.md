# Lesson 04 — Charts, Pictures, Reasons, and Evidence

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can read a chart and a picture as
information, and I can explain how an author uses reasons and
evidence to support a point."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; a pencil and
paper.
**Standards:** RI.4.7, RI.4.8.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If someone says 'school gardens are great,' is that
a point — or a reason?" (A point: the claim. A reason explains
*why* the point is true; evidence is the fact, example, or number
that backs the reason.) "Today we practice pulling points, reasons,
and evidence apart — and reading numbers the way we read words."

## Explicit teaching

- **Point:** what the author wants you to believe ("Every school
  should have a garden.").
- **Reason:** why the author thinks so ("Gardens teach children
  where food comes from.").
- **Evidence:** the fact, example, or number that supports the
  reason ("In one school garden, students harvested 59 pounds of
  tomatoes in a summer.").
- A **chart** or table is information too: read the title, the row
  and column labels, then compare and total before concluding.
  Always ask: "What does this add that the words alone do not say?"

### The lesson passage (original, written for this unit)

**"Should Every School Have a Garden?"**

> Every school should have a garden. First, gardens teach children
> where food comes from. A child who plants a seed, waters it, and
> watches it grow understands food in a way no textbook can teach.
> Second, gardens give children fresh food to taste. At Riverside
> School, students harvested 59 pounds of tomatoes and 48 pounds of
> carrots in one summer, and the school cook used them in lunch.
> Third, gardens get children outside and moving: digging, carrying
> water, and weeding are real exercise. For all these reasons,
> schools should make room for a garden.

### The harvest chart (fictional practice data — made up to teach chart reading, not real garden results)

**Pounds harvested from the Riverside School garden, one summer**

| Month | Tomatoes | Carrots |
|---|---|---|
| June | 12 | 8 |
| July | 20 | 10 |
| August | 18 | 14 |
| September | 9 | 16 |
| **Total** | **59** | **48** |

### Modeled example 1 — point, reason, evidence

*Adult models:* "Point: every school should have a garden. Reason
1: gardens teach where food comes from. Evidence for reason 1: a
child who plants, waters, and watches a seed grow understands food
in a way no textbook can teach. Reason 2: gardens give fresh food
to taste. Evidence for reason 2: 59 pounds of tomatoes and 48
pounds of carrots in one summer, used in school lunch. Notice the
numbers are evidence for reason 2 — not for reason 1. Evidence must
match its reason."

### Modeled example 2 — reading the chart

*Adult models:* "Title: pounds harvested, one summer. Rows are
months; columns are vegetables. Check the totals myself: tomatoes
12 + 20 + 18 + 9 = 59. Carrots 8 + 10 + 14 + 16 = 48. The totals in
the table check out. What does the chart add? It shows *when* the
harvest came: tomatoes peaked in July (20 pounds) and fell in
September (9), while carrots kept rising to 16 in September. The
passage alone never says that. A chart can show a pattern words
hide."

### A common trap (model it)

A learner says, "The chart proves gardens are great." The adult
answers: "The chart proves *how much* was harvested, month by
month. 'Great' is a judgment — the numbers support the reason
about fresh food, but they say nothing about exercise or learning.
Evidence has a reach: do not stretch it past what it shows."

## Guided practice (adult hints allowed)

1. Name the author's point. Then name all three reasons in your own
   words.
2. For reason 2, quote the evidence sentence. Why do the numbers
   count as evidence for "fresh food to taste" but not for
   "exercise"?
3. From the chart: in which month were the most tomatoes harvested,
   and how many pounds? In which month were the most carrots
   harvested?
4. What pattern does the chart show about carrots across the four
   months? (Hint: read the carrot column top to bottom.)

## Independent practice

1. Add up the September column yourself: 9 + 16 = ? What does that
   total mean, and what does it not mean?
2. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "The chart shows gardens make
   children smarter." Explain in two sentences why the chart cannot
   show that, and what kind of evidence could.
3. Write one new reason of your own for school gardens, plus one
   piece of evidence that would support it. (Your evidence may be
   invented for practice — label it "practice example, not a real
   fact.")
4. Picture check: look again at the Lesson 03 garden illustration
   (or its text-only alternative). What does the picture add to
   the news report that the report's words do not give? Name two
   things.

## Applied task

The adult picks a short opinion paragraph (from a children's
magazine, a library book, or one the adult writes on the spot). The
learner marks it up: underline the point once, circle each reason,
and put a star next to each piece of evidence. Then the learner
reports: does every reason have evidence? If a reason has none, the
learner says so — an unsupported reason is a finding, not a failure.

## Exit check

Without looking back: "What is the difference between a reason and
evidence? Give one of each from today's passage." (Reason: gardens
teach where food comes from. Evidence: a child who plants, waters,
and watches a seed grow understands food in a way no textbook can
teach — or the 59/48-pound harvest for the fresh-food reason.)

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading support:** read the passage twice — once for the point,
  once with a finger under each reason.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may build the
  point/reason/evidence chain with three stacked cards instead of
  writing sentences.
- **Extension:** rewrite the passage's conclusion so it only claims
  what its evidence supports ("Gardens give fresh food and outdoor
  activity") and explain what you removed and why.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) point = reason; (2) any number = proof
of anything; (3) a chart's total is the answer to every chart
question. Reteach by asking "evidence *for what*?" after every
number. Full solutions: [answer key](../answer-key.md) § Lesson 04.
