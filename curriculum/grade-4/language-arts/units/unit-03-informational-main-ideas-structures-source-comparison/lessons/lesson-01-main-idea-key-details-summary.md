# Lesson 01 — Main Idea, Key Details, and Summary

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find the main idea of an informational
text, point to the key details that support it, and summarize the text
in my own words."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; a pencil and
paper (or the unit notebook).
**Standards:** RI.4.1, RI.4.2.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If a passage is mostly about how beavers build dams,
is 'beavers' the main idea — or just the topic?" (Just the topic. The
topic is what the passage is about in one or two words. The **main
idea** is the whole point the author makes about that topic, in a full
sentence.) "Today we learn to tell topic, main idea, and summary
apart — and to prove a main idea with key details."

## Explicit teaching

- **Topic:** what the passage is about, in a word or two ("leaves in
  autumn").
- **Main idea:** the most important point the author makes about the
  topic, in a complete sentence ("In autumn, leaves change color
  because the green pigment fades and other colors show through.").
- **Key detail:** a fact or example that helps explain or prove the
  main idea. An **interesting detail** can be fun without being key:
  if you removed it, the main idea would still stand.
- **Summary:** a short retelling in your own words that keeps the
  main idea and the key details and leaves out the small stuff.

The routine (use it all unit):

1. **Read** the whole passage once.
2. **Ask:** "What is this mostly about, and what is the author trying
   to tell me about it?" Answer in one sentence — that is your
   candidate main idea.
3. **Test it:** list details. Cross out any detail the main idea does
   not need. The details left are the key details.
4. **Summarize:** main idea + key details, in your own words.

### The lesson passage

Read it aloud together first.

**"Why Do Leaves Change Color?" (original, written for this unit)**

> In spring and summer, leaves are green because they are full of a
> green pigment called chlorophyll. Chlorophyll helps the leaf make
> food from sunlight, water, and air. When autumn comes, the days get
> shorter and the air gets cooler. The tree stops making chlorophyll,
> and the green color fades away. As the green fades, yellow and
> orange colors that were hidden in the leaf all along begin to show.
> Some trees also make a red pigment in autumn, which is why some
> leaves turn bright red. After the color show, the leaves dry out,
> fall to the ground, and the tree rests until spring.

### Modeled example 1 — topic vs. main idea

*Adult models aloud:* "Topic: leaves in autumn — two words. Main
idea, in a full sentence: 'In autumn, leaves change color because
chlorophyll fades and hidden colors appear.' How do I know? Almost
every sentence is about the green fading or the other colors
showing. If I said the main idea was 'trees rest in winter,' that
would be wrong — only the last sentence is about resting, and it is
not what most of the passage explains."

### Modeled example 2 — key detail vs. interesting detail

*Adult models:* "Key detail: 'The tree stops making chlorophyll, and
the green color fades away.' Without it, the main idea makes no
sense — it is the reason the colors change. Interesting but not key:
the exact list 'sunlight, water, and air' — good science, but the
main idea still stands without it. Test every detail: 'If I remove
this, does the main idea fall apart?' If yes, it is key."

### A common trap (model it)

A learner says the main idea is "Leaves are green in summer." The
adult answers: "That is true, and it is in the passage — but it is
only the starting point. The passage is mostly about the *change*.
A main idea has to cover what most of the passage is doing, not just
its first sentence."

## Guided practice (adult hints allowed)

1. In your own words, what is the topic of the passage? What is its
   main idea? (Hint: start with "In autumn…")
2. Name one key detail and quote the sentence that proves it. Then
   name one interesting detail that is not key, and explain why the
   main idea survives without it.
3. Is "Some leaves turn bright red" a main idea or a key detail?
   Why? (Hint: does it cover the whole passage, or one part?)
4. Which sentence is the best one-sentence summary of the passage?
   (a) "Trees make food from sunlight." (b) "In autumn, leaves
   change color as chlorophyll fades and hidden colors appear."
   (c) "Leaves fall to the ground." Explain your choice.

## Independent practice

1. Write the main idea of the passage in one sentence of your own
   (not copied word for word).
2. List three key details, each with the sentence that proves it.
3. Write a summary of the passage in 2–3 sentences, in your own
   words. It must include the main idea and at least two key
   details, and no copied sentences.
4. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "Main idea: leaves are pretty."
   Explain in two sentences why that is a topic-flavored opinion,
   not a main idea — and give a better main idea.

## Applied task

Choose any short informational paragraph the adult selects (from a
library book, a magazine, or the resource pack). Apply the four-step
routine on paper: topic, candidate main idea, key details with
quotes, summary. Bring it to the next session; the adult checks the
routine, not just the answer.

## Exit check

Without looking back: "What is the difference between a topic and a
main idea? Give one example of each from today's passage." (Topic:
leaves in autumn. Main idea: in autumn, leaves change color because
chlorophyll fades and hidden colors appear.)

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the passage aloud first; the
  learner follows with a finger and re-reads the sentences they quote.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the summary
  while the adult scribes, then read it back and check it against
  the passage.
- **Extension:** write a main idea for the *last* paragraph alone
  ("After the color show, leaves fall and the tree rests") and
  explain why a paragraph can have its own smaller main idea inside
  a passage's bigger one.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) topic = main idea ("leaves"); (2) first
sentence = main idea; (3) any true detail = key detail. Reteach with
the removal test: cover a detail and ask whether the main idea still
makes sense. Full solutions: [answer key](../answer-key.md) § Lesson 01.
