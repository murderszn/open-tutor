# Lesson 03 — Text Structures, Firsthand and Secondhand Accounts

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can name how an informational text is
organized, tell a firsthand account from a secondhand one, and use a
picture as information."
**Time:** Two sessions of 30–35 minutes (part 1: structures; part 2:
accounts and the picture). **Materials:** this lesson; a pencil and
paper.
**Standards:** RI.4.5, RI.4.6, RI.4.7.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If you wanted to teach someone to make a sandwich,
would you list the steps in order — or compare sandwiches to salads?"
(In order.) "Authors choose an organization the way you choose a
tool: the job decides. Today we learn four organizations and two
kinds of accounts."

## Explicit teaching — part 1: four structures

| Structure | What it does | Signal words to listen for |
|---|---|---|
| **Chronology** | Puts events or steps in time order | first, then, next, after, finally, in the morning |
| **Comparison** | Shows how two things are alike and different | both, alike, different, however, while, on the other hand |
| **Cause/effect** | Explains why something happened and what followed | because, so, since, as a result, therefore |
| **Problem/solution** | Names a problem, then gives one or more solutions | the problem was, one solution, to solve this |

A passage can use more than one structure, but most short passages
have one main structure. Find it by asking: "Is this mostly telling
me a sequence, a comparison, a why, or a fix?"

### Four mini-passages (all original, written for this unit)

**A. "A Morning at the Market" (chronology)**

> At seven in the morning, the farmers unload their trucks. Next,
> they set out tables and arrange vegetables in neat rows. Then the
> market bell rings, and shoppers stream in. Finally, at noon, the
> farmers pack up what is left and drive home.

**B. "Two Ways to Cross a River" (comparison)**

> A bridge and a ferry both carry people across a river, but they
> work differently. A bridge stays in one place, and people walk or
> drive over it at any hour. A ferry, on the other hand, is a boat
> that moves back and forth, and riders must wait for it. Both are
> useful, but a bridge is faster, while a ferry can reach a shore
> where no bridge has been built.

**C. "Why the Playground Puddle Stayed" (cause/effect)**

> Last week it rained hard, and the schoolyard drain was blocked by
> leaves. Because the water had nowhere to go, a large puddle formed
> near the swings. As a result, the puddle stayed for three days,
> until the caretaker cleared the drain and the water flowed away.

**D. "The Noisy Library Problem" (problem/solution)**

> The library had a problem: the reading corner sat next to the
> hallway, and hallway noise made it hard to concentrate. To solve
> this, the librarian moved the reading corner to the quiet back
> room and put a thick rug on the floor. Now readers can hear
> themselves think.

### Modeled example 1 — naming a structure

*Adult models with passage C:* "Signal words: 'because,' 'as a
result,' 'until.' The passage explains why the puddle formed and
why it stayed — a why-and-what-followed passage. Structure:
cause/effect. The cause is the blocked drain plus the rain; the
effect is the puddle that stayed three days."

### Modeled example 2 — comparison is not just differences

*Adult models with passage B:* "A learner said passage B is only
about differences. But it starts 'both carry people across a river'
— that is a similarity. Comparison covers alike *and* different.
If you only list differences, you have half the structure."

## Explicit teaching — part 2: firsthand vs. secondhand

- A **firsthand account** is written by someone who was there and
  took part — a diary, a letter from a participant, a journal entry.
  It shows what the person saw, felt, and did, but only their corner
  of the event.
- A **secondhand account** is written by someone who was not there
  or did not take part — a news report, an article written from
  interviews. It often covers more of the event but misses what it
  felt like to be there.
- The test: "Was the writer *in* the event, or learning about it
  from others?" Then: "What does each account focus on, and what
  does only one of them tell us?"

### Two accounts of the same event (both original, written for this unit)

**"Maya's Garden Diary" (firsthand)**

> Tuesday. Today was garden day! I knelt in the dirt and pressed
> the soil around my little seedling with both hands. The soil was
> cool and smelled like rain. My friend Sam watered the row next to
> mine, and we laughed when the water splashed my shoe. I felt proud
> when Ms. Patel said my row was the straightest. I cannot wait to
> see my seedling grow taller than my hand.

**"School Garden Day in the News" (secondhand)**

> On Tuesday, the fourth grade held its annual garden day at
> Riverside School. Students planted seedlings in four raised beds
> under the supervision of teacher Ms. Patel. According to the
> school office, the garden club will water the beds twice a week
> until summer. The harvest will be donated to the town food bank.

### Modeled example 3 — comparing the accounts

*Adult models:* "Who wrote the diary? Maya — she was there, she
planted. Firsthand. Who wrote the news report? A reporter, learning
from the school office — secondhand. Focus: the diary focuses on
Maya's feelings and her own row ('I felt proud'); the report focuses
on the whole event (four beds, watering schedule, food bank). Only
the diary tells us the soil smelled like rain; only the report tells
us the harvest goes to the food bank. Neither is 'lying' — each
shows a different slice. And the picture? It matches the diary's
details — a girl pressing soil, a boy watering — which is what a
picture can do: show one moment, not the whole schedule."

### A common trap (model it)

A learner says the secondhand report is "more true" because it is
longer and official. The adult answers: "Longer is not truer. The
report cannot tell you how the soil felt or whether Maya was proud
— it was not there. Ask what question you need answered: for
feelings and firsthand details, trust the diary; for the schedule
and the whole school, trust the report."

## Guided practice (adult hints allowed)

1. Name the structure of each mini-passage (A–D) and point to two
   signal words in each.
2. Passage B: name one similarity and one difference it gives.
   What would be lost if the similarity sentence were removed?
3. Which account is firsthand, and how do you know? Quote one
   sentence that proves the writer was there.
4. Look at the picture (or read the text-only alternative). Name
   three details that match the diary, and one thing the picture
   cannot show that the news report tells us.

## Independent practice

1. Error analysis: a classmate labeled passage D "cause/effect"
   because it says the noise "made it hard to concentrate." Explain
   in two sentences why problem/solution fits better, quoting the
   solution sentence.
2. Write two sentences: one thing you would learn only from the
   diary, and one thing you would learn only from the news report.
3. If you wanted to know how many beds were planted, which account
   would you check, and why? If you wanted to know how planting
   felt, which would you check?
4. Picture as information: using only the picture (or its text-only
   alternative), write three facts about garden day that a reader
   could learn without reading either account.

## Applied task

The adult reads aloud a short news-style paragraph about a made-up
school event, then a short diary-style paragraph about the same
event (the adult may invent both on the spot, or reuse the garden
texts with names changed). The learner labels each account, names
its focus, and states one question each account answers better.

## Exit check

Without looking back: "Name the four structures, and give one
signal word for each. What is the one-question test for firsthand
vs. secondhand?" (Chronology — then; comparison — both/however;
cause/effect — because; problem/solution — to solve this. Test:
was the writer in the event, or learning about it from others?)

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading support:** color-code or underline signal words in each
  mini-passage before naming structures.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort four cards
  (one structure name each) onto the four passages, then explain
  each match aloud.
- **Extension:** rewrite passage C's last sentence as a
  problem/solution ending ("The problem was the blocked drain. The
  solution was…") and explain what changed — and what stayed true.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) comparison = differences only; (2) longer
or more official = more true; (3) a picture "proves" everything in
an account. Reteach with the focus question: "What does this source
show, and what can it not show?" Full solutions:
