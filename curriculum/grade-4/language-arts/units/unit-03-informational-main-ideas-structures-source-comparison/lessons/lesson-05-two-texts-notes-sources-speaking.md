# Lesson 05 — Two Texts, Notes, Sources, and Listening to a Speaker

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can put information from two texts
together to write and speak knowledgeably, take categorized notes,
list my sources, and identify the reasons and evidence a speaker
gives."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; a pencil and
paper.
**Standards:** RI.4.9, W.4.8, SL.4.3.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If one book tells you *how* to compost and another
tells you *why* compost helps the Earth, do you know more than from
either book alone?" (Yes — together they answer how *and* why.)
"Today we practice combining texts — and writing down where each
fact came from, so a reader can check us."

## Explicit teaching

- **Integrate** means to combine: take facts from two texts on the
  same topic and build one piece of writing or speaking that uses
  both. The result should say something neither text said alone.
- **Notes** are short phrases, not copied sentences. Copying whole
  sentences is not note-taking — it is copying.
- **Categories** are groups for your notes: for this lesson, *How
  to compost* and *Why compost helps*. Sorting notes into categories
  is what makes the final paragraph easy to write.
- A **source list** names each text you used, so a reader can find
  it. For this unit's original passages, the list gives the passage
  title and "written for this unit."
- Listening to a **speaker** uses the same skill as reading an
  author: find the point, then the reasons and the evidence.

### Two texts on the same topic (both original, written for this unit)

**Text A — "Composting at Home"**

> Composting turns kitchen scraps into rich soil. First, collect
> fruit and vegetable peels, eggshells, and dry leaves in a bin with
> small holes for air. Next, add a little water so the pile stays
> damp, like a wrung-out sponge. Then turn the pile with a fork
> every week or two to let air in. Tiny living things in the pile
> break the scraps down. After a few months, the pile becomes dark,
> crumbly compost that smells like earth. Spread it on garden soil
> to help plants grow.

**Text B — "Why Compost Helps the Earth"**

> When food scraps go to a landfill, they rot without air and make
> a gas that warms the planet. Composting avoids that problem: in a
> compost pile, air reaches the scraps, so they break down without
> making that gas. Compost also helps gardens: it holds water in the
> soil, so gardeners water less, and it feeds plants without store-
> bought fertilizer. Every banana peel a family composts is one less
> peel in a landfill.

### The talk (read aloud by the adult)

**"Why Our Town Needs More Trees" (short talk, read aloud)**

> Our town should plant one hundred new trees this year. First,
> trees clean the air: one large tree can absorb about 48 pounds of
> carbon dioxide in a year. Second, trees cool our streets: a
> shaded street can be several degrees cooler on a hot day, which
> means less air conditioning. Third, trees give birds and squirrels
> places to live. Planting trees is a simple step that helps the
> air, our homes, and our wildlife.

### Modeled example 1 — categorized notes

*Adult models (notes are phrases, not copies):*

| Category: How to compost (Text A) | Category: Why compost helps (Text B) |
|---|---|
| collect peels, eggshells, dry leaves | landfill scraps make warming gas |
| bin with air holes; keep damp | compost pile lets air in — no such gas |
| turn weekly with fork | compost holds water in soil |
| months later: dark, crumbly, earthy smell | feeds plants; less fertilizer needed |
| spread on garden soil | one less peel in a landfill |

*Adult says:* "Notice: no sentence is copied whole. 'Tiny living
things break the scraps down' became the phrase 'tiny living things
break scraps down' in my head — but I wrote only what I need. Notes
are for remembering, not for repeating."

### Modeled example 2 — one integrated paragraph

*Adult models:* "Composting at home is simple, and it helps the
Earth. A family collects peels, eggshells, and dry leaves in a bin
with air holes, keeps the pile damp, and turns it every week or two
until it becomes dark, crumbly compost (Text A). That matters
because food scraps in a landfill rot without air and make a gas
that warms the planet, while a compost pile lets air in and avoids
that gas — and the finished compost holds water in garden soil and
feeds plants without store-bought fertilizer (Text B). So one
kitchen habit helps both the garden and the planet."

*Adult points out:* "The paragraph uses both texts, says where each
part came from, and ends with a sentence neither text wrote: 'one
kitchen habit helps both the garden and the planet.' That sentence
is the integration — my own conclusion from both texts together."

### Modeled example 3 — listening to a speaker

*Adult models after reading the talk aloud:* "Point: plant one
hundred new trees. Reason 1: trees clean the air. Evidence: one
large tree can absorb about 48 pounds of carbon dioxide in a year.
Reason 2: trees cool streets. Evidence: a shaded street can be
several degrees cooler, which means less air conditioning. Reason 3:
trees give wildlife homes — and here the speaker gives no number or
example, so that reason has weaker evidence. Noticing a gap is part
of listening well."

### A common trap (model it)

A learner writes two paragraphs — one summarizing Text A, one
summarizing Text B — and calls it integration. The adult answers:
"That is two summaries stapled together. Integration weaves: one
paragraph where a fact from A sits next to a fact from B because
they answer the same question, and ends with a conclusion that
needs both."

## Guided practice (adult hints allowed)

1. Sort these notes into the two categories (How / Why): "turn
   weekly with fork," "holds water in soil," "bin with air holes,"
   "landfill scraps make warming gas."
2. Which text tells you *how long* composting takes, and what does
   it say? Quote the phrase.
3. Listen again as the adult re-reads the talk's first reason.
   What is the reason, and what is the evidence number?
4. Write one sentence that combines one fact from Text A and one
   fact from Text B. (Hint: start with "Composting helps because…")

## Independent practice

1. Write categorized notes (at least 3 per category) for the two
   compost texts, in phrases — no copied sentences. Then write a
   source list naming both texts.
2. Write an integrated paragraph (4–6 sentences) about composting
   that uses both texts and ends with your own conclusion sentence.
   Mark which sentences came from Text A, Text B, or your own
   thinking.
3. Listening: the adult re-reads the talk once. List its point, its
   three reasons, and the evidence for each — and note which reason
   has the weakest evidence and why.
4. Error analysis: a classmate's paragraph copies three whole
   sentences from Text A and adds "Composting is good." Explain in
   two sentences what is wrong (copying + no integration) and what
   the classmate should do instead.

## Applied task

Pick a topic with the adult (from the project topic list in
[project.md](../project.md)). The adult selects two short texts on
that topic. Take categorized notes from both, write a source list,
and draft one integrated paragraph. This draft becomes the start of
the project report.

## Exit check

Without looking back: "What is the difference between two summaries
and one integrated paragraph? What does a source list do?" (An
integrated paragraph weaves facts from both texts and ends with a
conclusion needing both; two summaries sit side by side. A source
list names each text used so a reader can find and check it.)

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading support:** the adult reads Text A aloud while the
  learner follows, then the learner reads Text B aloud; notes are
  taken after each reading, not during.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort note cards into
  two labeled boxes (How / Why) and dictate the integrated
  paragraph for the adult to scribe.
- **Extension:** add a third source — a chart, picture, or
  adult-selected paragraph — and write one sentence explaining what
  the third source adds that the first two did not.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) copying = note-taking; (2) two summaries
= integration; (3) a speaker's confidence = evidence. Reteach by
asking "whose words are these?" and "what fact backs that reason?"
