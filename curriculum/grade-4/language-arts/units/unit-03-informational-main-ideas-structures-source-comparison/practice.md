## Practice A — Main idea, key details, summary (after Lesson 01)

Read the passage, then answer.

**"How Do Fireflies Glow?"**

> On summer nights, some backyards twinkle with tiny lights. Those
> lights are fireflies, also called lightning bugs. A firefly makes
> light in its abdomen, the rear part of its body. Inside the
> abdomen, two chemicals mix with oxygen from the air, and the
> mixture gives off light. The light is cool — it makes almost no
> heat, unlike a light bulb. Fireflies flash in patterns: some blink
> slowly, some flash quickly, and each kind of firefly has its own
> pattern. Scientists think the flashes help fireflies find mates of
> their own kind. So the twinkle in the grass is not magic — it is a
> living light show with a purpose.

1. What is the topic? What is the main idea, in one sentence of
   your own?
2. List two key details and quote the sentence that proves each.
3. Name one interesting detail that is not key, and explain why the
   main idea survives without it.
4. Which of these is the best summary? (a) "Fireflies are insects."
   (b) "Fireflies make cool light in their abdomens through a
   chemical reaction, and flash in patterns that help them find
   mates." (c) "Summer nights are pretty." Explain your choice.
5. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "Main idea: fireflies are
   bugs." Explain why that is a topic, not a main idea, and give a
   better one.
6. Write your own 2-sentence summary of the passage, using no
   copied sentences.

## Practice B — What happened and why; domain words (after Lesson 02)

Read the passage, then answer.

**"How a Thermometer Works"**

> A thermometer measures how hot or cold something is. Inside the
> bottom of a thermometer is a small bulb filled with a liquid.
> When the bulb gets warm, the liquid expands — it takes up more
> room — and rises up a thin tube. When the bulb gets cold, the
> liquid contracts — it takes up less room — and falls back down
> the tube. Numbers marked along the tube, called a scale, show the
> temperature in degrees. So the height of the liquid tells you the
> temperature: higher means warmer, lower means cooler.

1. List the steps of how a thermometer works, in order.
2. Why does the liquid rise when it gets warm? Quote the sentence
   that proves it.
3. What does the scale do, and why would a thermometer be useless
   without it?
4. Define *expand* and *contract* in your own words, using the
   passage's clues. Then check a dictionary and note any correction.
5. Cause and effect: the cause is "the bulb gets cold." What is the
   effect? Write the pair.
6. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "The liquid rises because the
   numbers push it up." Correct this in two sentences, quoting the
   real reason.

## Practice C — Structures, accounts, and the picture (after Lesson 03)

1. Name the structure of each mini-passage, and quote one signal
   word for each.
   - (a) "First, wash your hands. Next, pour the soil into the pot.
     Then press the seed in. Finally, water it gently."
   - (b) "Bicycles and scooters both have two wheels, but a bicycle
     has pedals while a scooter is pushed with one foot."
   - (c) "The road was icy, so the bus drove slowly. As a result,
     school started fifteen minutes late."
   - (d) "The classroom was too dark to read in. To solve this, the
     teacher moved the reading table next to the window."
2. Which passage above is comparison? Name one similarity and one
   difference it gives.
3. Look at the unit's garden illustration
   ([assets/school-garden-planting.png](assets/school-garden-planting.png))
   or read its text-only alternative in Lesson 03. Name three
   details that match "Maya's Garden Diary," and one detail the
   picture shows that neither account mentions by name.
4. A diary entry says, "I was so nervous before the spelling bee
   that my hands shook." A news report says, "The spelling bee had
   twelve contestants." Which is firsthand, and how do you know?
   Which question does each answer better: "How did it feel?" or
   "How many contestants were there?"
5. Rewrite sentence (c) above as a problem/solution passage in two
   sentences. What changed, and what stayed true?
6. Explain in two sentences why a picture of one garden moment
   cannot prove everything in the news report (for example, the
   watering schedule or the food bank donation).

## Practice D — Reasons, evidence, and charts (after Lesson 04)

Read the passage and chart, then answer.

**"Why Should Children Walk to School?"**

> Children should walk to school when it is safe to do so. First,
> walking is healthy exercise: it wakes up the body and builds
> strong legs. Second, walking gives children time to talk with
> friends and notice their neighborhood. Third, fewer car trips
> mean cleaner air around the school. For these reasons, families
> should choose walking whenever they can.

**Children walking to school each morning, one week (fictional
practice data — made up to teach chart reading, not real counts)**

| Day | Children walking |
|---|---|
| Monday | 24 |
| Tuesday | 31 |
| Wednesday | 28 |
| Thursday | 35 |
| Friday | 30 |
| **Total** | **148** |

1. Name the author's point and its three reasons.
2. Which reason has no number or example as evidence? How could
   the author strengthen it?
3. From the chart: on which day did the most children walk, and how
   many? Check the total yourself: 24 + 31 + 28 + 35 + 30 = ?
4. What does the chart add that the passage's words do not say?
5. Error analysis: a classmate wrote, "The chart proves walking
   makes children healthy." Explain why the chart cannot prove
   that, and what kind of evidence could.
6. Write one new reason for walking to school, plus one piece of
   evidence that would support it. Label invented evidence as
   "practice example, not a real fact."

## Practice E — Two texts, notes, sources (after Lesson 05)

Read both texts, then answer.

**Text A — "Saving Water at Home"**

> Families can save water with small habits. First, turn off the
> tap while brushing teeth. Second, take shorter showers — cutting
> two minutes off a shower saves several gallons. Third, fix
> dripping faucets: one drip every second can waste thousands of
> gallons in a year. Finally, water plants in the early morning or
> evening, when less water evaporates.

**Text B — "Why Saving Water Matters"**

> Fresh water is limited, and cleaning it for drinking takes energy.
> When families use less water, treatment plants use less energy,
> and more water stays in rivers and lakes for fish and wildlife.
> Saving water also lowers a family's water bill. Small habits at
> home add up: if every home in a town saves a little, the town
> saves a great deal.

1. Sort these notes into "How to save" (Text A) and "Why it
   matters" (Text B): "turn off tap while brushing," "treatment
   plants use less energy," "fix dripping faucets," "more water
   stays in rivers."
2. Which text tells you how much water a two-minute-shorter shower
   saves, and what does it say?
3. Write categorized notes (at least 3 per category) for the two
   texts, in phrases — no copied sentences.
4. Write a source list naming both texts.
5. Write an integrated paragraph (4–6 sentences) about saving
   water that uses both texts and ends with your own conclusion
   sentence. Mark which sentences came from Text A, Text B, or your
   own thinking.
6. Error analysis: a classmate turned in two paragraphs — one
   summarizing Text A, one summarizing Text B. Explain why that is
   not integration, and rewrite the opening sentence so the two
   texts weave together.
