# Lesson 01 — Starting a Story: Situation, Narrator, Characters, Sequence

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can start a story by setting up a
situation, introducing a narrator and characters, and putting events
in an order that unfolds naturally."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; a pencil and
paper (or the unit notebook).
**Standards:** W.4.3.a.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If I say, 'A girl went to the park,' do you know
*who* she is, *why* she went, or *what happened next*?" (No — that
is a sentence, not a story beginning. A story beginning answers:
who is here, where are they, what is going on — and then the events
follow in an order that makes sense.) "Today we learn the four
parts every story beginning needs, and we meet the story we will
work with all unit."

## Explicit teaching

A story beginning does four jobs:

- **Situation:** what is going on, and where and when. ("On a
  windy Saturday morning, my little brother Theo and I carried our
  yellow kite to the park.")
- **Narrator:** who is telling the story. In a **first-person**
  story the narrator is a character in it and says "I" ("my little
  brother Theo and I…"). In a **third-person** story the narrator
  stands outside it and says "she" or "he" ("Mara carried the
  kite…").
- **Characters:** the people (or animals) the story is about,
  named and given something to do right away.
- **Event sequence:** the events in the order they happened — first
  this, then that. A natural order is one a reader can follow
  without getting lost: if event B needs event A to make sense, A
  comes first.

The test for a good beginning: after the first paragraph, a reader
can answer "Who is in this story?", "Where are they?", and "What is
about to happen?"

### The lesson passage

Read it aloud together first. This is the unit's anchor story; the
next four lessons will work on it again.

**"The Day the Kite Got Stuck" (original, written for this unit)**

> On a windy Saturday morning, my little brother Theo and I carried
> our yellow kite to the park. Biscuit, our small brown dog, trotted
> beside us with his ears flopping.
>
> First, Theo held the kite high while I ran with the string. Then
> the wind caught it, and the kite shot up into the gray sky. It
> danced and dipped above the big maple tree.
>
> Suddenly, a strong gust pushed the kite sideways. The string
> jerked in my hands. The yellow kite spun once, twice, and dropped
> — right into the branches of the maple tree.
>
> "It's stuck!" I shouted. My heart thumped like a drum.
>
> Theo pointed up at the branches. "Look! It's caught on that high
> branch," he said. "Don't pull too hard or the string will snap."
>
> I looked at the tangled string in my hands. Biscuit grabbed the
> loose end in his mouth and tugged. "Biscuit, no!" I said.
> "Drop it!"
>
> Then Theo had an idea. "Let's pull together, slow and steady,"
> he said. "On three. One… two… three!"
>
> We pulled gently. The kite wiggled. It slid along the branch,
> dropped a little, and then — whoosh! — the wind lifted it free.
> Theo caught it as it floated down like a big yellow leaf.
>
> "We did it!" Theo laughed. His face was red from pulling, but he
> was grinning.
>
> In the end, we flew the kite again, lower this time, away from
> the trees. Biscuit barked at every gust. I learned that a kite
> needs open sky and a steady wind — and that two pullers are better
> than one.

### Modeled example 1 — naming the four beginning jobs

*Adult models aloud:* "Situation: a windy Saturday morning at the
park, two children about to fly a kite. Narrator: Mara — she says
'my little brother' and 'I,' so this is first person; the narrator
is inside the story. Characters: Mara, her little brother Theo,
and Biscuit the dog — all named in the first paragraph, and all
doing something. Event sequence: they carry the kite to the park,
Theo holds it, Mara runs, the wind catches it, a gust pushes it
into the tree. Each event needs the one before it. If the kite were
in the tree in the first sentence, nothing before it would make
sense."

### Modeled example 2 — first person vs. third person

*Adult models:* "Listen to the same opening told the other way:
'On a windy Saturday morning, Mara and her little brother Theo
carried their yellow kite to the park.' Same situation, same
characters — but now the narrator is outside the story, saying
'Mara' instead of 'I.' That is third person. The events do not
change; only the teller changes. When you write your own story,
pick one teller and stay with it — do not switch from 'I' to 'she'
halfway through."

### A common trap (model it)

A learner writes: "One day a boy did stuff. It was fun. The end."
The adult answers: "That has no situation — where, when, what is
going on? No named characters — which boy? No order — what did he
do first? A story beginning must let the reader stand in the scene
with the characters. 'One day' is not a setting, and 'stuff' is not
an event."

## Guided practice (adult hints allowed)

1. Who is the narrator of the anchor story? How do you know?
   (Hint: look for the word "I" — and whose little brother Theo is.)
2. Name the situation in one sentence: when, where, and what is
   going on at the start.
3. List the first four events of the anchor story in order, in your
   own words. (Hint: start with carrying the kite to the park.)
4. Which sentence tells you the setting is a park on a windy day?
   Quote it — and say which words name the place and the weather.

## Independent practice

1. Write the situation of the anchor story in your own words (one
   sentence): who, where, when, what.
2. Name the three characters and one thing each one does in the
   story.
3. The events below are out of order. Number them 1–4 in the order
   they happened in the anchor story:
   (a) The kite drops into the maple tree. (b) Theo and Mara pull
   together and free the kite. (c) Theo holds the kite while Mara
   runs with the string. (d) Biscuit grabs the loose end of the
   string.
4. Error analysis: a classmate wrote this beginning: "There was a
   kid. He went somewhere. Then things happened." Explain in two
   sentences what is missing — and write a better first sentence
   for that story, with a named character, a place, and a situation.

## Applied task

Think of a real thing that happened to you this week (a walk, a
game, a meal, a chore). Tell it aloud to the adult in order, in
three sentences: first the situation (who, where, when), then what
happened first, then what happened next. The adult listens for the
four beginning jobs and asks one question about anything missing.

## Exit check

Without looking back: "What four jobs does a story beginning do?
Name them, and give one example of each from the kite story."
(Situation: windy Saturday morning at the park. Narrator: Mara,
first person. Characters: Mara, Theo, Biscuit. Event sequence:
carry the kite → hold and run → wind catches it → gust pushes it
into the tree.)

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the anchor story aloud
  first; the learner follows with a finger and re-reads the first
  paragraph to find the situation.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate the
  situation sentence and the ordered events while the adult
  scribes, then read them back and check the order.
- **Extension:** rewrite the anchor story's first paragraph in
  third person ("Mara and her little brother Theo…") and explain
  one thing that changes about the story when the narrator stands
  outside it.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) a list of events with no situation
("First we did this, then this") — the reader never arrives in the
scene; (2) switching narrators mid-story ("I ran… then Mara
said…"); (3) events in the order the learner *thought of them*,
not the order they happened. Reteach with the reader test: "After
your first paragraph, can I answer who, where, and what is about
to happen?" Full solutions: [answer key](../answer-key.md)
