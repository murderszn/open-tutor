# Lesson 05 — Endings and Revision: Closure and Making It Better

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can give my story a sense of closure
and revise it — add, remove, move, and swap — with a partner's
help, then edit it clean."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; a pencil and
paper (or the unit notebook); the project draft.
**Standards:** W.4.3.e; W.4.5; W.4.4.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Listen to two endings. 'We went home.' And: 'In
the end, we flew the kite again, lower this time, away from the
trees. I learned that a kite needs open sky and a steady wind —
and that two pullers are better than one.' Which one feels
finished, and why?" (The second one: it tells what changed and
what Mara learned. A story should land, not just stop.) "Today we
learn to land a story — and to revise a draft until it is ready
to share."

## Explicit teaching

A **sense of closure** answers the reader's last question: "So
what happened in the end — and what did it mean?" Good closures do
one of these:

- **Tell what changed:** "In the end, we flew the kite again,
  lower this time, away from the trees."
- **Say what the character learned:** "I learned that a kite
  needs open sky and a steady wind."
- **Echo the beginning:** the kite that started the trouble is
  flying again — the story comes full circle.
- **Look ahead:** "Tomorrow we will fly it even higher."

A closure is not a summary of the whole story, and it is not a
new problem. "And then a storm came" is not an ending — it is a
new beginning.

**Revise** means to re-see the story and make it better. **Edit**
means to fix spelling and punctuation. Revise first, edit last —
there is no point polishing sentences you are about to delete.

Four revision moves (say them aloud while you work):

| Move | Ask | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Add** | What is missing? | Add a sensory detail to the park scene. |
| **Remove** | What does the story not need? | Remove a repeated sentence. |
| **Move** | What is in the wrong place? | Move the kite getting stuck before the pulling. |
| **Swap** | What word could be stronger? | Swap "nice" for "bright yellow". |

A **peer conference** is a short, kind meeting about a draft: the
writer reads aloud, the listener says one strength and one
suggestion, and the writer chooses what to change. The writer
always decides — the listener suggests.

### The lesson passage

"The Quiet Garden" — a weak paragraph to revise (original, written
for this unit):

> The garden was nice. Sam went there. He did stuff. It was good.
> Then he went home. The end.

### Modeled example 1 — giving a story closure

*Adult models aloud:* "The kite story's ending works because it
does two closure jobs at once. 'In the end, we flew the kite
again, lower this time' tells what changed — they learned to stay
away from the trees. 'I learned that a kite needs open sky and a
steady wind — and that two pullers are better than one' says what
Mara learned. Now try a bad ending: 'And then we went home and ate
lunch.' It answers nothing. The reader is left asking, 'But what
about the kite?' A closure must answer the story's own question —
here, the question was 'Will they get the kite back?' The ending
answers it: yes, and they fly it again."

### Modeled example 2 — revising with the four moves

*Adult models, revising "The Quiet Garden" aloud:* "First **add**:
Sam needs a situation — 'On Saturday morning, Sam carried his
watering can to the quiet garden behind his house.' Now **remove**:
'He did stuff' says nothing — cut it. Now **move**: 'Then he went
home' belongs after the work, not before the ending — keep it
last. Now **swap**: 'nice' becomes 'green and buzzing with bees,'
and 'good' becomes 'the smell of wet soil after rain.' Revised:
'On Saturday morning, Sam carried his watering can to the quiet
garden behind his house. The garden was green and buzzing with
bees. He watered the tomatoes and smelled the wet soil after the
rain. In the end, he picked one red tomato and carried it home
like a prize.' Four moves, and a list became a story."

### A common trap (model it)

A learner "revises" by copying the draft neatly in better
handwriting and fixing two spellings.
The adult answers: "That is editing, not revising — and the story
is exactly the same as before. Copying neatly does not add a
detail, remove a repeat, move an event, or swap a weak word.
Revision changes the *story*; editing changes the *spelling*.
Do the story work first, then polish the spelling on the final
copy."

## Guided practice (adult hints allowed)

1. Which of these is a closure, and which is just a stop? (a) "We
   went home." (b) "In the end, we flew the kite again, and I
   learned that two pullers are better than one." Explain the
   difference in one sentence.
2. Name one **add** move and one **swap** move you could make to
   "The Quiet Garden" above. (Hint: what is missing — a sense?
   a feeling? Which vague word is weakest?)
3. Read the kite story's last paragraph. What changed, and what
   did Mara learn? Answer in two sentences.
4. A partner says, "Your story is good." Is that a useful
   revision suggestion? Why or why not? What would be more
   useful?

## Independent practice

1. Write a closure (2 sentences) for this unfinished story: "Lena
   found a lost puppy under the school steps. She fed it her
   sandwich and hid it in her backpack. The puppy barked during
   math class." Your closure must tell what changed or what Lena
   learned.
2. Revise this weak paragraph with two named moves (say which move
   you used for each change): "The beach was nice. We did stuff.
   It was fun. Then we left." (Label your moves: Add / Remove /
   Move / Swap.)
3. Error analysis: a classmate "revised" by fixing three spelling
   mistakes and nothing else. Explain in two sentences why that is
   editing, not revising — and name one revision move the draft
   still needs.
4. Write a peer-conference note for the anchor story: one strength
   (something it does well) and one suggestion (one move that
   would make it better). Be specific — name the sentence or
   detail.

## Applied task

Take the project draft to a peer conference (a classmate, a
sibling, or the adult). Read it aloud. The listener names one
strength and one suggestion. Record both, then make at least two
revision moves (add, remove, move, or swap) and mark them in the
margin with their names. Then edit: check dialogue punctuation,
capital letters, and spelling — and make the final copy.

## Exit check

Without looking back: "What are the four revision moves, and what
is the difference between revising and editing? Name two things a
good closure does." (Add, remove, move, swap. Revising changes the
story — details, order, words; editing fixes spelling and
punctuation. A closure tells what changed, says what was learned,
echoes the beginning, or looks ahead.)

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the weak paragraph and the
  revised version back to back; the learner points to the exact
  place where each revision move happened.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may use revision
  cards (ADD / REMOVE / MOVE / SWAP) and place a card on each
  sentence of the draft that needs that move, then dictate the
  changes while the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** write a second, different closure for the kite
  story — one that looks ahead ("Tomorrow…") instead of saying
  what Mara learned — and explain which closure fits the story
  better and why.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) revision = neat recopying or spelling
fixes — reteach with the four moves and the "story vs. spelling"
test; (2) closure = summary of everything — reteach: a closure
answers the story's own question in one or two sentences; (3) peer
feedback as praise only ("it's good") or as orders ("change
this") — model the strength + specific suggestion frame, and
remind the writer that the writer decides. Full solutions:
