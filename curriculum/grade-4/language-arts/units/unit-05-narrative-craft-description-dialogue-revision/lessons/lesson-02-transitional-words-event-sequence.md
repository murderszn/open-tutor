# Lesson 02 — Transitions: Moving Through Time in Order

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can use transition words and phrases
to show the order of events, so a reader can follow my story from
one thing to the next."
**Time:** 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson; a pencil and
paper (or the unit notebook).
**Standards:** W.4.3.c.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Listen: 'We carried the kite to the park. The
kite got stuck in the tree. We pulled it free.' What is missing?"
(The jumps. A reader cannot tell how much time passed or which
thing caused the next.) "Today we learn the words that carry a
reader from one event to the next — the bridges between the
sentences of a story."

## Explicit teaching

**Transitions** are words and phrases that show time and order.
They answer "When did this happen, compared to the last thing?"

| Job | Transition words and phrases |
|---|---|
| Start | first, at first, one morning, to begin with |
| Next step | then, next, after that, soon, later |
| Sudden change | suddenly, just then, all at once |
| Meanwhile | while, at the same time, meanwhile |
| Last step | finally, in the end, at last, after a while |

Rules that make transitions work:

1. **One transition per move.** "Then" moves the story one step
   forward. "Suddenly" means something unexpected happened.
   Do not stack them: "Then suddenly later…" confuses the reader.
2. **Match the word to the time.** "First" belongs at the start;
   "finally" belongs near the end. "Meanwhile" means two things
   are happening at the same time.
3. **A transition is not a substitute for an event.** "Then we
   won" skips the whole middle. The transition carries the reader
   to the next *event* — the event still has to be written.

### The lesson passage

The anchor story's middle, with its transitions underlined by the
adult as you read:

> **First,** Theo held the kite high while I ran with the string.
> **Then** the wind caught it, and the kite shot up into the gray
> sky. **Suddenly,** a strong gust pushed the kite sideways. The
> string jerked in my hands. **Then** Theo had an idea. "Let's pull
> together, slow and steady," he said. **In the end,** we flew the
> kite again, lower this time, away from the trees.

### Modeled example 1 — what each transition does

*Adult models aloud:* "'First' tells me this is the starting
move — before anything else happens, Theo holds the kite. 'Then'
moves me one step forward: the wind catches it. 'Suddenly' warns
me that something unexpected is about to happen — the gust — and
after it, the story is in trouble. 'Then Theo had an idea' moves
me from the problem to the plan. 'In the end' tells me the story is
closing: this is the last thing that happened. Take the transitions
out and the events are still there, but the reader has to guess the
order. Put them in and the path is marked."

### Modeled example 2 — choosing the right transition

*Adult models:* "Three gaps, three choices. '___ we ate lunch.
___ it started to rain. ___ we ran inside.' The first gap is a
start, so 'First' fits. The second gap is a next step in time, so
'Later' or 'Then' fits — but 'Suddenly' also fits if the rain was
a surprise. The third gap follows the rain, so 'Then' or 'At last'
fits. Now watch a wrong choice: 'Finally, we ate lunch' — 'finally'
at the very start tells the reader the story is almost over when it
has just begun. The word must match where you are in the story."

### A common trap (model it)

A learner writes: "Then we went to the park. Then we flew the
kite. Then it got stuck. Then we pulled. Then we went home."
The adult answers: "Every step says 'then,' so every step sounds
exactly the same — the reader cannot tell which step is the
problem and which is the ending. Vary the transitions: 'First' to
open, 'Suddenly' for the trouble, 'In the end' to close. A story
with only 'then' is a list wearing a story's clothes."

## Guided practice (adult hints allowed)

1. Read the lesson passage above. List the five transitions in
   order, and say what job each one does (start, next step,
   sudden change, last step).
2. Fill the gap with the best transition: "___ Biscuit grabbed the
   string, the kite was still stuck in the tree." (a) First
   (b) Meanwhile (c) In the end. Explain your choice.
3. Fix this sentence pair: "We pulled the string. The kite came
   free." Add a transition and one more detail so the reader knows
   *how* it came free.
4. Which transition is wrong here, and why? "Finally, Theo woke
   up. Then he brushed his teeth. First, he ate breakfast."

## Independent practice

1. Copy these events and add a transition at the start of each
   one (choose from the table — do not use "then" more than once):
   ___ Mara carried the kite to the park. ___ the wind caught it.
   ___ a gust pushed it into the tree. ___ they pulled it free.
2. Write two sentences about a morning routine (waking up,
   getting dressed, eating breakfast) using "first" and "after
   that" correctly.
3. Error analysis: "Suddenly, I woke up. Suddenly, I ate
   breakfast. Suddenly, I went to school." Explain in two
   sentences why repeating "suddenly" is a problem — and rewrite
   the three sentences with three different transitions.
4. Write a three-sentence mini-story about losing and finding
   something. Use one transition for a sudden change and one for
   the ending.

## Applied task

Open the project draft beginning from Session W19 S4 (or start
one now). Underline every transition word in it. If you used
"then" more than twice, swap at least one for a different
transition from the table. Read the beginning aloud and check:
can a listener follow the order without asking "wait, when did
that happen?"

## Exit check

Without looking back: "Name three transitions for starting,
three for moving forward, and two for ending a story — and say
what 'suddenly' warns the reader about." (Start: first, at first,
one morning. Forward: then, next, after that, soon, later.
Ending: finally, in the end, at last. "Suddenly" warns that
something unexpected is about to happen.)

## Supports, alternate modes, extension

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the lesson passage aloud,
  pausing at each transition; the learner claps once each time a
  transition word appears.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may sort printed
  transition cards into start / forward / sudden / end piles
  instead of writing the lists, then dictate two sentences using
  one card from each pile.
- **Extension:** rewrite the anchor story's middle paragraph
  using only "meanwhile" and "while" to show two things happening
  at the same time (Mara pulling, Biscuit tugging), and explain
  what changes when events overlap instead of following one after
  another.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "then" as the only transition — a
monotone list; (2) "suddenly" used for ordinary next steps;
(3) transitions stacked ("then suddenly later"); (4) "finally"
used at the start. Reteach with the table: point to the job column
first ("what job does this sentence do?"), then pick the word.
