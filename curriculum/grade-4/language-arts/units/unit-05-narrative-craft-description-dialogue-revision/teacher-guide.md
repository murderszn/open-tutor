# Teacher Guide — Unit 05

Read this before teaching. All solutions are in
[answer-key.md](answer-key.md). Do not hand the answer key to the
learner; keep it separate from every student page.

## Teaching the unit in order

**Week 19 — beginnings and sequence.** Lesson 01's reader test
("After your first paragraph, can I answer who, where, and what is
about to happen?") is the unit's most reusable tool — use it again
whenever a beginning is disputed. The anchor story is the unit's
shared text: keep a copy visible all four weeks, because Lessons
02–05 all work on it. Lesson 02's transition table (start /
forward / sudden / end) is the second tool: point to the job column
first, then pick the word.

**Week 20 — dialogue.** Teach Lesson 03 in two sessions: rules and
modeling first, the picture activity second. The four punctuation
rules are taught in one order every time: capital letter, quotation
marks, comma, paragraph break. When dialogue turns into "said on
every line," steer to the words themselves: "Wait for me!" already
sounds worried without any tag at all. The picture activity is the
unit's image task — the text-only version in the lesson carries
every fact, so no activity requires seeing the picture.

**Week 21 — description and revision.** Lesson 04's rule is "one
concrete picture beats five vague adjectives" — use it whenever a
draft piles on "very" and "super." Lesson 05's four moves (add,
remove, move, swap) are the revision vocabulary for the rest of the
year: insist the learner name the move out loud before changing
anything. The peer conference frame is one strength + one specific
suggestion; the writer always decides.

**Week 22 — project, quiz, assessment.** The quiz is formative:
reteach anything it exposes before the unit assessment. The share
circle doubles as the SL.4.4 speaking assessment: listeners must
name one description they could picture.

## Misconceptions and reteaching

| If the learner… | Reteach by… |
|---|---|
| Lists events with no situation ("First we did this, then this") | Asking the reader test: "Who is in this story? Where are they? What is about to happen?" after the first paragraph. |
| Switches narrators mid-story ("I ran… then Mara said…") | Having the learner circle every "I" and every character name in the draft, then pick one teller and fix the other. |
| Uses "then" for every step | The transition table: cover "then" and ask which job each sentence does — start, sudden change, or ending — then pick the matching word. |
| Uses "suddenly" for ordinary next steps | Asking "Was it a surprise?" — if the answer is no, the word is "then" or "next". |
| Puts two speakers in one paragraph | Reading the dialogue aloud in two voices and stopping at each voice change: "New voice — new paragraph." |
| Puts the comma outside the quotation mark | The rule, said aloud: "The comma lives inside the quotes when a speaker tag follows." |
| Says "said" on every line, or a fancy tag on every line | Covering all the tags and asking whether the words alone show the feeling; keep tags only where they add meaning. |
| Piles adjectives on a vague word ("very, very pretty") | Asking the three questions: "What did it look like? What did it sound like? What did it feel like?" and keeping one concrete answer. |
| Describes a character with looks only | Asking for one thought, one spoken line, and one action from the text (RL.4.3). |
| "Revises" by recopying neatly or fixing spelling | The story-vs.-spelling test: "Did the story change, or only the spelling?" Then one named move from the table. |
| Ends with a summary or a new problem | Asking the story's own question ("Will they get the kite back?") and checking the ending answers it. |
| Gives praise-only feedback ("it's good") | The strength + specific suggestion frame, with the suggestion naming a sentence or detail. |

## Scoring guidance

- **Lessons and practice:** credit any answer that matches the
  key's reasoning, even if the wording differs. For open writing
  (dialogue, description, closures), accept any response that meets
  the stated requirement — correct punctuation, a named transition
  job, a concrete picture — and reject responses that miss the
  requirement even if they are grammatical.
- **Quiz (10 items):** 9–10 = ready for the assessment; 7–8 = brief
  reteach of the missed skill; below 7 = reteach the lesson for
  that skill before the assessment.
- **Unit assessment (12 items, 30 points):** score items 1–3, 5–6,
  8–9 as correct/partial/incorrect against the key; item 4 (3
  points) needs a quoted line plus a correct inference about Ms.
  Patel; item 7 (3 points) needs two text details plus what they
  show; item 10 (3 points) needs one sensory detail, one dialogue
  line, and correct punctuation; item 11 (4 points) is 1 point per
  required element (transition, dialogue, sensory detail, closure);
  item 12 (3 points) needs a named move with a matching reason.
  25–30 = unit objectives met; 19–24 = met with reteaching notes;
  below 19 = reteach and re-assess with a fresh passage.
- **Project:** use the rubric in [project.md](project.md). A first
  draft is never graded for perfection; record one strength and one
  next step for every learner.

## Readiness and next steps

**Ready for Unit 06 (opinion writing: evidence, reasons, and
organization) when the learner can:** set up a situation with a
narrator and characters, order events with transitions, write
correctly punctuated dialogue that shows a response, describe with
concrete words and sensory details, close a story, and revise with
two named moves. If dialogue punctuation is still shaky, repeat
Lesson 03's step-by-step model on a new exchange before starting
Unit 06 — opinion drafting will not fix a punctuation gap.

**If the learner is ahead:** the Lesson 03 extension (strip the
speaker tags and test which lines still work), the Lesson 04
extension (describe the same scene twice, once per character's
feelings), and the Lesson 05 extension (write a second, different
closure) are the enrichment path. Do not accelerate into Unit 06's
opinion content early; depth here pays off in Units 06–07 (opinion
and research writing).

## Accessibility and safety notes

- Every check has an oral, pointing, or drawing mode: the learner
  may dictate stories, dialogues, and descriptions while the adult
  scribes, then read them back for checking.
- The park illustration's text-only alternative (Lessons 03–04)
  carries every fact the picture activities need; no activity
  requires seeing the picture.
- All passages are original to this unit; no published text is
  reproduced. The people, dog, kite, and library events are
  invented, not reports of real events.
- Project stories are real or imagined and learner-chosen; no real
  names of other children, addresses, or family details go in the
  story or the read-aloud.
