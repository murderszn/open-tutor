# Project — My Neighborhood Story

Write and share a real or imagined story about something that
happens in a neighborhood like yours. Work on it across Weeks
19–22: plan it, draft it, revise it with a partner, and read it
aloud in the share circle.

## Your task

Write a story of **8–12 sentences** (about one page) that a reader
can follow from beginning to end. It must have:

- A **situation** with a narrator and at least two characters
- Events in a **natural order**, joined by **transition words**
- At least **one dialogue exchange** (2 or more lines) that shows
  how a character responds
- At least **two sensory details** and **two concrete words** that
  replace vague ones
- A **sense of closure** — what changed, or what the narrator
  learned

## Materials

- The unit notebook (or paper and pencil)
- The story map below (copy it before you start)
- The picture ([park-story-scene.png](assets/park-story-scene.png))
  or any of the six story starters in Practice E, if you need an
  idea
- A partner (a classmate, a sibling, or the adult) for the peer
  conference

## Steps

1. **Choose and map (Week 19, Session 2).** Pick a real memory or
   an imagined event in a neighborhood: a lost pet, a storm, a new
   neighbor, a broken bike, a garden, a kite. Complete the story
   map. Check: can a reader answer who, where, and what is about
   to happen from your beginning row?
2. **Draft the beginning (Week 19, Session 4).** Write 2–3
   sentences: the situation, the narrator and characters, and the
   first event. Use at least two transitions.
3. **Draft the middle (Week 20, Session 7).** Write 3–4 sentences:
   the problem or surprise, in order, with at least one dialogue
   exchange that shows how a character responds. Punctuate the
   dialogue: quotation marks, comma with the speaker tag, new
   paragraph per speaker.
4. **Read aloud in pairs (Week 20, Session 8).** Read your draft
   so far to your partner. The listener names one thing the
   dialogue showed. Note anything the listener found confusing.
5. **Add description (Week 21, Session 10).** Underline every
   vague word in your draft ("nice," "big," "good," "stuff,"
   "things"). Replace at least two with concrete words and add at
   least two sensory details.
6. **Write the ending and revise (Week 21, Sessions 11–12).**
   Write a 2-sentence closure. Then hold a peer conference: read
   the whole draft aloud, hear one strength and one suggestion,
   and make at least two revision moves (add, remove, move, swap).
   Mark each move in the margin.
7. **Final copy (Week 22, Session 13).** Edit for dialogue
   punctuation, capital letters, and spelling. Copy the story
   neatly. Draw an illustration if you like (optional).
8. **Share circle (Week 22, Session 14).** Read your story aloud
   in order, at an understandable pace. Listeners name one
   description they could picture.

## Deliverables

- The completed story map
- The draft with peer-conference notes and marked revision moves
- The final copy (8–12 sentences)
- The share-circle read-aloud

## Rubric (40 points)

| Criterion | 5 — Strong | 3 — Developing | 1 — Starting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Situation and characters (W.4.3.a) | Situation, narrator, and two characters are clear in the opening; reader knows who, where, and what is going on | Some parts are clear; one of situation, narrator, or a character is missing or unclear | Opening names almost nothing; reader cannot tell who or where the story happens |
| Event sequence (W.4.3.a) | Events unfold in a natural order a reader can follow without questions | Order is mostly clear; one event is out of place or unexplained | Events jump around; the reader cannot tell what happened when |
| Transitions (W.4.3.c) | Three or more different transitions used correctly, matched to their jobs | One or two transitions, or "then" repeated for every step | No transitions, or transitions used in the wrong places |
| Dialogue (W.4.3.b; L.4.2.b) | At least one exchange of 2+ lines that shows a character's response; punctuation correct (quotation marks, commas, new paragraph per speaker) | Dialogue is present but does not show a response, or has 1–2 punctuation errors | No dialogue, or dialogue with repeated punctuation errors |
| Description and precise words (W.4.3.d; L.4.3.a) | At least two sensory details and two concrete words replacing vague ones; reader can picture the scene | One sensory detail or one concrete replacement; some vague words remain | No sensory detail; vague words ("nice," "stuff") throughout |
| Closure (W.4.3.e) | Ending tells what changed or what was learned in 1–2 sentences; the story feels finished | Ending is present but only stops the story ("We went home") without saying what changed | No ending, or a new problem is introduced at the end |
| Revision shown (W.4.5) | Two or more revision moves are marked and visible in the final copy; peer-conference notes recorded | One revision move visible, or notes recorded without visible changes | No revision moves or peer notes; draft and final copy are identical except spelling |
| Read-aloud (SL.4.4) | Story read in order, clearly, at an understandable pace, with descriptive details the listeners can name back | Read aloud but rushed, too quiet, or out of order in places | Not read aloud, or listeners cannot follow the story |

**Scoring:** 36–40 = strong; the learner is ready for Unit 06
(opinion writing). 28–35 = developing; name one row to strengthen
before Unit 06. Below 28 = reteach the lowest row with a fresh
short story before moving on. A first draft is never graded for
perfection; record one strength and one next step for every
learner.

## Safety and accessibility notes

- Stories are real or imagined; the learner chooses what to share.
  No real names of other children, addresses, or family details go
  in the story or the read-aloud.
- The learner may dictate the draft while the adult scribes, then
  read it back and check it. Drawing the scene first is an
  accepted planning step.
- The peer conference is kind and specific: one strength, one
  suggestion. The writer decides which suggestions to use.
