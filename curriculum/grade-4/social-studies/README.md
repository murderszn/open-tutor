# Grade 4 Social Studies

Independent, grade-specific materials in the public OpenTutor curriculum library.
This track is a **draft in progress**: the audit and scope-and-sequence are delivered;
the eight units are planned, not yet written. See [scope-and-sequence.md](scope-and-sequence.md).

**Do not refer a learner to another grade track.** This folder is self-contained for
one grade-4 learner at a time; a guiding adult adapts pacing, materials, and
supports. Keep completed learner work, names, photos, schedules, and grades in
private storage — never in this public repository.

See also: [Grade 4 track](../README.md), [curriculum index](../../README.md).

## What this track covers

A full-year (about 36 weeks) grade-4 social studies course organized around
U.S. regions: geographic tools and map evidence; Indigenous nations, their
geography, and their perspectives; exploration encounters, exchange, and
consequences; colonial regions, communities, and economic life; state and local
government with rights and responsibilities; resources, migration, industry,
and regional economies; historical sources, timelines, and regional change;
and a regional-history and community-inquiry capstone.

Sessions are about 35 minutes, four per week: a concept/introduction session, a
skills-practice session, an investigation or application session, and a
review/check session each week. Work is map- and evidence-based: learners read
and construct maps, sequence events across centuries, compare historical
sources and perspectives, weigh economic choices, and practice classroom
deliberation. Independent written practice is short and reviewed by the adult
the same day.

Difficult history (conquest, displacement, enslavement) is named honestly and
plainly at an age-appropriate level with adult guidance — never glossed, never
graphic — and every encounter is examined from at least two perspectives.

## Track objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the year the learner will be able to:

1. **Do geographic inquiry at U.S.-regional scale:** construct maps and
   graphic representations of familiar and unfamiliar places; use maps,
   photographs, and other representations to explain how location connects to
   environmental characteristics; use maps of different scales to describe
   cultural and environmental characteristics. (C3 D2.Geo.1–3.3-5)
2. **Explain how environment shapes regions and Indigenous nations' ways of
   life:** explain how culture influences how people modify and adapt to
   environments; explain how places change over time; describe how
   environmental and cultural characteristics shape where people settle;
   explain how settlements relate to natural resources; explain why
   environmental characteristics vary among world regions. (C3 D2.Geo.2, 4–6,
   8, 10.3-5)
3. **Explain movement:** describe how cultural and environmental
   characteristics affect the movement of people, goods, and ideas; describe
   how exploration routes, migration, and trade connected regions; describe how
   economic activity patterns change through interaction with nearby and
   distant places; explain probable causes and effects of these developments.
   (C3 D2.Geo.7, 11.3-5; D2.His.14.3-5)
4. **Take multiple perspectives:** explain why people in the same historical
   period held different perspectives; explain how historical context connects
   to people's perspectives; identify the beliefs, experiences, and values
   behind their own and others' points of view. (C3 D2.His.4, 5.3-5;
   D2.Civ.10.3-5)
5. **Work with historical evidence:** build and use chronological sequences;
   compare life in past time periods to life today; summarize how different
   kinds of historical sources explain past events; compare sources about the
   same past event; infer a source's audience and purpose from the source
   itself; judge a source's usefulness for a topic; explain probable causes and
   effects; use evidence to develop a claim about the past. (C3 D2.His.1, 2,
   9–13, 16.3-5)
6. **Understand state and local government:** distinguish the responsibilities
   and powers of government officials at different levels and branches; examine
   the origins and purposes of rules, laws, and key U.S. constitutional
   provisions; explain how groups make rules that create responsibilities and
   protect freedoms; explain the origins, functions, and structure of systems
   of government, including those in the U.S. and state constitutions; explain
   how people change rules and laws. (C3 D2.Civ.1–5, 12.3-5)
7. **Reason economically:** compare benefits and costs of choices; identify
   incentives that influence decisions; identify human, physical, and natural
   resources used to produce goods and services; explain why people and
   businesses specialize and trade; explain money's role in exchange; explain
   how profits influence sellers; identify external benefits and costs; explain
   how government pays for the goods and services it provides; explain how
   trade creates economic interdependence and how interdependence affects
   different groups. (C3 D2.Eco.1–5, 7, 8, 12, 14, 15.3-5)
8. **Explain how catastrophic events affect settlements:** analyze the effects
   of catastrophic environmental and technological events on human settlements
   and migration, and explain how such events in one place affect people living
   in other places (adult-guided, observation-level). (C3 D2.Geo.9, 12.3-5)
9. **Inquire and argue from evidence:** identify disciplinary concepts behind
   compelling and supporting questions; explain how supporting questions help
   answer compelling questions; determine which sources will help; gather
   information from multiple sources using origin, structure, and context;
   distinguish fact from opinion to judge credibility; identify evidence from
   multiple sources; develop claims and construct arguments and explanations
   with reasons, sequence, examples, and details; present summaries to others.
   (C3 D1.2–1.5, D3.1–3.4, D4.1–4.3.3-5)
10. **Take informed action:** use deliberative and democratic procedures to
    decide on and act on a classroom, school, or community civic problem;
    explain strategies people use to address local problems and predict
    possible results. (C3 D4.6–4.8.3-5)

Standards source: the College, Career, and Civic Life (C3) Framework for Social
Studies State Standards (National Council for the Social Studies), grades 3–5
band. The four Dimensions and D2 sub-strands were re-confirmed on the NCSS C3
landing page 2026-10-04; codes and descriptions were verified against the
framework's 3–5 indicator tables (merged grade-3 audit, 2026-10-03). **No state
adoption, accreditation, or alignment certification is claimed.** The 3–5 band
spans three grades; this track teaches each indicator at the grade-4 mid-band
level. Indicators the framework defers to later bands (D2.His.7–8, 9–12;
D2.His.15, 6–8) are not taught here.

this track has an answer key; the unit sections and R00 will supply them.

## Accessibility and safety

- Maps and diagrams always pair color with symbols and labels (never
  color-only); every image carries alt text and a text-only description.
- Map work offers oral, pointing, drawing, and manipulative options; the adult
  scribes written explanations when writing is not the skill being measured.
- Tables are screen-reader-friendly; videos (in later unit Resource Packs) will
  require captions or transcripts.
- Investigations are observation- and interview-based; any community contact is
  adult-supervised and stays private — no learner names, photos, addresses, or
  schedules enter this repository. Capstone presentations are shared only with
  the guiding adult's approval, never published from this track.
- Encounter, conquest, and enslavement content is adult-led with
  pre-selected, age-appropriate sources presenting multiple perspectives;
  nothing graphic is assigned.
