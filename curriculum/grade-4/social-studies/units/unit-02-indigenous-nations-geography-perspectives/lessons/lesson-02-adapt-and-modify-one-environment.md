# Lesson 02 — Adapt to the Land, Shape the Land

**Time:** two sessions, about 35 minutes each · **Materials:** the unit
illustration
([`culture-areas-environments.png`](../assets/culture-areas-environments.png));
pencil and paper; two-column chart paper (or a drawn T-chart)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn the difference
between **adapting to** an environment (changing how you live to fit the
land) and **modifying** an environment (changing the land to fit how you
live). By the end, you will sort real examples into the two columns and
explain each with evidence.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks: "Yesterday we matched environments to
ways of life. Give me one example: an environment clue and the way of
life it explains." Accept any Lesson 01 answer. Then: "Did the people
change to fit the land there, or change the land to fit them — or both?"

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Two big words run this lesson:

- **Adapt** — to change *yourself* to fit the environment. Wearing a
  warm coat in winter is adapting. Building a boat that fits icy water
  is adapting.
- **Modify** — to change the *environment* to fit you. Digging a ditch
  to bring river water to a dry field is modifying. Cutting trees to
  clear a farm field is modifying.

Indigenous nations did both — often in the same place. The skill is to
spot which is which and to give the evidence.

**Worked example 1 — the Southwest: adobe pueblos and irrigated fields
(modify + adapt):**

> Adult points to the bottom-left illustration panel. "The Hopi and
> other Pueblo nations lived where rain was scarce — a desert with mesas.
> **Adapt:** they built homes of **adobe** — bricks of earth, straw, and
> water dried in the sun. Thick adobe walls stay cool in desert heat and
> warm on cold desert nights. They changed their *building* to fit the
> desert: adapt. **Modify:** they dug channels to carry river or flood
> water to their cornfields — **irrigation**. They changed the *land's*
> water pattern to fit their farms: modify. You can see it in the panel:
> neat rows of corn with a thin stream running between the rows — that
> stream did not get there by itself. Evidence for adapt: the earth
> houses shaped by desert heat. Evidence for modify: the water channels
> shaped by human digging."

**Worked example 2 — the Arctic: kayaks, skin clothing, and stored food
(adapt) + fish weirs (modify):**

> "Inuit and Iñupiat nations faced ice, sea, and long darkness.
> **Adapt:** the **kayak** (qayaq) — a narrow, skin-covered boat that one
> hunter could paddle and even roll upright in freezing water. Clothing
> sewn from caribou skin and fur, with the fur facing in or out depending
> on the weather. Snow-block shelters (**iglu**) raised as quick hunting
> camps — built from the only building material around: snow. Every one
> of these changes the *person's* tools and shelter to fit the cold:
> adapt. **Modify:** wooden **fish weirs** — fences set across streams to
> guide fish into traps — changed how the stream *worked* for people:
> modify. Rule of thumb: ask 'who changed — the people or the land?' If
> the people changed their ways, it is adapt. If the land was rearranged,
> it is modify."

Key idea to say plainly: **Adapting changes people to fit the land;
modifying changes the land to fit people. Strong answers always name
the evidence: the changed thing.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Northwest Coast nations built long houses from cedar planks — the
   forest gave huge straight trees. Adapt or modify? Evidence?"
   *(adapt — the people changed their building material to fit the
   forest; they did not rearrange the forest)*
2. "The same nations set wooden stakes across salmon streams to funnel
   fish into nets. Adapt or modify? Evidence?" *(modify — the stream's
   flow pattern was rearranged by people)*
3. "Eastern Woodlands nations cleared forest patches to plant corn,
   beans, and squash together — the **Three Sisters**. Each plant helps
   the others: corn stalks hold up bean vines, beans feed the soil, and
   squash leaves shade out weeds. Adapt, modify, or both? Evidence?"
   *(both — clearing the forest modified the land; planting the three
   together adapted farming to what the soil and climate allowed)*
4. "Great Plains tipis: cone-shaped tents of bison hide over wooden
   poles, raised in an hour, taken down and carried to follow the herds.
   Adapt or modify? Evidence?" *(adapt — the shelter changed to fit a
   mobile hunting life on open grassland)*
5. "Plateau nations dug pit houses — round homes sunk partly underground
   for winter warmth — and set up tipis for summer travel. Which part is
   adapt? Which part is modify?" *(pit house: adapt — shelter shaped to
   survive winter; digging the pit: a small modify — the ground was
   reshaped. Either answer earns credit with evidence.)*
6. Error-analysis: "A friend says, 'Modifying the environment is always
   bad for nature.' What would you say?" *(not always — irrigation ditches
   and fish weirs fed people for centuries without wrecking the land;
   whether a change harms or helps depends on what it does. The unit
   judges each example by its evidence, not by a slogan.)*

## Independent practice

- The learner draws a T-chart labeled **ADAPT (people changed)** and
  **MODIFY (land changed)** and sorts six examples: adobe houses,
  irrigation ditches, kayaks, fish weirs, cleared cornfields, fur
  clothing. Each entry needs a 3–6 word evidence note.
- The learner writes two "because" sentences: one adapt example and one
  modify example, each with the evidence named
  (e.g., "The Pueblo nations modified their land *because* the
  illustration shows water channels they dug to fields in a desert.").
- The learner finds one adapt and one modify in their *own* life (coat
  vs. garden bed; umbrella vs. sidewalk) and explains each in one
  sentence.

## Application

The learner picks one illustration panel and writes a short "land
report" (4–6 sentences): name the area, describe its environment from
visible clues, give one adapt example and one modify example from the
lesson, and name the evidence for each. The adult checks that every
claim points at something visible or stated.

## Exit check

1. In your own words: what is the difference between adapt and modify?
2. "A nation burns small patches of underbrush so berry bushes grow
   better next year." Adapt or modify? What changed — the people or the
   land? *(modify — the land's plant pattern was rearranged; note: many
   nations managed land this way for generations)*
3. Give one adapt and one modify from today's lesson, each with its
   evidence.

## Present-day connection

Adaptation and modification continue today. Pueblo farmers still dry-farm
and irrigate in the Southwest; Inuit hunters still read sea ice — now
alongside snowmobiles and GPS. Nations also *restore* environments: many
tribal programs replant native grasses, clean rivers, and manage forests
with the same controlled-burn knowledge their ancestors used.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads all examples aloud; the learner
  sorts by pointing or speaking.
- **Alternate response mode:** the T-chart may be built with picture
  cards the learner moves between columns.
- **Language support:** sentence frames — "This is adapt because the
  people changed ___." / "This is modify because the land changed ___."

## Extension

The learner picks the California area (acorn processing, plank canoes
called **tomols** built by the Chumash) and decides: is grinding acorns
into flour adapt or modify? Is building a sewn-plank canoe adapt or
modify? Defend both answers with evidence in three sentences.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners often label everything "adapt." Force the "who changed?"
  test every time; digging, damming, clearing, and channeling are the
  modify tells.
- Learners may think Indigenous peoples only "lived off the land"
  without shaping it. This lesson exists to correct that: irrigation,
  weirs, cleared fields, and managed burns are engineering.
- Do not let "modify" become a villain word — the guided-practice item 6
  guards against this. (Answer key: `answer-key.md`, Lesson 02.)
