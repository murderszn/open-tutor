# Lesson 05 — Context Shapes Perspective; Homelands Change Over Time

**Time:** two sessions, about 35 minutes each · **Materials:** a U.S.
map showing the Southeast and the Cherokee homeland (adult-provided);
pencil and paper; a simple timeline strip (learner-drawn)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will connect a historical
**context** — what was happening around people — to the
**perspectives** they held, and you will trace how one homeland changed
over time. By the end, you will build a context → perspective chain and
sequence then-and-now changes with map evidence.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks: "Last time we learned that a
perspective has reasons, and reasons come from a person's situation.
What was one reason the Wampanoag account emphasized diplomacy?"
Accept any situation-based answer. "Today we give that 'situation' its
history name: **context**."

**Framing (adult reads):** **Context** is everything happening around
people at a given time — the laws, the pressures, the land, the recent
past. Context does not excuse choices, but it explains why people saw
limited options. Today's history includes something painful and true:
in the 1800s, the U.S. government forced Southeast nations, including
the Cherokee, off their homelands. We will name it plainly, learn why
it happened, hear how the Cherokee resisted, and see that the Cherokee
Nation exists today.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **context → perspective chain** links three boxes with arrows:

**CONTEXT (what was happening) → SITUATION (what it meant for these
people) → PERSPECTIVE (how they saw it and what they did)**

**Worked example 1 — the Cherokee and removal (1830s):**

> "CONTEXT: By the 1830s, white settlers wanted Cherokee farmland in
> Georgia and other Southeast states. In 1830, the U.S. Congress passed
> the **Indian Removal Act**, a law saying Southeast nations must move
> west of the Mississippi River. Gold had been found on Cherokee land,
> which made the pressure worse.
>
> SITUATION: The Cherokee Nation had its own constitution, newspaper
> (the *Cherokee Phoenix*, printed in both Cherokee and English using
> Sequoyah's syllabary), farms, and schools. They took their case to the
> U.S. Supreme Court — and **won** (*Worcester v. Georgia*, 1832): the
> Court said Georgia's laws had no force on Cherokee land. But the
> president and the state ignored the ruling.
>
> PERSPECTIVE → ACTION: Cherokee leaders saw removal as the theft of a
> lawful homeland they had every right to keep, so most resisted: they
> petitioned, published, sued, and refused to sign away land. A small
> group signed a removal treaty the Nation had not authorized. In 1838,
> U.S. soldiers forced about 16,000 Cherokee west at bayonet point; cold,
> hunger, and sickness killed roughly 4,000 on the way. The Cherokee call
> it *Nunna daul Tsuny* — 'the trail where they cried.' Americans later
> called it the **Trail of Tears**.
>
> Read the chain backward to check it: the perspective ('this is theft
> of our lawful land') makes sense **because** of the situation (a
> constitutional nation that won in court and was ignored anyway), which
> makes sense **because** of the context (land hunger, gold, and a
> removal law)."

**Worked example 2 — then and now with map evidence:**

> "Take the Cherokee homeland on the map: in 1800 it covered parts of
> present-day Georgia, Tennessee, North Carolina, and Alabama — point to
> those states. **Then:** Cherokee towns, farms, and the *Cherokee
> Phoenix* newspaper there. **Change:** the 1830 law, the ignored court
> victory, the forced march of 1838. **Now:** the Cherokee Nation's
> capital is Tahlequah, Oklahoma, where most Cherokee were forced to go —
> but Cherokee people also live in North Carolina (the Eastern Band, who
> resisted removal and stayed) and across the country. The Nation runs
> schools, health clinics, and businesses, and teaches the Cherokee
> language to new generations. Map evidence: trace the route from the
> Southeast to Oklahoma — that long arrow *is* the change. A homeland can
> be lost on a map and kept in a nation."

Key idea to say plainly: **Context explains why people saw few good
choices; perspectives explain what they did. And "then → now" shows that
nations survive change — the story never ends at the painful part.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Name the three boxes of a context → perspective chain."
   *(context → situation → perspective/action)*
2. "In the Cherokee chain, what was the context?" *(settlers wanted the
   land; gold discovery; the 1830 Indian Removal Act)*
3. "What was the Cherokee situation — what did the Nation have that
   made removal feel like theft?" *(a constitution, a bilingual
   newspaper, farms, schools — and a Supreme Court victory that was
   ignored)*
4. "Why did most Cherokee leaders refuse to sign removal treaties?
   Tie it to the chain." *(their perspective — 'this land is lawfully
   ours' — came from their situation as a constitutional nation that
   had won in court)*
5. Cause/effect with map evidence: "Point to Georgia on the map, then
   to Oklahoma. What caused people to move between them, and what was
   one effect?" *(cause: the Removal Act and forced military removal;
   effect: thousands died; the Nation rebuilt in Oklahoma — evidence:
   the map distance and the 'then/now' locations)*
6. Error-analysis: "A friend says, 'The Cherokee just gave up and
   moved.' What does the chain prove wrong?" *(they petitioned,
   published, and won in court first — resistance came before removal;
   'gave up' ignores the whole situation box)*

## Independent practice

- The learner draws the three-box chain for the Cherokee removal from
  memory, writing 1–2 facts per box (word bank allowed: Removal Act,
  gold, constitution, newspaper, Supreme Court, 1838, Tahlequah).
- The learner writes a then/now paragraph (4–6 sentences): "In 1800 the
  Cherokee homeland was ___. By 1838 ___ had changed it. Today ___."
  Each sentence needs a map or lesson fact as evidence.
- The learner sequences four events on a timeline strip: Sequoyah's
  syllabary (~1821) → Removal Act (1830) → court victory (1832) →
  forced removal (1838), and writes one sentence under each saying what
  changed.

## Application — one homeland's changes over time

The adult selects one more nation with approved sources (for example,
the Seminole of Florida — some resisted removal in the swamps and their
descendants live in Florida today — or the Nez Perce of the Plateau).
The learner builds a mini then/now: homeland on the map **then**, the
change event, the homeland **now**, plus one present-day fact (tribal
government, language program, or news item). Deliverable: a labeled
map + 5-sentence write-up.

## Exit check

1. What is historical context, and why do historians learn it before
   judging people's choices? *(what was happening around people; it
   explains what options they saw)*
2. Finish the chain: "Because settlers wanted Cherokee land and
   Congress passed the Removal Act (context), the Cherokee Nation
   ___ (situation), so its leaders ___ (perspective/action)."
3. Name one way the Cherokee Nation exists today. *(capital at
   Tahlequah, Oklahoma; schools and language programs; the Eastern Band
   in North Carolina — any one with the map or lesson as evidence)*

## Present-day connection

There are three federally recognized Cherokee tribes today — the
Cherokee Nation and United Keetoowah Band in Oklahoma, and the Eastern
Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina — with hundreds of thousands
of citizens combined. The *Cherokee Phoenix* still publishes. Language
classes teach children the syllabary Sequoyah created. Removal is a
true chapter, not the whole book.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the chain boxes aloud; the
  learner arranges pre-written fact cards into the three boxes.
- **Alternate response mode:** the then/now may be drawn as two maps
  (1800 / today) with arrows and labels instead of a paragraph.
- **Language support:** sentence frames — "The context was ___.
  That meant ___. So they ___."

## Extension

The learner compares the Cherokee chain with the Wampanoag story from
Lesson 04: "Both nations faced land pressure from newcomers. How were
their situations different, and how did their actions differ?" (Three
sentences with evidence; the adult keeps the comparison respectful —
different centuries, different choices, no ranking of suffering.)

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners may think the Trail of Tears was a voluntary migration.
  Name it plainly every time: forced removal by soldiers; thousands
  died. The chain's situation box is the antidote.
- Learners may believe court victories always protect people. The 1832
  ruling *was* ignored — a hard, true lesson about law vs. power. Keep
  it factual, not cynical: the Cherokee used every lawful tool they had.
- End on the present: nations endure. Never close a lesson on removal
  without the present-day box. (Answer key: `answer-key.md`, Lesson 05.)
