# Lesson 03 — Comparing Two Nations with Evidence

**Time:** two sessions, about 35 minutes each · **Materials:** the unit
illustration
([`culture-areas-environments.png`](../assets/culture-areas-environments.png));
a North America culture-area map (adult-provided); pencil and paper;
two-column evidence chart (drawn or printed)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will compare how two nations
in different environments lived — what they ate, where they lived, and
how they traveled — and prove every claim with map evidence or source
evidence. By the end, you will build a two-column evidence chart and
explain what the comparison teaches.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks: "From the last two lessons, give me one
adapt and one modify with evidence." Accept any correct pair. Then:
"Today we compare two nations side by side. What should a fair
comparison include?" (Guide toward: same categories on both sides, and
evidence for each claim.)

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Comparing well means comparing **the same categories** on both sides and
attaching **evidence** to every claim. The geographer's comparison chart
has four rows — **Environment, Food, Shelter, Travel** — and one rule:
no claim without evidence. Evidence comes from two places: **map
evidence** (what the map's labels, symbols, and positions prove) and
**source evidence** (what a museum page, tribal source, or the
illustration shows).

**Worked example 1 — modeled comparison: Northwest Coast (Tlingit) vs.
Southwest (Hopi):**

> Adult builds the chart aloud, thinking visibly:
>
> | Category | Northwest Coast — Tlingit | Southwest — Hopi |
> |---|---|---|
> | Environment | Rainy evergreen coast; ocean and salmon rivers. *Evidence:* the top-right illustration panel shows dense evergreens, misty mountains, drying salmon. | Hot dry desert; mesas; scarce rain. *Evidence:* the bottom-left panel shows red mesas, bright sun, dry ground. |
> | Food | Salmon (dried and stored), shellfish, berries, sea mammals. *Evidence:* the salmon rack in the panel; museum sources describe seasonal salmon harvests. | Corn, beans, squash from irrigated and dry-farmed fields. *Evidence:* the panel's crop rows with water channels; Pueblo farming is documented by tribal and museum sources. |
> | Shelter | Long houses of cedar planks; carved house posts. *Evidence:* the panel's plank house with a carved post. | Multi-story adobe pueblos. *Evidence:* the panel's stacked earth-colored dwellings. |
> | Travel | Large cedar canoes on ocean and rivers. *Evidence:* coastal geography — water is the road where forests are thick. | On foot; later horses. *Evidence:* desert mesas and canyons made water travel rare; footpaths connected villages. |
>
> "Now the comparison sentence a geographer writes: 'The Tlingit and the
> Hopi both built with what their environment gave — cedar planks where
> forests towered, adobe where earth baked in the sun — but their foods
> differed because one environment gave salmon and the other gave
> farmland.' Every claim above points at a clue you can check."

**Worked example 2 — the same event, different evidence test:**

> "Suppose someone claims, 'The Hopi fished for salmon like the
> Tlingit.' Test it: the Southwest panel shows desert, mesas, and crop
> fields — no ocean, no salmon rivers, no drying racks. The claim fails
> its evidence test. A comparison is only as strong as the evidence
> behind each cell. When a cell is empty, write 'no evidence found' —
> never invent one."

Key idea to say plainly: **Compare the same categories, prove each
claim, and let empty cells stay empty. The chart is honest only when the
evidence column is full.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Build a two-column chart for the **Arctic (Inuit)** and the
   **Eastern Woodlands (Haudenosaunee)**. Fill the Environment row for
   both sides with evidence from the illustration." *(Arctic: ice, sea,
   snow-block shelter panel; Woodlands: autumn forest, bark longhouse,
   garden panel)*
2. "Fill the Food row for both sides." *(Arctic: sea mammals and fish —
   nothing grows on ice; Woodlands: corn, beans, squash, plus hunted
   deer — forest and soil gave farms and game)*
3. "Fill the Shelter row for both sides." *(Arctic: snow-block shelters
   for hunting camps, plus sod-and-wood houses — built from ice and
   whatever wood drifted in; Woodlands: bark-covered longhouses holding
   several families — built from forest trees)*
4. "Write one comparison sentence using the word 'because' that explains
   a difference between the two." *(e.g., "Inuit shelters were small and
   portable because ice and sea gave no trees for big houses, while
   Haudenosaunee longhouses were large because forests gave bark and
   poles.")*
5. "Now compare the **Great Plains (Lakota)** and the **Southeast
   (Cherokee)** on Travel only. What is your evidence?" *(Plains: horses
   and travois across open grassland — the flat land made horse travel
   fast and useful; Southeast: rivers by canoe and footpaths through
   forest towns — rivers connected farming towns. Evidence: grassland vs.
   river-and-forest geography.)*
6. Error-analysis: "A friend's chart says the Tlingit grew corn in
   irrigated fields. What is wrong, and how would you prove it?"
   *(wrong area's evidence attached to the wrong nation — the
   irrigation evidence belongs to the Southwest panel; the Northwest
   Coast panel shows salmon and cedar, not fields. Fix: move the claim
   or delete it.)*

## Independent practice

- The learner completes the Inuit/Haudenosaunee chart's Travel row and
  writes two comparison sentences (one similarity, one difference),
  each with a "because" and named evidence.
- The learner picks any *other* pair of areas and fills a fresh
  four-row chart from the illustration and lesson notes, marking one
  cell "no evidence found" if the illustration does not show it.
- The learner writes a three-sentence "geographer's verdict": what the
  comparison proves about environment → way of life.

## Application — map-and-photograph task

With the adult's culture-area map: (1) locate and label the homelands of
the two compared nations (Tlingit — southeast Alaska coast; Hopi —
northeastern Arizona); (2) trace the nearest major water with a finger —
ocean/rivers for the Tlingit, the Little Colorado River system for the
Hopi — and say how water shaped each nation's food; (3) find one modern
city near each homeland and note that the nations live there still.
Then, from the Resource Pack's museum links, the adult shows one real
photograph (totem pole; pueblo village) and the learner names two
differences between the photograph and the AI illustration.

## Exit check

1. What four rows go in a geographer's comparison chart?
   *(Environment, Food, Shelter, Travel)*
2. What are the two kinds of evidence, and what proves each?
   *(map evidence — labels/symbols/positions on the map; source evidence
   — what a museum page, tribal source, or illustration shows)*
3. Your chart says the Hopi traveled by canoe. Prove or fix it.
   *(fix it — desert mesas and canyons give no water roads; evidence
   supports foot travel; the canoe claim has no evidence and must go)*

## Present-day connection

Comparisons work in the present too. Today the Tlingit run tribal
governments and fishing programs in southeast Alaska, and the Hopi run
their villages and farms on three mesas in Arizona — both nations
adapting ancient knowledge to modern life. A comparison chart could add
a fifth row: **Today**.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads chart cells aloud; the learner
  points to evidence in the illustration.
- **Alternate response mode:** charts may be dictated or built with
  picture cards; comparison sentences may be spoken.
- **Language support:** sentence frames — "The ___ and the ___ both
  ___, because ___. They differed in ___, because ___."

## Extension

The learner adds a fifth row, **Clothing**, to the Tlingit/Hopi chart
using the Resource Pack's museum sources (cedar-bark and woven
garments vs. cotton and woven wool), with source evidence cited by
page name.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners often compare different categories on each side ("Food" vs.
  "Fun facts"). Insist on identical rows before any sentences.
- Learners may copy the modeled chart instead of building their own.
  The guided pair (Inuit/Haudenosaunee) is deliberately different from
  the model — require fresh evidence.
- Watch for invented evidence ("the Hopi had kayaks"). Celebrate "no
  evidence found" as a correct, honest answer.
