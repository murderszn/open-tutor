# Lesson 01 — Eight Culture Areas and Their Environments

**Time:** two sessions, about 35 minutes each · **Materials:** a printed
North America map showing the eight culture areas (the adult prepares or
prints one from an approved source — see [resource-pack](../resource-pack.md));
the unit's generated illustration
[`culture-areas-environments.png`](../assets/culture-areas-environments.png);
pencil and paper; crayons or colored pencils

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn the eight culture
areas of North America and how each area's environment — its land, water,
weather, plants, and animals — shaped the way the Indigenous nations
living there ate, sheltered, and traveled. By the end, you will match
environments to ways of life and explain your matches with evidence.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks: "Name two ways the land around our home
affects how we live — what we eat, wear, or how we get around." Accept any
reasonable answers (near a lake → fishing; cold winters → warm coats).
Then: "Point to where we live on the U.S. map." Re-teach cardinal
directions if needed (Unit 01).

**Important framing (adult reads):** Indigenous nations are living
communities, not people only from the past. When we study how nations
lived long ago, we are studying ancestors of people who are alive today —
running tribal governments, speaking their languages, and caring for
their homelands. We use the word **nation** for these communities because
that is what many call themselves, and because nations make their own
laws and decisions.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **culture area** is a region where the nations living there developed
similar ways of life because they faced similar environments. North
America is usually divided into eight culture areas. Learn their names
and, more importantly, their *environments* — because the environment
explains the way of life.

The eight areas, with the environment clues that matter:

1. **Arctic** (far north — Alaska, northern Canada): long freezing
   winters, sea ice, tundra. Few plants grow; the sea gives food.
2. **Northwest Coast** (Pacific coast from Alaska to Washington): rainy,
   dense evergreen forests, rich ocean and rivers full of salmon.
3. **Plateau** (inland Pacific Northwest — eastern Washington/Oregon,
   Idaho): dry grasslands and river valleys; salmon runs and root plants.
4. **Great Plains** (the flat middle of the continent): vast grasslands,
   huge bison herds, few trees, big rivers.
5. **Eastern Woodlands** (east of the Mississippi to the Atlantic):
   forests, rivers, lakes; long growing season in the south of the area.
6. **Southeast** (the southeastern U.S.): warm, humid, long growing
   season; rivers, forests, and coasts.
7. **Southwest** (Arizona, New Mexico, and nearby): hot, dry desert and
   mesas; little rain; rivers in canyons.
8. **California** (most of present-day California): mild climate, coast,
   valleys, oak woodlands; acorns and ocean foods.

The geographer's rule for this unit: **environment → way of life.**
Cold sea + ice → hunt sea mammals, wear skin clothing, travel by kayak
and sled. Dense forest + salmon rivers → build with cedar planks, fish
with nets and weirs, carve and trade. Desert + little rain → farm with
irrigation, build with sun-dried earth (adobe). Forest + good soil →
farm corn, beans, and squash and build with bark and wood.

**Worked example 1 — reading environment clues from the illustration:**

> Adult opens `culture-areas-environments.png` (an AI-generated teaching
> illustration — not a photograph, not a real place) and points to the
> top-left panel. "I see a dome made of snow blocks, sea ice, gray water,
> and a narrow boat on a wooden rack. The snow and ice tell me this place
> is freezing most of the year — nothing grows here, so food must come
> from the water. The narrow boat is a **kayak**, built for one hunter on
> cold seas. This is the **Arctic** culture area. My evidence: snow-block
> shelter + sea ice + kayak = a freezing coastal environment, which
> matches the Arctic, not the desert or forest panels."

**Worked example 2 — environment → way of life, step by step:**

> "Now the bottom-left panel. I see flat-topped red rock (mesas), bright
> sun, dry ground — and box-shaped houses stacked like steps, made of
> earth-colored clay. In front, neat rows of small green plants with a
> thin stream of water running between the rows. Steps: (1) The mesas,
> sun, and dry ground tell me this is a **hot, dry desert** — the
> **Southwest**. (2) Little rain falls here, so the rows of plants with
> water running between them must be **irrigated fields** — the people
> brought water to the crops. (3) The stacked earth houses are **pueblos**,
> built of **adobe** (sun-dried earth bricks), which stays cool in desert
> heat. Environment → way of life: desert + scarce water → irrigate crops,
> build thick earth walls."

Key idea to say plainly: **A culture area is not one nation — it is a
group of nations whose environments pushed their daily lives in similar
directions. Read the land first (water? trees? rain? cold?), and the way
of life starts to make sense.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Look at the top-right panel: tall evergreen trees, misty mountains,
   a wooden house with a carved post, a tall carved pole, and fish
   hanging on a rack. Which culture area is it, and what is your
   evidence?" *(Northwest Coast — evergreens + carved pole (totem pole) +
   drying fish = rainy forest coast with salmon)*
2. "Bottom-right panel: a long bark-covered house in autumn woods, with
   tall corn, climbing vines, and round squash growing together. Which
   area? Evidence?" *(Eastern Woodlands — forest + bark longhouse + the
   three farm plants = woodlands with good soil)*
3. "Name the environment clue in each panel that told you the most."
   *(ice/sea; totem pole + salmon; mesas + irrigation; longhouse +
   corn/beans/squash)*
4. "The Plateau area has dry grasslands and salmon rivers. Would you
   expect its nations to farm large fields like the Southeast, or to fish
   and gather roots like the Northwest Coast? Why?" *(fish and gather —
   dry grassland with rivers matches fishing and root-gathering better
   than big farms; the Southeast's long warm rainy season suits farming)*
5. "California has oak trees full of acorns and a mild coast. What two
   foods would you predict nations there relied on?" *(acorns and ocean
   foods — fish, shellfish, seaweed; accept reasonable predictions tied
   to the clues)*
6. Error-analysis: "A friend says, 'Everyone in the same culture area
   lived exactly the same way.' What is wrong with that?" *(a culture
   area holds many different nations; the environment pushed in similar
   directions, but each nation had its own language, leaders, and
   customs — like neighbors with similar weather but different families)*

## Independent practice

- The learner writes the eight culture-area names from memory (word bank
  allowed), then writes one environment word beside each (e.g.,
  "Arctic — ice/sea," "Southwest — desert/mesas").
- The learner picks any two panels of the illustration and writes two
  sentences: "This panel is the ___ area. I know because ___." Each
  sentence must name at least two visible clues.
- The learner draws a quick sketch of one more culture area not shown in
  the four panels (Great Plains, Plateau, Southeast, or California),
  including at least three environment clues, and labels the clues.

## Application

The learner becomes the "environment detective": the adult names a
mystery area by describing only its environment ("hot, dry, flat-topped
rock hills, one river in a canyon"). The learner names the culture area
(Southwest), predicts one food, one shelter, and one travel method, and
explains each prediction from the environment clues. Then they switch
roles: the learner describes a mystery environment and the adult
predicts.

## Exit check

1. Name all eight culture areas. (Word bank allowed; order does not
   matter.)
2. Look at any panel of the illustration: which area is it, and what two
   clues prove it?
3. Finish the sentence with reasoning: "The Northwest Coast nations built
   with cedar planks and fished for salmon *because* ___."
   *(because tall evergreen forests gave cedar wood and rivers/coast gave
   salmon — the environment supplied both)*

## Present-day connection

Indigenous nations live in every one of these eight areas today. For
example, Pueblo nations still live in adobe villages in New Mexico, and
Inuit communities still hunt and fish in the Arctic — now also running
tribal governments, schools, and businesses. "Long ago" is only the
beginning of the story.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads all area names and vocabulary
  aloud; the learner points to panels and speaks answers.
- **Alternate response mode:** matches may be drawn (lines from panel to
  area name) or spoken instead of written.
- **Language support:** word cards for the eight areas and environment
  words (ice, desert, forest, river, mesa, salmon, corn, kayak).

## Extension

The learner researches (with the adult) one real nation from any area —
for example, the Hopi (Southwest) or the Tlingit (Northwest Coast) — and
adds one present-day fact (a tribal website, a language program, a news
item) to their culture-area notes, citing where the fact came from.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners often think "culture area" means "one tribe." Stress that
  each area contains many distinct nations with their own languages —
  the area is a geographer's grouping, not a nation.
- Learners may treat the illustration as photographs of real places. It
  is an AI-generated teaching picture; say so every time it is used.
  Real photographs come from the Resource Pack's museum sources.
- Learners may assume Indigenous peoples vanished. The framing paragraph
  and the present-day connection exist to prevent this — use them.
