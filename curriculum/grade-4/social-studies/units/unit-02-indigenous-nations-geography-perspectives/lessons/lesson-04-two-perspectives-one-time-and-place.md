# Lesson 04 — Two Perspectives, One Time and Place

**Time:** two sessions, about 35 minutes each · **Materials:** the
adult-selected pair of age-appropriate accounts (see
[resource-pack](../resource-pack.md) — Plimoth Patuxet's *You Are the
Historian* and the NMAI's Native Knowledge 360° materials); pencil and
paper

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn what a
**perspective** is — how a person's situation shapes what they notice,
what they care about, and how they tell a story. By the end, you will
name two perspectives on one event, give each one's reasons, and explain
why they differ.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks: "Two kids watch the same soccer game —
one's team wins, the other's loses. Will they tell the same story about
the game? Why not?" Accept any version of: same event, different
feelings and interests. "History works the same way. Today we practice
hearing both stories."

**Framing (adult reads):** A **perspective** is a point of view shaped
by who you are and what is happening to you. Learning two perspectives
does not mean both are equally right about every fact — it means we
understand *why* each side saw things its way. We never guess what real
people thought; we use what the sources actually say. The adult chose
today's accounts from museum and tribal sources.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

When historians compare two accounts of one event, they ask five
questions about **each** account:

1. **Who** made it (creator)? 2. **When** (date)? 3. **For whom**
   (audience)? 4. **Why** (purpose — to celebrate? to warn? to remember?
   to persuade?)? 5. **What evidence** does it give (names, numbers,
   details you can check)?

Then they ask the perspective question: **"Given who this person was
and what was happening to them, why would they see it this way?"**

**Worked example 1 — the 1621 harvest gathering, two retellings
(teacher's simplified models; the adult reads the real accounts from
the Resource Pack first):**

> "Our event: the 1621 harvest gathering at Patuxet/Plymouth, the meal
> many Americans call 'the First Thanksgiving.' **Account A** — told the
> way an English colonist might remember it: 'After a hard year in which
> half of us died, God gave us a good harvest. Our leader invited the
> Wampanoag leader Massasoit, and about ninety of his men came and
> feasted with us for three days. We felt thankful and safe.' Creator: an
> English settler. Purpose: to tell family back in England that the
> colony survived. What it notices: the harvest, the invitation, the
> thankfulness.
>
> **Account B** — told the way a Wampanoag person might remember it:
> 'Our homeland is Patuxet. A sickness brought by Europeans had emptied
> our villages. These newcomers were starving until our people — including
> Tisquantum — showed them how to plant corn and where to fish. When
> their harvest came in, our leader Massasoit brought ninety men — a show
> of strength and diplomacy, to watch these armed strangers and protect
> our towns. We had our own harvest ceremonies long before they arrived.'
> Creator: a Wampanoag person. Purpose: to remember *their* homeland and
> *their* decisions. What it notices: the homeland, the sickness, the
> teaching, the diplomacy.
>
> Same three days. Different perspectives — **because** their situations
> were different: newcomers fearing starvation vs. a nation protecting
> its homeland after terrible loss. Neither account is 'the whole story'
> alone; together they are closer to true."

**Worked example 2 — why perspectives differ (the reasons test):**

> "Take Account A's line 'we invited them.' A historian asks: why would
> a colonist remember it as an invitation? Reasons: (1) the colonists
> were few and afraid — being hosts felt safer than admitting they were
> guests on Wampanoag land; (2) writing to family in England, the
> colonist wanted the colony to sound strong and godly. Now Account B's
> 'a show of strength': why would the Wampanoag remember ninety men
> arriving? Reasons: (1) Massasoit's nation had been weakened by
> sickness — arriving strong protected the towns; (2) diplomacy was how
> nations dealt with newcomers. **The rule: a perspective always has
> reasons, and reasons come from the person's situation.** We find the
> situation first, then the viewpoint makes sense."

Key idea to say plainly: **Same event, different situations, different
stories. Ask who, when, for whom, why — then ask what situation made
that viewpoint make sense.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "In Account A, who is the creator and who is the audience? What is
   the purpose?" *(creator: an English colonist; audience: family/friends
   in England; purpose: to report survival and give thanks)*
2. "In Account B, who is the creator and what does the account notice
   that Account A does not?" *(creator: a Wampanoag person; notices the
   homeland name Patuxet, the sickness, the teaching of planting and
   fishing, the diplomacy — Account A skips these)*
3. "Why would Account A leave out the sickness that emptied Wampanoag
   villages? Give a reason tied to the colonist's situation."
   *(possible: the colonist may not have fully understood it; or a
   letter home focused on the colony's own story; accept any reason tied
   to situation, not mind-reading)*
4. "Why would Account B emphasize 'our leader brought ninety men'? Give
   a reason tied to the Wampanoag situation." *(after devastating
   sickness, showing strength protected the towns; diplomacy with armed
   strangers required caution)*
5. "A textbook says, 'The Pilgrims invited the Indians to the first
   Thanksgiving.' Using today's lesson, what is incomplete about that
   sentence?" *(it tells only Account A's perspective; it leaves out the
   Wampanoag homeland, their losses, their teaching, and their
   diplomacy — one perspective is not the whole story)*
6. Error-analysis: "A friend says, 'Since both sides had reasons, both
   stories are equally true.' What is wrong?" *(reasons explain a
   viewpoint; they do not make every claim true. We still check claims
   against evidence — e.g., the harvest happened; who 'invited' whom is
   interpreted differently. Perspectives need reasons AND evidence.)*

## Independent practice

- The learner fills a two-column "perspective card" for Accounts A and
  B: creator / audience / purpose / two things noticed / one reason the
  viewpoint makes sense.
- The learner writes two sentences: "Account A sees the gathering as
  ___ because ___. Account B sees it as ___ because ___." Each
  "because" must name the person's situation.
- The learner draws the same three-day scene twice — once from above
  the colonists' houses, once from the Wampanoag town's edge — and
  writes one caption per drawing naming what that viewer would notice
  first.

## Application — perspective task

The adult picks one more event from the unit's approved sources (for
example: a trade meeting between a nation and European newcomers, from
the Resource Pack's museum links). The learner: (1) names the two
perspectives; (2) lists what each side would notice; (3) writes a
three-sentence explanation of *why* they differ, citing each side's
situation. The adult checks that no invented thoughts are assigned to
real people — only situation-based reasons.

## Exit check

1. What is a perspective? *(a point of view shaped by who someone is
   and what is happening to them)*
2. Name the five questions historians ask about an account.
   *(who, when, for whom, why, what evidence)*
3. "Two accounts of one event disagree. Does that mean one side is
   lying?" *(not necessarily — different situations produce different
   true-noticed details; check each claim against evidence before
   judging)*

## Present-day connection

The Wampanoag Nation exists today — the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe and the
Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) are federally recognized, with
tribal governments, language-revival programs, and museums. Every
November, many observe a National Day of Mourning alongside
Thanksgiving — a present-day perspective rooted in the same history.
The adult can show the Plimoth Patuxet teacher page (Resource Pack) for
an 8-minute elementary video, *The Wampanoag Way*.

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads both accounts aloud with voices
  or props marking the switch; the learner tracks "who is speaking."
- **Alternate response mode:** perspective cards may be drawn (two faces
  with thought bubbles labeled "notices / wants") or spoken.
- **Language support:** word cards — perspective, creator, audience,
  purpose, evidence, situation; sentence frame: "___ saw it as ___
  because their situation was ___."

## Extension

The learner compares how *two modern sources* describe the same
Thanksgiving — for example, a parade broadcast vs. the National Day of
Mourning observance — using the five questions, and writes which
perspective each source centers and why.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners may treat "two perspectives" as "pick your favorite truth."
  Redirect: perspectives explain viewpoints; evidence still judges
  claims.
- Learners may invent dialogue or thoughts for Massasoit or colonists.
  Today's models are explicitly labeled as the teacher's simplifications;
  the real accounts come from the Resource Pack. Never present invented
  words as quotations.
- This lesson is adult-guided throughout: preview the accounts, keep
  discussion calm and curious, and close by naming the Wampanoag present.
