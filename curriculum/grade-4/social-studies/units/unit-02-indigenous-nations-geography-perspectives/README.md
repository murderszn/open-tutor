# Unit 02 — Indigenous Nations: Geography and Historical Perspectives

**Grade:** 4 (ages 9–10) · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#25](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/25)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 7–10 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, about 35 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

This unit teaches Indigenous nations as **living communities with
homelands** — never as a past-tense topic. Learners first master the
**eight North American culture areas** (Arctic, Northwest Coast,
Plateau, Great Plains, Eastern Woodlands, Southeast, Southwest,
California) and the geographer's rule **environment → way of life**:
reading land, water, climate, plants, and animals to explain food,
shelter, and travel. They then learn to sort **adapt** (people changing
to fit the land) from **modify** (land changed to fit people) with
evidence — irrigation channels, fish weirs, cleared Three Sisters
fields, kayaks, adobe, and longhouses. A modeled **two-nation
comparison** (Tlingit vs. Hopi) teaches the four-row evidence chart and
the honesty rule: *no claim without evidence; empty cells stay empty.*

The second half builds historical thinking: what a **perspective** is,
the five questions historians ask of any account (who, when, for whom,
why, what evidence), and why the same 1621 gathering looks different
from a colonist's and a Wampanoag person's situation. Learners then
build **context → perspective chains** and trace **then → now** change —
including the plainly named Cherokee removal of the 1830s (Indian
Removal Act, the ignored 1832 Supreme Court victory, the 1838 forced
march) — always closing on the present: the Cherokee Nation at
Tahlequah, language programs, and the *Cherokee Phoenix* still
publishing. The unit's project, the **homeland study**, sends the
learner to investigate one nation — map its homeland, chart its
environment → way-of-life evidence, and document its today from a
tribal or museum source. The evidence-chart habit and the
two-perspective rule return in Units 03, 04, 07, and 08.

## Prerequisites (developmental expectations; re-taught here as needed)

- Read a map's title, key, compass rose, and labels; describe directions
  between places (Unit 01).
- Back a claim with map evidence — point at the part of the map that
  proves it (Unit 01).
- Write 3–5 sentences in the learner's own words; draw and label with
  clear intent.
- Discuss respectfully with an adult; no learner is asked to speak
  beyond the adult-supervised setting.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Name** the eight North American culture areas and **explain** how
   each area's environment (landforms, water, climate, plants, animals)
   shaped the food, shelter, and travel of the nations living there
   (D2.Geo.2.3-5, D2.Geo.6.3-5).
2. **Distinguish** adapting to an environment from modifying it, and
   **explain** each with evidence from at least two nations
   (D2.Geo.4.3-5).
3. **Compare** two nations' ways of life in a four-row evidence chart
   (Environment, Food, Shelter, Travel), **citing** map evidence or
   source evidence for each claim (D2.Geo.2.3-5, D2.Geo.3.3-5).
4. **Explain** why people in the same time and place held different
   perspectives, **naming** each viewpoint, its audience and purpose,
   and at least one situation-based reason (D2.His.4.3-5, D2.His.5.3-5,
   D2.Civ.10.3-5).
5. **Build** a context → situation → perspective chain for a historical
   event and **sequence** how one homeland changed over time (then →
   now) with map evidence (D2.His.2.3-5, D2.His.14.3-5, D2.Geo.5.3-5).

## Essential question

*How did the land shape the lives of Indigenous nations — and how do
different perspectives, past and present, help us understand their
histories truthfully?*

## Vocabulary

**Culture area** — a region where nations developed similar ways of life
because they faced similar environments. · **Environment** — the land,
water, weather, plants, and animals of a place. · **Nation** — a
community of people with shared identity who make their own laws and
decisions; the word many Indigenous communities use for themselves. ·
**Homeland** — the land a nation has lived on and cared for, past and
present. · **Adapt** — to change how you live to fit the environment. ·
**Modify** — to change the environment to fit how you live. · **Adobe**
— sun-dried earth bricks used for building in the desert Southwest. ·
**Pueblo** — a multi-level adobe dwelling village of Southwest nations.
· **Iglu** — a snow-block shelter raised as a quick camp by Arctic
hunters. · **Kayak (qayaq)** — a narrow skin-covered Arctic hunting boat.
· **Longhouse** — a long bark- or plank-covered dwelling housing several
families. · **Totem pole** — a carved cedar crest pole of Northwest
Coast nations telling family and nation histories. · **Three Sisters**
— corn, beans, and squash planted together so each helps the others. ·
**Irrigation** — bringing water to dry fields through dug channels. ·
**Perspective** — a point of view shaped by who someone is and what is
happening to them. · **Context** — everything happening around people
at a given time: laws, pressures, land, recent past. · **Evidence** —
the map part, source detail, or visible clue that proves a claim. ·
**Oral history** — history passed down by spoken telling across
generations. · **Removal / displacement** — being forced off one's
homeland. · **Syllabary** — a writing system where each symbol stands
for a syllable (Sequoyah's Cherokee syllabary, ~1821).

## Sensitive-content commitments

- Indigenous nations are taught in the **present tense** as living
  communities; every lesson includes a present-day connection and the
  project requires a sourced "today" section.
- Difficult history (the 1830s Cherokee removal) is named **plainly and
  honestly, never graphic**; no invented dialogue or imagined suffering.
- Perspectives work is **adult-guided**; sources carry Indigenous
  authorship or museum/tribal provenance.
- The generated illustration is labeled an **AI teaching picture** at
  every use — never a photograph, never a real place. It shows
  dwellings and tools without depicting any community's members.
