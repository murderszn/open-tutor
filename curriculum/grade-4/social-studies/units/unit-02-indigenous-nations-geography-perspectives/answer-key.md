# Answer Key — Unit 02: Indigenous Nations, Geography, and Perspectives

**For the guiding adult only.** Every learner question in the unit's
lessons, practice sets, quiz, and assessment is solved here. Accept any
answer that names the required evidence and reasoning, even if worded
differently. Open responses list acceptable content, not exact wording.

## Lesson 01 — Eight culture areas and environment

**Guided practice**
1. Northwest Coast — evidence: dense evergreen trees, carved pole (totem
   pole), fish drying on a rack; all three are visible in the top-right
   panel.
2. Eastern Woodlands — evidence: bark-covered longhouse and the
   corn/bean-vine/squash garden, both visible in the bottom-right panel.
3. Strongest single clues: ice + sea (Arctic); totem pole + drying
   salmon (Northwest Coast); mesas + watered crop rows (Southwest);
   longhouse + Three Sisters garden (Eastern Woodlands). Accept any one
   well-defended clue per panel.
4. Fish and gather — accept with environment reasoning: dry grassland
   with salmon rivers supports fishing and root-gathering better than
   large-scale farming; the Southeast's long, warm, rainy growing season
   is what makes big farms work there.
5. Acorns and ocean foods (fish, shellfish). Accept any two foods tied
   to oak woodland + coast clues.
6. Error-analysis (acceptable): "A culture area is a geographer's
   grouping of many nations with similar environments — not one nation.
   Each nation had its own language, leaders, and customs, like
   neighbors sharing weather but not families."

**Independent practice**
- Eight names: Arctic, Northwest Coast, Plateau, Great Plains, Eastern
  Woodlands, Southeast, Southwest, California (any order). Environment
  words (acceptable): Arctic — ice/sea; Northwest Coast — rainy
  forest/salmon; Plateau — dry grassland/rivers; Great Plains —
  grassland/bison; Eastern Woodlands — forest/lakes; Southeast — warm/
  humid; Southwest — desert/mesas; California — mild coast/oaks.
- Two panel sentences: each must name the area + two visible clues
  (see guided 1–2 for the four pictured panels).
- Sketch: must show the chosen area with at least three labeled
  environment clues (e.g., Great Plains: flat grass, bison, river,
  few trees).

**Application (environment detective)** — accept any mystery round
where the learner's prediction is tied to stated clues (e.g., Southwest
→ corn, adobe, foot travel — because desert/mesas/canyon river).

**Exit check**
1. The eight names (word bank allowed).
2. Any panel: area + two clues (see above).
3. "…because tall evergreen forests gave cedar wood and the rivers and
   coast gave salmon — the environment supplied both." Accept any
   version naming forest → wood and water → fish.

## Lesson 02 — Adapt to the land, shape the land

**Guided practice**
1. Adapt — the people changed their building material to fit the
   forest; the forest itself was not rearranged.
2. Modify — wooden stakes rearranged how the stream flowed for people;
   the land/water pattern changed.
3. Both — clearing the forest modified the land; planting the Three
   Sisters together adapted farming to the soil and climate. Evidence:
   corn stalks hold bean vines, beans feed soil nitrogen, squash leaves
   shade weeds (accept "they help each other grow").
4. Adapt — the shelter was designed to fit a mobile bison-hunting life
   on open grassland; the people changed, not the land.
5. Pit house: adapt (shelter shaped for winter survival); digging the
   pit: modify (ground reshaped). Accept either label with the
   "who changed?" reasoning.
6. Error-analysis (acceptable): "Not always — irrigation ditches and
   fish weirs fed people for centuries without wrecking the land.
   Whether a change harms or helps depends on what it does; judge each
   example by its evidence."

**Independent practice**
- T-chart: ADAPT — adobe houses (walls shaped for desert heat), kayaks
  (boat shaped for icy seas), fur clothing (clothing shaped for cold);
  MODIFY — irrigation ditches (water pattern rearranged), fish weirs
  (stream flow rearranged), cleared cornfields (forest removed for
  farms). Each needs its evidence note.
- Two "because" sentences: accept any correct adapt/modify pair with
  named evidence.
- Own-life examples: accept any correct pair (e.g., adapt: wearing a
  coat; modify: planting a garden bed) with one-sentence explanations.

**Application (land report)** — 4–6 sentences: area named, environment
described from visible clues, one adapt + one modify with evidence.
Check that each claim points at something visible or stated in lessons.

**Exit check**
1. Acceptable: "Adapting changes people (or their ways) to fit the
   land; modifying changes the land to fit people."
2. Modify — the land's plant pattern was rearranged by people.
3. Any correct adapt + modify pair with evidence (see independent
   practice).

## Lesson 03 — Comparing two nations with evidence

**Guided practice**
1. Environment row — Arctic/Inuit: freezing coast, sea ice, tundra
   (evidence: top-left panel — snow-block dome, sea ice, kayak).
   Eastern Woodlands/Haudenosaunee: forests, rivers, lakes (evidence:
   bottom-right panel — autumn forest, longhouse, garden).
2. Food row — Arctic: sea mammals, fish (nothing grows on ice;
   evidence: sea and ice in the panel). Woodlands: corn, beans, squash,
   deer (evidence: garden in the panel; forest game from lesson).
3. Shelter row — Arctic: snow-block hunting shelters + sod/wood houses
   (built from ice and driftwood; evidence: iglu in the panel).
   Woodlands: bark longhouses for several families (built from forest
   trees; evidence: longhouse in the panel).
4. Acceptable: "Inuit shelters were small and portable because ice and
   sea gave no trees for big houses, while Haudenosaunee longhouses
   were large because forests gave bark and poles." Any "because"
   sentence tying a shelter difference to an environment difference.
5. Travel — Plains/Lakota: horses across open grassland (flat land made
   horse travel fast; evidence: grassland geography). Southeast/
   Cherokee: canoes on rivers + footpaths (rivers connected farming
   towns; evidence: river-and-forest geography).
6. Error-analysis: the irrigation evidence belongs to the Southwest
   panel; the Northwest Coast panel shows salmon and cedar, not fields.
   Fix by moving the claim to the Hopi column or deleting it.

**Independent practice**
- Travel row (Inuit/Haudenosaunee): Inuit — kayaks, sleds (ice and sea
  are the roads); Haudenosaunee — canoes on rivers, footpaths (forest
  and rivers). Two "because" sentences: one similarity (both used what
  the environment gave), one difference (ice vs. forest → different
  vehicles).
- Fresh pair chart: four rows filled; one "no evidence found" cell
  allowed if the illustration does not show it — celebrate honesty.
- Geographer's verdict: three sentences stating what the comparison
  proves about environment → way of life, with evidence.

**Application (map-and-photograph task)** — (1) Tlingit homeland:
southeast Alaska coast; Hopi homeland: northeastern Arizona — labeled
on the map. (2) Water traced: ocean/rivers for the Tlingit (food =
salmon); Little Colorado River system for the Hopi (food = irrigated
crops). (3) One modern city near each (e.g., Juneau, AK; Flagstaff, AZ
— accept any reasonable). Photograph vs. illustration: two differences
(e.g., "the photo is a real place with people and weather; the
illustration is a simplified drawing with no people").

**Exit check**
1. Environment, Food, Shelter, Travel.
2. Map evidence — labels, symbols, positions on the map. Source evidence
   — what a museum page, tribal source, or illustration shows.
3. Fix it: desert mesas and canyons give no water roads; foot travel is
   the evidenced answer; the canoe claim has no evidence and must go.

## Lesson 04 — Two perspectives, one time and place

**Guided practice**
1. Creator: an English colonist. Audience: family/friends in England.
   Purpose: to report that the colony survived and to give thanks.
2. Creator: a Wampanoag person. Notices: the homeland name (Patuxet),
   the sickness, the teaching of planting/fishing, the diplomacy —
   all absent from Account A.
3. Accept any situation-based reason (e.g., the letter home centered
   the colony's own story; the colonist may not have grasped the full
   loss). Reject mind-reading ("he was evil").
4. Accept any situation-based reason (e.g., after devastating sickness,
   arriving strong protected the towns; diplomacy with armed strangers
   required caution).
5. Acceptable: "It tells only the colonist's perspective. It leaves out
   the Wampanoag homeland, their losses, their teaching, and their
   diplomacy — one perspective is not the whole story."
6. Error-analysis (acceptable): "Reasons explain *why* someone saw
   things their way; they don't make every claim true. We still check
   each claim against evidence."

**Independent practice**
- Perspective card (Account B): creator — a Wampanoag person; audience
  — their own people / future generations; purpose — to remember the
  homeland and the nation's decisions; noticed — Patuxet, the sickness,
  the teaching, the diplomacy (any two); reason — situation-based
  (e.g., protecting towns after loss).
- Two "because" sentences: each names a viewpoint + the situation
  behind it.
- Two drawings: each captioned with what that viewer would notice
  first (colonist: houses, harvest, safety; Wampanoag viewer: the
  town's edge, the strangers, the land).

**Application (perspective task)** — two perspectives named; what each
notices listed; three-sentence "why they differ" citing each side's
situation. Check: no invented thoughts for real people.

**Exit check**
1. Acceptable: "A point of view shaped by who someone is and what is
   happening to them."
2. Who, when, for whom, why, what evidence.
3. Not necessarily — different situations produce different true
   details; check each claim against evidence before judging.

## Lesson 05 — Context shapes perspective; homelands change over time

**Guided practice**
1. Context → situation → perspective/action.
2. Context: settlers wanted Cherokee farmland; gold found on Cherokee
   land; the 1830 Indian Removal Act.
3. Situation: a constitutional nation with farms, schools, and a
   bilingual newspaper — that won in the Supreme Court and was ignored
   anyway — so removal felt like theft of lawful land.
4. Acceptable: "Their perspective was 'this land is lawfully ours,'
   from their situation as a constitutional nation with a court victory;
   so they resisted with petitions, the newspaper, and lawsuits instead
   of signing."
5. Cause: the Removal Act and forced military removal in 1838. Effect:
   thousands died; the Nation rebuilt with its capital at Tahlequah,
   Oklahoma. Map evidence: the Georgia → Oklahoma distance; the then/now
   locations.
6. Error-analysis (acceptable): "They didn't give up — they petitioned,
   published, and won in court *before* removal. 'Gave up' ignores the
   whole situation box: resistance came first."

**Independent practice**
- Three-box chain: context (Removal Act, gold, land hunger), situation
  (constitution, newspaper, court victory ignored), perspective/action
  (resistance, then forced removal 1838, capital at Tahlequah).
- Then/now paragraph: 1800 homeland (GA/TN/NC/AL towns and farms) →
   change (1830 law, ignored 1832 victory, 1838 forced march) → today
  (Tahlequah capital; Eastern Band in NC; schools/language programs).
- Timeline: ~1821 syllabary (Cherokee writing, newspaper possible) →
  1830 Removal Act (law ordering removal) → 1832 court victory
  (Cherokee win, then ignored) → 1838 forced removal (soldiers, deaths,
  the Trail of Tears). One "what changed" sentence per stop.

**Application (second nation)** — labeled map (then homeland, change
arrow, now location) + 5-sentence write-up with a named present-day
source. Accept Seminole, Nez Perce, or any approved nation with
sourced facts.

**Exit check**
1. Acceptable: "Context is what was happening around people. It shows
   what options they saw, so we understand choices before judging them."
2. Acceptable: "…the Cherokee Nation *had a constitution, a newspaper,
   and a Supreme Court victory that was ignored* (situation), so its
   leaders *saw removal as theft and resisted with petitions and
   lawsuits* (perspective/action)."
3. Any one: capital at Tahlequah, Oklahoma; three recognized tribes;
   schools and language programs; the *Cherokee Phoenix* still
   publishes; the Eastern Band in North Carolina.
