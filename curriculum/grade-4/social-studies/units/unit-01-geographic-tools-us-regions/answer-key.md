## Lesson 01 — Reading maps like a geographer

**Guided practice**
1. Accept any accurate paraphrase, e.g.: "This map shows the states
   (and capitals/cities) of the United States."
2. Whatever the map's key says — read it fresh. On a standard U.S.
   classroom map the star marks a capital city; the learner points to
   one star on the map.
3. West. Evidence: the compass rose (west points left) + the Florida
   and California labels.
4. North. Evidence: the compass rose (north points up) + the Maine and
   Texas labels.
5. Method: trace the river's blue line with a finger and count the
   state labels it crosses. On a standard U.S. map the Mississippi
   touches about ten states — well over three — so the claim is TRUE;
   the evidence is the traced line and the counted labels.
6. Tell them: a U.S. map is drawn at a small scale to fit fifty states
   on one page — there is no room for streets. Their street needs a
   large-scale map (like a town map).

**Independent practice** (acceptable examples)
- "Ohio is east of Indiana" (compass rose + labels); "Montana is north
  of Wyoming" (compass rose + labels); "Florida is south of Georgia"
  (compass rose + labels).
- Six parts: title (what the map shows), key (what symbols mean),
  compass rose (directions), scale bar (real distances), labels (names),
  grid (exact squares).
- Example claims: "The school is east of the park — evidence: the
  compass rose and the two labels." "Denver is in square C3 — evidence:
  the grid."

**Exit check**
1. Title, key (legend), compass rose, scale bar, labels, grid — with
   each one's job stated.
2. North. Evidence: compass rose + the Washington and Oregon labels.
3. Because a claim anyone can check beats a claim only you believe —
   map evidence lets another person verify it.

## Lesson 02 — Scale, grids, and direction

**Guided practice**
1. Accept: "Each centimeter on the map stands for ___ real miles" (fill
   in your map's bar).
2. Map-dependent. Check: (cm measured) × (bar value) = estimate; the
   learner must show all three steps.
3. The learner traces column C to row 2 and reads what the square
   holds (on your gridded map, confirm it is the lake).
4. North (on a standard grid with numbers growing downward, C3 sits
   above E6... verify against YOUR map's grid orientation). Evidence:
   the grid squares + the compass rose. **Adult: confirm the direction
   against your own map before scoring — grids vary.**
5. The state map — it shows both cities and the roads between them;
   the neighborhood map is too zoomed in.
6. They forgot to multiply by the scale: 3 cm × 20 miles = about 60
   miles, not 3 miles.

**Independent practice** (method checks)
- Verify the three steps and the multiplication; accept any reasonable
  measurement (±1 cm).
- Verify each square's contents against your map.
- Example: "Austin (D4) is about 300 miles southwest of Little Rock
  (E3) — evidence: the grid squares, the compass rose, and the scale
  bar." (Adult: verify the direction and estimate against your map.)

**Exit check**
1. 5 × 100 = about 500 miles.
2. Go to column B, then down to row 2 — the square names the exact
   spot.
3. Because map measurements are never perfect; the answer is close to
   the truth, not exact.

## Lesson 03 — U.S. regions and their environments

**Guided practice**
1. Accept any two: red barn, silo, neat crop rows, flat land to the
   horizon, big open sky.
2. Accept Panel A (rocky coast, lighthouse, sailboat on the ocean) or
   Panel C (bayou water, moss, heron) — either is fine WITH two clues
   named. (If the learner picks B or D, ask what water each shows.)
3. South (subregion "United States South").
4. Accept any accurate contrast, e.g.: "The South (Panel C) is wet —
   still water and mossy trees — while the West (Panel D) is dry —
   cacti and red rock."
5. The U.S. map shows desert landforms inside the West region, and
   Panel D shows a U.S. desert landscape — deserts exist inside the
   United States.
6. One shared feature is not enough evidence: the cacti, red rock, and
   dry bright sky together point to the West; good evidence weighs all
   the clues, and the Northeast panel is the rocky coast (Panel A).

**Independent practice**
- Maine → Northeast; Ohio → Midwest; Georgia → South; Arizona → West;
  Vermont → Northeast; Iowa → Midwest; Louisiana → South; Oregon →
  West.
- Region profile: check 3 sentences (environment with evidence named,
  one landmark/feature, one fact).
- Compelling question: must start with why/how and need evidence
  (example: "Why is the West the most environmentally varied region?");
  two supporting questions must be smaller steps (example: "What
  landforms does the West hold?" "Where does the West's rain fall?").

**Exit check**
1. Northeast, Midwest, South, West.
2. The `subregion` column; Montana's reads "United States West."
3. Accept any two: still dark water, moss-draped trees, lush green
   growth, the heron (standing water means plenty of rain).

## Lesson 04 — Oceans, seas, capitals, time zones

**Guided practice**
1. Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic, Southern. The Southern Ocean is
   the newly recognized fifth (source: NOAA).
2. A sea (the Gulf is a partly enclosed sea). Evidence: the map shows
   it smaller than the oceans and partly enclosed by land; the label
   names it.
3. No. Capital = Helena (`capital` column); biggest city = Billings
   (`biggest_city` column). The two columns are different.
4. 9:00 AM. Zones: Chicago = Central, Denver = Mountain — one zone
   west, one hour earlier: 10:00 − 1 = 9:00.
5. 11:00 AM. New York = Eastern — one zone EAST of Chicago, so one
   hour LATER: 10:00 + 1 = 11:00.
6. The model is backwards: the sun does not travel around Earth. Earth
   rotates (spins), turning places toward the sun (day) and away from
   it (night) — the globe-and-lamp model shows the globe moving, not
   the lamp.

**Independent practice**
- Five oceans labeled correctly; one sentence citing NOAA for the
  count of five.
- Six capitals: verify each against the `capital` column; verify the
  biggest-city check against the `biggest_city` column.
- Time-zone table: verify zone names first, then the ±1-hour-per-zone
  arithmetic.

**Exit check**
1. Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic, Southern.
2. A capital is where the state's government is located. Do not guess
   it, because the biggest city is often NOT the capital — check the
   `capital` column (e.g., California's capital is Sacramento, not Los
   Angeles).
3. 7:00 PM. New York = Eastern, Los Angeles = Pacific — three zones
   east, three hours later: 4:00 + 3 = 7:00.

## Lesson 05 — Comparing regions with data

**Guided practice**
1. Sacramento; 39,538,000; 163,695 (all "about" — rounded).
2. California — `population_approx` 39,538,000 vs. New York 20,201,000.
3. Illinois — density 221.2 vs. Montana 7.4 people per square mile.
4. No — 733,000 rounded stays 733,000. Rounded numbers stay useful
   because they capture big differences; tiny changes do not move
   them.
5. Similarity (example): both are large Southern states by population
   (Texas ~29.1M, Florida ~21.5M). Difference (example): Texas's area
   (268,596 sq mi) is about four times Florida's (65,758 sq mi).
6. Montana: 1,084,000 people on 147,040 sq mi; Rhode Island: 1,097,000
   on 1,545 sq mi. Nearly the same number of people on hugely
   different land — area and population are different columns.

**Independent practice**
- Verify the three comparisons (area, population, density) with
  numbers and column names; accept "about" phrasing.
- "Approximate" explanation must include: rounded, not exact, changes
  over time — with one dataset example.
- `state_fact` connection: accept any reasonable link to the region's
  environment (e.g., Florida's coastline fact → the South's warm
  water).

**Exit check**
1. `population_approx` = the rounded, approximate number of people.
   "Approx" = rounded, not exact; the real count changes constantly.
2. Washington: 71,298 sq mi vs. Hawaii: 10,932 sq mi — Washington is
   larger by about 60,000 sq mi (accept "about six times as big").
3. CAN: which of two states is more crowded (density column). CANNOT:
   anything not in the columns — e.g., what it feels like to live
   there, what the weather is today.

11. (a) 9:00 AM (two zones west: 11:00 − 2). (b) 12:00 noon (one zone
    east: 11:00 + 1). (c, 2 pts) Accept: "Earth's rotation turns

    happens at different moments. Time zones divide the world so each
    zone's clocks match its daylight."

    Write "about" because the real count changes constantly and the
    figure is rounded, not exact.
13. (a) Montana: 147,040 sq mi vs. Illinois: 57,914 sq mi — Montana
    is larger. (b) Illinois: 221.2 vs. Montana: 7.4 people per sq
    mi — Illinois is more crowded. (c, 2 pts) Accept: "This proves the

    density column answers it." CANNOT (example): "What it feels like
    to live there — feelings are not in any column." Difference: "The
    dataset answers questions its columns measure; everything else
