# Practice Sets — Unit 01

Weekly practice with hints. Answers are held in
[answer-key.md](answer-key.md) — learner pages never contain answers.
The adult may read any item aloud; pointing and spoken answers are full
response modes.

## Week 3 — Map tools and map evidence

*After Lessons 01–02. Success: the learner names map parts, reads a
scale bar, uses a grid, and cites map evidence for every claim.*

**A1.** Label the six map parts on a blank outline of a map: title, key,
compass rose, scale bar, labels, grid.
*Hint: the key is the box that explains the symbols.*

**A2.** The key says a triangle means "mountain" and a wavy blue line
means "river." On the map you find three triangles and one wavy blue
line. What did you find?
*Hint: read the key first, then match each symbol.*

**A3.** On the U.S. map: is Oregon north or south of California? Write
the claim and name your two pieces of map evidence.
*Hint: evidence = the compass rose AND the two state labels.*

**A4.** A map's scale bar says 1 cm = 50 miles. You measure 6 cm between
two towns. About how far apart are they? Show the three steps.
*Hint: read the bar, measure, multiply — then say "about."*

**A5.** The grid reference for a lake is C2. Describe how a friend with
the same map finds it.
*Hint: columns are letters, rows are numbers — which do you read first?*

**A6.** Which map would you use to find the library inside your town — a
U.S. map or a town map? Why?
*Hint: think large-scale (small area, lots of detail) vs. small-scale.*

**A7.** Error-analysis: "A friend measured 3 cm on a map with a
1 cm = 20 miles scale bar and said the towns are 3 miles apart." What
went wrong, and what is the right estimate?
*Hint: what should they have multiplied?*

**A8.** Make one claim about the U.S. map that uses BOTH a grid reference
and the compass rose as evidence. Write the claim, then name both
pieces of evidence.
*Hint: example shape — "[City] in square ___ is ___ of [City] in square
___."*

## Week 4 — U.S. regions

*After Lesson 03. Success: the learner sorts states by region from data
and connects each region to its environment with evidence.*

**B1.** Name the four Census regions. Beside each, write one
environmental characteristic (landform, water, plants, or climate).
*Hint: your four region profiles from Lesson 03 are allowed.*

**B2.** Sort these states into regions using the dataset's `subregion`
column: Maine, Ohio, Georgia, Arizona, Vermont, Iowa, Louisiana, Oregon.
*Hint: read the row, drop the words "United States," keep the region.*

**B3.** Look at the unit's illustration
([four-us-regions-landscapes.png](assets/four-us-regions-landscapes.png)).
Which panel matches the Midwest? Name two visible clues.
*Hint: look for the barn, the silo, and what the land is doing.*

**B4.** Which panel matches the South? Name two visible clues.
*Hint: water, moss, the bird — what do they tell you about rain and
warmth?*

**B5.** Text-only version of B3–B4 (same questions, no picture needed):
Panel B shows a red barn, a silo, and flat cornfields under a big sky.
Panel C shows moss-draped cypress trees, still dark water, and a heron.
Which panel is the Midwest and which is the South? Name one clue for
each.
*Hint: farmland needs flat land; bayous need lots of water.*

**B6.** A friend says, "Every state in the West looks like a desert."
Use Panel D AND your region knowledge to answer in two sentences.
*Hint: the West is the most varied region — what else does it hold?*

**B7.** Write a compelling geographic question about one region, plus
two supporting questions.
*Hint: compelling questions start with why or how and need evidence to
answer; supporting questions are smaller steps toward the answer.*

**B8.** Explain in 2–3 sentences why the South is generally warmer than
the Northeast, using the words *location* and *evidence*.
*Hint: where does each region sit on the map? What does the
illustration show?*

## Week 5 — Oceans, seas, capitals, time zones

*After Lesson 04. Success: the learner names the five oceans, looks up
capitals from data, and converts times between U.S. zones.*

**C1.** Name the five oceans. Which one is the "newest" on modern maps,
and what does the U.S. officially recognize?
*Hint: NOAA is the source the lesson used.*

**C2.** Label the five oceans on a blank world outline map. Then write
one sentence explaining why older maps sometimes show only four.
*Hint: what did people used to do with the Southern Ocean's water?*

**C3.** Look up the capitals of these states in the dataset: Texas,
Montana, Florida, New York. For each, say whether it is also the
state's biggest city (check the `biggest_city` column).
*Hint: the capital column and the biggest-city column are different —
read both.*

**C4.** It is 2:00 PM in Denver (Mountain). What time is it in Chicago
(Central)? Show your zone thinking.
*Hint: name each city's zone, count the zones between, add or subtract.*

**C5.** It is 9:00 AM in Los Angeles (Pacific). What time is it in New
York (Eastern)?
*Hint: how many zones east is New York from Los Angeles?*

**C6.** It is 6:00 PM in New York (Eastern). What time is it in Seattle
(Pacific)?
*Hint: count the zones west from Eastern — Central, Mountain, Pacific —
and subtract one hour per zone.*

**C7.** Error-analysis: "A friend says time zones exist because the sun
travels around the Earth once a day." Explain the correct model in two
sentences, using the word *rotation*.
*Hint: what did the globe-and-lamp model show moving?*

**C8.** Why do we have time zones at all, instead of one time for the
whole world? Two sentences.
*Hint: imagine waking up at "7 AM" in pitch darkness — what would that
feel like?*

## Week 6 — Comparing regions with data

*After Lesson 05. Success: the learner reads dataset columns, compares
states, and treats rounded numbers as approximate.*

**D1.** What does "approx" mean in `population_approx`? Write the
definition in your own words and give one example with the word
"about."
*Hint: rounded, not exact — and it changes over time.*

**D2.** Compare Texas and Florida: which has the larger area, which has
more people, and which is more crowded? Write all three answers with
the numbers and column names.
*Hint: read both rows; compare column by column.*

**D3.** Which is more crowded — Alaska or Rhode Island? By about how
many people per square mile?
*Hint: density column; subtract to find "by about how many."*

**D4.** Montana and Rhode Island have almost the same population
(about 1.08M vs. 1.10M). How can that be, when Montana's area is about
95 times larger? Two sentences.
*Hint: area and population are different columns — land does not create
people.*

**D5.** Find the `statehood_year` for Hawaii and Vermont. Which became
a state first, and how many years earlier?
*Hint: smaller year = earlier; subtract.*

**D6.** Pick any two states from DIFFERENT regions. Write one
similarity and one difference, each backed by a dataset number.
*Hint: similarity and difference both need column names and numbers.*

**D7.** A friend says, "The dataset proves Texas is the best state."
What is wrong with this claim? Two sentences.
*Hint: what CAN data prove — and what is a matter of opinion?*

**D8.** Write a 3-sentence "data detective" summary: what did comparing
states by area, population, and density teach you about U.S. regions?
*Hint: think about the Montana/Rhode Island surprise and the
Texas/Florida comparison.*
