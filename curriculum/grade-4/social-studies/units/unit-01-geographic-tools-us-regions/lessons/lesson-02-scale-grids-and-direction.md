# Lesson 02 — Scale, Grids, and Direction

**Time:** two sessions, about 35 minutes each · **Materials:** a U.S. map
with a scale bar; a ruler (or a strip of paper marked in centimeters); a
U.S. map with a letter–number grid (or the adult draws grid lines A–E,
1–5 over a printed map); the learner's mini-map from Lesson 01's
application

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how a map's scale bar
turns measurements into real distances, and how a grid names exact spots.
By the end, you will estimate a real distance between two cities and find
places by grid reference — and use both as map evidence.

**Readiness check:** Adult holds the Lesson 01 mini-map: "Point to the
scale bar. What do you think it is for?" Accept any reasonable guess;
today's lesson answers it.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A U.S. map is far smaller than the United States. The **scale bar** tells
you exactly how much smaller. It might read "1 cm = 200 miles." That means:
every centimeter you measure on the map stands for 200 miles in the real
world. To estimate a real distance: **(1) read the scale bar, (2) measure
on the map, (3) multiply.**

Big idea number two: **large-scale maps show a small area with lots of
detail; small-scale maps show a big area with little detail.** A
neighborhood map is large-scale — you can see streets. A U.S. map is
small-scale — you see states, but no streets. Neither map is wrong; each
is right for its job.

Big idea number three: a **grid** names exact spots. Letters run down one
side (A, B, C…), numbers across the top (1, 2, 3…). A place in column C,
row 4 is "C4." Two people with the same map and the same grid reference
find the same spot — that is why grids count as map evidence.

**Worked example 1 — estimating a real distance:**

> Adult reads the U.S. map's scale bar: "1 centimeter = 200 miles. I
> measure from the label 'Denver' to the label 'Kansas City': 4
> centimeters. 4 × 200 miles = 800 miles. So Denver and Kansas City are
> about 800 miles apart in real life. Steps: (1) read the scale bar,
> (2) measure carefully, (3) multiply. The answer is an **estimate** —
> my measurement is not perfect, so I say 'about 800 miles,' not
> 'exactly.'"

**Worked example 2 — grid reference and a direction claim:**

> Adult looks at the gridded map: "The grid says Chicago sits in square
> D3 and St. Louis sits in square D5. Same letter column, higher number —
> on this map the numbers grow downward, so St. Louis is south of
> Chicago. My claim: 'St. Louis is south of Chicago.' My evidence: (1)
> the grid squares D3 and D5; (2) the compass rose confirming south runs
> down the page. Now a scale check: the scale bar says 1 cm = 100 miles;
> Chicago to St. Louis measures about 3 cm, so about 300 miles."

Key idea to say plainly: **A scale bar turns map centimeters into real
miles: read it, measure, multiply — and call the answer an estimate. A
grid turns "somewhere on the map" into an exact square like C4. Both are
map evidence you can point to.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Read our map's scale bar out loud. In your own words, what does it
   mean?" *(e.g., "each centimeter stands for 200 real miles")*
2. "Measure from your state's capital label to a neighboring state's
   capital. How many centimeters? So about how far in real life?"
   *(measure, multiply; adult checks the arithmetic)*
3. "The grid reference for Miami is E6. Find square E6 — what do you see
   there?" *(Miami's label; learner traces column E, row 6)*
4. "Denver is in C3. Is C3 north or south of E6? What is your evidence?"
   *(north — grid letters/numbers plus the compass rose)*
5. "Which map would you use to plan a drive between two cities in our
   state — a neighborhood map or a state map? Why?" *(state map: it shows
   both cities and the roads between them; the neighborhood map is too
   zoomed in)*
6. Error-analysis: "A friend measures 2 cm on a map whose bar says
   1 cm = 50 miles, and says the real distance is 2 miles. What went
   wrong?" *(they forgot to multiply by the scale — it is about 100
   miles, not 2)*

## Independent practice

- The learner picks two cities on the U.S. map, measures the map distance,
  and estimates the real distance with the scale bar (adult supplies the
  map's scale and checks the multiplication). The learner writes the three
  steps.
- The learner finds five labeled places by grid reference (adult calls out
  references like B2, D4) and writes what sits in each square.
- The learner writes one claim that uses BOTH scale and grid evidence
  (e.g., "Austin (D4) is about ___ miles southwest of Little Rock (E3)").

## Application

The learner **uses the scale bar on their Lesson 01 mini-map** (or adds
one: "1 cm = 1 step"): measure two real distances in the room, estimate
with the bar, and check by counting actual steps. Write: the two
estimates, the two step-counts, and one sentence on how close the scale
came. Then the learner labels the mini-map's grid squares A1–C3 and writes
the grid reference of two features.

## Exit check

1. A map's bar says 1 cm = 100 miles. You measure 5 cm between two
   cities. About how far apart are they? *(5 × 100 = about 500 miles)*
2. A place is in grid square B2. How would you explain to a friend how to
   find it? *(go to column B, row 2; the square names the exact spot)*
3. Why do we say "about" when we give a scale estimate? *(measurements
   are not perfect; the answer is close, not exact)*

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads scale bars and grid labels aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** estimates may be spoken; grid finds may be
  pointed to rather than written.
- **Math support:** the adult supplies a multiplication table for the
  scale math; the geography thinking — read, measure, multiply — stays
  with the learner.

## Extension

The learner compares the same two cities on a small-scale U.S. map and a
larger-scale state map: which map makes measuring easier, and which one
shows the roads between them? Two to three sentences, citing what each
map's scale allows.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners multiply by the wrong number (e.g., multiplying centimeters by
  centimeters). Always write the scale as a sentence first: "1 cm stands
  for ___ miles."
- Grid letters vs. numbers get swapped (3C for C3). Establish the
  column-letter, row-number order once and keep it all unit.
- "About 800 miles" feels wrong to learners who want exact answers.
  Normalize estimates: geographers estimate from maps and measure exactly
  only on the ground. (Answer key: `answer-key.md`, Lesson 02.)
