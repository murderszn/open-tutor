# Lesson 01 — Reading Maps Like a Geographer

**Time:** two sessions, about 35 minutes each · **Materials:** a printed
U.S. map with a title, key, compass rose, scale bar, and labels (any
classroom U.S. map works); a second simple map the adult draws of a
familiar place (bedroom, classroom, or block) with 4–6 labeled features;
pencil and paper

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn the parts of a map and
how geographers use them to make claims they can prove. By the end, you will
read a U.S. map, describe directions between places, and back up a claim
with map evidence.

**Readiness check:** Adult points to any map: "Find the title. What does it
tell us this map is about?" Then: "Point north on the compass rose." Accept
any reasonable answers; re-teach map parts if the learner cannot name the
title or the compass rose.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **map** is a drawing of a place seen from above, made much smaller than
the real place. Every useful map has the same helper parts. Learn their
names, because geographers use them the way a carpenter uses tools.

1. **Title** — tells you what the map shows ("Map of the United States").
2. **Key (legend)** — the box that explains what each symbol means. A star
   might mean "capital city"; a blue line might mean "river." Nothing on a
   map is a mystery once you read the key.
3. **Compass rose** — the star-like drawing that shows directions. It marks
   the four **cardinal directions**: **north, south, east, west**. Between
   them sit the four **intercardinal directions**: **northeast** (between
   north and east), **southeast**, **southwest**, and **northwest**. A memory
   trick: going clockwise from north, the order is N–NE–E–SE–S–SW–W–NW.
4. **Scale bar** — the ruler-like drawing that turns map measurements into
   real distances (full lesson in Lesson 02).
5. **Labels** — the names printed on the map. A label is evidence: if the
   label says "Texas," you can prove Texas is on this map.
6. **Grid** — letter-and-number squares across the map for naming exact
   spots, like B4 (full lesson in Lesson 02).

Now the geographer's habit that matters most in this unit: **a claim needs
map evidence.** A claim is a sentence like "Florida is in the South" or
"Denver is northwest of Dallas." Map evidence is the specific part of the
map that proves it — the label, the symbol, the compass rose reading, the
key entry. "I just know" is not map evidence. Pointing at the proof is.

**Worked example 1 — reading a key:**

> Adult opens the U.S. map and reads the key aloud: "The key says a red
> star means the country's capital, a black dot means a city, and a blue
> line means a river. Now I find the red star — the key proves this dot is
> Washington, D.C., the capital. I find a blue line running through
> several states — the key proves it is a river, and the label on it says
> Mississippi River. Steps: (1) read the key, (2) find the matching symbol
> on the map, (3) read its label. The key plus the label is my evidence."

**Worked example 2 — direction with the compass rose, and a claim with evidence:**

> Adult places a finger on Texas, then on Montana. "First I check the
> compass rose: north points up on this map. Montana sits above Texas, so
> Montana is **north** of Texas. Now my claim: 'Montana is northwest of
> Texas.' My evidence: (1) the compass rose shows north is up and west is
> left; (2) Montana's label sits up-and-left from Texas's label. Two map
> parts, one claim. If someone disagrees, I point at the compass rose and
> the two labels — that is how geographers settle it."

Key idea to say plainly: **A map's parts — title, key, compass rose, scale
bar, labels, grid — are tools. A geographer's claim is only as strong as
the map evidence behind it: point to the part of the map that proves what
you said.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Read the title of our U.S. map. In your own words, what is this map
   about?" *(e.g., "the states of the United States")*
2. "Find the key. What does the star symbol mean? Point to one on the map."
   *(capital — learner points; adult confirms against the key)*
3. "Put your finger on Florida. Now find California. Is California east or
   west of Florida?" *(west — evidence: compass rose + two labels)*
4. "Is Maine north or south of Texas? How do you know?" *(north — compass
   rose shows north is up; Maine's label is above Texas's)*
5. Make a claim: "The Mississippi River touches more than three states."
   "What map evidence proves or disproves it?" *(trace the blue river line
   with a finger; count the state labels it crosses — the line and labels
   are the evidence)*
6. Error-analysis: "A friend says, 'This map is wrong — my street is not
   on it!' What would you tell them?" *(a U.S. map is drawn at a scale that
   shows states, not streets; small things have no room — preview of
   Lesson 02's scale idea)*

## Independent practice

- The learner picks any three states on the U.S. map and writes one
  direction sentence for each pair (e.g., "Ohio is east of Indiana"),
  naming the compass-rose evidence under each sentence.
- The learner studies the adult's hand-drawn familiar-place map and lists
  its six map parts, writing what each one tells a reader.
- The learner makes two claims about the U.S. map ("___ is north of ___")
  and writes the map evidence for each (compass rose + labels).

## Application

The learner **draws a mini-map of the classroom (or bedroom)** with all
six parts: title, key (at least 3 symbols), compass rose, scale bar (any
simple bar — it will be used in Lesson 02), labels, and a letter–number
grid. Then the learner writes one claim about the room ("The door is east
of the window") with the map evidence named.

## Exit check

1. Name all six map parts and say what each one does (title, key, compass
   rose, scale bar, labels, grid).
2. On the U.S. map: is Washington state north or south of Oregon? What is
   your map evidence? *(north; compass rose + the two state labels)*
3. Why is "I just know it" not good enough for a geographer? *(claims
   need map evidence anyone can check)*

## Supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads all labels and key entries aloud;
  the learner points and speaks answers.
- **Alternate response mode:** direction sentences may be spoken or drawn
  with arrows instead of written.
- **Language support:** word cards for the six map parts and eight
  directions; the learner matches cards to map parts before answering.

## Extension

The learner finds a map in a book or online (adult-approved) that is
missing one of the six parts and explains, in two sentences, what a reader
cannot do without it (e.g., "Without a key, I cannot tell which dots are
capitals").

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- Learners often treat "up" on the page as north without checking the
  compass rose. Always require the rose check; some maps rotate it.
- "Near" is not a direction. Insist on the eight direction words.
- Learners may think the key's symbols are the same on every map. They are
  not — every map's key must be read fresh. (Answer key: `answer-key.md`,
  Lesson 01.)
