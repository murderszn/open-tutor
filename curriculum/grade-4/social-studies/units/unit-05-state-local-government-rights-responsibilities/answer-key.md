# Answer Key — Unit 05

**Audience:** the guiding adult only. Keep this key away from learner
pages. Accept any answer that matches the reasoning below in the
learner's own words; sample wordings are models, not scripts. All
arithmetic was re-solved for this key.

## Lesson 01 — Three levels of government

- **G1.** National: president, senator. State: governor, state
  legislator. Local: mayor, city council member.
- **G2.** Wrong because laws are written by the legislative branch
  (Congress), not the president; the president carries laws out.
  Truthful rewrite (sample): "Congress writes the country's laws,
  and the president carries them out."
- **G3.** Accept one state job and one local job with a level reason,
  for example: "State: the state highway system, because it runs
  across the whole state"; "Local: the neighborhood park, because it
  serves one town." A job without a size reason is partial.
- **I1.** National — whole country — president. State — one state —
  governor. Local — a city, town, or county — mayor.
- **I2.** Sample: "A mayor runs one town and a president runs the
  whole country — the jobs cover places of very different sizes, and
  one person cannot know every town's roads, schools, and parks while
  also leading the country. That is why the work is shared across
  levels." The size argument must appear.
- **I3.** State park — state job (serves the whole state, run by the
  state). Playground on your street — local job (serves one
  neighborhood, run by the city or town). Both with the size reason.
- **Applied task.** Three rows (national, state, local) × three
  columns (legislative, executive, judicial), officials in words in
  every box, one real job per row from the learner's town or state.
- **Exit check.** Levels biggest to smallest: national, state, local.
  Branches: legislative makes laws; executive carries them out;
  judicial explains what laws mean.

## Lesson 02 — Rights and responsibilities

- **G1.** The 4th Amendment — protection against unreasonable
  searches of a home or belongings.
- **G2.** Wrong because the Bill of Rights promised basic freedoms in
  1791 but did not give them to everyone at once; enslaved people
  had no rights under the law, and women could not vote until 1920.
  Correction (sample): "The Bill of Rights wrote down freedoms in
  1791, but many people were still excluded. Later amendments —
  1865, 1920, 1971 — expanded who enjoyed those rights." Both
  sentences (exclusion + later change) required.
- **G3.** Accept one reason each, for example: "The rule might exist
  because meetings need to stay orderly and on time"; "The child
  should still be heard because the crosswalk affects children
  directly and they have the right to ask the government to fix
  things (petition)." Both parts required.
- **I1.** The five 1st Amendment freedoms: religion, speech, press,
  assembly, petition.
- **I2.** Sample: "Federalism means power is shared. The national
  government handles things for the whole country, like the military
  and relations with other countries. States handle things closer to
  home, like most schools and roads — and some jobs are shared." Both
  halves required.
- **I3.** Order: Bill of Rights 1791, 13th Amendment 1865, 19th
  Amendment 1920, 26th Amendment 1971. Gaps: 1865 − 1791 = 74 years;
  1920 − 1865 = 55 years; 1971 − 1920 = 51 years. All three
  subtractions must be shown.
- **Applied task.** One Bill of Rights right and one later-amendment
  right, each in own words with its year and one paired
  responsibility, plus a completed "not enjoyed by everyone at
  first" sentence naming an excluded group.
- **Exit check.** Three real Bill of Rights rights (for example:
  speech, religion, fair trial); one voting amendment with its year
  (13th 1865, 19th 1920, or 26th 1971); one genuinely paired
  responsibility.

## Lesson 03 — How a bill becomes a law

- **G1.** Order: 1 idea; 2 bill introduced; 3 committee studies the
  bill; 4 House votes; 5 Senate votes; 6 president signs. (The
  conference-committee step sits between the two chamber votes when
  versions differ; accept it placed there.)
- **G2.** Wrong because a bill also needs the other chamber to pass
  it in the same form and the president to sign it (or Congress to
  override a veto). Truthful rewrite (sample): "A bill becomes a law
  only after both chambers pass it and the president signs it."
- **G3.** The override fails: the House needs two-thirds of 435,
  which is 290, and 250 is less than 290. (The Senate is not reached
  — both chambers must clear two-thirds.)
- **I1.** Sample: "Checks and balances means each branch can limit
  the others. The president can veto a bill Congress passed, and
  Congress can override that veto with two-thirds in both chambers —
  so no branch rules alone." Veto and override both required.
- **I2.** Sample path: idea → council discussion → council vote →
  mayor signs (or vetoes). The mayor can refuse to sign — a local
  veto — and the council would need its own override rule to pass it
  anyway. All four steps plus the veto alternative required.
- **I3.** The judicial branch acted; it limited the legislative
  branch (the law's makers). Sample: "That check matters because it
  keeps laws inside the Constitution — a law the people did not
  agree to through the Constitution cannot stand." Branch, limited
  branch, and reason all required.
- **Applied task.** Seven boxes in order (idea, introduced,
  committee, floor vote, other chamber, president, law) with the
  "signs → law" and "vetoes → back to Congress" arrows and the
  override numbers House 290, Senate 67.
- **Exit check.** Seven steps in order (idea, introduced, committee,
  floor debate/vote, other chamber, president, law/override) and one
  real check naming both branches and the limit.

## Lesson 04 — Taxes pay for services

- **G1.** 10% of $10 is $1. Total: $10 + $1 = $11. (Check: 10 × 0.10
  = 1.)
- **G2.** Wrong because tax money comes back as services everyone
  uses. Two services required (any two from the table: schools,
  roads, firefighters, police, parks, libraries, clean water).
- **G3.** (a) Food safety — FDA (Food and Drug Administration), part
  of the Department of Health and Human Services. (b) Space — NASA,
  an independent agency. (c) Air and water — EPA, an independent
  agency.
- **I1.** Any four-service split in whole cents totaling 100, with
  the addition shown (for example: 30 + 30 + 25 + 15 = 100). Each
  service labeled with one thing it pays for.
- **I2.** Sample: "One family cannot buy a road or a fire truck
  alone, and a park only works if everyone can use it. Taxes let
  everyone chip in a little so the whole community can share the big
  things." The sharing argument must appear.
- **I3.** Two departments and two agencies, each with a one-sentence
  job (any four correct pairs from the Lesson 04 table).
- **Applied task.** Coin labeled "1 tax dollar" with the chain
  paid → collected → spent → used; a 100-cent split at "spent"
  (lesson split or the learner's I1 split, with addition); one town
  service named at "used" with who uses it.
- **Exit check.** Two real taxes (sales, property, income); the
  chain paid → spent → used in order; one department and one agency
  with correct jobs (not swapped).

## Lesson 05 — Participation and changing laws

- **G1.** Vote (or consensus) fits best: many people share the
  decision, there is time to talk, and everyone eats the lunch. A
  leader deciding alone is a poor fit here. Procedure + reason
  required.
- **G2.** Wrong because voting is only one way to participate.
  Two other ways required (any two: speaking at a meeting,
  petitioning, volunteering, serving on a jury as an adult,
  following laws and respecting rights).
- **G3.** Mara values having a say (everyone votes, everyone has a
  say). Mr. Ortiz values deciding quickly and safely (adults decide
  safety rules fast). One sentence each, both labeled "invented
  teaching passage."
- **I1.** Sample: "Both are votes where people choose. But a class
  vote is informal — the teacher runs it and anyone in the class
  votes. An official election is run by the government, only adult
  citizens who are registered can vote, and the result chooses a real
  leader like a mayor." Same + different both required. (Filed with
  the kept `civic-voting-research.md` assignment.)
- **I2.** Two sentences from different viewpoints, labeled "invented
  teaching sentences," for example: a student — "Running is faster
  and fun between classes"; a teacher — "Running in a crowded
  hallway can hurt someone." Both viewpoints required.
- **I3.** Four ways with one unit example each: amend (13th/19th/26th
  Amendments); pass a new law (bill-to-law path, Lesson 03); repeal
  (vote a law out through the same path); petition and speak (1st
  Amendment right, Lesson 02).
- **Applied task.** One real classroom problem; the rule in one
  sentence; its reason in one sentence; the procedure used
  (consensus or vote) and why it fit; the helps/limits sentence.
  The adult checks fairness to everyone affected.
- **Exit check.** Three age-appropriate participation ways (for
  example: speak at a meeting, petition, volunteer); vote vs.
  consensus compared in one sentence each (vote = most votes win;
  consensus = everyone agrees); one lawful way to change a law.
