# Lesson 05 — Participation and Changing Laws

**Goal:** I can explain how democracy relies on participation,
compare decision procedures, and propose a policy for a real problem
with steps.
**Time:** two 35-minute sessions. **Materials:** paper, colored
pencils or markers, the kept `civic-voting-research.md` assignment
(see the teacher guide).

**Readiness check (adult-led, 3 minutes):** In Lesson 02 you learned
that the 26th Amendment lowered the voting age from 21 to 18. Why
would young people argue they should be allowed to vote at 18?
(They could work, pay taxes, and serve in the military at 18 — so
they said they should also have a say. Accept any version of that
reasoning.)

## Teaching

### How people take part

A democracy only works if people take part in it. Voting is the
most famous way, but it is not the only one:

| Way to participate | Who can do it | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Vote in an election | Adult citizens (18 and older, registered) | Chooses leaders and sometimes laws |
| Speak at a public meeting | Anyone, including children, when the meeting allows it | Tells officials what a community needs |
| Petition | Anyone | Asks the government in writing to change or do something |
| Volunteer and help | Anyone | Improves the community directly — a park cleanup, a food drive |
| Serve on a jury | Adult citizens when called | Helps decide a court case fairly |
| Follow laws and respect rights | Everyone | Keeps the shared rules working |

The kept assignment for this week,
[`civic-voting-research.md`](../../../assignments/civic-voting-research.md),
asks the learner to read an educator-selected explanation of the
26th Amendment and explain what it protects, then compare an
informal group vote with an official election and describe two
age-appropriate ways to participate. The adult selects the reading;
the learner does the comparing.

### Three ways groups decide

Groups do not all decide the same way. Three procedures cover most
decisions a learner will meet:

| Procedure | How it works | When it fits | Its risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consensus** | Everyone talks until the whole group can agree | Small groups, important shared decisions | Takes a long time; one person can block it |
| **Vote** | Everyone votes; the most votes win | Fair when many people must decide quickly | The losers may feel unheard |
| **Leader decides** | One person decides after hearing (or not hearing) views | Emergencies, or tiny decisions | The decision may ignore good ideas |

A classroom rule works best by consensus or vote — everyone who
lives under the rule should have a say in it. An emergency (a fire
drill, a sudden storm) works best when a leader decides fast. The
skill is matching the procedure to the decision, not always using
the same one.

### How people change laws

Laws are not permanent. People change them in four ways, and this
unit has taught all four:

1. **Amend** — change the Constitution itself, as the 13th, 19th,
   and 26th Amendments did (Lesson 02).
2. **Pass a new law** — through the bill-to-law path (Lesson 03).
3. **Repeal** — vote a law out of existence through the same path.
4. **Petition and speak** — ask, in writing or at a meeting, for a
   change — the 1st Amendment right (Lesson 02).

Deliberation is the habit underneath all four: careful discussion
before a decision, hearing different views. Lesson 02's "is this
situation fair?" cards and this lesson's rule-writing are
deliberation practice — the same skill, at classroom scale, that
legislators use at national scale.

### Two invented deliberation passages

The passages below are **invented teaching passages written for
this unit** — they are not real documents, not quotations, and not
records of any real meeting. The adult reads them aloud and reminds
the learner of that before and after.

**Passage A (invented):** "Mara, a fourth-grader, says: 'Our class
should vote on the playground rules, because we are the ones who
use the playground every day. If we vote, everyone has a say, even
if they lose the vote.'"

**Passage B (invented):** "Mr. Ortiz, a teacher, says: 'Voting takes
time, and some rules are about safety. The adults should decide the
safety rules quickly, and the class can vote on the fun rules, like
which games we play on Fridays.'"

Neither passage is "the answer." Deliberation means holding both
views at once: the class deserves a say *and* safety decisions
sometimes need an adult to decide fast. The fair rule in the applied
task below tries to honor both.

### Worked example 1 — Match the procedure

*Question: "A class must choose a name for its reading corner. A
fire alarm rings during the discussion. Which procedure fits each
moment, and why?"*

Reasoning: Naming a reading corner is a small shared decision with
time to talk — consensus or a vote fits, because everyone will live
with the name. A fire alarm is an emergency — the teacher decides,
fast, and everyone follows. Answer: vote or consensus for the name;
leader decides for the alarm. The procedure matches the decision's
urgency, not the teacher's preference.

### Worked example 2 — Change a law, step by step

*Question: "A town has a law: 'No dogs in the park.' Many families
want dogs allowed on leashes. Name two lawful ways to change it."*

Reasoning: The town council can pass a new law through the local
bill path (idea → council discussion → vote → mayor signs), or
citizens can petition the council asking for the change, and the
council can then vote. Answer: petition the council, and have the
council pass a new law replacing the old one. (Breaking the law and
hoping no one notices is not a lawful way — the adult says so
plainly if the learner suggests it.)

## Guided practice (adult reads aloud if needed)

**G1.** A school must decide the lunch menu for a month. Which
procedure — consensus, vote, or leader decides — fits best, and why?
*Hint: many people, one shared decision, time to talk.*

**G2.** Error-analysis: "Voting is the only way to participate in a
democracy." What is wrong with that sentence? Name two other ways
from the table.
*Hint: what can a 10-year-old do that is not voting?*

**G3.** Read Passages A and B. What does each person value most —
having a say, or deciding quickly and safely? Write one sentence for
each passage, labeled "invented teaching passage."
*Hint: Mara's key words are "everyone has a say"; Mr. Ortiz's are
"decide quickly."*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Compare an informal group vote (your class voting on a game)
with an official election (adults voting for a mayor) in 3–4
sentences: what is the same, and what is different? (This is the
kept `civic-voting-research.md` comparison; the adult files the
learner's answer with that assignment.)

**I2.** A new rule is proposed: "No running in the hallway." Write
two sentences: one argument for the rule and one argument against
it, each from a different person's point of view. Label both
"invented teaching sentences."

**I3.** Name the four ways laws change (amend, pass a new law,
repeal, petition and speak) and give one example of each from this
unit.

## Applied task

Write a fair classroom rule with a reason (Week 21, Session 3
deliberation; finished in the project): pick one real classroom
problem, write the rule in one sentence, write its reason in one
sentence, and name the decision procedure your class used
(consensus or vote) and why it fit. The rule must be fair to
everyone it affects — the adult checks: does it name who it helps
and who it limits, and does the reason hold for both?

## Exit check

Without looking at the tables, name three ways to participate that
a 10-year-old can do, compare vote and consensus in one sentence
each, and name one lawful way to change a law. The adult checks:
participation ways are real and age-appropriate, the comparison
names the real difference, and the change method is lawful.
