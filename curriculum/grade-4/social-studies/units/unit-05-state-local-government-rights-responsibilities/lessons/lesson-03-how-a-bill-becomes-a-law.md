# Lesson 03 — How a Bill Becomes a Law

**Goal:** I can trace how a bill becomes a law and explain checks
and balances with two examples.
**Time:** one 35-minute session, plus the Week 22 quiz session.
**Materials:** paper, colored pencils or markers.

**Readiness check (adult-led, 3 minutes):** In Lesson 01, which
branch makes laws? (Legislative — Congress, the state legislature,
the city council.) Today we follow one idea all the way from "someone
has an idea" to "it is a law" — and see what can stop it along the
way.

## Teaching

### From idea to law: seven steps

A **bill** is a proposed law — an idea written down in the form of a
law, waiting for votes. Here is the national path, from the
repository's `resources/government_basics.md` and Congress's own
legislative-process guide:

1. **Idea.** A bill starts as an idea — from a citizen, a group, or
   a member of Congress.
2. **Introduced.** A member of the House or the Senate introduces
   the bill, and it gets a number.
3. **Committee work.** A small group of members — a committee —
   studies the bill, holds hearings, and changes it. Most bills stop
   here: the committee can simply never vote on one.
4. **Floor debate and vote.** The full House or Senate debates the
   bill and votes. If it passes, it goes to the other chamber.
5. **The other chamber.** The Senate (or House) repeats steps 3–4.
   If the two versions differ, a **conference committee** — members
   from both chambers — works out one version, and both chambers vote
   again.
6. **The president.** The president signs the bill, and it becomes a
   law — or **vetoes** it, refusing to sign, and sends it back with
   reasons.
7. **Override or end.** Congress can override a veto with a
   two-thirds vote in *both* chambers — 290 of the 435 House members
   (two-thirds of 435 is exactly 290) and 67 of the 100 senators
   (two-thirds of 100 is 66⅔, so 67 votes are needed). If Congress
   does not override, the bill dies.

The same shape works at the state level (a bill → committee →
legislature → governor) and at the local level (an idea → council
discussion → council vote → mayor), with fewer steps and different
names. The pattern is what matters: *propose, study, debate, vote,
sign.*

### Checks and balances: no branch rules alone

The Constitution does not trust any one branch with all the power.
Each branch can limit the others — that system is called **checks
and balances**:

| Check | Who limits whom |
|---|---|
| The president can veto a bill | Executive limits legislative |
| Congress can override a veto with two-thirds in both chambers | Legislative limits executive |
| Courts can declare a law unconstitutional | Judicial limits legislative (and executive) |
| The president nominates judges; the Senate confirms them | Executive and legislative share the judicial branch |
| Congress controls the budget and can impeach and remove officials | Legislative limits executive |

Two checks to remember by heart: *the veto* and *the override*.
They are the clearest example of the branches pushing back on each
other — and they show why a bill needs more than one person's
agreement to become a law.

### Worked example 1 — Trace a bill

*Question: "A senator introduces a bill to build a new national
park. The Senate committee changes it, the Senate passes it, the
House passes a different version, and a conference committee agrees
on one version that both chambers pass. The president signs it. At
which step did the bill become a law, and what happened before that?"*

Reasoning: A bill becomes a law only at the president's signature
(step 6). Before that, it went through introduction, committee
work, two floor votes, and a conference committee to settle the
differences. Answer: it became a law when the president signed it;
before that it passed both chambers in the same form.

### Worked example 2 — Count the override

*Question: "The president vetoes a bill. The House votes 300 to 135
to override, and the Senate votes 70 to 30. Does the bill become a
law? Show the numbers."*

Reasoning: The House needs two-thirds of 435, which is 290. 300 is
more than 290, so the House overrides. The Senate needs two-thirds
of 100, which is 66⅔ — so at least 67 votes. 70 is more than 67, so
the Senate overrides too. Answer: yes — both chambers cleared
two-thirds, so the veto is overridden and the bill becomes a law.
(Check the arithmetic: 435 ÷ 3 = 145, and 145 × 2 = 290. 100 ÷ 3 =
33⅓, and 33⅓ × 2 = 66⅔, so 67 is the smallest whole number above
it.)

## Guided practice (adult reads aloud if needed)

**G1.** Order these steps: "president signs," "committee studies
the bill," "idea," "House votes," "bill introduced," "Senate votes."
Number them 1–6.
*Hint: an idea has to be written down before anyone can study it.*

**G2.** Error-analysis: "Once the House votes yes, a bill is a law."
What is wrong with that sentence? Correct it in one sentence.
*Hint: how many chambers and whose signature?*

**G3.** The president vetoes a bill, and the House votes 250 to 185
to override. Does the override succeed? Show the number it needed.
*Hint: two-thirds of 435.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** In 3–4 sentences, explain checks and balances to a friend
using the veto and the override as your two examples.

**I2.** A city council wants a new rule: "No parking on Main Street
on Saturdays." Write the local path in four steps: idea, council
discussion, council vote, mayor. What can the mayor do instead of
signing?

**I3.** A court declares a law unconstitutional. Which branch acted,
which branch's work was limited, and why does that check matter?
Answer in 2–3 sentences.

## Applied task

Draw the bill-to-law path (you will finish it in the project): seven
boxes in order, from "Idea" to "Law," with one short label per box.
Under the "President" box, add two arrows: "signs → law" and
"vetoes → back to Congress." Under the veto arrow, write the two
override numbers: House 290, Senate 67.

## Exit check

Without looking at the steps, name the seven steps in order and
give one example of a check one branch has on another. The adult
checks: order correct, and the check names both branches and the
limit.
