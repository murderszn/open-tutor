# Lesson 04 — Taxes Pay for Services

**Goal:** I can explain how taxes pay for public services and name
what executive departments and agencies do day to day.
**Time:** one 35-minute session. **Materials:** paper, play money or
paper coins (practice money only), colored pencils or markers.

**Readiness check (adult-led, 3 minutes):** Look back at the town
illustration from Lesson 01. The school, the fire truck, the road
crew, the park — who pays for those? (Learners usually say "the
government" or "taxes." Accept either, then say: today we trace
one tax dollar from a pocket to a service.)

## Teaching

### What is a tax?

A **tax** is money people and businesses pay to a government so the
government can pay for things everyone uses. A **public service** is
something the government provides for everyone: roads, schools,
parks, firefighters, police, libraries, clean water, and more. The
deal is simple: everyone chips in a little, and everyone can use the
shared things. Three taxes a grade-4 learner will actually meet:

- **Sales tax** — a small extra amount added when you buy something
  in a store. (Practice example: a $20 toy with a 10% sales tax
  costs $20 + $2 = $22, because 10% of $20 is $2.)
- **Property tax** — a yearly amount paid on a house or land; it is
  the main way local schools and local services are paid for.
- **Income tax** — a part of the money adults earn from work, paid to
  the national government (and, in most states, to the state too).
  Adults pay it; children do not.

All dollar amounts in this lesson are **practice numbers** invented
for teaching. Real tax rates and real budgets change by place and
year — the adult checks any real local figure before quoting it.

### Trace one tax dollar

Follow 100 practice cents of local tax money — a whole pretend
dollar — and see where it goes. (Practice split, invented for this
lesson: it shows the *idea*, not any real town's budget.)

| Service | Cents of the practice dollar | What it pays for |
|---|---|---|
| Schools | 40¢ | Teachers, books, the school bus, the library |
| Roads and water | 25¢ | Paving, plowing, pipes, clean drinking water |
| Firefighters and police | 20¢ | The fire station, the fire truck, emergency help |
| Parks and libraries | 15¢ | The playground, the park, library books |
| **Total** | **100¢** | 40 + 25 + 20 + 15 = 100 — the whole dollar, accounted for |

The chain to remember: *people pay taxes → the government collects
them → the government spends them on services → everyone uses the
services.* When a road is fixed or a library opens, that is the end
of the chain you can see. When people disagree about the split —
more for roads or more for parks? — that disagreement is decided by
the elected officials from Lesson 01, through the budget votes from
Lesson 03.

### Who does what day to day? Departments and agencies

The president cannot run every service alone. The executive branch
is organized into **departments** — big offices led by the
president's cabinet — and **agencies** — offices with one specific
job. From the repository's `resources/government_basics.md`:

| Department or agency | What it does day to day |
|---|---|
| Department of State | Talks with other countries (foreign policy) |
| Department of Defense | Runs the military |
| Department of Justice (and the FBI) | Enforces federal law |
| Department of Agriculture | Farming, food safety, rural programs |
| Department of Health and Human Services (CDC, FDA) | Public health, food and drug safety |
| Department of Homeland Security (FEMA, TSA) | Disasters (FEMA), airport and border safety |
| Department of Transportation | Roads, air travel, rail |
| Department of Education | Schools and federal education programs |
| NASA (independent agency) | Space exploration and science |
| EPA (independent agency) | Protects air, water, and land |
| GAO — Government Accountability Office | Checks government spending for Congress |
| CBO — Congressional Budget Office | Works out what bills and budgets will cost |

Some agencies are **independent** — kept separate from the
president's departments on purpose, so their work is not decided by
politics. The EPA and NASA are the two in this table. The GAO and
CBO are different again: they work for Congress, not the president —
Congress's own checkers and calculators.

### Worked example 1 — Trace the dollar

*Question: "A town collects 100 practice cents in tax. It spends
40¢ on schools, 25¢ on roads and water, 20¢ on firefighters and
police, and 15¢ on parks and libraries. How much is left unspent?
Show the addition."*

Reasoning: Add the spending first: 40 + 25 = 65; 65 + 20 = 85;
85 + 15 = 100. The town collected 100¢ and spent 100¢. Answer:
nothing is left — 100 − 100 = 0¢. Every cent is accounted for.

### Worked example 2 — Which agency?

*Question: "A hurricane damages homes in a coastal town, and
families need emergency help. Which agency helps, and which
department is it part of?"*

Reasoning: Emergency disaster help is FEMA's job — the Federal
Emergency Management Agency. FEMA sits inside the Department of
Homeland Security. Answer: FEMA, part of the Department of Homeland
Security. (A learner who says "the Department of Homeland Security"
has the department right; the agency name is the extra detail.)

## Guided practice (adult reads aloud if needed)

**G1.** A family buys a $10 book. The store charges 10% sales tax.
How much tax is that, and what is the total? Show the multiplication.
*Hint: 10% of $10.*

**G2.** Error-analysis: "Taxes disappear into the government and
nothing comes back." What is wrong with that sentence? Name two
services from the table that come back to everyone.
*Hint: follow the chain — where does the dollar end up?*

**G3.** Which department or agency fits each job: (a) checking that
food in stores is safe, (b) exploring space, (c) protecting the air
and water? Name the department or agency for each.
*Hint: FDA, NASA, EPA — but which is a department and which are
independent agencies?*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Draw your own tax-dollar split for a pretend town: divide
100 practice cents among four services of your choice, in whole
cents, and show the addition that proves it totals 100. Label each
service with one thing it pays for.

**I2.** In 3–4 sentences, explain why the government charges taxes
instead of asking each family to buy its own road, fire truck, and
park.

**I3.** Name two departments and two agencies from the table, and
write one sentence for each saying what it does day to day.

## Applied task

Begin your tax-dollar trail (you will finish it in the project):
draw one coin labeled "1 tax dollar" and an arrow chain with four
stops — "paid," "collected," "spent," "used." At the "spent" stop,
write your I1 split (or the lesson's 40/25/20/15 split). At the
"used" stop, name one service from the town illustration and who
uses it.

## Exit check

Without looking at the table, name two kinds of tax, trace one tax
dollar in three steps (paid → spent → used), and name one department
and one agency with its job. The adult checks: the chain is in
order and the department and agency are not swapped.
