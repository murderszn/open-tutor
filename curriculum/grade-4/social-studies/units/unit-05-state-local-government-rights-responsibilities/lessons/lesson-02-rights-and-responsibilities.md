# Lesson 02 — Rights and Responsibilities

**Goal:** I can explain rights the Bill of Rights and later
amendments protect, name responsibilities that pair with them, and
explain federalism, states, territories, and Washington, D.C.
**Time:** two 35-minute sessions. **Materials:** paper, colored
pencils or markers.

**Readiness check (adult-led, 3 minutes):** In Unit 04, who could
vote in colonial town meetings and assemblies — and who could not?
(Women, enslaved people, and Indigenous nations could not vote in
those bodies.) Today we ask: how did rights grow after that, and
what do we owe each other in return?

## Teaching

### The Bill of Rights: ten promises written down

The U.S. Constitution is the written plan for the national
government — the highest law in the country. Soon after it was
written, people worried it did not protect individual freedoms
clearly enough. So the first ten **amendments** — changes added to
the Constitution — were ratified on December 15, 1791. Together
they are the **Bill of Rights**. A **right** is a freedom the
government must protect. The most important ones for this lesson:

| Amendment | What it protects (plain words) |
|---|---|
| 1st | Freedom of religion, speech, the press, peaceful assembly, and petition — asking the government to fix something |
| 2nd | The right to keep and bear arms |
| 3rd | Soldiers may not be housed in private homes without permission |
| 4th | Protection against unreasonable searches of your home or belongings |
| 5th | Fair treatment before the law — no one may be tried twice for the same crime, or forced to testify against themselves |
| 6th | A speedy, fair trial by jury, with a lawyer |
| 7th | A jury trial in civil (non-criminal) cases |
| 8th | No excessive bail, and no cruel or unusual punishment |
| 9th | The rights listed here are not the only rights people have |
| 10th | Powers not given to the national government belong to the states or to the people |

Two things to notice. First, the 1st Amendment holds five freedoms
in one sentence — religion, speech, press, assembly, petition —
because they work together: you can believe, say, publish, gather,
and ask for change. Second, the 10th Amendment is the seed of
**federalism**: power is *shared* between the national government
and the states. The national government handles things for the whole
country (like the military and relations with other countries);
states handle things closer to home (like most schools and roads);
and some jobs, like taxes, are shared.

### Rights grew — slowly, and not all at once

The Bill of Rights did not give rights to everyone at once. For
most of American history, enslaved people had no rights at all under
the law, women could not vote, and many others were excluded. The
Constitution was changed again and again to fix that. Four changes
matter for this unit:

| Year | Change | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 1791 | Bill of Rights (1st–10th Amendments) | Wrote down basic freedoms |
| 1865 | 13th Amendment | Ended slavery in the United States |
| 1870 | 15th Amendment | The right to vote cannot be denied because of race |
| 1920 | 19th Amendment | The right to vote cannot be denied because of sex — women won the vote |
| 1971 | 26th Amendment | The voting age was lowered from 21 to 18 |

The gaps tell the story: 1865 − 1791 = 74 years from the Bill of
Rights to the end of slavery; 1920 − 1865 = 55 more years until women
could vote everywhere in the country; 1971 − 1920 = 51 more years
until 18-year-olds could vote. Rights are promises, and promises
have to be kept — sometimes it takes generations, and sometimes it
takes people speaking up, petitioning, and voting to make the
promise real. That is why the 1st Amendment's petition right matters:
it is the tool for asking government to change.

### Responsibilities: the other half

A right without a responsibility is a one-sided deal. For every
right in this lesson, there is a responsibility that pairs with it:

| Right | Paired responsibility |
|---|---|
| Freedom of speech | Listen to others' speech too; speak truthfully and without threatening others |
| Freedom of religion | Respect other people's beliefs |
| Freedom of assembly | Gather peacefully and leave shared places in good order |
| The right to vote (adults) | Learn about the choices, and vote |
| A fair trial | Serve on a jury when called (adults); tell the truth |
| Protection of your home and belongings | Respect other people's homes and belongings |

For a grade-4 learner, the daily responsibilities are smaller but
real: follow the rules your community agreed to, respect others'
rights, take care of shared places (the park, the library, the
school), and speak up — kindly and with reasons — when a rule seems
unfair. Lesson 05 turns that last responsibility into practice.

### States, territories, and Washington, D.C.

The United States has **50 states**. States are not all the same
size: in the 2020 census, the largest state by population was
California, with 39,538,223 people, and the smallest was Wyoming,
with 576,851 people. Each state has its own constitution, governor,
and legislature — that is federalism in action.

Five inhabited places belong to the United States but are not
states. They are called **territories**: Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S.
Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Northern Mariana Islands.
People in the territories are U.S. citizens or U.S. nationals, but
territories do not vote in presidential elections and do not have
voting members of Congress.

**Washington, D.C.** is different again: it is the national capital,
a federal district — not a state and not a territory. The people who
live there pay federal taxes and have a non-voting delegate in the
House of Representatives, but no senators. The adult can add: this
is a live debate in American civics, and people disagree about what
should change — the unit teaches the facts, not a side.

### Worked example 1 — Match the right to the amendment

*Question: "A student writes a letter to the mayor saying the park
needs new swings. Which amendment protects that, and how do you
know?"*

Reasoning: Writing to the mayor is speech, and it is also a
petition — asking the government to fix something. Both are in the
1st Amendment. Answer: the 1st Amendment — freedom of speech and
the right to petition.

### Worked example 2 — Read the timeline

*Question: "A woman born in 1850 wanted to vote. Could she vote in
1900? In 1925? Explain with the timeline."*

Reasoning: In 1900, no — the 19th Amendment had not been ratified
yet (1920), so women could not vote everywhere in the country. In
1925, yes — after 1920, the vote could not be denied because of sex.
Answer: no in 1900, yes in 1925 — the 19th Amendment changed the
rule in 1920. (Some states let women vote earlier; the amendment
made it the rule everywhere.)

## Guided practice (adult reads aloud if needed)

**G1.** A police officer wants to search a family's home without a
good reason. Which amendment says the family is protected? What
does it protect?
*Hint: which amendment talks about searches?*

**G2.** Error-analysis: "The Bill of Rights gave everyone in America
all their rights in 1791, and nothing changed after that." What is
wrong with that sentence? Correct it in two sentences.
*Hint: look at the timeline — who was still excluded, and when did
that change?*

**G3.** Is this situation fair? A town rule says only adults may
speak at a town meeting. A 10-year-old wants to ask for a safer
crosswalk. Give one reason the rule might exist and one reason the
child should still be heard.
*Hint: who does the rule protect, and who does it leave out?*

## Independent practice

**I1.** List the five freedoms in the 1st Amendment from memory,
then check and fix them. Circle any you missed.

**I2.** In 3–4 sentences, explain federalism to a friend: what does
the national government handle, what do states handle, and why
share the power?

**I3.** Put these events in order and give the gap in years between
each neighboring pair: Bill of Rights (1791), 13th Amendment
(1865), 19th Amendment (1920), 26th Amendment (1971). Show all
three subtractions.

## Applied task

Begin your rights card (you will finish it in the project): pick one
right from the Bill of Rights and one from the later amendments.
For each, write the right in your own words, the year it was added,
and one responsibility that pairs with it. Add one sentence: "This
right was not enjoyed by everyone at first, because …" — and finish
the sentence honestly.

## Exit check

Without looking at the tables, name three rights from the Bill of
Rights, one amendment that expanded voting, and one responsibility
that pairs with a right you named. The adult checks: rights matched
to real amendments, the voting amendment dated correctly, and the
responsibility genuinely paired.
