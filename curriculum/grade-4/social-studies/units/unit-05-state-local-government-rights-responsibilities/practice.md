# Practice Sets — Unit 05

The adult may read items aloud; spoken, pointed-to, and drawn answers
count. Answers are in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md) — this page
contains no answers.

## Week 20 — Levels and branches

**A1.** Sort these officials into levels — national, state, or local:
president, governor, mayor, senator, city council member, school
board member. Write the level beside each.

**A2.** Which branch fits? "A group of elected representatives writes
a new law about school lunches." Name the branch and the job it does.

**A3.** A state highway needs repairs, and a neighborhood street
needs repairs. Which is a state job and which is a local job?
Explain each with the size of the place it serves.

**A4.** True or false, with a correction if false: "The president
writes all the laws in the United States."

**A5.** Using the Lesson 01 illustration's text alternative only,
list three services visible in the town picture and the level of
government that handles each, with a reason each. Name one thing
you cannot tell from the picture and what would settle it.

**A6.** A town wants a new library and a new interstate highway.
Which level handles each, and why? Answer in two sentences.

**A7.** Error-analysis: "The mayor runs the whole country, and the
president runs the town." Correct the sentence, naming who runs
each level.

**A8.** Draw the levels-and-branches chart from Lesson 01's applied
task from memory (three rows, three columns, officials in words),
then check it against the lesson table and fix errors in a
different color.

## Week 21 — Rights and responsibilities

**B1.** Match each right to its amendment: (a) freedom of speech,
(b) a fair trial by jury, (c) no unreasonable searches — to: (i) 4th
Amendment, (ii) 1st Amendment, (iii) 6th Amendment.

**B2.** Order these events and give the gap in years between each
neighboring pair: Bill of Rights (1791), 13th Amendment (1865), 19th
Amendment (1920). Show both subtractions.

**B3.** A student wants to start a school newspaper and write about
the new playground. Which freedom protects that, and what
responsibility pairs with it?

**B4.** Which U.S. state has the largest population, and which has
the smallest? Give the 2020 census numbers for both.

**B5.** Explain federalism to a friend in two sentences: what does
the national government handle, and what do states handle?

**B6.** True or false, with a correction if false: "People in U.S.
territories vote in presidential elections just like people in the
states."

**B7.** Error-analysis: "Rights are only for adults." Correct the
sentence and name two rights children have.

**B8.** Write one right and one paired responsibility for each: (a)
freedom of assembly, (b) freedom of religion. (Two pairs total.)

## Week 22 — Bills, taxes, agencies

**C1.** Order these bill-to-law steps: "president signs," "committee
studies the bill," "idea," "House votes," "bill introduced," "Senate
votes." Number them 1–6.

**C2.** The president vetoes a bill. How many votes does the House
need to override, and how many does the Senate need? Show how you
worked each number out.

**C3.** A town collects 100 practice cents in tax. It spends 40¢ on
schools, 25¢ on roads and water, 20¢ on firefighters and police, and
15¢ on parks and libraries. How much is left unspent? Show the
addition.

**C4.** Match each job to its department or agency: (a) exploring
space, (b) emergency help after a hurricane, (c) checking that food
in stores is safe — to: (i) FEMA, (ii) FDA, (iii) NASA.

**C5.** A family buys a $20 toy. The store charges 10% sales tax.
How much tax is that, and what is the total? Show the multiplication.

**C6.** Why does the government charge taxes instead of asking each
family to buy its own road, fire truck, and park? Answer in 2–3
sentences.

**C7.** Error-analysis: "A court can write a new law whenever it
wants." Correct the sentence, naming what courts actually do.

**C8.** Name one check the executive branch has on the legislative
branch, and one check the legislative branch has on the executive
branch. (Two checks total.)

## Week 23 — Participation and changing laws

**D1.** Name three ways a 10-year-old can participate in democracy
that do not involve voting, and say what each one does.

**D2.** A class must pick a book to read together. A fire alarm rings
during the discussion. Which decision procedure fits each moment,
and why? (Two procedures, two reasons.)

**D3.** A town law says "No dogs in the park." Many families want
dogs allowed on leashes. Name two lawful ways to change the law.

**D4.** Match each procedure to its best fit: (a) consensus, (b)
vote, (c) leader decides — to: (i) an emergency, (ii) a small group
choosing a shared name, (iii) a big group deciding quickly.

**D5.** Write one argument for and one argument against this rule:
"No running in the hallway." Write each from a different person's
point of view, and label both "invented teaching sentences."

**D6.** Explain in 3–4 sentences why the 26th Amendment matters:
what was the voting age before 1971, what did the amendment change,
and why did young people argue for it?

**D7.** A class votes 14 to 10 for a new game, and the 10 who lost
feel unheard. Is the vote unfair? Explain in 2–3 sentences what the
class could do so the losers still feel heard.

**D8.** Error-analysis: "Laws can never change, so there is no point
in speaking up." Correct the sentence, naming two lawful ways laws
change.
