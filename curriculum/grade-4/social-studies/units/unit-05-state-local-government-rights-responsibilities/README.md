# Unit 05 — State and Local Government: Rights and Responsibilities

**Grade:** 4 (ages 9–10) · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#25](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/25)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 20–23 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, about 35 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

Government is not one faraway thing. It is three levels — national,
state, and local — and each level has three branches that make laws,
carry them out, and explain what they mean. Learners sort real
community needs (a road with a pothole, a school library, a fire
station, a park) to the level that handles them; meet the officials
who do each job (president, governor, mayor, legislators, judges);
and learn the words that make government work: law, branch, veto,
tax, and citizen.

Then the unit turns to what government is *for*: the rights the Bill
of Rights and later amendments protect, and the responsibilities that
pair with them. Learners trace how a right grew — the Bill of Rights
in 1791, the end of slavery in 1865, the vote for women in 1920, the
vote at 18 in 1971 — and practice the democratic skills themselves:
deliberating a fair classroom rule, comparing decision procedures,
following a bill from idea to law, tracing one tax dollar to a
service, and proposing a real school policy with steps.

Two cautions run through the unit. First, rights were not given to
everyone at once: the unit names plainly, at an age-appropriate
level, that enslaved people, women, and many others were excluded
from voting and from power for long stretches of American history,
and that amendments and movements changed that — slowly, and not
all at once. Second, government in this unit is about *how it
works*, not about which party or candidate is better: the adult
keeps examples nonpartisan and present-tense-free (no current
office-holders are named as lesson content; the adult supplies local
names at use time).

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** the national, state, and local levels of
   government and the three branches at each level, and **name** what
   officials at each level do (D2.Civ.1.3-5, D2.Civ.5.3-5).
2. **Explain** rights protected by the Bill of Rights and by the 13th,
   19th, and 26th Amendments, **explain** federalism (power shared
   between national and state governments), and **describe** how
   states, territories, and Washington, D.C. differ (D2.Civ.3.3-5,
   D2.Civ.4.3-5).
3. **Trace** how a bill becomes a law and **explain** checks and
   balances with at least two examples (D2.Civ.1.3-5, D2.Civ.5.3-5).
4. **Explain** how taxes pay for public services and **name** what
   executive departments and agencies do day to day (D2.Eco.12.3-5,
   D2.Civ.1.3-5).
5. **Explain** how democracy relies on participation, **compare**
   decision procedures, and **propose** a policy for a real problem
   with steps (D2.Civ.2.3-5, D2.Civ.9.3-5, D2.Civ.11.3-5,
   D2.Civ.13.3-5, D2.Civ.7.3-5, D2.Civ.8.3-5).

## Essential question

*Who makes the rules where we live — and what rights and
responsibilities do we all share in making them fair?*

## Vocabulary

**Government** — the people and institutions that make and carry out
rules for a community, state, or country. · **Citizen** — a member of
a country with rights and responsibilities under its laws. ·
**National (federal) government** — the government of the whole
country. · **State government** — the government of one state. ·
**Local government** — the government of a city, town, or county. ·
**Branch** — one of the three parts of government: legislative,
executive, judicial. · **Legislative branch** — the part that makes
laws (Congress at the national level; a state legislature; a city
council). · **Executive branch** — the part that carries out laws
(the president; a governor; a mayor). · **Judicial branch** — the
part that explains what laws mean (courts and judges). · **Congress**
— the national legislature: the House of Representatives and the
Senate. · **Governor** — the elected leader of a state government. ·
**Mayor** — the elected leader of a city or town government. ·
**Law** — a rule a government makes that everyone in its area must
follow. · **Bill** — a proposed law, not yet voted into a law. ·
**Veto** — when the president (or a governor) refuses to sign a bill
so it does not become a law. · **Checks and balances** — the system
in which each branch can limit the power of the other branches. ·
**Constitution** — the written plan for a government; the U.S.
Constitution is the highest law of the country. · **Amendment** — a
change or addition to a constitution. · **Bill of Rights** — the
first ten amendments to the U.S. Constitution, added in 1791. ·
**Right** — a freedom the government must protect. ·
**Responsibility** — a duty a citizen owes to the community, such as
following laws, respecting others' rights, and helping keep shared
places in good order. · **Federalism** — power shared between the
national government and state governments. · **Territory** — land
belonging to the United States that is not a state. · **Tax** —
money people and businesses pay to a government to pay for public
services. · **Public service** — a service a government provides for
everyone, such as roads, schools, parks, firefighters, and libraries.
· **Agency** — a government office with a specific job, such as
protecting the environment or running the postal service. ·
**Deliberation** — careful discussion before a decision, hearing
different views. · **Consensus** — a decision everyone in the group
can agree to. · **Petition** — a written request to the government
asking it to change or do something.

- Government facts (checked 2026-11-06 against Ben's Guide to the
  U.S. Government, the National Constitution Center, and the
  repository's `resources/government_basics.md`): three branches —
  legislative makes laws, executive carries them out, judicial
  interprets them; Congress is the House of Representatives (435
  members, by population) and the Senate (2 per state, 100 total);
  435 + 100 = 535 members of Congress; a presidential veto can be
  overridden by a two-thirds vote in both chambers (290 of 435 in
  the House; 67 of 100 in the Senate, since two-thirds of 100 is
  66⅔); the Bill of Rights is the first ten amendments, ratified
  December 15, 1791; the 13th Amendment (1865) ended slavery in the
  United States; the 19th Amendment (1920) says the vote cannot be
  denied because of sex; the 26th Amendment (1971) lowered the
  voting age from 21 to 18. Rights timeline gaps: 1865 − 1791 = 74
  years; 1920 − 1865 = 55 years; 1971 − 1920 = 51 years.
- States and territories facts (checked 2026-11-06 against the 2020
  U.S. Census results): 50 states; largest by population is
  California (39,538,223 people in the 2020 census); smallest is
  Wyoming (576,851). Five inhabited U.S. territories: Puerto Rico,
  Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Northern
  Mariana Islands. Washington, D.C. is a federal district, not a
  state and not a territory; its residents have a non-voting
  delegate in the House of Representatives and no senators. All
  practice tax numbers in this unit are invented for teaching and
  are labeled as practice numbers, never quoted as real tax rates or
  budgets.
- The two deliberation passages in Lesson 05 are **original teaching
  passages written for this unit** — invented, clearly labeled, and
  never presented as real documents, quotations, or records of real
  meetings. The generated town illustration is an AI imagining of a
  generic town, never evidence of a specific place, office, or event.

## Accessibility and safety

- Every image carries alt text and a text-only alternative; charts
  pair color with words and symbols, never color alone.
- Rights-history content (slavery, exclusion from voting) is
  adult-led at the unit's plain, non-graphic level; the learner may
  answer orally, by pointing, or by drawing, and the adult scribes
  when writing is not the skill being measured.
- No learner names, photos, addresses, schedules, or grades enter
  this repository. The fair display is shared only with the guiding
  adult.
- No current office-holders, parties, or candidates are named in
  lesson content; the adult supplies local officials' names at use
  time and keeps examples nonpartisan. The learner never contacts a
  real official for this unit; the policy proposal is addressed to
  the classroom or school, not sent anywhere.
