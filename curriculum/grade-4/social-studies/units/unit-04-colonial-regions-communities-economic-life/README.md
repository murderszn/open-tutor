# Unit 04 — Colonial Regions, Communities, and Economic Life

**Grade:** 4 (ages 9–10) · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#25](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/25)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 15–18 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, about 35 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

By the 1700s, thirteen British colonies stretched along the Atlantic
coast, and they did not all live the same way. Learners compare the
three colonial regions — New England, the Middle Colonies, and the
Southern Colonies — and ask why: how soil, climate, rivers, and forests
shaped what people grew, made, and traded; how specialization and trade
connected the regions to each other and to the wider world; how money,
barter, and credit made exchange easier (and harder); and how colonists
began to govern themselves through town meetings, written agreements,
and elected assemblies.

Colonial economic life included enslaved labor, and this unit names
that plainly at an age-appropriate level with adult guidance: enslaved
people were forced to work without freedom or pay, and plantation
profits went to the people who owned the land and claimed ownership of
the workers — not to the workers themselves. No graphic detail is
assigned, and the focus stays on systems (labor, crops, trade, ports)
and on the people who resisted — by running away, by slowing work, by
keeping family and cultural practices alive, and by seeking freedom.
Indigenous nations are living communities in this unit, not a
past-tense topic: colonial settlement took land, changed trade, and met
resistance and diplomacy, and every colonial event in Lesson 05 is
examined from more than one perspective. The adult pre-selects every
source and co-reads the perspective passages.

## Prerequisites (developmental expectations; re-taught here as needed)

- Read a map with title, key, compass rose, and scale; name the
  cardinal directions and the three colonial regions' positions
  (north, middle, south along the Atlantic coast) (Unit 01).
- Place events on a timeline and say which came first (Units 01–03).
- Explain that people in the same time and place can see the same
  event differently (Units 02–03).
- Explain a production step in order (field → mill → barrel → ship)
  and weigh a benefit against a cost (Unit 03 exchange chains).
- Write 3–5 sentences in the learner's own words; draw and label with
  clear intent; present a short summary aloud.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Compare** the three colonial regions' environments, settlements,
   and daily life, and **explain** how environment shaped what each
   region produced (D2.Geo.11.3-5, D2.His.2.3-5, D2.His.14.3-5).
2. **Explain** colonial economic life: **identify** natural, human, and
   physical resources, **trace** a production chain from raw material
   to shipped good, and **explain** why regions specialized and traded
   rather than making everything themselves (D2.Eco.2.3-5,
   D2.Eco.3.3-5, D2.Eco.4.3-5, D2.His.14.3-5).
3. **Trace** trade connections between colonial regions and the wider
   world, **explain** how ports and trade routes changed where people
   settled and worked, and **name** who benefited from a trade and who
   was harmed or forced to work within it (D2.Geo.11.3-5,
   D2.Eco.2.3-5, D2.Eco.4.3-5, D2.His.14.3-5).
4. **Explain** how barter, commodity money, coins, and credit worked in
   the colonies, **use** a coin as evidence about trade, and **solve**
   a simple pieces-of-eight (bits) exchange (D2.Eco.5.3-5,
   D2.Eco.3.3-5, D2.His.2.3-5).
5. **Explain** three beginnings of colonial self-rule — a written
   agreement, a town meeting, and an elected assembly — and **explain**
   the same colonial event from two different perspectives, naming
   whose freedom or land was at stake (D2.Civ.3.3-5, D2.Civ.6.3-5,
   D2.His.4.3-5, D2.His.5.3-5).

## Essential question

*Why did three groups of colonies living on the same coast work,
trade, and govern themselves so differently — and who paid the price
for the way each region made its living?*

## Vocabulary

**Region** — an area with shared geographic and human features.
· **Colony** — a settlement ruled by a distant country; the thirteen
British colonies were ruled from Britain. · **Cash crop** — a crop
grown mainly to sell, not to eat at home. · **Plantation** — a large
farm growing a cash crop, in the Southern Colonies worked largely by
enslaved labor. · **Specialization** — concentrating on making what
you can make best, and trading for the rest. · **Production chain**
— the steps that turn a raw material into a finished, shipped good.
· **Natural resource** — something from nature people use, such as
soil, timber, fish, or a river. · **Human resource** — people's work
and skills. · **Physical capital** — tools, workshops, mills, and
ships people use to produce goods. · **Incentive** — something that
encourages a choice (a good price encourages a farmer to grow more of
a crop; a bad price discourages it). · **Profit** — what remains for
a seller after costs are paid. · **Barter** — trading goods or
services directly, without money. · **Commodity money** — a useful
good, such as tobacco or animal skins, accepted as payment because
people agree on its value. · **Piece of eight** — a Spanish silver
dollar widely used in the colonies; it could be cut into eight
"bits," so two bits made a quarter of a dollar. · **Credit** — buying
now and paying later, on a promise. · **Port** — a harbor town where
ships load and unload. · **Assembly** — a group of elected
representatives who make laws. · **Town meeting** — a New England
gathering where community members discussed and voted on local
decisions. · **Perspective** — a point of view shaped by a person's
beliefs, experiences, and place in the world. · **Enslaved person**
— a person forced to work without freedom or pay; use person-first
language ("enslaved people," not "slaves") throughout this unit.

- Region facts (checked 2026-10-31 against standard colonial-region
  summaries and the track audit): New England colonies — New
  Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut; rocky soil,
  short growing season, forests, fishing, shipbuilding, trade. Middle
  Colonies — New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware; fertile
  soil, wheat and other grains ("breadbasket"), diverse peoples,
  market towns and ports. Southern Colonies — Maryland, Virginia,
  North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia; long growing season,
  tobacco, rice, and indigo as cash crops, plantation agriculture
  worked largely by enslaved labor. Maine is **not** listed as a
  separate colony: in this period it was part of Massachusetts.
- Money facts (checked 2026-10-31: U.S. National Park Service,
  "Spanish Coins"; Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia, "Money in
  Colonial Times"): Britain forbade most colonial coin-making and
  British coin was scarce, so the Spanish silver dollar — the piece
  of eight — became the colonies' most widely used coin; it could be
  cut into eight bits, and two bits made a quarter. Colonists also
  bartered, used commodity money such as tobacco, and used wampum
  (shell beads) in early trade; Massachusetts struck its own Pine
  Tree Shilling in 1652 despite the ban, and colonies later printed
  paper bills of credit. Exact prices in this unit are practice
  numbers, never quoted as historical prices.
- Self-rule facts (checked 2026-10-31 against standard summaries):
  Virginia's House of Burgesses, 1619 — the first elected
  legislative assembly in the English colonies; the Mayflower
  Compact, 1620 — the Plymouth settlers' written agreement to govern
  themselves by majority rule; New England town meetings — local
  gatherings where community members discussed and voted on town
  decisions. These were beginnings of self-rule for the settlers who
  could take part: they did not include women, enslaved people, or
  Indigenous nations, and the unit says so plainly. Jamestown
  (1607) to the House of Burgesses (1619) is 12 years; Jamestown to
  Plymouth (1620) is 13 years; Burgesses to Compact is 1 year.
- The two perspective passages in Lesson 05 are **original teaching
  passages written for this unit** — invented, clearly labeled, and
  never presented as real historical documents or quotations. Real
  primary sources are adult-side only (see the Resource Pack).
  The generated port illustration is an AI imagining, never evidence
  of a specific place, ship, or event.
