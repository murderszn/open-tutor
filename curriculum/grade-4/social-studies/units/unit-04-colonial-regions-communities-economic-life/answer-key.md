# Answer Key — Unit 04

**Audience:** the guiding adult only. Keep this key away from learner
pages. Accept any answer that matches the reasoning below in the
learner's own words; sample wordings are models, not scripts. All
arithmetic was re-solved for this key.

## Lesson 01 — Three colonial regions

- **G1.** Southern Colonies. Environment clue: long warm summers.
  Work clue: fields of tobacco. Both match the Southern row.
- **G2.** Wrong because the colonies shared a ruler but not land,
  climate, work, or settlement patterns. Truthful rewrite (sample):
  "The thirteen colonies were all British, but their different
  environments made their daily lives different."
- **G3.** Accept any true pair with a reason, for example: "Fishing
  continued, but boats now have motors and weather forecasts
  (technology changed)"; "Bread still comes from wheat, but mills are
  factories and flour travels by truck (transport changed)."
- **I1.** New England: New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island,
  Connecticut — rocky soil, short season, forests, coastline. Middle:
  New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware — fertile soil, mild
  climate, wide rivers. Southern: Maryland, Virginia, North
  Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia — long warm season, fertile
  coastal soil, wide rivers.
- **I2.** Sample: "A New England town clustered around a meetinghouse
  because rocky soil made small farms and sea/workshop jobs the
  pattern, while a Southern settlement spread along rivers because
  plantations needed river landings and there were fewer towns.
  Environment and settlement facts must be matched to the right
  region; accept any correct pairing."
- **I3.** Accept two environment-based predictions, for example:
  "They stop fishing and start growing wheat, because Pennsylvania's
  fertile soil and mild climate suit grain"; "They trade at a market
  town instead of a fishing port, because rivers carry flour to
  ports." A prediction without an environment reason is partial.
- **Applied task.** Three bands in order (New England, Middle,
  Southern), colonies labeled, one environment and one work symbol
  per band, words on every band.
- **Exit check.** North to south: New England (rocky soil; fish or
  lumber or ships), Middle Colonies (fertile soil; wheat or flour),
  Southern Colonies (long warm season; tobacco, rice, or indigo).

## Lesson 02 — Work, production, specialization

- **G1.** Natural: fish (and the sea). Human: the fisher. Physical
  capital: boat, nets, salt, barrels. If the boat is missing, the
  catch step stops first; if salt is missing, the preserving/packing
  step stops first. Accept any correctly classified trio.
- **G2.** Wrong because enslaved workers were forced, not willing,
  and received no pay or share of profit. Truthful rewrite (sample):
  "Plantations were profitable for their owners because enslaved
  people were forced to work without pay."
- **G3.** Accept two profit-driven decisions, for example: "Plant
  more tobacco (high price = more profit per barrel)"; "Buy more
  land for tobacco (expand the profitable crop)." Incentive in both:
  higher expected profit. A decision without an incentive is partial.
- **I1.** Sample chain: forest → timber cut (N: trees; H: sawyer) →
  planks sawn (P: sawmill) → shipyard assembly (H: shipbuilder) →
  ship launched and carrying goods (P: ship). All three resource
  kinds must appear and be labeled.
- **I2.** Sample: "The miller grinds grain best and the shipbuilder
  builds ships best. By specializing and trading flour for ships,
  each gets the other's product better and cheaper than by doing
  both jobs badly. 'Specialization' must appear with this reasoning."
- **I3.** Accept two with chain links, for example: "Lumber suppliers
  lose a buyer (no ships need planks)"; "Merchants waiting for a new
  ship cannot carry goods (no ship, no cargo space)"; "Port workers
  lose loading work." A job without a link is partial.
- **Applied task.** Five ordered cards with a resource each; honesty
  sentence present and truthful — for tobacco/rice chains it must
  name forced labor and that workers were unpaid and unfree.
- **Exit check.** Specialization = concentrating on what you can
  make best and trading for the rest. Example: New England ships/fish
  traded for Southern tobacco (or Middle flour for New England
  lumber/ships).

## Lesson 03 — Trade webs and ports

- **G1.** Lumber: probably New England (forests, shipbuilding).
  Grain: probably the Middle Colonies (wheat, "breadbasket"). Each
  answer needs its Lesson 01 reason.
- **G2.** Benefit: the planter gains a market in Britain for tobacco
  (or gains cloth he cannot make at home). Problem: Britain set the
  trade rules and the planter had no vote on them. Both parts needed.
- **G3.** Missing: the forcible transport and sale of enslaved African
  people. Truthful rewrite (sample): "Atlantic trade carried goods
  like sugar, fish, and cloth — and it also forcibly carried enslaved
  people, who were treated as property, not cargo." Any rewrite that
  names forced transport of people is acceptable.
- **I1.** Four places with origin-matched arrows (for example: New
  England → fish; Middle → flour; Southern → tobacco; Britain →
  cloth), plus a distinct arrow for enslaved people carried by force
  from Africa to the Americas, with a sentence explaining that this
  arrow moved people against their will and is not equivalent to a
  goods arrow.
- **I2.** Sample: "A harbor town grew faster because ships could load
  and unload there, so trade brought warehouses, workshops, and
  workers, while an inland town without a port or river landing
  could not move goods cheaply. 'Port,' 'trade,' and 'settlement'
  (or 'grew') must all appear with this reasoning."
- **I3.** Sample (flour): "Wheat grown (farmer) → milled (miller) →
  packed and carried to port (cooper/porter) → shipped and sold
  abroad (sailor/merchant)." Four steps, a worker at each step.
- **Applied task.** Lists: (a) lumber — New England; (b) grain —
  Middle Colonies; (c) cannot tell — barrels (contents not shown).
  For each "cannot tell" item, one sentence naming settling evidence
  (a label, shipping list, or merchant's record). Accept any
  correctly reasoned sorting.
- **Exit check.** Three scales: between regions (for example, fish
  for tobacco), across the Atlantic to Britain (tobacco for cloth),
  with the West Indies/Africa (fish/lumber for sugar; enslaved people
  carried by force). Harmed group named plainly (enslaved African
  people and/or Indigenous nations whose land was taken), with a reason.

## Lesson 04 — Money, coins, credit

- **G1.** 8 − 3 = 5 bits of change. 5/8 of a dollar. (Check: 3 + 5 = 8.)
- **G2.** Commodity money (tobacco as payment). Problem: it can spoil,
  vary in quality, or be valued differently by buyer and seller.
  Accept any one real problem.
- **G3.** Wrong because British coin was scarce and Britain forbade
  most colonial coin-making. Two omitted kinds (any two): barter,
  commodity money (tobacco/skins/wampum), Spanish coins, paper bills
  of credit, credit.
- **I1.** (a) 8 − 5 = 3 bits. (b) 2 + 2 = 4 bits total; 8 − 4 = 4 bits
  of change (a half dollar). Both subtractions must be shown.
- **I2.** Silver was trusted everywhere, portable, and consistent in
  value, so a shopkeeper could spend it anywhere. Tobacco money was
  still useful in Virginia because it was grown there, widely held,
  and accepted at an agreed value where coin was scarce.
- **I3.** Sample: "The Spanish coin suggests this port traded in a
  network where Spanish silver circulated (likely via the West
  Indies). The Pine Tree Shilling shows Massachusetts made its own
  coin in 1652 despite Britain's ban. Neither coin proves who spent
  it, what they bought, or whether the trade was fair."
- **Applied task.** Per coin: denomination, figures/symbols, design,
  one verified mint-source note, one evidence sentence, one limit
  sentence. The adult verifies the mint source; learner work stays
  private.
- **Exit check.** Four kinds: barter, commodity money, coins (mostly
  Spanish pieces of eight), paper/credit. Change: 8 − 4 = 4 bits, a
  half dollar.

## Lesson 05 — Self-rule and perspectives

- **G1.** The House of Burgesses (1619) came first. 1620 − 1619 = 1
  year later came the Mayflower Compact.
- **G2.** Voters in this period: free adult men, usually property
  owners, among the settlers. Two groups left out (any two): women,
  enslaved people, Indigenous nations (also: non-property-owning men
  in many colonies — accept if named with the period qualifier).
- **G3.** Wrong because self-rule for voting settlers coexisted with
  enslavement and with taking Indigenous land. Truthful rewrite
  (sample): "Some colonial settlers governed themselves, while
  enslaved people remained unfree and Indigenous nations were not
  represented in those governments."
- **I1.** (a) Mayflower Compact; (b) town meeting; (c) House of
  Burgesses (assembly).
- **I2.** Sample: "A small town can gather everyone in one room and
  vote directly, but a large colony cannot fit everyone in one room,
  so it elects representatives — an assembly — to vote for their
  communities. 'Representative' and 'direct' must both appear."
- **I3.** Two perspective sentences, each labeled "invented teaching
  sentences" (sample: merchant — "The warehouse will bring more ships
  and jobs to our port"; fisher — "The warehouse blocks the landing
  where I have always pulled up my boat"). The second perspective
  must be stated from that person's interests, respectfully.
- **Applied task.** Two labeled invented perspective sentences plus
  one "fair for whom?" sentence, checked as in I3.
- **Exit check.** Three beginnings: Mayflower Compact (1620, written
  agreement, majority rule); town meeting (New England, direct local
  vote); House of Burgesses (Virginia, 1619, first elected assembly).
  "Not rule by everyone" explained by exclusion: women, enslaved
  people, and Indigenous nations could not vote in these bodies.

## Practice sets

### Week 15 — A

- **A1.** New England: Massachusetts, Connecticut. Middle: Pennsylvania,
  Delaware. Southern: Georgia, Virginia.
- **A2.** Middle Colonies. Environment clue: fertile soil. Work clue:
  wheat fields / flour loaded at a port.
- **A3.** Accept one difficulty each with an environment reason, for
  example: "The New England farmer struggles with long heat and
  tobacco growing (unfamiliar crop/climate)"; "The Southern planter
  cannot grow tobacco in rocky soil with a short season (crop needs
  long warmth)."
- **A4.** False. Maine was part of Massachusetts in this period, not
  a separate colony.
- **A5.** Accept one continuation (fishing still happens) and one
  change (motorboats, forecasts, refrigeration, paid free labor —
  any true change with a reason).
- **A6.** Most likely Southern Colonies: scattered river settlements
  and plantations, with rivers as roads, fit that pattern better
  than clustered New England towns or Middle market towns.
- **A7.** Correction (sample): "Many Southern colonists lived on
  small farms or worked as craftspeople or in port towns; plantations
  were one important pattern, not the only way of living." Two other
  ways required.
- **A8.** Strip matches the Lesson 01 table after correction in a
  different color; adult checks order, colonies, and symbols.

### Week 16 — B

- **B1.** 1 grown in a field; 2 leaves cut and dried; 3 packed in
  barrels; 4 loaded on a ship.
- **B2.** Sample: N — soil, wheat, sunlight; H — farmer, miller;
  P — mill, barrels, wagon. Each kind labeled and plausible.
- **B3.** Accept two with links, for example: "Farmers cannot turn
  wheat into flour to sell (no milling step)"; "Bakers/port sellers
  lose flour supply (broken chain link)."
- **B4.** Accept a profit-driven decision (plant less rice, switch
  crops, or store rice for a better price) with incentive = falling
  profit. Cost sentence must note that enslaved workers bore costs
  without choice or pay regardless of the owner's decision (for
  example, harder conditions or being sold/moved at the owner's
  decision — do not invent a specific sale; the general point is
  that workers had no say and no wages).
- **B5.** Two sentences explaining specialization with the
  shipbuilder/grower example, as in Lesson 02's exit check.
- **B6.** Wheat. Rice needs a long, warm, wet season and flooded
  fields — a Southern lowcountry pattern, not a Middle Colony one.
- **B7.** Correction (sample): "Profit from a plantation went to the
  owner; the enslaved workers who did the work received no pay and
  had no freedom." Both halves (owner received; workers did not)
  required.
- **B8.** Sample: caught at sea → salted/dried → packed in barrels →
  loaded on a ship → sold abroad. Backward check passes: no step
  works without the one before it.

### Week 17 — C

- **C1.** Sample: caught (fisher) → salted/dried (processor) → packed
  and carried to port (cooper/porter) → shipped and sold in the West
  Indies (sailor/merchant). Four steps, a worker at each.
- **C2.** Web arrows origin-matched: fish from New England, flour
  from a Middle port, tobacco/rice from a Southern port, cloth/tools
  from Britain. Any origin-matched set is acceptable.
- **C3.** Town: ships and their fees leave; warehouses, shipyards,
  and port jobs shrink. Inland region: its farmers lose their cheap
  route to market (goods must travel farther overland or through
  another port). Two town consequences + one inland consequence.
- **C4.** Lumber — New England (forests). Grain — Middle Colonies
  (wheat). Cannot tell — barrels (contents not shown); settling
  evidence: a label, shipping list, or merchant's record.
- **C5.** Counterexample (either is full credit): enslaved African
  people, forced to labor without pay; or Indigenous nations, whose
  land was taken as settlement spread. Reason must accompany the group.
- **C6.** Sample: "They were ports where ships could load and unload."
  "Trade routes brought goods, workers, and workshops to them, so
  they grew." Both sentences (port; trade routes) required.
- **C7.** Farmer benefit: a market beyond his own town for his flour.
  Buyer benefit: a supply of flour/food in the West Indies. One
  sentence each.
- **C8.** Correction (sample): "The Atlantic trade system carried
  goods, and it also forcibly transported enslaved people from Africa
  to the Americas." The forced transport of people must be named.

### Week 18 — D

- **D1.** (a) 8 − 2 = 6 bits. (b) 5 + 2 = 7 bits total; 8 − 7 = 1 bit
  of change. All subtractions shown.
- **D2.** Commodity money (animal skins). Advantage: useful, widely
  held, accepted at an agreed value where coin was scarce. Problem:
  quality varies, skins are bulky, or values shift — accept any one
  real problem.
- **D3.** Evidence (sample): "The town traded in a network where
  Spanish silver circulated, and someone needed change smaller than
  a whole dollar." Limit (sample): "It does not prove who spent it,
  what they bought, or that the trade was fair."
- **D4.** (a) → (ii); (b) → (iii); (c) → (i).
- **D5.** Order: Jamestown 1607, House of Burgesses 1619, Mayflower
  Compact 1620. Gaps: 1619 − 1607 = 12 years; 1620 − 1619 = 1 year.
- **D6.** Two labeled invented perspective sentences (settler: road
  helps trade/vote; Indigenous nation member: road crosses long-used
  land without consent) plus one "fair for whom?" sentence (sample:
  "Fair for the settlers who voted, but not for the nation whose land
  was crossed without asking."). All three sentences required.
- **D7.** Sample: "Settlers in towns and colonies voted in meetings
  and assemblies and made local laws — that self-rule was real. But
  it was limited: women, enslaved people, and Indigenous nations
  could not vote in those bodies, even though their decisions
  affected them." At least two excluded groups required.
- **D8.** Money correction: colonists also used barter, commodity
  money, Spanish coins, paper bills, and credit — not British pounds
  alone. Fairness correction: trade and plantation profit rested on
  forced enslaved labor and on taking Indigenous land, so not every
  trade was fair. Two sentences, one per half.

## Formative quiz — Week 17

- **Q1.** C — New England.
- **Q2.** B — Pennsylvania.
- **Q3.** B — concentrate on what you make best, trade for the rest.
- **Q4.** B — packed in barrels.
- **Q5.** C — colony to West Indies (Atlantic trade).
- **Q6.** B — ships could load/unload there, drawing trade, workshops,
  and workers.
- **Q7.** 8 − 3 = 5 bits of change.
- **Q8.** Any non-coin kind with a real problem: barter (needs a double
  match of wants); commodity money (spoilage/quality variation);
  paper bills of credit (trust/value risk, over-issue); credit (debt
  if harvest/ship fails). Kind + problem both required.
- **Q9.** Full credit only for a sentence naming forced, unpaid labor
  behind the profit, for example: "Plantation profits went to owners
  because enslaved people were forced to work without pay, so the
  word 'profits' hides whose labor created them."
- **Q10.** Full credit only with both parts, for example: "Benefited:
  shipowners/merchants earned fees and profits from carrying goods.
  Harmed: enslaved African people, forcibly transported and forced to
  labor without pay." (Indigenous land-taking is an acceptable harmed
  group with its reason.)

  4 shipped to the West Indies.
