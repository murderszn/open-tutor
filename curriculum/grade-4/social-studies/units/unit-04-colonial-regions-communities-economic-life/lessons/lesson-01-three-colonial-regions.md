# Lesson 01 — Three Colonial Regions

**Goal:** I can compare the three colonial regions and explain how
each region's environment shaped what its people produced.
**Time:** two 35-minute sessions. **Materials:** U.S. outline map,
region-strip paper, colored pencils or markers.

**Readiness check (adult-led, 3 minutes):** Point north, middle, and
south on the Atlantic coast of the outline map. In Unit 01, what did
you learn about how a place's land and water shape how people live
there? (Land, water, soil, and climate shape food, work, and travel.)

## Teaching

### One coast, three ways of living

By the 1700s, thirteen British colonies lined the Atlantic coast. A
colony was a settlement ruled by a distant country — here, Britain.
Historians group the thirteen into three **regions**, areas that share
geographic and human features. The regions are not just lines on a
map: different soil, weather, forests, and rivers pushed each region
toward different work, different towns, and different daily lives.
That is the question of this lesson: *how did where you lived change
how you lived?*

### The region table

| Region | Colonies | Environment | What people produced | Settlements and daily life |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **New England** | New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut | Rocky soil, short growing season, cold winters, thick forests, long coastline | Fish, lumber, ships, trade goods; small farms growing food for the family | Small towns clustered around a meetinghouse and common; fishing villages and port towns; many people worked at sea or in workshops |
| **Middle Colonies** | New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware | Fertile soil, mild climate, wide rivers | Wheat and other grains, flour, iron goods; called the "breadbasket" colonies | Farms of many sizes, market towns, busy ports (New York, Philadelphia); many different peoples and faiths living side by side |
| **Southern Colonies** | Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia | Long, warm growing season, fertile coastal soil, wide rivers | Tobacco, rice, and indigo as cash crops — crops grown mainly to sell | Scattered farms and large plantations along rivers; rivers were the roads; towns were fewer and farther apart |

Two cautions. First, Maine is not a separate colony in this table: in
this period it was part of Massachusetts. Second, "Southern" does not
mean "only plantations": the South also had small farms, craftspeople,
and port towns. Regions describe patterns, not every single person.

### Environment → work: the chain of reasoning

Historians explain a region's work in three steps: (1) name the
environment, (2) name what it allows or blocks, (3) name the work that
follows. Rocky soil and a short summer block large-scale farming, so
New Englanders turned to the sea and the forest. Fertile soil and a
mild climate allow grain farming, so the Middle Colonies grew wheat.
A long, warm season allows tobacco and rice, so the Southern Colonies
grew cash crops — and, shamefully, used enslaved labor to grow them
at a scale no family farm could match. That last step is not a
footnote: in Lesson 02 you will see whose work made plantation
profits possible, and in Lesson 05 you will hear a perspective that
is not the plantation owner's.

### Then and now

Compare colonial daily life with life today, carefully: some things
changed completely, some only partly. A New England fisher still
fishes, but from a motorboat with weather forecasts, not a wooden
sailboat. A Middle Colony farmer's wheat still becomes flour and
bread, but the mill is now a factory and the flour travels by truck,
not by barrel on a riverboat. A Southern port still ships goods, but
the workers are paid employees, not enslaved people — that change,
from forced labor to free labor, is the most important difference of
all, and it came much later, after a war and a constitutional change
you will study in later grades. Comparing "then" and "now" means
naming both what continued and what changed.

### Worked example 1 — Sort by environment

*Question: "A colony has rocky soil, a short summer, and a long
coastline. Its people catch fish, cut timber, and build ships. Which
region is it, and how do you know?"*

Reasoning: Rocky soil blocks big farms, so farming cannot be the main
work. A long coastline and forests point to the sea and timber. Fish
+ lumber + ships is the New England pattern in the table. Answer: New
England — the environment clues match that row, not the fertile-soil
Middle row or the long-season Southern row.

### Worked example 2 — Explain, don't just label

*Question: "Why did the Middle Colonies grow so much wheat instead of
fishing like New England?"*

Reasoning: Start from the environment, not from a label. The Middle
Colonies had fertile soil and a mild climate — wheat grows well
there, and rivers carried flour to ports. Fishing was possible, but
it was not the best use of that land. The region did what its land
let it do best. A good answer gives the environment reason, not just
"because they were the Middle Colonies."

## Guided practice (adult reads aloud if needed)

**G1.** A traveler writes: "Long warm summers, wide rivers, fields of
tobacco as far as I can see." Which region? Name one environment clue
and one work clue from the sentence.
*Hint: which row has both long seasons and tobacco?*

**G2.** Error-analysis: "A friend says the thirteen colonies were all
the same because they were all British." What is wrong with that
sentence? Rewrite it truthfully in one sentence.
*Hint: same ruler, different land.*

**G3.** Name one thing about colonial daily life in your region row
that is still true today, and one thing that has changed. Give a
reason for the change.
*Hint: boats, bread, and paid work are three places to look.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Copy the region table's first three columns (region, colonies,
environment) from memory as far as you can, then check and fix it.
Circle the colony names you missed.

**I2.** In 3–4 sentences, explain why a New England town and a
Southern plantation settlement looked different — use one environment
fact and one settlement fact for each.

**I3.** A family moves from rocky-soil New England to fertile-soil
Pennsylvania in 1750. Predict two ways their daily work changes, and
explain each prediction with an environment reason.

## Applied task

Begin your region strip (you will finish it in the project): a long
strip of paper, north at the top, south at the bottom, divided into
three bands. Label each band with its region name and its colonies,
and draw or write one environment symbol (rocky ground, wheat stalk,
tobacco leaf) and one work symbol (ship, mill, riverboat) per band.
Color is never the only label — every band carries words too.

## Exit check

Without looking at the table, name the three regions in north-to-south
order and give one environment fact and one product for each. The
adult checks: order correct, facts matched to the right region.
