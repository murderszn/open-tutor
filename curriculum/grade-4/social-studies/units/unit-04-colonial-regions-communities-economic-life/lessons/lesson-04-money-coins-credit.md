# Lesson 04 — Money, Coins, and Credit

**Goal:** I can explain how colonists paid for things, use a coin as
evidence about trade, and solve a pieces-of-eight exchange.
**Time:** one 35-minute session, plus the coin investigation begun in
Session 2 of Week 18. **Materials:** play coins or paper bits (8
equal pieces = 1 piece of eight), educator-provided coin images from
the kept currency-history assignment.

**Readiness check (adult-led, 3 minutes):** If you have no coins at
all, how could you still buy bread? (Trade something the baker wants,
promise to pay later, or pay with a good everyone accepts.)

## Teaching

### The problem money solves

Barter — trading goods directly — works only when both people want
what the other has. A fisher with fish who wants shoes must find a
shoemaker who wants fish, today. Money solves that double problem: the
fisher sells fish for money, then spends the money on shoes whenever
a shoemaker is selling. Money is a go-between that everyone agrees to
accept. That is D2.Eco.5: money makes exchange easier — but only when
people trust it and there is enough of it. Colonial America often had
neither, which is why colonists used so many kinds of payment at once.

### Four ways colonists paid

1. **Barter.** Goods or work swapped directly: fish for firewood,
   a day's labor for a bushel of corn. Simple, but slow and fiddly.
2. **Commodity money.** A useful good everyone agrees to take as
   payment. In Virginia and Maryland, tobacco itself was money: taxes
   and debts could be paid in tobacco at an agreed value. Animal
   skins worked the same way on the frontier. Wampum — shell beads
   made by Indigenous peoples — was used in early colonial trade and
   even accepted for some official payments in Massachusetts for a
   time. Commodity money works until the good spoils, varies in
   quality, or floods the market.
3. **Coins — mostly Spanish.** Britain forbade most colonial
   coin-making and kept British coin scarce in America, so the coin
   colonists actually used most was the Spanish silver dollar, the
   **piece of eight** ("eight" because it was worth eight reales,
   called "bits"). A piece of eight could literally be cut into
   eight bits to make change: two bits = a quarter of a dollar
   ("two bits" is still a nickname for a quarter). Massachusetts
   broke the ban in 1652 and struck its own Pine Tree Shilling, but
   Spanish dollars stayed the everyday coin for much of the 1600s
   and 1700s. A coin's ridged edge mattered: it showed nobody had
   shaved silver off the rim.
4. **Paper and credit.** When coin ran short, colonies printed paper
   bills of credit — promises to pay in coin later. And everywhere,
   people bought on **credit**: take the goods now, pay after the
   harvest or after the ship comes in. Credit kept trade moving, but
   it also trapped people in debt when a harvest failed or a ship
   was lost. A farmer who owed the merchant could not easily say no
   to the merchant's prices.

### Coins as evidence

A coin is a small historical source. From one coin a historian can
ask: Where was it made? (A Spanish coin in a Virginia shop means
trade reached Spanish ports or the West Indies.) What is it made of?
(Silver — valuable everywhere, which is why it traveled.) What does
its wear or cutting show? (Cut marks mean someone needed small
change.) What does it *not* show? (It does not show who earned it,
whether they were free or enslaved, or whether the trade was fair.)
That last question is the habit this unit builds: evidence has
limits, and naming them is part of using evidence well. The kept
currency-history assignment (educator-provided coin images, verified
against an official mint source) begins here and finishes in Unit 07.

### The bits arithmetic (practice numbers)

One piece of eight = 8 bits. So: a quarter = 2 bits; a half = 4 bits;
three-quarters = 6 bits. Change from one piece = 8 minus the price in
bits. These are the only numbers you need, and every price in this
lesson is a practice number invented for arithmetic — never quote a
lesson price as a historical price.

### Worked example 1 — Make change

*Question: "A basket of grain costs 6 bits. You pay with one piece of
eight. How many bits of change do you get, and what fraction of a
dollar is that?"*

Reasoning: One piece = 8 bits. 8 − 6 = 2 bits of change. Two bits out
of eight = 2/8 = 1/4, a quarter of a dollar. Check: price (6) +
change (2) = 8, the whole piece. If price + change does not equal 8,
the subtraction is wrong.

### Worked example 2 — Read a coin as evidence

*Question: "An archaeologist finds a cut Spanish coin in a colonial
port town. What does it suggest about trade, and what does it not
prove?"*

Reasoning: It suggests the town traded in a network where Spanish
silver circulated (likely via the West Indies), and that someone
needed change smaller than a whole dollar — so the coin was cut.
It does not prove who spent it, what they bought, or that the trade
was fair. Evidence suggests; it rarely proves the whole story.

## Guided practice (adult reads aloud if needed)

**G1.** A tool costs 3 bits. You pay with one piece of eight. How much
change, in bits and as a fraction of a dollar? Show the subtraction.
*Hint: 8 − 3 = ?*

**G2.** A farmer pays his debt in tobacco instead of coin. Which kind
of payment is that, and what is one problem with it? (Tobacco can
spoil or vary in quality; the merchant may value it differently.)
*Hint: the good itself is the money.*

**G3.** Error-analysis: "A friend says colonial money was simple:
everyone used British pounds." What is wrong with that sentence?
Name two payment kinds the friend left out.
*Hint: Spanish coins and barter were everyday reality.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Solve: (a) A rope costs 5 bits; change from one piece?
(b) Two baskets at 2 bits each; total, and change from one piece?
Show both subtractions.

**I2.** In 2–3 sentences, explain why a shopkeeper would rather be paid
in Spanish silver than in tobacco. Then give one reason tobacco money
was still useful in Virginia.

**I3.** You find two coins in a port town: one Spanish, one
Massachusetts Pine Tree Shilling (1652). Write two evidence sentences
(one per coin) and one "does not prove" sentence for the pair.

## Applied task

Begin the kept currency-history assignment: from the educator's three
coin images, record each coin's denomination, figures or symbols, and
design, and verify one design against an official mint source (the
adult supplies the source; see the Resource Pack). Record one
evidence sentence and one limit sentence per coin. Keep completed
work private; no coin is bought or removed from any collection.

## Exit check

Name the four ways colonists paid, in your own words, and solve:
a 4-bit purchase paid with one piece of eight — change? (4 bits, a
half dollar.) The adult checks: four kinds named, subtraction shown.
