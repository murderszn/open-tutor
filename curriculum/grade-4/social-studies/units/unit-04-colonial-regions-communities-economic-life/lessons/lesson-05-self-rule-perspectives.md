# Lesson 05 — Self-Rule and Perspectives

**Goal:** I can explain three beginnings of colonial self-rule and
explain one colonial event from two perspectives.
**Time:** one 35-minute session. **Materials:** the two passages
below, the Lesson 01 region table.

**Readiness check (adult-led, 3 minutes):** Who made the rules in a
colonial town — someone in Britain, or people in the town? (Both:
Britain claimed the final say, but towns and colonies also made many
of their own rules. That tension is this lesson.)

## Teaching

### Rules from far away — and rules made at home

Britain claimed authority over the colonies: governors were often
appointed from Britain, and Parliament in London made trade laws.
But an ocean is wide, and a letter took weeks. So colonists also made
rules at home — and over time, making rules at home became a habit,
then an expectation, then a demand. Three beginnings matter at this
grade:

1. **A written agreement — the Mayflower Compact (1620).** Before
   landing at Plymouth, the Mayflower's settlers wrote and signed an
   agreement to form a government and obey laws made by majority
   rule. It was a promise to govern themselves together, not a gift
   from a king.
2. **A town meeting — New England.** In New England towns, community
   members gathered to discuss and vote on local decisions: roads,
   schools, fences, taxes for the town. It was direct democracy at
   town scale — people deciding face to face, by vote.
3. **An elected assembly — the House of Burgesses (Virginia, 1619).**
   Virginia's settlers elected representatives — burgesses — to meet
   and make laws for the colony. It was the first elected legislative
   assembly in the English colonies: representation instead of
   everyone gathering in one room, which mattered once a colony was
   too big for one meeting.

Timeline check: Jamestown was founded in 1607; the House of
Burgesses followed 12 years later (1619 − 1607 = 12); the Mayflower
Compact followed one year after that (1620 − 1619 = 1); Jamestown to
Plymouth is 13 years (1620 − 1607 = 13).

### Who was left out — say it plainly

These beginnings of self-rule were real, and they were limited. The
people who voted in town meetings and assemblies were, in this
period, almost entirely free adult men who owned property — and
usually only settlers. Women, enslaved people, and Indigenous nations
were not represented in these bodies, even though the decisions made
there affected their land, labor, and lives. A town meeting that
votes to take more land is "self-rule" for the settlers voting — and
a threat to the Indigenous nation whose land it is. Both sentences
are true, and a good historian holds both at once. That is why this
lesson never asks "was this fair?" as a yes/no question, but "fair
for whom?"

### Two perspectives on one event (invented teaching passages)

The two passages below are **invented teaching passages written for
this unit. They are not historical documents, not quotations, and
must never be cited as if real people said them.** They exist so you
can practice naming a perspective and its reasons.

*Passage A — a settler at a town meeting (invented):* "Our town voted
to build a new road to the river landing. More wagons can reach the
port, and our wheat will sell faster. We all had a say, and the vote
was fair."

*Passage B — a member of a neighboring Indigenous nation, hearing of
the road (invented):* "The road they voted for crosses the hunting
ground our families have used for generations. Nobody asked us, and
no vote of theirs can give them land that is not theirs to give."

Read them together: same road, two perspectives. Passage A's reasons
are trade and a vote; Passage B's reasons are prior use and consent.
The context explains the difference: the settlers' context is a town
that governs itself; the Indigenous nation's context is a homeland
being crossed without permission. Neither passage proves what
actually happened on any real road — invented passages cannot prove
history. They practice the skill; real sources, chosen by the adult,
are what historians check claims against.

### Worked example 1 — Name the beginning

*Question: "A Virginia town elects a man to travel to a meeting and
vote on colony laws for them. Which beginning of self-rule is this,
and why is it not a town meeting?"*

Reasoning: Elected representatives meeting to make colony laws is
an assembly — the House of Burgesses pattern. A town meeting is
different: the community itself gathers and votes directly, without
sending a representative. The difference is direct vote versus
elected representative. Both are self-rule; they solve the problem of
size differently.

### Worked example 2 — Two perspectives, fairly stated

*Question: "Using Passages A and B, explain the road decision from
each perspective in one sentence each, without saying one side is
lying."*

Reasoning: Passage A: the settlers see a fair vote that helps their
town's trade. Passage B: the Indigenous nation sees a decision made
without their consent about land they have long used. Both sentences
report what each side would say and why — beliefs, experiences, and
position — without upgrading either invented passage into fact.
"Fair for whom?" is the question that keeps both sentences honest.

## Guided practice (adult reads aloud if needed)

**G1.** Which came first: the House of Burgesses or the Mayflower
Compact? By how many years? Show the subtraction.
*Hint: 1620 − 1619.*

**G2.** A New England town votes to build a school. Who votes, in this
period, and who does not get a vote? Name two groups left out.
*Hint: free adult men with property voted; women, enslaved people,
and Indigenous nations did not.*

**G3.** Error-analysis: "A friend says colonial self-rule meant
everyone was free and equal." What is wrong with that sentence?
Rewrite it truthfully in one sentence.
*Hint: self-rule for some settlers coexisted with enslavement and
land-taking.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Match each description to its beginning (Compact, town
meeting, assembly): (a) settlers sign a written agreement before
landing; (b) townspeople gather and vote on a road; (c) elected men
meet to make colony laws.

**I2.** In 3–4 sentences, explain why a big colony needed an assembly
while a small town could use a meeting. Use the words "representative"
and "direct."

**I3.** Write two perspective sentences about a new port warehouse:
one from a merchant who wants it built, one from a fisher whose
landing it blocks. Label both "invented teaching sentences."

## Applied task

Perspective card for the project: choose one colonial event from this
unit (a new road, a new port, a tobacco price rise, a town vote).
Write two perspective sentences (settler view; Indigenous or
enslaved person's view), each labeled invented, and one "fair for
whom?" sentence underneath. The adult checks that the second
perspective is stated respectfully and from that person's interests,
not as a villain or a bystander.

## Exit check

Name the three beginnings of self-rule with one fact each (year or
form), and explain in one sentence why "self-rule" did not mean
"rule by everyone." The adult checks: three beginnings present, the
exclusion named plainly.
