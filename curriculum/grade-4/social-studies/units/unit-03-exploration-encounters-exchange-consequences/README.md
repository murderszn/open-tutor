# Unit 03 — Exploration, Encounters, Exchange, and Consequences

**Grade:** 4 (ages 9–10) · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#25](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/25)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 11–14 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, about 35 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

This unit is taught as **encounter, not discovery**. The lands European
sailors reached already had names, governments, farms, towns, and histories,
and the peoples who lived there — Indigenous nations — are living
communities today, not a past-tense topic. Learners follow five voyages
across the Atlantic, ask why people sailed and what each expedition sought
and found, trace the **Columbian Exchange** of crops, animals, goods, and
diseases in both directions, compare two accounts of one encounter,
and build a claim about what changed — and what continued — after contact.

Difficult history is named plainly and honestly at an age-appropriate
level with adult guidance, never glossed and never graphic: conquest,
displacement, disease, and forced labor are part of the consequences, and
every voyage is examined from at least two perspectives. There is no
heroic framing of explorers. The adult pre-selects every source, co-reads
the encounter passages, and stops for discussion whenever the learner
needs it.

## Prerequisites (developmental expectations; re-taught here as needed)

- Read a map with title, key, compass rose, scale bar, and grid; name
  cardinal directions (Unit 01).
- Place events on a timeline and say which came first (diagnostic Weeks
  1–2; Unit 02 perspectives work).
- Explain that people in the same time and place can see the same event
  differently (Unit 02).
- Write 3–5 sentences in the learner's own words; draw and label with
  clear intent; present a short summary aloud.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Sequence** five exploration voyages on a timeline, **name** what
   each expedition sought and found, and **explain** two motives for
   sailing and two tools that made long ocean voyages possible
   (D2.His.1.3-5, D2.Geo.7.3-5, D2.His.3.3-5).
2. **Trace** at least three items in each direction of the Columbian
   Exchange and **explain** one probable effect of the exchange on each
   side of the Atlantic, including disease and forced labor, in plain,
   age-appropriate language (D2.Eco.14.3-5, D2.His.14.3-5, D2.Geo.7.3-5).
3. **Compare** two accounts of the same encounter, **infer** each
   account's audience and purpose from clues inside the account, and
   **sort** statements into fact and opinion (D2.His.4.3-5, D2.His.5.3-5,
   D2.His.6.3-5, D2.His.10.3-5, D2.His.11.3-5, D3.2.3-5, D2.Civ.10.3-5).
4. **Explain** probable causes and effects of contact for one region,
   naming what changed and what continued (D2.His.14.3-5, D2.Geo.7.3-5,
   D2.Civ.3.3-5).
5. **Ask** a compelling question with at least two supporting questions
   and **build** an explanation that states a claim, gives two pieces of
   evidence, and uses correct sequence (D1.2.3-5, D1.3.3-5, D1.4.3-5,
   D4.2.3-5).

## Essential question

*When strangers meet across an ocean, who gains, who loses, and how do we
know — and whose account should we believe?*

## Vocabulary

**Encounter** — a meeting between peoples who did not know each other
before. · **Expedition** — a journey made for a purpose, such as finding
a trade route. · **Motive** — a reason for doing something. · **Route**
— the path a journey follows. · **Compass** — a tool that shows
direction using Earth's magnetism. · **Astrolabe** — an old tool sailors
used to find their latitude from the sun and stars. · **Caravel** — a
small, fast sailing ship with lateen (triangular) sails that could sail
closer to the wind. · **Northwest Passage** — a hoped-for water route
through or around North America to Asia; it was never found in this
period. · **Claim** — a statement that a country or ruler said a land
belonged to them (a claim is not the same as agreement by the people
already living there). · **Columbian Exchange** — the large transfer of
plants, animals, goods, people, and diseases between the Americas and
Europe, Africa, and Asia after 1492, named for Christopher Columbus.
· **Consequence** — something that happens as a result of an earlier
event. · **Perspective** — a point of view shaped by a person's beliefs,
experiences, and place in the world. · **Source** — something that gives
information about the past. · **Primary source** — a source made at the
time being studied. · **Secondary source** — a later account that explains
the past using other sources. · **Fact** — something that can be checked
against evidence. · **Opinion** — what someone thinks or feels about
something. · **Evidence** — a fact, map detail, or source detail that
supports a claim.

- Voyage dates (checked 2026-10-24 against standard voyage tables and
  the Fairfax County route-map table): Columbus, sailing for Spain,
  reaches the Caribbean in **1492**; Cabot, sailing for England, reaches
  the Newfoundland coast in **1497**; Cartier, sailing for France,
  explores the Gulf of St. Lawrence and the St. Lawrence River in
  **1534–35**; de Soto, sailing for Spain, travels the Southeast and
  reaches the Mississippi River in **1541** (expedition about
  1539–1542; sources differ by a year on its end date, and the unit
  says so); Hudson, sailing for the Netherlands, explores the river
  that bears his name in **1609**. None of the five reached Asia.
- Exchange items (checked 2026-10-24 against Britannica Kids,
  "Columbian Exchange"): Americas to the Old World — maize, potatoes,
  tomatoes, beans, squash, chili peppers, peanuts, pineapple, cacao,
  tobacco, turkeys. Old World to the Americas — wheat, barley, rice,
  sugarcane, coffee, citrus, bananas; horses, cattle, pigs, sheep,
  chickens; diseases including smallpox, measles, and influenza.
  Disease death totals are **not** pinned to one number in this unit:
  historians' estimates vary widely, and the lessons say so. The
  syphilis-origin question is also unsettled and is not taught as fact.
- The two encounter passages in Lesson 04 are **original teaching
  passages written for this unit** — invented, clearly labeled, and
  never presented as real historical documents or quotations. Real
  primary sources are adult-side only: the Library of Congress holds
  Columbus's writings (see the Resource Pack), and the adult may read a
  short, pre-selected excerpt aloud. An AI-generated period scene is a
  reconstruction, never corroborating historical evidence.
