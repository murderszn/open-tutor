# Answer Key — Unit 03

**Audience:** the guiding adult only. Accept wording that keeps the
same meaning; for open responses, the "acceptable" line states what
must be present. Do not hand this key to the learner.

## Lesson 01

**G1.** Trade and wealth. A second motive on the same voyage could be
claims/power, spreading religion, or curiosity — any one, named.
**G2.** Astrolabe; it measures latitude (how far north or south) from
the sun and stars.
**G3.** Wrong: people already lived in the Americas before 1492.
Truthful rewrite (example): "In 1492 Columbus, sailing for Spain,
reached Caribbean islands where Indigenous peoples already lived."
**I1.** Four motives, each with a clue phrase — trade/wealth ("bring
back goods to sell"); claims/power ("plant a flag / claim land");
religion ("spread our faith / priests sailed too"); curiosity
("find out what lies across the ocean / better maps").
**I2.** Caravel; its lateen (triangular) sails let it sail closer to
the wind than square sails alone.
**I3.** Must contain both halves: a better map gave the next sailors
known coasts, routes, and hazards (so they could plan); it still had
to be checked because it carried the earlier maker's mistakes and
missing peoples/places.

## Lesson 02

**G1.** Timeline in order: 1492, 1497, 1534, 1541, 1609, with the
1492–1609 bracket labeled "117 years."
**G2.** Hudson: start — the Netherlands / a Dutch-funded voyage;
direction — west across the Atlantic, then up the North American
coast and into the river; stop/turn — the river that bears his name
(present-day New York); left out — the Northwest Passage was never
found, and the peoples already living along the river.
**G3.** Two errors: (1) Indigenous peoples already lived in the
Americas; (2) Columbus reached Caribbean islands in 1492, not the
mainland of North America.
**I1.** Five sought/found sentences, one pair per voyage (see the
Lesson 02 table; wording may vary but sought ≠ found).
**I2.** Closest together: Columbus 1492 and Cabot 1497 — 1497 − 1492
= 5 years. Farthest from 1492: Hudson 1609 — 1609 − 1492 = 117 years.
**I3.** Two shore-viewpoint sentences ("We see…") and two sailor
sentences ("We see…") describing the same scene: shore sentences
should notice the ship's strangeness/size and caution; sailor
sentences should notice the shore, canoes, and a possible harbor.
Both pairs must describe the same picture, not two different scenes.

## Lesson 03

**Worked example 1.** Sort: Americas → Old World: potato, tomato.
Old World → Americas: horse, wheat, smallpox, cattle. Potato trace:
Americas → Europe; potatoes grew in cool, thin soils where wheat
struggled, so in parts of Europe they became a cheap, filling food
feeding more people.
**Worked example 2.** Sugarcane chain: (1) Sugarcane grows well in
warm climates, so plantations grew in the Caribbean and beyond.
(2) Plantations needed many workers, so owners used forced labor,
including enslaved African people. (3) Sugar sold in Europe made
plantation owners and merchants wealthy, while the workers who grew it
were not paid and were not free.
**Worked-example check.** Missing rows: animals and diseases. Examples: animals —
horses, cattle, pigs; diseases — smallpox, measles, influenza
(one example per row is enough).
**G1.** Sort: Americas → Old World: maize,
peanut, squash, turkey. Old World → Americas: sheep, coffee, pig,
banana. Columns are 4 and 4.
**G2.** Horse trace in three steps, containing "over time":
horses arrived with Old World ships → spread gradually through trade
and movement → over time changed travel, hunting, and camp movement
for some Plains peoples. Reject any version saying the change happened
in a day.
**G3.** Missing: the animals row and the disease row, and the
forced-labor/inequality row — a "fair swap" claim ignores disease
deaths and forced labor.
**I1.** One crop each direction, each with direction + one probable
effect (for example: maize, Americas → Old World, became a staple in
parts of Africa and Asia; wheat, Old World → Americas, became a staple
grain in the Americas).
**I2.** Disease chain (three links) + survival sentence. Example:
Contact brought smallpox and other Old World diseases → they spread
among peoples with no prior protection → many people died and towns
were weakened. Survival sentence: Indigenous nations survived and are
living communities today. Reject any ending that says peoples vanished.
**I3.** Sugarcane chain in own words, containing "forced" and "not
free": sugarcane grew well in warm climates → plantations needed many
workers → workers were forced, including enslaved African people, who
were not free, while owners and merchants kept the profit.

## Lesson 04

**G1.** Two agreed facts (any two): ship anchored in the morning;
canoes came out; food was offered. One thing only Passage A mentions:
the bay's shape recorded for the captain, the count of twelve canoes,
or the "good harbor" judgment.
**G2.** "As guests are fed" shows hospitality/guest-care as a value:
guests are fed even when strangers are uncertain. It is a value shown
by a custom — accept "fact about the custom as described" or "an
inference from the custom" if the learner explains it; reject a bare
"opinion" with no reasoning.
**G3.** Too strong because "friendly" is only the sailor's impression
("seemed friendly") plus the act of offering food — it does not prove
intent or lasting friendliness. Weaker sentence (example): "The
sailor thought the people seemed friendly because they brought food."
**I1.** Audience: the writer's own community (present and future
members). Purpose: remember the morning and judge whether caution was
right. Each sentence must quote a clue: audience clue — "our elders"
or "we"; purpose clue — "watched… to learn what these strangers
wanted" or "right to be careful."
**I2.** (1) Fact. (2) Opinion. (3) Fact (count given in Passage A).
(4) Opinion. (5) Unknown. (6) Fact (Passage B: elders sent the canoes).
**I3.** Must contain: different places/knowledge/worries shape what
each writer notices and records; purpose shapes the account
(navigation record vs. community memory/judgment); and the word
"perspective" and the word "purpose" both used correctly.

## Lesson 05

**G1.** Claim about disease in the St. Lawrence region, frame
complete, two different evidences (example evidences: new diseases
arrived with sailors/traders; towns were weakened as many fell sick
and died). Reject two evidences that repeat one fact.
**G2.** Fails because both sentences state the same fact (France's
1534 claim). Different second evidence (examples): French traders
wanted beaver pelts; Indigenous trappers supplied pelts; disease
arrived with traders; the St. Lawrence is a great river system.
**G3.** One change + one continuation, each with a reason (examples:
changed — fur trade grew because French traders wanted pelts;
continued — Indigenous nations kept governing their homelands and
trading as partners).
**I1.** Trade claim, frame complete, two different evidences, "So"
sentence follows from them. Topic may be any region from Lessons 02–03.
**I2.** Probable cause/effect pair about the exchange + an honest
"cannot prove" completion (examples: exact death totals; strangers'
private plans; what would have happened without contact).
**I3.** Repaired claim (2–3 sentences) keeping the trade fact and
adding a harm (disease deaths or forced labor) with the conclusion
that effects were mixed/unequal. Reject any repair that still says
"only helped" or "good for everyone."

## Practice — Week 11 (A)

**A1.** Columbus 1492, Cabot 1497, Cartier 1534, de Soto 1541, Hudson 1609.
**A2.** 1534 − 1492 = 42 years.
**A3.** 1609 − 1497 = 112 years.
**A4.** Hudson; he found the Hudson River (present-day New York), not
a Northwest Passage to Asia.
**A5.** Sought: gold and riches in the Southeast. Found: the
Mississippi River (1541) after traveling through the Southeast.
**A6.** Cabot's voyage; it sought a westward route to Asia.
**A7.** Wrong: none of the five reached Asia. True sentence: "All five
sought a route to Asia, and none reached it."
**A8.** Accept any detail showing a shore viewpoint: the picture is
drawn from the shore, the ship is distant, the sailors are too far
away to see clearly, and the watchers (not the sailors) fill the
foreground.

## Practice — Week 12 (B)

**B1.** Americas → Old World: potato, tomato, maize. Old World →
Americas: cattle, wheat, horse.
**B2.** Potato: from the Americas to Europe; grew in cool, thin soils
and became a cheap, filling food feeding more people in parts of Europe.
**B3.** Missing rows: animals and diseases. Examples: horses (animals);
smallpox (diseases).
**B4.** Pig chain (three sentences, for example): Pigs arrived on Old
World ships → pigs spread quickly because they reproduce fast and live
off the land → pigs became a food source in the Americas and also
damaged some fields and food stores. Accept either effect if reasoned.
**B5.** Wrong because horses spread gradually through trade over
generations. Truthful rewrite must contain "over time" (example:
"Over time, horses spread and changed travel and hunting for some
Plains peoples.").
**B6.** One crop each direction + one effect each (see Lesson 03 I1).
**B7.** Too strong because records from the time are incomplete and
historians' estimates vary widely; no exact total can be counted from
them.
**B8.** Three sentences containing "forced" once and "not free" once
(example): "Sugarcane grew well in warm climates. Plantations used
forced labor, including enslaved African people who were not free. The
sugar sold in Europe made owners and merchants wealthy."

## Practice — Week 13 (C)

**C1.** Fact-shaped claim: twelve is a count another witness could
check. (In the invented passage, treat it as a checkable detail.)
**C2.** Opinion; the marker is "I think."
**C3.** Audience: the writer's own community. Clue phrases (any one):
"our elders," "we," or "as guests are fed."
**C4.** Agreed fact (any one): ship anchored in the morning; canoes
came out; food was offered. Only Passage B: the elders' decision, the
reef, or the "trade or take" caution.
**C5.** (1) Fact. (2) Opinion. (3) Unknown. (4) Fact.
**C6.** One passage shows only what its writer saw and thought; plans
and intentions are not visible from outside. Two sentences required.
**C7.** Too strong: caution ("right to be careful") is not proof of
danger. Weaker sentence (example): "The shore watchers were careful
because they did not know whether the strangers came to trade or to take."
**C8.** One invented shore-child sentence agreeing on one shared fact
(ship, canoes, or food) plus one new observation, labeled "invented
teaching sentence." Reject any unlabeled sentence presented as history.

## Practice — Week 14 (D)

**D1.** Fur-trade claim, frame complete, two different evidences
(Lesson 05's worked example may be adapted but not copied word for word
— accept a copy only if the learner changes at least the claim or one
evidence; note this in feedback, do not fail it).
**D2.** Probable cause: French traders wanted beaver pelts to sell in
Europe. Probable effect: a fur trade grew along the St. Lawrence with
Indigenous trappers and traders.
**D3.** Change + continuation, each with a reason (see Lesson 05 G3).
**D4.** Fails because both sentences state the same 1534 claim fact.
Different second evidence: any other St. Lawrence fact (pelts, disease,
river system, Indigenous governance).
**D5.** Repaired claim (2–3 sentences) keeping the food fact and adding
a harm (disease or forced labor), concluding effects were mixed/unequal.
**D6.** Disease cause/effect pair + honest "cannot prove" completion
(examples: exact death totals; what the death share was in one town).
**D7.** Claim Y is stronger: it gives two evidences a reader can check,
while Claim X gives an opinion with no support.
**D8.** One continuation sentence for the D1 claim (for example:
"Indigenous nations continued to govern their homelands and trade as
partners after the fur trade grew.").

## Unit assessment

**T1.** Columbus 1492, Cabot 1497, Cartier 1534, de Soto 1541, Hudson 1609.
**T2.** 1609 − 1492 = 117 years; 1534 − 1497 = 37 years.
**T3.** Cartier: sought a passage to Asia; found the Gulf of St.
Lawrence and the St. Lawrence River. Hudson: sought a Northwest
Passage to Asia; found the Hudson River.
**T4.** Compass; it points north by magnetism without sun, stars, or
land. The astrolabe needs the sun or stars, which clouds hide.
**T5.** Americas → Old World: maize, peanut, squash, turkey. Old
World → Americas: sheep, coffee, pig, banana.
**T6.** One crop each direction + one probable effect each (see B6).
**T7.** Three-link sugarcane chain with "forced" in the labor link
(see B8).
**T8.** Agreed fact (any one): morning anchoring, canoes, food.
Only Passage B: elders' decision, reef, or caution. Neither proves:
the strangers' plans (trade or take).
**T9.** Audience: the captain and future sailors. Purpose: a usable
record for finding the bay again. Must quote "so our captain could
find it again" (or the full clause).
**T10.** (a) Fact. (b) Opinion. (c) Unknown. (d) Fact.
**T11.** St. Lawrence change claim, frame complete, two different
evidences, "So" follows from them. Accept fur-trade, claims, or
disease claims if the frame holds.
**T12.** One continuation sentence (peoples, trade, or governance
continuing) + one honest uncertainty phrased as a question (examples:
"How many people died in one town?" "What did the strangers plan?").
A guess is not an uncertainty — if the learner writes a guess, ask for
a question instead.

## Project

Score with the rubric in [project.md](project.md) (12 points).
Sample claim cards for the adult's reference — a "changed" card:
Claim: "The fur trade changed the St. Lawrence region." Evidences:
(1) French traders wanted beaver pelts to sell in Europe. (2)
Indigenous trappers and traders supplied the pelts. So: "The trade
depended on both sides." A "continued" card: Claim: "Indigenous
nations continued to govern their homelands." Evidences: (1) Nations
already governed their homelands before Cartier. (2) Nations stayed
active traders and decision-makers in the fur trade. So: "Contact
changed trade without erasing governance." Learner cards need not
match these words; they must pass the frame rules.
