# Lesson 05 — Causes, Effects, and Evidence Claims

**Goal:** I can explain probable causes and effects of contact for one
region and build a claim with two pieces of evidence.
**Time:** two 35-minute sessions. **Materials:** cause/effect chain
template, claim frame cards, project exhibit draft from Week 13.

**Readiness check:** What is the difference between a cause and an
effect? (Cause: what makes something happen. Effect: what happens as a
result.) Give one cause/effect pair from your own day.

## Teaching

### Probable, not certain

Historians often write "probable cause" and "probable effect," not just
"cause" and "effect." Why? Because contact involved many causes at
once — motives, tools, diseases, trade, claims, and force — and one
effect usually had several causes. Saying "probable" is honest when
the evidence supports a link but does not prove it alone. A strong
answer names the most likely chain and admits what it cannot prove.

### One region, three chains

Pick the St. Lawrence region (Cartier's voyages) as the worked region.
Three chains, each with a cause, an effect, and a continuation:

**Chain 1 — Trade.** Cause: French sailors wanted furs, especially
beaver pelts, to sell in Europe. Effect: a fur trade grew between
French traders and Indigenous peoples along the St. Lawrence, with
Indigenous trappers and traders as essential partners. Continuation:
trade relationships shaped the region for generations, and Indigenous
nations remained active traders and decision-makers, not bystanders.

**Chain 2 — Claims and rules.** Cause: France claimed the St. Lawrence
lands for its king (Cartier's claim, 1534). Effect: French maps and
records began treating the region as French, even though Indigenous
nations already governed their homelands there. Continuation: claims
on paper and lived governance disagreed — and that disagreement is
itself a historical fact worth naming. Early claims, charters, and
trade rules were the first "rules" outsiders tried to impose; the
peoples already there had their own governments and laws.

**Chain 3 — Disease and change.** Cause: new diseases arrived with
sailors and traders. Effect: many Indigenous people in the region fell
sick and died, weakening towns and trade networks. Continuation:
nations survived, rebuilt, and persist today; disease changed the
region's population and power, but it did not erase its peoples.

Notice the pattern: every chain ends with a continuation, not an
ending. "What continued?" is as important a question as "what changed?"

### From evidence to claim

A claim is a sentence you can defend. An explanation adds reasons in
the right order. Use this frame:

> **My claim:** [one sentence about what changed or continued].
> **Evidence 1:** [a fact from a lesson, map, table, or passage].
> **Evidence 2:** [a second, different fact].
> **So:** [what the evidence together shows, in one sentence].

Rules: the two pieces of evidence must be different (not the same fact
twice), each must be a fact you can point to (not "I feel"), and the
"So" sentence must follow from the evidence, not introduce a new claim.

### Worked example 1 — Build a claim

*Question: Build a claim about the fur trade in the St. Lawrence region.*

Reasoning with the frame:

> **My claim:** The fur trade changed the St. Lawrence region, but
> Indigenous peoples stayed essential to it.
> **Evidence 1:** French traders wanted beaver pelts to sell in Europe
> (Lesson 05, Chain 1).
> **Evidence 2:** Indigenous trappers and traders supplied the pelts
> and knew the land and routes.
> **So:** The trade depended on both sides — it was not something the
> French did alone.

Check the rules: two different facts, both checkable in the lesson, and
the "So" sentence follows from them. If Evidence 2 had repeated
Evidence 1 ("the French wanted furs" twice), the claim would fail the
two-evidence rule.

### Worked example 2 — Spot a weak claim

*Question: "Contact was good for everyone because new foods arrived."
What is wrong with this claim?*

Reasoning: First, "good for everyone" is contradicted by the unit's
evidence: disease killed many Indigenous people, and forced labor
harmed enslaved and Indigenous workers. A claim that ignores the harms
is not supported. Second, "new foods" is only one row of the exchange
table. A repaired claim would be: "Contact brought new foods to both
sides, but it also brought disease and forced labor, so its effects
were mixed and unequal." A good claim survives the harms in the
evidence; a weak claim hides them.

## Guided practice

**G1.** Copy the frame and build a claim about disease in the
St. Lawrence region, using Chain 3. Your two evidences must be
different facts.
*Hint: one evidence can be the arrival of disease, the other the
effect on towns — but not the same sentence twice.*

**G2.** Error-analysis: "A friend's claim uses two evidences: (1)
Cartier sailed in 1534, (2) Cartier sailed in 1534–35." Why does this
fail the two-evidence rule? Rewrite evidence 2 as a genuinely different
fact about the St. Lawrence region.
*Hint: same voyage, same fact — count facts, not sentences.*

**G3.** Name one thing that changed and one thing that continued in
the St. Lawrence region after contact. Each answer must be one
sentence with a reason.
*Hint: use Chains 1–3.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Build a claim about trade (not disease) for a region of your
choice from Lessons 02–03, using the frame with two different
evidences.

**I2.** Write a "probable cause / probable effect" pair for the
Columbian Exchange in your own words, and add one sentence beginning
"What we cannot prove from this evidence alone is…".

**I3.** Repair this weak claim: "Exploration only helped people
because it created trade." Write the repaired claim (2–3 sentences)
that keeps the trade fact but adds a harm from the unit's evidence.

## Applied task — Claim cards for the exhibit

Make two claim cards for your project exhibit (see [project](../project.md)):
one claim about what changed, one claim about what continued. Each
card follows the frame (claim + two evidences + "So"). Trade cards
with the adult: the adult checks the two-evidence rule on each card
before the card goes into the exhibit.

## Exit check

State one claim about contact in this unit, give two different pieces
of evidence, and say what continued after contact. The adult checks
the two-evidence rule and the continuation sentence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the three chains stay visible; the claim frame
  is printed on a card the learner keeps.
- **Alternate response mode:** build claims orally with the adult
  scribing; use picture evidences (map arrow, item card) as Evidence 1
  or 2, labeled in words by the adult.
- **Extension:** build a counter-claim: a claim a different historian
  might defend with different evidence from the same lessons, and
  explain in two sentences why both claims cannot be fully right at
  the same time — or why they can both be partly right.

## Teacher note

Watch for: claims that hide harms; two "evidences" that are one fact
twice; claims that end Indigenous history at contact. The continuation
sentence is required on every claim in this lesson and in the project.
Answers: see [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05).
