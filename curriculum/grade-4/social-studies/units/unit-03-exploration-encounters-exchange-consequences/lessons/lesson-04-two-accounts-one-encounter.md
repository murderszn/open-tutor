# Lesson 04 — Two Accounts, One Encounter

**Goal:** I can compare two accounts of the same encounter, infer each
writer's audience and purpose, and sort fact from opinion.
**Time:** two 35-minute sessions. **Materials:** the two teaching
passages below (read aloud by the adult), a two-column comparison chart.

**Readiness check:** What is the difference between a fact and an
opinion? (Fact: can be checked against evidence. Opinion: what someone
thinks or feels.) Give one example of each about your school lunch.

> **Important label — read this first.** The two passages below are
> **original teaching passages written for this lesson**. They are
> invented to show how viewpoint shapes an account. They are **not**
> real historical documents, **not** quotations from any explorer or
> Indigenous person, and must never be cited as evidence of what any
> real person said. Real primary sources are adult-side only (see the
> Resource Pack: the Library of Congress holds Columbus's writings).

## Teaching

### The two passages — one morning, two viewpoints

**Passage A — From a sailor's log (teaching passage — invented).**

> "On the morning we anchored, the water was calm and the shore was
> green with trees. People came out in canoes to meet us, bringing food
> and bright feathers. I wrote down the shape of the bay so our captain
> could find it again, and I counted the canoes: twelve. The people
> seemed friendly, and I believe this bay will make a good harbor for
> our ships."

**Passage B — From the shore (teaching passage — invented).**

> "At dawn a great ship with white wings stopped beyond the reef. We
> had never seen a vessel so large, and we did not know whether its
> people came to trade or to take. Our elders sent canoes with food, as
> guests are fed, and watched from the shore to learn what these
> strangers wanted. I think we were right to be careful, because a ship
> that large does not come for nothing."

Both passages describe the same morning: a ship anchored, canoes went
out, food was offered. Everything else — what each writer notices,
what each writer fears or hopes, what each writer counts — differs,
because each writer stands in a different place with different
knowledge and different worries.

### What agrees, what differs

| | Passage A (sailor) | Passage B (shore) |
|---|---|---|
| Agrees | Ship anchored in the morning; canoes came out; food was offered | Same three facts |
| Notices | The bay's shape, the number of canoes (twelve), a future harbor | The ship's size, the reef, the elders' decision, caution |
| Feels | Hopeful: "friendly," "good harbor" | Careful: "trade or take," "right to be careful" |
| Purpose (inferred) | Help the captain return; report a useful harbor | Remember and judge the strangers; warn the community |

### Audience and purpose — clues inside the text

You do not need to be told who a writer wrote for. Clues inside the
text tell you:

- **"So our captain could find it again"** → the audience includes
  the captain and future sailors; the purpose is practical: a usable
  record.
- **"Our elders sent canoes… as guests are fed"** → the audience is
  the writer's own community, present and future; the purpose is
  memory and judgment: what happened, and were we right to be careful?
- **"I believe" / "I think"** → opinion markers. They tell you the
  writer is judging, not just recording.
- **"Twelve canoes" / "the water was calm"** → checkable details.
  Another witness could confirm or deny them. Those are fact-shaped
  claims, even in an invented passage.

A writer's perspective shapes what they record: the sailor counts
canoes and maps the bay because his job is navigation; the shore
writer watches the elders because her job is her community's safety.
Neither writer is lying — but neither writer sees the whole morning.

### Fact vs. opinion in encounter texts

- **Fact:** "The ship anchored in the morning." (Checkable: another
  account or a log could confirm the time.)
- **Fact:** "Twelve canoes came out." (Countable — in the invented
  passage, and in real history, counts are the most checkable details.)
- **Opinion:** "This bay will make a good harbor." (A judgment about
  the future.)
- **Opinion:** "We were right to be careful." (A judgment about a
  decision.)
- **Neither proven by one passage alone:** "The strangers came to
  trade" or "came to take." Both passages only show what the writers
  *thought* — the strangers' real plans are not proven by either one.
  A good historian writes "we do not know yet" rather than guessing.

### Worked example 1 — Infer audience and purpose

*Question: Passage A says, "I wrote down the shape of the bay so our
captain could find it again." Who is the audience, and what is the
purpose?*

Reasoning: The clue "so our captain could find it again" names the
audience (the captain, and sailors who follow) and the purpose (a
record useful for returning). You inferred both from words inside the
passage — you did not need outside information. That is what
D2.His.11 asks for: infer from the source itself.

### Worked example 2 — Agree, differ, or unknown?

*Question: Sort these three claims: (a) canoes went out with food;
(b) the strangers came to trade; (c) the bay is a good harbor.*

Reasoning: (a) Both passages agree, so it is the strongest claim in
this pair — agreed by two viewpoints. (b) Unknown: Passage B only
wonders "trade or take," and Passage A does not say. Do not upgrade a
wonder into a fact. (c) Opinion: "good" is a judgment, and it comes
only from the sailor who wants a harbor. Agree / differ / unknown is a
better sorting box than true / false for encounter sources.

## Guided practice

**G1.** List two facts both passages agree on, and one thing only
Passage A mentions. Use the table above, then check your answers
against the passages.
*Hint: start with the morning itself.*

**G2.** Passage B says, "as guests are fed." What does that clue tell
you about the shore community's values? Is that a fact or an opinion
about their values? Explain in one sentence.
*Hint: values show in customs.*

**G3.** Error-analysis: "A friend says Passage A proves the strangers
were friendly." Why is that too strong? What weaker, truer sentence
could replace it?
*Hint: who called them friendly, and what did they actually do?*

## Independent practice

**I1.** For Passage B, infer audience and purpose in two sentences,
quoting one clue phrase for each (audience clue, purpose clue).

**I2.** Sort these six statements into Fact / Opinion / Unknown
(using only the two passages): (1) The ship anchored in the morning.
(2) The strangers were friendly people. (3) Twelve canoes came out.
(4) The bay will make a good harbor. (5) The strangers came to take
things. (6) The elders sent the canoes.
*Hint: three are facts, two are opinions, one is unknown — count as you go.*

**I3.** Write 3–4 sentences explaining why two honest writers can
describe the same morning differently. Use the words "perspective"
and "purpose."

## Applied task — Source-comparison investigation

Take one fact from Lesson 02 (for example, "Cartier sailed up the
St. Lawrence in 1534") and imagine two short accounts of it: one from a
sailor, one from a person living along the river. Write two sentences
for each account (clearly labeled "invented teaching sentences"), then
a comparison row: one agreement, one difference, and one thing neither
account proves. Label your work "invented — not a historical document"
at the top.

## Exit check

Answer without looking: what is one clue in Passage A that shows its
purpose, and what is one opinion in Passage B? The adult checks that
the clue is quoted from inside the passage and that the opinion is a
judgment, not a checkable fact.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads both passages aloud twice;
  opinion markers ("I believe," "I think") are underlined together
  before any sorting.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort I2 statements as cards into
  three labeled boxes; dictate I3 to the adult.
- **Extension:** with the adult, read one short, pre-selected excerpt
  from a real primary source (Library of Congress, Columbus's
  writings — see the Resource Pack) and list only what it proves and
  what it does not prove. Do not copy the excerpt into learner work.

## Teacher note

The most common error is upgrading one writer's hope or fear into a
fact ("friendly," "came to take"). Keep returning to the question:
"Who says so, and what did they actually see?" Answers: see
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
