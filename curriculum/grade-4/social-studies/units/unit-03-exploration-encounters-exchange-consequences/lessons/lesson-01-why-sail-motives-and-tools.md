# Lesson 01 — Why Sail? Motives and Tools

**Goal:** I can explain why Europeans sailed across the Atlantic in the
1400s–1600s and how new tools made long ocean voyages possible.
**Time:** two 35-minute sessions. **Materials:** world map or globe,
paper timeline strip, compass (real or classroom model).

**Readiness check (adult-led, 3 minutes):** Point to Europe and to North
America on the map. Which ocean lies between them? (The Atlantic.) What
did sailors need before they could cross it and come back alive?

## Teaching

### The land was already home to someone

Before any European ship crossed the Atlantic, millions of people already
lived in the Americas, in nations with governments, farms, towns, trade
networks, and histories of their own. When this unit says "encounter,"
it means a meeting between peoples. It does **not** mean the land was
empty, and it does not mean anyone "discovered" a place that already had
people living in it. Keep that sentence in mind for every voyage in this
unit: the sailors were strangers arriving, not finders of an empty land.

### Why sail? Four motives

European rulers and merchants in the 1400s wanted goods from Asia —
spices to flavor and preserve food, silk cloth, and other goods that
could be sold for a high price. The overland routes to Asia were long,
slow, and controlled by other traders, so a sea route looked like a
shortcut to wealth. That is motive one: **trade and wealth**.

Motive two: **claims and power**. A king or queen who paid for a voyage
could claim new lands and trading posts for their country, which made
their country look stronger than its rivals. Motive three: **spreading
religion** — many voyages carried priests as well as sailors, and rulers
said they wanted more people to follow their faith. Motive four:
**curiosity and knowledge** — better maps, stories of distant lands, and
a wish to know what lay across the ocean.

No single motive explains every voyage. A good historian asks, for each
voyage, "Which motives mattered most *here*?" — and checks the answer
against what the expedition actually did.

### Tools that made it possible

Three tools matter most at this grade:

- **Compass** — a needle that points north, so sailors could hold a
  direction even when land was out of sight and clouds hid the sun.
- **Astrolabe** — a round instrument sailors used with the sun and
  stars to work out their latitude, roughly how far north or south they
  were.
- **Caravel** — a small, fast ship with lateen (triangular) sails. A
  lateen sail lets a ship sail closer to the wind than a square sail
  alone, which matters when you must return home against the wind.

Better maps mattered too. Each voyage's map became the next voyage's
starting point — and each map also carried the mistakes of the voyage
before it. A map is evidence, not truth: it shows what its maker knew
and believed.

### Worked example 1 — Name the motive

*Question: A queen pays for a voyage and tells the captain, "Bring back
spices we can sell, and plant my flag where you land." Which motives are
those?*

Reasoning: "Bring back spices we can sell" is trade and wealth — the
voyage is meant to make money. "Plant my flag where you land" is a claim
for power — the queen wants the land counted as hers. One voyage, two
motives. A careful answer names both, not just one.

### Worked example 2 — Name the tool

*Question: A ship is far from land on a cloudy night. The captain still
knows which way is north. Which tool helps, and why?*

Reasoning: The compass. It does not need the sun, the stars, or land —
its needle points north by magnetism. The astrolabe would not help on a
cloudy night because it needs the sun or stars. Matching the tool to the
problem is the skill: compass for direction, astrolabe for latitude,
caravel for sailing against the wind.

## Guided practice (adult reads aloud if needed)

**G1.** A merchant says, "If I find a sea route to Asia, I can buy
spices cheaper and sell them for more." Which motive is that? Name one
other motive that could be present on the same voyage.
*Hint: follow the money.*

**G2.** A captain wants to know how far north his ship is. He waits for
a clear night and uses a round brass instrument. Which tool is it, and
what does it measure?
*Hint: it uses the stars.*

**G3.** Error-analysis: "A friend says explorers discovered America
because nobody lived there before 1492." What is wrong with that
sentence? Rewrite it truthfully in one sentence.
*Hint: who was already home?*

## Independent practice

**I1.** List the four motives in your own words. Beside each, write one
clue phrase a historian could look for (for example, beside trade:
"bring back goods to sell").

**I2.** A ship must sail home against the wind. Which ship design helps
most, and what is special about its sails?

**I3.** Explain in 2–3 sentences why a better map from one voyage helped
the next voyage — and why the next sailors still had to be careful
about trusting that map.

## Applied task

Make the first section of your timeline strip (you will finish it in
Lesson 02): mark 1492 and write one sentence — "Before 1492, Indigenous
nations already lived across the Americas." Draw a small house-and-field
symbol above the strip for "peoples already home." This section stays on
every timeline you make in this unit.

## Exit check

Say or write: one motive for sailing, one tool, and one reason the word
"discovery" is misleading for 1492. The adult checks that the answer
names a real motive, a real tool, and the peoples already living here.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the teaching section aloud;
  key words (motive, compass, astrolabe, caravel) stay on a word wall.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer G1–G3 and the exit check orally
  or by pointing to labeled tool pictures; the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** research one more tool (the quadrant or the
  cross-staff) and explain in two sentences what problem it solved.
  Do not turn the extension into a required fact for the quiz.

## Teacher note

Misconception to catch early: "explorers discovered empty land" and
"one motive explains everything." Both return in Lessons 02 and 04.
