# Lesson 03 — The Columbian Exchange

**Goal:** I can trace what moved in each direction after 1492 and
explain probable effects on both sides of the Atlantic.
**Time:** two 35-minute sessions. **Materials:** two-column exchange
chart (blank), item cards (maize, potato, tomato, horse, wheat,
sugarcane, smallpox, and others from the table below).

**Readiness check:** Name one food you ate this week that comes from a
plant first grown in the Americas (maize/corn, potato, tomato, beans,
squash, chili pepper, peanut, pineapple, or cacao/chocolate are all
correct). Foods travel — this lesson is about the biggest food journey
in history.

## Teaching

### What the name means

The **Columbian Exchange** is the large transfer of plants, animals,
goods, people, and diseases between the Americas and Europe, Africa,
and Asia after 1492. It is named for Christopher Columbus, whose voyages
first linked the two sides of the Atlantic by regular sailing. It was
not a fair trade between equals: the two sides did not choose equally,
and the harms and benefits did not fall equally. "Exchange" is the
historian's word for the movement, not a claim that it was fair.

### What moved each way

| Americas → Old World (Europe, Africa, Asia) | Old World → Americas |
|---|---|
| Maize (corn), potatoes, tomatoes, beans, squash, chili peppers, peanuts, pineapple, cacao, tobacco | Wheat, barley, rice, sugarcane, coffee, citrus fruits, bananas |
| Turkeys | Horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, chickens |
| (People, ideas, and knowledge also moved — see below) | Diseases: smallpox, measles, influenza |

Read the table in both directions. A common error is to list only foods
and forget the other two rows: animals and diseases changed lives as
much as any crop.

### Trace one item: maize to Europe

Maize was grown in the Americas for thousands of years before 1492.
After contact, maize seeds traveled to Europe and then to Africa and
Asia, where maize grew well in places wheat grew poorly. More food
meant more people could be fed. That is a probable effect, not a
guarantee for every place: maize helped where the soil and rain suited
it, and it did not help where they did not.

### Trace one item: horses to the Plains

Horses came from the Old World to the Americas. Over time — not
overnight — horses spread through trade and movement, and some Plains
peoples became skilled horse peoples: horses changed how far people
could travel, how they hunted, and how they moved camp. The key phrase
is "over time": a horse arriving on a ship in 1493 did not instantly
change life a thousand miles inland. Change traveled slowly, along
trade routes and generations.

### The hardest row: disease

Old World diseases such as smallpox, measles, and influenza spread
among Indigenous peoples who had never encountered them before, so
their bodies had no prior protection against them. Many Indigenous
people died — whole towns and families were weakened or lost. This is
the most serious consequence in the unit, and the adult leads this part
slowly. Two cautions:

1. **Do not pin one number on the deaths.** Historians' estimates
   vary widely, because records from the time are incomplete. This unit
   says "many people died" and "historians disagree about the exact
   share" — that is the honest answer at this grade.
2. **Do not write peoples out of the story.** Indigenous nations
   survived these epidemics, adapted, and are living communities today.
   A sentence that ends with "and then they were gone" is wrong.

### The hardest row, continued: forced labor

Sugarcane and other plantation crops needed many workers. In the
Americas after contact, that labor was often forced: Indigenous people
were forced to work, and enslaved African people were brought across
the Atlantic in large numbers and forced to work, in cruel conditions,
for the profit of others. Say it plainly: **forced labor is not a job,
and enslaved people did not choose it.** The wealth from sugar and
other crops in Europe was built on this forced labor. That connection —
crop, labor, profit — is one of the unit's central cause-and-effect
chains.

### Worked example 1 — Sort and trace

*Question: Sort these six items into the two directions, then trace one
item's effect: potato, horse, wheat, tomato, smallpox, cattle.*

Reasoning: Americas → Old World: potato, tomato. Old World →
Americas: horse, wheat, smallpox, cattle. Trace the potato: potatoes
grew in cool, thin soils where wheat struggled, so in parts of Europe
(especially Ireland and northern Europe) potatoes became a cheap, filling
food that helped feed more people. One item, one direction, one
probable effect — that is a complete trace.

### Worked example 2 — Consequence chain

*Question: "Sugarcane traveled to the Americas. So what?" Build a chain
of three links.*

Reasoning: Link 1 — sugarcane grows well in warm climates, so
plantations grew in the Caribbean and later Brazil and the southern
mainland. Link 2 — plantations needed many workers, so owners used
forced labor, including enslaved African people. Link 3 — sugar sold in
Europe made plantation owners and merchants wealthy, while the workers
who grew it were not paid and were not free. A chain must show *how*
each link leads to the next; "sugarcane caused wealth" alone skips the
labor link and hides the harm.

## Guided practice

**G1.** Sort these eight items into the two columns: maize, sheep,
peanut, coffee, pig, squash, banana, turkey. Which column has more
items in your sort? Count and check.
*Hint: four go each way — if your columns are 5 and 3, recheck one item.*

**G2.** Trace the horse: write three steps showing how horses changed
life over time (arrival → spread → new travel/hunting). Use the phrase
"over time" in your answer.
*Hint: do not say the change happened in one day.*

**G3.** Error-analysis: "A friend says the Columbian Exchange was a
fair swap because both sides got new foods." What is missing from that
claim? Name two missing rows from the table.
*Hint: foods are only one row.*

## Independent practice

**I1.** Choose one crop from each direction. For each, write: the item,
its direction, and one probable effect, in one sentence each.

**I2.** Write a consequence chain for disease with three links:
contact → spread → effect on a community. End your chain with a true
sentence about survival today (peoples survived and are living
communities now).

**I3.** In 3–4 sentences, explain the sugarcane–forced-labor–profit
chain in your own words. Use the words "forced" and "not free" correctly.

## Applied task — Consequence chains investigation

Pick one exchange item that is not maize, horse, or sugarcane (for
example, potato, tomato, wheat, or pig). Draw a three-link chain:
item → where it went → one probable effect, with a reason for each
arrow. Then add a second chain for the same item if it had a different
effect somewhere else (for example, a crop that helped in one place and
caused trouble in another). Present both chains to the adult.

## Exit check

Name two items that moved each way (four total) and one effect of the
exchange on each side of the Atlantic. The adult checks directions and
that one effect names a harm, not only a benefit.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the exchange table stays visible; item cards
  may be sorted physically before writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort cards aloud, dictate chains to
  the adult, or draw the chain as pictures with labels.
- **Extension:** investigate one more item (for example, cacao/
  chocolate or chili peppers) and trace it in both directions — where
  it grew first, where it traveled, and one effect. Note any claim you
  cannot verify as "not checked yet."

## Teacher note

Misconceptions: "exchange = fair trade"; "horses changed everything
instantly"; pinning one death percentage; ending Indigenous history at
contact. The adult leads the disease and forced-labor sections and
checks that every chain ends with survival/continuity, not erasure.
