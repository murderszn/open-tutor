# Answer Key — Unit 03: Informational Text (ADULT ONLY)

Do not hand this file to the learner. Every item in the lessons,
practice sets, quiz, and unit assessment is solved here. Open responses
accept any wording that carries the same meaning and the same evidence;
the samples below show the meaning and evidence required.

## Lesson 01 — Main idea and key details

1. Sample: "Beavers build dams and lodges to make a safe, warm home."
   Accept any one-sentence version covering beavers + dam + lodge +
   safe/warm home.
2. Dam detail: the dam is a wall of sticks and mud that slows the water
   and makes a deep pond. Evidence: "This wall of sticks and mud is
   called a dam. The dam slows the water and makes a deep pond."
3. Lodge detail: the lodge is a dome-shaped home of sticks and mud with
   a room above the water and a door under the water. Evidence: "A
   lodge is a dome-shaped home made of sticks and mud" and "The door of
   the lodge is under the water."
4. The underwater door lets the family swim in and out safely; the pond
   protects them from animals that might hunt them. Evidence: "so the
   family can swim in and out safely" and "The pond protects them from
   animals that might hunt them."
5. (b) is best. (a) fails the too-small test: teeth appear in one
   sentence only. (c) fails the too-big test: the passage is about
   beavers and their homes, not animals in general.
6. Sample question: "What is a dam made of?" Answer: sticks and mud,
   packed with mud between them. Evidence: "They pile the sticks across
   the water and pack mud between them."
7. Sample: "In a hive, different bees do different jobs that help the
   whole hive survive."
8. Any two, with quotes: queen lays eggs ("The queen bee lays eggs");
   workers gather nectar ("Worker bees gather nectar from flowers");
   workers build cells ("build wax rooms called cells"); workers feed
   babies ("feed the baby bees"); guards guard the door ("Guard bees
   stand at the hive door and keep out intruders").
9. Sam is wrong by the whole-passage test: teeth appear in one sentence
   ("cut down small trees with their strong teeth") and do not fit
   paragraphs 3–4 about the lodge and why beavers build. A main idea
   must fit the whole passage.

## Lesson 02 — Sequence

1. In order: (1) farmer plants a seed in spring; (2) seed sprouts, root
   down, stem up; (3) vine grows, flowers bloom, bees visit; (4) tiny
   green pumpkin grows bigger all summer; (5) pumpkin turns orange in
   fall and the farmer picks it.
2. "Then" (for the vine step; "After that" marks the pumpkin-growing
   step).
3. No. The pumpkin grows where a flower was, and it turns orange only in
   fall after growing all summer. Evidence: "After that, a tiny green
   pumpkin begins to grow" and "Finally, in fall, the pumpkin turns
   orange. The farmer cuts it from the vine."
4. Sample retelling: "First, the farmer plants a seed in spring. Next,
   the seed sprouts. Then a vine grows and flowers bloom. After that, a
   tiny green pumpkin grows. Finally, in fall, the pumpkin turns orange
   and the farmer picks it." Any retelling with the five steps in order
   and at least three sequence words earns full credit.
5. In order: (c) take out bread and cheese; (b) put cheese between
   slices; (d) put the sandwich on a plate; (a) eat the sandwich.
6. Sample: "A pumpkin grows from a seed through steps from planting in
   spring to picking in fall." Supporting steps: planting the seed;
   the pumpkin turning orange and being picked (any two steps stated as
   key details earn credit).
7. In order: (1) wet hands; (2) rub soap all over, front and back, about
   twenty seconds; (3) rinse; (4) dry with a clean towel.
8. Sample: "First, wet your hands. Next, rub soap all over for about
   twenty seconds. Then rinse the soap off. Finally, dry your hands."
9. "Plant" is out of place: Maya put sprouting first, but the passage
   says "First, in spring, the farmer plants a pumpkin seed." Nothing
   sprouts before it is planted; the correct order starts with planting.

## Lesson 03 — Cause/effect and comparison

1. Cause: less sunlight in fall (shorter days, cooler air). Signal word:
   "Because" — "Because there is less sunlight, trees stop making
   chlorophyll."
2. Sample: "When the green fades, the yellow and orange colors that were
   already in the leaf show through, so the trees look bright and
   colorful." (Either "when" or "as a result" earns the signal-word
   credit.)
3. No. The passage says leaves dry out and then fall; the color change
   reveals colors but does not cause falling. Order is not cause.
4. Alike: both carry people and goods from one place to another.
   Evidence: "Both wagons and cars carry people and goods from one
   place to another."
5. Any two with features: speed — a wagon trip took a whole day, a car
   trip less than an hour; power — horses pull the wagon, fuel powers
   the car; needs on the way — horses need food, water, rest; a car
   needs fuel and a driver following road rules.
6. Comparison connection: it compares wagons and cars on the feature of
   speed. It is not sequence (no steps) and not cause/effect (no
   why/what-happened pair).
7. Two valid chains: (a) cause: it rained all night; effect: the river
   rose ("so the river rose"). (b) Cause: the river rose; effect: the
   road was closed and the bus took a different route ("Because the
   river rose, the road beside it was closed").
8. Sample: alike — "Both a bicycle and a bus carry people from one place
   to another" (feature: purpose). Different — "A bicycle is powered by
   pedaling, while a bus is powered by fuel" (feature: power). Any
   feature-named pair earns credit.
9. Dan is wrong: the passage never says color change causes falling; it
   says leaves dry out and fall. A better-supported pair: less sunlight
   causes trees to stop making chlorophyll; the green fading causes
   yellow and orange to show.

## Lesson 05 — Illustrations and point of view

1. The dam is the long wall of sticks stretching across the stream in
   the foreground, from bank to bank. The lodge is the dome of sticks
   rising from the water in the middle of the pond. They are two
   different structures.
2. Any one: the sticks interlock and crisscross; the dam spans bank to
   bank; the lodge sits in the middle of open water, away from the
   banks; the beaver carries a stick while swimming.
3. Any one: the lodge has a dry room inside; the lodge door is under
   the water — neither can be seen from outside in the picture.
4. The author thinks beavers are remarkable builders who deserve
   admiration. Evidence (any two): "the most remarkable builders in the
   animal world"; "a clever piece of work"; "amazingly"; "Even more
   impressive"; "I think beavers deserve our admiration."
5. Sample: "The author thinks beavers are remarkable builders." / "I
   think beavers are interesting, but I am not sure about the trees
   they cut down." Any pair that keeps the two views separate earns
   credit, whether they agree or not.
6. "The lodge has a door under the water" is a fact — it can be checked
   against the passage's description. "Beavers are remarkable" is an
   opinion — "remarkable" is a judgment word, not a checkable fact.
7. Sample: "The dam is in the foreground, stretching across the water
   from bank to bank." / "The lodge is in the middle of the pond."
8. Any one not in Modeled example 2's quotes: "clever," "amazingly,"
   "Even more impressive," "deserve our admiration" — with its sentence
   copied. (Modeled example 2 quoted "most remarkable builders" and
   "I think beavers deserve our admiration"; other opinion words earn
   credit.)
9. Kim reported an opinion as a fact. Corrected: "The author thinks
   beavers are the most remarkable builders in the animal world." The
   word "remarkable" is the author's judgment, not a fact the passage
   proves.

## Lesson 06 — Comparing two texts

1. Text A sample: "In a bee colony, every bee has a job that helps the
   whole hive survive." Text B sample: "A beekeeper cares for a hive
   and takes some honey while leaving plenty for the bees."
2. Sample shared point: the colony's survival matters and depends on
   care/work. Text A: "helps the whole hive survive." Text B: "makes
   sure the colony is healthy." (Also acceptable: both texts are about
   bees living in hives, with honey as a shared topic — but a shared
   *point* with quotes earns full credit; a shared *topic* alone earns
   partial credit.)
3. Only Text A: the dance that tells other workers where flowers are
   (also: queen lays eggs; workers build cells; guards guard the door).
   Only Text B: the beekeeper's veil and gloves; checking for the queen
   and honey stores; taking honey in late summer.
4. "Bees live and work together in a colony" is a most important point:
   remove it and both texts' main ideas collapse. The dance is a detail
   inside Text A — interesting, but Text A's main idea survives without
   it.
5. No, they do not disagree. They cover different parts of one topic:
   Text A covers the bees' jobs; Text B covers the beekeeper's care.
   No claim in one contradicts a claim in the other.
6. Sample: "Both texts say bees live in hives and their survival depends
   on work and care. Text A also says workers dance to show where
   flowers are. Text B also says the beekeeper takes some honey in late
   summer and leaves plenty for the bees."
7. Sample: the beekeeper's most important job is checking that the
   colony is healthy and has enough honey stored. It connects to Text
   A's main idea because both serve the same goal: keeping the whole
   hive alive.
8. Venn chart: Text A only — dance, queen lays eggs, guards guard the
   door (any two). Both — bees live in a hive/colony, honey, colony
   survival matters (any two). Text B only — veil and gloves, honey
   harvest in late summer, checking honey stores (any two).
9. Lee is wrong: Text B being about a person does not make it disagree
   with Text A. Both texts are about honeybees and hives; Text A
   describes the bees' jobs and Text B describes the beekeeper's care
   for the same colony. Correct restatement: the texts complement each
   other — same topic, different coverage.

## Practice

### Practice A

- A1. Sample: "Students grow vegetables in the school garden and eat
  them for lunch."
- A2. Any two with quotes: students grow tomatoes, carrots, and
  lettuce ("students grow tomatoes, carrots, and lettuce"); each class
  waters and weeds ("Each class takes a turn watering the plants and
  pulling weeds"); the cafeteria cooks the vegetables for lunch ("the
  cafeteria cooks the vegetables for lunch").
- A3. (a) is best. (b) fails the too-small test (one vegetable's color).
  (c) fails the too-big/off-topic test (the passage is about the
  garden, not playgrounds).
- A4. Sample: "Who waters the plants?" Answer: each class, in turn.
  Evidence: "Each class takes a turn watering the plants and pulling
  weeds."
- A5. Watering is one step/detail, not what the whole passage is about.
  The passage covers growing, tending, picking, cooking, and eating —
  watering alone fails the whole-passage test.

### Practice B

- B1. Order: 1 — (e) dig a small hole; 2 — (b) put the seed in the soil;
  3 — (d) cover the seed with soil; 4 — (a) water the soil; 5 — (c)
  watch the sprout push up.
- B2. Sample: "First, dig a hole. Next, put the seed in. Then cover it
  with soil. After that, water it. Finally, watch the sprout push up."
- B3. No — watering before the seed is in the soil waters empty ground;
  the seed must be placed and covered first for the watering to help it
  grow. (The swap breaks the process's sense, even though water on soil
  is physically possible.)
- B4. 1 — fold back and forth; 2 — pinch one end and tape it; 3 —
  spread the other end open.
- B5. The fan would not hold its shape: without the pinched, taped end
  there is nothing to hold the folds together, so the paper falls open
  flat instead of fanning.

### Practice F

- F1. Text C sample: "Rain in the city runs off into drains to rivers,
  washing streets and watering park trees." Text D sample: "Rain on the
  farm soaks into fields, giving crops water, though too much rain can
  flood a field."
- F2. Shared point: rain gives water to plants/trees. Text C: "waters
  the park trees." Text D: "it gives the plants the water they need."
- F3. Only Text C: storm drains carry water to rivers; rain washes dust
  off streets. Only Text D: too much rain can flood a field and hurt
  the crop.
- F4. No. Both say rain helps; Text D adds a caution (flooding) that
  Text C does not mention, but a caution is not a contradiction. The
  texts cover different places, not opposing claims.
- F5. Full credit for either argued answer: (a) "Rain gives plants the
  water they need" is the shared most important point — both texts rest
  on it; dust-washing is a city-only detail. (b) Both matter, one per
  text — dust-washing is Text C's main benefit, plant-watering is Text
  D's. The required element is the "fall apart without it" reasoning,
  not which side is picked.

## Unit assessment

- A1. Sample: "Rain water runs downhill through streams and rivers to a
  lake or the sea, helping plants and animals but sometimes causing
  floods." Any one sentence covering the journey + help + flood risk
  earns full credit; a journey-only sentence earns partial credit.
- A2. Any two with quotes: rain falls on hills and fields ("rain falls
  on hills and fields"); streams join into a bigger river ("the streams
  join together into a bigger river"); rain gives plants water ("It
  gives plants the water they need to grow"); rain fills ponds and
  lakes ("It also fills ponds and lakes where animals drink").
- A3. In order: first, rain falls on hills and fields; next, water runs
  downhill in small streams; then streams join into a bigger river;
  finally, the river carries the water to a lake or the sea.
- A4. Cause: too much rain at once; effect: flood — the river rises too
  high and spills over its banks onto nearby fields. Signal: "can
  cause" ("too much rain at once can cause a flood") or "When" ("When a
  river rises too high, the water spills over its banks").
- A5. Both: good — rain gives plants water to grow and fills ponds and
  lakes where animals drink; bad — too much rain floods fields.
  Evidence: "It gives plants the water they need to grow" and "the
  water spills over its banks and covers nearby fields."
- B1. The caption adds that in spring, melting snow makes the river run
  fast and high — the heading alone says only that the section is about
  the river in spring, not why it runs fast.
- B2. The author thinks rivers are wonderful and every town should have
  a river park. Evidence (any two): "the most wonderful places on
  Earth"; "Nothing beats sitting beside a river"; "every town should
  have a river park."
- B3. Sample: "The author thinks rivers are the most wonderful places on
  Earth." / "I think rivers are nice, but I like lakes better." Any pair
  keeping the views separate earns credit.
- C1. Text I sample: "Some seeds, like dandelion seeds, travel by wind

  like burrs, travel by hitching a ride on animals to new places where
  new plants can grow."
- C2. Shared: seeds travel to new places so new plants can grow. Text I:
  "where it lands, a new dandelion can grow." Text J: "where it lands,

  tiny parachute. Only Text J: tiny hooks that catch on an animal's fur.

  important point: remove it and both texts' main ideas collapse. The

- D1. Score with the assessment guide: main idea as first sentence (2);

  support its main idea loses the detail points even if it is
  well-written.
