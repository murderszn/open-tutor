# Lesson 05 — Illustrations and the Author's Point of View

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can learn from pictures as well as words,
and I can tell what the author thinks apart from what I think."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the passage and illustration
below; a pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RI.3.7 (illustrations + words), RI.3.6 (point of view).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult shows the illustration below (or reads the text-only
alternative) and asks: "What do you see?" The learner lists what the
picture shows. The adult says: "A picture in informational text is not a
decoration. It carries information — sometimes information the words
never say. Today you will find what the picture adds, and you will also
learn to spot what the *author* thinks, which is not always what *you*
think."

## Passage — "Beavers Are Remarkable Builders" (original, written for this unit)

Beavers are the most remarkable builders in the animal world. No other
animal changes its home landscape the way a beaver does.

A beaver dam is a clever piece of work. The beaver piles sticks across a
stream, packs mud between them, and — amazingly — the wall holds back the
water and makes a deep, safe pond.

Even more impressive is the lodge. The beaver builds a dome of sticks in
the middle of the pond, with a dry room inside and a secret door under
the water. I think beavers deserve our admiration: they build a home, a
pond, and a safe place for their family, all with teeth, sticks, and mud.

## Explicit teaching

**Illustrations teach too (RI.3.7).** Photographs, diagrams, and maps
show where, when, why, and how things happen. When you read informational
text, always ask: "What does the picture show that the words do not?"
and "What do the words say that the picture cannot show?" Understanding
comes from *both* together. Two common traps: (1) treating the picture as
decoration and skipping it; (2) treating the picture as proof of
something it does not show — a picture of one beaver does not prove all
beavers build the same way.

**Point of view (RI.3.6).** A **point of view** is what someone thinks
about a topic. The **author's point of view** is what the writer thinks;
*your* point of view is what you think. They can agree or differ, and
differing is fine — but you must not mix them up. Clues to an author's
point of view: opinion words (*remarkable, clever, amazing, impressive,
deserve, I think*), and what the author chooses to praise or criticize.
Facts ("the lodge has a door under the water") are not points of view;
opinions ("beavers are remarkable") are.

### Modeled example 1 — What the picture adds

*Adult thinks aloud, looking at the illustration:* "The passage says a
dam is a wall of sticks across a stream. The picture shows me something
more: the dam stretches all the way from bank to bank, and it is made of
*interlocking* sticks — I can see how they crisscross and hold each
other. The passage says the lodge is a dome in the pond. The picture
shows me the dome's shape and that it sits *in the middle* of open
water, away from the banks. And the picture settles a mix-up: the dam
and the lodge are two different structures. The dam is the long wall in
the foreground; the lodge is the dome in the middle. Words alone left
that easy to confuse."

"What do the words add that the picture cannot show? That the lodge has
a dry room inside and a door under the water — you cannot see inside
from the picture. Picture plus words together give the full
understanding."

### Modeled example 2 — Author's view vs. my view

*Adult thinks aloud:* "The author's point of view: beavers are remarkable
and deserve admiration. Evidence: 'the most remarkable builders in the
animal world,' 'a clever piece of work,' 'amazingly,' 'I think beavers
deserve our admiration.' Those are opinion words, not facts."

"My point of view might be different. I might think: 'Beavers are
interesting, but they also flood fields and cut down trees people
planted, so I am not sure I admire them.' That is a fair point of view
too — and it is *mine*, not the author's. The skill is keeping the two
separate: when I retell the passage, I say 'the author thinks…' for the
author's view, and 'I think…' for mine."

## Guided practice

Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05--illustrations-and-point-of-view).

1. From the illustration (or its text-only alternative): what is the
   difference between the dam and the lodge? Point to where each one is
   in the picture.
2. Name one thing the illustration shows that the passage's words do not
   say.
3. Name one thing the passage's words say that the illustration cannot
   show.
4. What is the author's point of view about beavers? Quote two opinion
   words or phrases that prove it.
5. Write one sentence starting "The author thinks…" and one sentence
   starting "I think…" about beavers. They may agree or disagree.
6. Is "The lodge has a door under the water" a fact or an opinion?
   Is "Beavers are remarkable" a fact or an opinion? Explain each choice.

## Independent practice

7. Look at the illustration again (or re-read the text-only alternative).
   Write two sentences: one about where the dam is, and one about where
   the lodge is. Use the words *foreground* and *middle* correctly.
8. Find one opinion word in the passage that was not quoted in Modeled
   example 2, and copy the sentence it appears in.
9. Error analysis: Kim wrote, "Beavers are remarkable builders" as a
   fact she learned from the passage. Explain why that sentence is the
   author's opinion, not a fact — and rewrite it as an accurate
   "the author thinks…" sentence.

## Application

Find a picture in any book at home (or remember one). Tell the adult:
(a) one thing the picture shows that the words do not, and (b) one thing
the words say that the picture cannot show. Then say whether the author
of that book seems to admire, criticize, or simply describe the topic —
and quote the word that tells you.

## Exit check

In your own words: (a) why is a picture in informational text not just a
decoration? (b) How do you tell an author's point of view from your own?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** learners who cannot see the image use the text-only
  alternative — it carries every position, shape, and color fact the
  activities need. The adult reads it aloud twice, slowly.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the dam and the lodge in the
  picture (or on a printed copy) instead of writing about them; speak
  the "author thinks / I think" sentences while the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** draw your own labeled diagram of a dam and a lodge
  (drawn from the passage and illustration, not copied), with a caption
  that adds one fact the drawing alone does not show.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) dam = lodge — this lesson's illustration
exists to separate them; check with Guided practice item 1 before moving
on. (2) "The author thinks X" reported as fact — require the "author
thinks" frame whenever an opinion is retold. (3) Treating the generated
illustration as a photograph or as proof — say plainly: it is an
AI-generated illustration made for this lesson, not a photograph of a
real pond, and it illustrates the passage rather than proving it. See
the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-05--illustrations-and-point-of-view) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05--illustrations-and-point-of-view).
