# Lesson 01 — Main Idea and Key Details

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find what a passage is mostly about, and
point to the details that prove it."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson's passage below; a
pencil and paper; two colored pencils if available (one for the main idea,
one for details).
**Standards:** RI.3.1, RI.3.2.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If I tell you about my dog — her name, what she eats, and
where she sleeps — what am I mostly telling you about?" (My dog.) "That is
the **main idea**: what the whole thing is mostly about. The name, the
food, and the sleeping place are **details** — small pieces that tell you
more about the main idea. A detail is not the main idea, even if it is the
most interesting part."

## Explicit teaching

Informational text teaches facts. To understand it, do three things:

1. **Read the whole passage first.** Do not decide the main idea from the
   first sentence. The first sentence is often just an introduction.
2. **Ask: "What is almost every paragraph about?"** The answer that fits
   the whole passage — not just one part — is the main idea. Say it in your
   own words, in one sentence.
3. **Find key details.** A **key detail** is a fact that supports the main
   idea — it tells you more about it or proves it. If you removed all the
   key details, the main idea would stand alone with nothing to back it up.
   **Evidence** is what you point to in the text when someone asks, "How do
   you know?" You answer by pointing to the exact words.

Three tests for a main idea:

- **The whole-passage test:** does it fit every paragraph, not just one?
- **The too-small test:** if it names only one detail ("beaver teeth"),
  it is too small to be the main idea.
- **The too-big test:** if it could be about anything ("animals"), it is
  too big. A main idea is about *this* passage.

## Passage — "Beavers Build a Home" (original, written for this unit)

Beavers are animals that build their own homes in ponds and streams. A
beaver family works together to build two things: a dam and a lodge.

First, the beavers cut down small trees with their strong teeth. They carry
sticks and branches to the stream. They pile the sticks across the water
and pack mud between them. This wall of sticks and mud is called a dam. The
dam slows the water and makes a deep pond.

Next, the beavers build a lodge in the pond. A lodge is a dome-shaped home
made of sticks and mud. It has a room inside, above the water, where the
beaver family sleeps and stays warm. The door of the lodge is under the
water, so the family can swim in and out safely.

Beavers build dams and lodges to stay safe and warm. The pond protects them
from animals that might hunt them, and the lodge gives them a dry place to
live all year.

### Modeled example 1 — Finding the main idea

*Adult reads the passage aloud, then thinks aloud:* "What is almost every
paragraph about? Paragraph 1: beavers build a dam and a lodge. Paragraph
2: how they build the dam. Paragraph 3: how they build the lodge.
Paragraph 4: why they build both. Every paragraph is about beavers
building a home. So my main idea, in my own words, is: **Beavers build
dams and lodges to make a safe, warm home.**"

"Now the tests. Whole-passage test: does it fit paragraph 2? Yes — the dam
is part of the home. Paragraph 3? Yes — the lodge is part of the home.
Too-small test: 'beaver teeth' would fail — teeth appear in only one
sentence. Too-big test: 'animals' would fail — this passage is not about
all animals, only beavers and their homes."

### Modeled example 2 — Finding key details and evidence

*Adult points to paragraph 2:* "A key detail for the main idea: **the dam
is a wall of sticks and mud that slows the water and makes a deep pond.**
Why is it key? Because it explains how part of the home works. Evidence:
the words 'This wall of sticks and mud is called a dam. The dam slows the
water and makes a deep pond.'"

"Another key detail, from paragraph 3: **the lodge has a room above the
water and a door under the water.** Evidence: 'It has a room inside,
above the water' and 'The door of the lodge is under the water.' If
someone asks 'How do you know beavers stay safe?' I point to those words
and to the last paragraph: 'The pond protects them from animals that
might hunt them.' That is answering with evidence (RI.3.1)."

"A detail that is NOT key for this main idea: beavers cut trees with
strong teeth. It is true and interesting, but it is a small step inside
the dam-building detail, not one of the main supports. Key details are the
big supports, not every fact."

## Guided practice

The adult reads each item aloud; the learner answers orally or in writing.
Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01--main-idea-and-key-details).

1. What is the main idea of "Beavers Build a Home," in your own words?
   (One sentence.)
2. Name one key detail about the dam, and point to the words that prove it.
3. Name one key detail about the lodge, and point to the words that prove it.
4. The passage says the lodge door is under the water. Why does that help
   the beaver family? Use the passage's own reason.
5. Which of these is the best main idea? (a) Beavers have strong teeth.
   (b) Beavers build dams and lodges to make a safe, warm home.
   (c) Animals live in ponds. Explain why the other two fail a test.
6. Ask a question about the passage that can be answered from the text
   (for example, "What is a dam made of?"), then answer it with evidence.

## Independent practice

7. Read this short passage and write its main idea in one sentence:

   > Honeybees live together in a hive. Inside the hive, different bees do
   > different jobs. The queen bee lays eggs. Worker bees gather nectar
   > from flowers, build wax rooms called cells, and feed the baby bees.
   > Guard bees stand at the hive door and keep out intruders. Every job
   > helps the whole hive survive.

8. List two key details from the honeybee passage that support your main
   idea, and copy the words that prove each one.
9. Error analysis: Sam said the main idea of the beaver passage is "Beaver
   teeth are strong." Explain why Sam is wrong, using one of the three
   tests.

## Application

Write (or dictate) a main-idea sentence for something you know well — your
school day, a game you play, or how your family makes dinner — and then
list three key details that support it. Read it to the adult, who checks:
does every detail support the main idea, or did a stray detail sneak in?

## Exit check

In your own words: what is a main idea, and how is it different from a key
detail? Give one example of each from today's beaver passage.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if choosing a main idea is hard, cover the passage and ask,
  "If you could only tell a friend one sentence about this, what would
  you say?" That sentence is usually the main idea.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the sentence that best shows the
  main idea instead of writing it; draw the dam and the lodge and label
  which detail each picture shows.
- **Extension:** find the main idea of a second paragraph on your own, and
  argue — with evidence — against a tempting wrong main idea the adult
  offers.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) the first sentence *is* the main idea — it
rarely is; it introduces the topic. (2) The most interesting or longest
detail is the main idea. (3) A main idea must copy words from the passage
— paraphrase is better; it proves understanding. If the learner keeps
picking details, use the whole-passage test on every paragraph before
accepting an answer. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-01--main-idea-and-key-details)
and [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01--main-idea-and-key-details).
