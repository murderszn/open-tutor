# Lesson 03 — Cause and Effect, and Comparison

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can name what caused something and what
happened because of it, and I can say how two things are alike and
different."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the two passages below; a
pencil and paper; a simple two-column chart (Cause | Effect) the adult
draws.
**Standards:** RI.3.3 (cause/effect), RI.3.8 (logical connections:
comparison, cause/effect, sequence).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "You left your ice pop in the sun, and it melted. What
caused the melting? What was the effect?" (Cause: sun warmed it. Effect:
it melted.) "Now: how are an ice pop and an ice cube alike? How are they
different?" (Alike: both frozen and cold. Different: shape, flavor, one
is on a stick.) "Today we read for exactly those two connections: what
causes what, and how things compare."

## Explicit teaching

**Cause and effect.** A **cause** is why something happens. An
**effect** is what happens because of it. Signal words: **because, so,
since, therefore, as a result, this makes, this causes**. Ask two
questions of any event: "Why did this happen?" (cause) and "What happened
because of it?" (effect). Watch out: just because two things happen one
after another does not mean the first caused the second — the cause must
actually explain the effect.

**Comparison.** To **compare** is to say how things are alike; to
**contrast** is to say how they are different. Signal words: **alike,
same, both, also** (alike) and **but, different, however, unlike, while**
(different). A good comparison names the feature being compared — speed,
size, cost, or color — not just "they are different."

**Connections between sentences.** Sentences in informational text are
joined by logic: one sentence may give a cause and the next its effect;
one paragraph may compare two things; steps may follow in sequence
(Lesson 02). Naming the connection is RI.3.8: describe *how* sentences
and paragraphs link up, not just what they say.

## Passage A — "Why Leaves Change Color" (original, written for this unit)

In fall, the days get shorter and the air gets cooler. Because there is
less sunlight, trees stop making chlorophyll, the green color in leaves.

When the green fades, other colors that were already in the leaf show
through. Many leaves turn yellow or orange. Some trees, like maples, make
a red or purple color as well.

As a result, the trees look bright and colorful for a few weeks. Then the
leaves dry out, fall off, and rest on the ground until spring.

## Passage B — "Two Ways to Travel: Long Ago and Today" (original, written for this unit)

Long ago, many people traveled in a wagon pulled by horses. A wagon trip
to the next town could take a whole day. The wagon moved slowly, and the
horses needed food, water, and rest along the way.

Today, many people travel by car. A car can reach the next town in less
than an hour. Cars move fast, but they need fuel, and the driver must
follow road rules.

Both wagons and cars carry people and goods from one place to another.
They are different in speed, in what powers them, and in what they need
along the way.

### Modeled example 1 — A cause and its effect (Passage A)

*Adult thinks aloud:* "Find an effect first: 'trees stop making
chlorophyll.' Why? The passage says, 'Because there is less sunlight,
trees stop making chlorophyll.' Cause: less sunlight in fall. Effect:
trees stop making chlorophyll. Signal word: **because**."

"One more chain: cause — the green color fades. Effect — yellow and
orange colors show through. Signal: 'When the green fades, other colors
that were already in the leaf show through.' Notice the cause comes first
in the sentence here ('when the green fades'), but a cause can also come
second — 'trees stop making chlorophyll **because** there is less
sunlight.' The signal word tells you which part is the cause, wherever it
sits."

"A non-cause to reject: leaves fall off *after* the colors change, but the
colors do not *cause* the falling. The passage says the leaves dry out
and then fall. Order is not the same as cause."

### Modeled example 2 — Comparing wagon and car (Passage B)

*Adult draws a small chart and thinks aloud:* "Alike: both carry people
and goods from one place to another — the passage says so in the last
paragraph. Different: speed — a wagon trip took a whole day, a car trip
less than an hour. Different: power — horses pull the wagon; fuel powers
the car. Different: needs on the way — horses need food, water, and
rest; a car needs fuel and a driver following road rules."

"So a comparison sentence: 'Wagons and cars are alike because both carry
people and goods, but they are different because wagons are slow and
horse-powered while cars are fast and fuel-powered.' Naming the feature
(speed, power) is what makes the comparison useful — 'they are different'
alone says nothing."

## Guided practice

Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03--causeeffect-and-comparison).

1. Passage A: what causes trees to stop making chlorophyll? Quote the
   signal word and the cause.
2. Passage A: what is the effect of the green color fading? Answer in a
   full sentence with "so" or "as a result."
3. Passage A: the leaves fall off after they change color. Did the color
   change *cause* the leaves to fall? Explain using the passage.
4. Passage B: name one way wagons and cars are alike, and quote the
   sentence that says so.
5. Passage B: name two ways wagons and cars are different, naming the
   feature for each (for example, speed).
6. The passage says wagons and cars are "different in speed." Is that a
   sequence connection, a cause/effect connection, or a comparison
   connection? Explain your choice.

## Independent practice

7. Read this short passage and name one cause and its effect:

   > It rained all night, so the river rose. Because the river rose, the
   > road beside it was closed in the morning, and the school bus took a
   > different route.

8. Compare a bicycle and a bus: write one sentence about how they are
   alike and one sentence about how they are different. Name the feature
   in each sentence.
9. Error analysis: Dan said, "The cause of the leaves falling is that
   they changed color." Explain why the passage does not support Dan's
   claim, and give a better-supported cause/effect pair from Passage A.

## Application

Pick a cause/effect chain from your own life (for example: you practiced,
so you got better; you forgot your umbrella, so you got wet). Draw a
two-box chain — Cause → Effect — and write one sentence explaining the
chain with a signal word. Then compare two things in your home (two
chairs, two cups, two shoes) in one alike sentence and one different
sentence that names the feature.

## Exit check

Without looking: (a) what is a cause, and what is an effect? (b) Give one
cause/effect pair from Passage A. (c) Give one alike and one different
for wagon vs. car.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** use hand signals — point backward for cause ("why?"), point
  forward for effect ("what happened?"). For comparison, hold hands
  together for alike, apart for different.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw the cause/effect chain as two boxes
  with an arrow instead of writing sentences; speak comparison sentences
  while the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** find a second cause/effect chain in Passage A that was
  not modeled, and write it as "because ___ , ___" and as "___ , so ___,"
  showing the cause can sit in either half of the sentence.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) order = cause — explicitly teach the reject in
Modeled example 1. (2) "Different" without a feature — require the
feature word every time. (3) Mixing up which half is cause when the
signal word sits mid-sentence — have the learner cover each half and ask,
"Is this the why, or the what-happened?" See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-03--causeeffect-and-comparison) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03--causeeffect-and-comparison).
