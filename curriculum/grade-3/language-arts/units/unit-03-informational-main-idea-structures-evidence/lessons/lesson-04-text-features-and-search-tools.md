# Lesson 04 — Text Features and Search Tools

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find answers fast using headings,
captions, glossaries, and indexes — without reading every page."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** this lesson's feature-rich
passage below; a pencil and paper; a real book with a table of contents
and index, if one is at hand (any book works — the skill transfers).
**Standards:** RI.3.5 (text features and search tools), RI.3.4 (word
meanings, revisited).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult holds up any book and asks: "If you wanted to know what this
book is about without reading it all, where would you look first?" The
learner names possibilities (cover, contents page, pictures). The adult
says: "Those shortcuts are called **text features**. Readers who know
them find answers in seconds. Readers who do not know them read every
page. Today you become a fast finder."

## Explicit teaching

**Text features** are parts of a book or passage that help you find and
understand information:

- **Title** — what the whole text is about.
- **Table of contents** — a list of chapters or sections and the page
  each starts on. Use it to pick *where* to read.
- **Heading** — the title of one section. Headings tell you what that
  section is about before you read it.
- **Caption** — words under a picture that explain what the picture
  shows. Always read the caption — it often holds a fact the paragraph
  does not.
- **Sidebar** — a small box beside the main text with an extra fact or
  example. It is not the main text, but it counts as evidence too.
- **Glossary** — a mini-dictionary at the back of a book, with meanings
  for hard words in *that* book.
- **Index** — an alphabetical list of topics and the pages where each
  appears. Use it to find *every* place a topic shows up.
- **Key word** — a word you search for (in a book's index, or on a
  screen) to jump to the right place. Pick the most specific word: "bee
  jobs" beats "insects."

**The fast-finder routine:**

1. Read the question. Circle the key word in it.
2. Check the table of contents: which section would hold the answer?
3. Read that section's heading, then its first sentence.
4. If a hard word appears, check the glossary — do not skip it and do
   not guess.
5. Answer from that section, and point to the words that prove it.

## Feature-rich passage — "The Honeybee's Job" (original, written for this unit)

**Table of contents (for this passage's pretend book, "All About Bees")**

1. The Hive at Home — page 2
2. Jobs Inside the Hive — page 3
3. From Flower to Honey — page 5
4. Bees and People — page 7

**Glossary (from the back of "All About Bees")**

- **colony:** a large group of bees that live and work together in one hive.
- **nectar:** a sweet liquid flowers make; bees gather it to make honey.
- **pollen:** a fine yellow powder flowers make; bees carry it and it helps
  new flowers grow.

**Index (from the back of "All About Bees")**

- bees, jobs of — pages 3, 4
- honey, how it is made — page 5
- queen bee — pages 2, 3

### 2. Jobs Inside the Hive

Honeybees live together in a colony. Inside the hive, different bees do
different jobs.

The queen bee's job is to lay eggs. A queen can lay more than a thousand
eggs in one day.

Worker bees do most of the other jobs. Some workers gather nectar from
flowers. Some build wax rooms called cells. Some feed the baby bees.
Guard bees stand at the hive door and keep out intruders.

> **Sidebar — Did you know?** A worker bee visits hundreds of flowers in
> a single day.

*[Picture: a honeybee on a yellow flower.]*
**Caption:** A worker bee gathers nectar from a flower. The bee carries
the nectar back to the hive in a special stomach.

### Modeled example 1 — Finding an answer with the contents page

*Adult thinks aloud:* "Question: 'What is a colony?' Key word: colony.
Do I read the whole book? No. The glossary is the tool for word meanings
— and there it is: **'a large group of bees that live and work together
in one hive.'** Answer found in seconds, and I can point to the exact
words."

"Question: 'Where do I read about the queen bee's job?' I check the
table of contents: section 2 is 'Jobs Inside the Hive,' page 3. And the
index agrees: 'queen bee — pages 2, 3.' Two tools pointing to the same
place — that is a good sign."

### Modeled example 2 — Reading a caption and a sidebar for new facts

*Adult thinks aloud:* "The main paragraph says workers gather nectar. But
the caption adds something new: the bee carries nectar **in a special
stomach**. That fact appears nowhere in the paragraph — if I skipped the
caption, I would miss it. The sidebar adds another new fact: a worker
visits **hundreds of flowers in a single day**. Captions and sidebars are
not decorations. They are part of the text, and their facts count as
evidence just like paragraph facts do."

## Guided practice

Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04--text-features-and-search-tools).

1. Using only the table of contents: on which page does "From Flower to
   Honey" begin?
2. Using only the index: on which pages is the queen bee mentioned?
3. Using only the glossary: what is nectar? Answer in your own words, then
   point to the glossary words.
4. What new fact does the caption give that the paragraph does not?
5. What new fact does the sidebar give that the paragraph does not?
6. A friend asks, "What jobs do worker bees do?" Which section would you
   open, and which key word would you search for? Explain your choice.

## Independent practice

7. You want to know how honey is made. Which tool — contents, glossary,
   or index — gets you there fastest, and what page does it point to?
8. Write one question that can be answered from the heading "Jobs Inside
   the Hive" alone, and one question that needs the glossary. Answer both.
9. Error analysis: Jo read only the main paragraphs and said, "The
   passage never says how the bee carries nectar." What did Jo skip, and
   what does it say?

## Application

Take any real book at home with a table of contents or index. Ask the
adult to give you a question about its topic. Find the answer using only
the features — contents, headings, index, glossary, captions — and time
yourself. Report: which tool found it, and how many pages did you *not*
have to read?

## Exit check

Name four text features and, for each, finish this sentence: "I use it
when I want to ___." Then: what is a key word, and why is "bee jobs"
better than "insects" for this passage?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** give the learner a features checklist card (title,
  contents, heading, caption, sidebar, glossary, index) to tick off while
  searching. The card turns searching into a game.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the feature that holds each
  answer instead of writing; dictate answers while the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** build a mini-index for this unit's beaver passage
  (Lesson 01): list five topics alphabetically with the paragraph number
  where each appears.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) captions and sidebars are "extra" and skippable
— this lesson's modeled example 2 exists to kill that habit. (2) The
glossary is for spelling — no, it is for meaning; spelling is the
dictionary's other job. (3) Searching means reading everything faster —
no, searching means *not* reading most of it. If the learner reads the
whole passage to answer a contents question, re-teach the fast-finder
routine. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-04--text-features-and-search-tools) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04--text-features-and-search-tools).
