# Lesson 06 — Comparing Two Texts on One Topic

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can read two texts about the same thing and
say what they agree on, what only one says, and which points matter
most."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the two texts below; a
pencil and paper; a Venn-style chart (two overlapping circles) the
adult draws.
**Standards:** RI.3.9 (compare two texts), RI.3.8 (connections), RI.3.2
(main idea and details, revisited).

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Two friends both tell you about the same movie. One
says the funny parts were best; the other says the music was best. Do
they disagree about the movie? Or did they just notice different
things?" (They noticed different things — both can be true.) "Texts do
the same thing. Two texts on one topic can share points and still differ
in what each treats as most important. Today you compare, not just read."

## Explicit teaching

To compare two texts on the same topic:

1. **Find each text's main idea.** Write each in one sentence. If you
   cannot, you are not ready to compare — go back to Lesson 01.
2. **Find shared points.** What important points appear in *both* texts?
   Put those in the middle of your Venn chart.
3. **Find points found in only one text.** Put those in that text's side
   of the chart. A point in only one text is not wrong — it is just
   covered by only one author.
4. **Judge importance.** The "most important points" are the ones tied
   to the main idea — the points the text would fall apart without. A
   fun extra fact (a queen lays more than a thousand eggs a day) is
   interesting, but if it is not tied to the main idea, it is not one of
   the most important points.
5. **Say it in sentences.** "Both texts say… Text A also says… Text B
   also says… The most important shared point is… because…"

Watch out for two traps: (1) saying texts "disagree" when they merely
cover different parts of a topic; (2) treating every detail as equally
important — comparing means weighing, not just listing.

## Text A — "Honeybees at Work" (original, written for this unit)

Honeybees live together in a colony, and every bee has a job that helps
the whole hive survive. The queen bee lays eggs. Worker bees gather
nectar from flowers, build wax cells, feed the baby bees, and guard the
hive door. When a worker finds good flowers, it dances to tell other
workers where to go. Every job matters: without workers, the colony
could not gather food or raise new bees.

## Text B — "A Beekeeper's Day" (original, written for this unit)

A beekeeper is a person who keeps bees in wooden boxes called hives. In
the morning, the beekeeper puts on a veil and gloves, then opens a hive
to check it. The beekeeper looks for the queen, checks that the bees have
enough honey stored, and makes sure the colony is healthy. In late
summer, the beekeeper carefully takes some honey from the hive, leaving
plenty for the bees. Beekeeping helps people and bees: people get honey,
and the bees get a safe home and care.

### Modeled example 1 — Main ideas and shared points

*Adult thinks aloud:* "Text A's main idea: **in a bee colony, every bee
has a job that helps the whole hive survive.** Text B's main idea:
**a beekeeper cares for a hive and takes some honey while leaving plenty
for the bees.**"

"Shared points: both texts are about honeybees living in hives, and both
say the colony's survival matters — Text A through the bees' jobs, Text
B through the beekeeper's care. Both mention honey: Text A says workers
gather nectar; Text B says the beekeeper takes some honey and leaves
plenty for the bees."

### Modeled example 2 — Only-one-text points and most important points

*Adult thinks aloud:* "Only in Text A: the waggle dance that tells other
workers where flowers are; the specific jobs (queen lays eggs, workers
build cells, guards guard the door). Only in Text B: the beekeeper's
veil and gloves; checking for the queen and honey stores; taking honey
in late summer."

"Most important points overall: (1) bees live and work together in a
colony — both texts rest on this. (2) Different jobs and care keep the
colony alive — Text A's jobs and Text B's beekeeper checks both serve
it. The dance is fascinating, but it is a detail inside Text A, not one
of the most important points of the pair. The veil and gloves are a
detail inside Text B for the same reason."

## Guided practice

Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06--comparing-two-texts).

1. Write the main idea of Text A in one sentence, and the main idea of
   Text B in one sentence.
2. Name one important point that appears in both texts, and quote a
   phrase from each text that shows it.
3. Name one important point found only in Text A, and one found only in
   Text B.
4. Which is a most important point of the pair: "bees dance to show
   where flowers are" or "bees live and work together in a colony"?
   Explain your choice using the "would the text fall apart without it?"
   test.
5. Do the two texts disagree about anything? Answer with evidence, or
   explain why they do not disagree even though they cover different
   things.
6. Write a three-sentence comparison: "Both texts say… Text A also
   says… Text B also says…"

## Independent practice

7. Reread Text B. What is the beekeeper's most important job, in your
   words? Explain how it connects to Text A's main idea.
8. Draw a Venn chart for the two texts with at least two points in each
   section (Text A only, both, Text B only).
9. Error analysis: Lee said, "The texts disagree because Text A is about
   bees and Text B is about a person." Explain why Lee is wrong, and
   restate the relationship between the texts correctly.

## Application

Pick any topic you know from two angles (for example: "my school" as
told by you and by a teacher, or "a pet" as told by its owner and by a
vet). Tell the adult one shared point, one only-one-side point for each
side, and which point matters most — and why.

## Exit check

Without looking: (a) what are the three steps of comparing two texts?
(b) Name one shared point and one only-one-text point for today's pair.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** use the Venn chart for every comparison before writing
  sentences — the picture organizes the thinking, and the sentences
  then write themselves.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort pre-written point cards into three
  piles (Text A only, both, Text B only) instead of writing the chart.
- **Extension:** write a short paragraph that combines both texts' most
  important points into one account of "how a bee colony survives," citing
  which text each point came from.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "different = disagree" — Modeled example 2
and Guided practice item 5 exist for this. (2) Listing every detail
instead of weighing importance — require the "fall apart without it"
test for every claimed most-important point. (3) Comparing topics
instead of points — "both are about bees" is a shared *topic*, not a
shared *point*; insist on point-level claims with quotes. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-06--comparing-two-texts) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06--comparing-two-texts).
