# Lesson 01 — Break and Blend: Reading Multisyllable Words

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can break a long word into syllables and read
it piece by piece."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the unit word sets ([unit README](../README.md#unit-word-sets-authored-for-this-unit-reused-across-lessons-and-practice));
word cards the adult writes by hand; a pencil and paper.
**Standards:** RF.3.3.c.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult says *sunset* slowly: "How many beats, or parts, do you hear?"
(*sun-set* — two.) "Those parts are called **syllables**. Every syllable has
exactly one vowel sound — that is the rule that makes this work." The adult
says *paper* ("pa-per" — two) and *happy* ("hap-py" — two). If the learner
cannot hear the beats, they put a hand under their chin: each time the jaw
drops is one syllable.

## Explicit teaching

A **syllable** is one beat of a word, and every syllable contains exactly one
vowel sound. Big words are just syllables lined up in a row. You never have
to swallow a whole word at once — you break it into syllables, read each
syllable, then blend them together. This is called **break and blend**.

The steps:

1. **Find the vowels.** Circle or underline every vowel letter (a, e, i, o,
   u, and sometimes y). Each vowel sound is the heart of one syllable.
2. **Break between the syllables.** Look for consonants sitting between two
   vowels — most words break between them (rab/bit, nap/kin). If a word ends
   with a consonant + *le*, that chunk is its own syllable (ta/ble — not in
   this unit's sets, but a good rule to keep).
3. **Read each syllable.** Use the vowel sounds you already know. Some
   syllables will be "closed" (vowel + consonant, like *rab* — short vowel)
   and some "open" (ending in a vowel, like *pa* in *pa-per* — the vowel says
   its name).
4. **Blend them together.** Say the syllables fast until they melt into the
   word: "rab … bit … rabbit."

Two cautions the adult states plainly: you are allowed to be wrong about
*where* the break goes on the first try — a nearby break still lets you read
the word, and then you fix it by checking what sounds right. And if you get
stuck on a syllable, read the rest of the sentence: context often tells you
the word (this previews Lesson 04).

**Why one vowel sound per syllable matters:** if you count three vowel
sounds, you must have three syllables. *Problem* (prob-lem): two vowel
sounds, two syllables. *Carpet* (car-pet): two and two. A silent e does not
count — *like* has one vowel sound, so one syllable.

### Modeled example 1 — rabbit

*Adult writes* rabbit *on a card and underlines the vowels:* r**a**bb**i**t.
"Two vowel sounds — a and i — so two syllables. Between them sit two
consonants, b and b, and we break between them: rab | bit. Read the first
part: r-a-b — *rab*, short a, closed syllable. Second part: b-i-t — *bit*,
short i. Blend: rab … bit … *rabbit*." The adult shows that breaking after
the first b (ra | bbit) still reads the same way — close enough breaks are
fine; the word reveals itself when you blend.

### Modeled example 2 — paper, then pocket

*Adult writes* paper *and underlines the vowels:* p**a**p**e**r. "Two vowel
sounds — a and e — so two syllables. One consonant between them: break
before it. pa | per. The first syllable is open — it ends in a vowel — so
the a says its name: *pa*. Blend: pa … per … *paper*."
*Adult writes* pocket: p**o**ck**e**t. "Two vowel sounds. Between them sit
c and k — break between them: pock | et. Read: *pock* … *et* … *pocket*."
"Notice: the break is not always in the same place. Vowels tell you *how
many* syllables; the consonants between them suggest *where*."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 6 tasks)

1. "Find the vowels in *lemon*." (e, o — two vowel sounds.) "Where do we
   break?" (Between m and o: lem | on.) "Read it." (*lemon*.)
2. "Break *tiger*." (ti | ger — open first syllable, long i.) "Read it."
   (*tiger*.)
3. **Chin-drop check:** "How many syllables in *dentist*?" (The jaw drops
   twice: den-tist.) "Find the vowels to prove it." (e, i.)
4. "Break *magnet*." (mag | net.) "Read each part, then blend."
   (*magnet*.)
5. **Error-analysis:** the adult writes *button* and "reads" it as
   "butt-on" with the wrong stress: BUTT-on. "Did that sound right?" (No.)
   "What should I check?" (The vowel sounds — u and o — and try the blend
   again: but-ton, with the stress on the first part.) "Wrong stress is a
   normal first guess; blending again usually fixes it."
6. **Tricky one:** "Break *garden*." (gar | den.) "What sound does the a
   make here?" (The r changes it — "ar" says /ar/, not short a. We still
   break the same way.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 6 tasks)

1. Break and read: *picnic, basket, napkin, silent*. (pic-nic, bas-ket,
   nap-kin, si-lent — open i says its name.)
2. Break and read: *insect, absent, happen, finish*. (in-sect, ab-sent,
   hap-pen, fin-ish.)
3. Ear-only: the adult says *carpet, pumpkin, sister, never*. The learner
   says the syllable count without seeing the words. (2, 2, 2, 2.)
4. Find-the-vowels proof: circle every vowel in *problem* and *robot*,
   then break and read. (prob-lem, ro-bot — open o says its name.)
5. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why does *paper* have two syllables?" (Two
   vowel sounds — a and e — and every syllable has exactly one vowel
   sound.)
6. Speed round: the adult shows 6 word cards from the unit set; the learner
   breaks and reads each. Record the count correct.

## Application

"Syllable surgeon." The learner picks any 6 multisyllable words from the
unit word sets, writes each on a card, draws the break line(s), circles the
vowels, and reads them to the adult "in surgery" — slowly for the
operation (each syllable separately), then "closing up" (blended fast).
One card must be a word the learner got wrong today.

## Exit check

The adult writes two words the learner has not practiced today from the
unit set — for example, *pumpkin* and *carpet*. The learner breaks each,
reads it, and names the vowel sound in each syllable (pump-kin: short u,
short i; car-pet: /ar/, short e). Success: both read correctly with
breaks shown.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads every word aloud first on
  the learner's request; syllable beats can be tapped on the table or
  stepped across the room.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may point to cards, clap beats,
  or dictate breaks for the adult to write.
- **Extension:** break three-syllable words from the suffix sets —
  *celebration* (cel-e-bra-tion — four), *comfortable* (comf-ort-a-ble —
  four). What pattern do you notice in how *-tion* and *-able* sound?

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

Learners often try to read the whole word at once and guess from the first
letters. Redirect every guess to the steps: "Vowels first — how many
syllables?" They also break in the wrong place and then freeze; normalize
it: a nearby break still yields the word, and re-blending fixes the
remainder. Watch for silent-e counting (like → one syllable) and for
treating *y* as always a vowel (it counts in *happy*). See
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-01).
