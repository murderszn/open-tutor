# Lesson 05 — Look It Up: Glossaries, Dictionaries, and Rule-Breaker Words

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can find a word's exact meaning in a glossary
or dictionary, and I can read the words that break the rules."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the unit word sets; a beginning
print dictionary (library or household) and/or an adult-supervised digital
dictionary; paper and pencil.
**Standards:** L.3.4.d, RF.3.3.d.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Yesterday our detective tools failed on a word — what do
good detectives do then?" (Look it up.) "A **glossary** is a mini-dictionary
at the back of a book, listing only that book's hard words. A
**dictionary** lists words for the whole language, in ABC order, with each
word's spelling, pronunciation, part of speech, and meanings. Today you
learn to use both — and you meet the words no tool can sound out."

## Explicit teaching

### Part 1 — Using glossaries and dictionaries (L.3.4.d)

A dictionary entry has parts. Take this beginner-style entry the adult
writes on paper:

> **re·build** (ree-BILD) *verb* — **1.** to build again. **2.** to make
> strong again.

Reading it: the dots show syllables (*re·build*); the capitals show which
part you stress (ree-BILD); *verb* tells you it is an action word; the
numbered meanings are listed most-common-first — **meaning 1 is usually the
one you want**, but context decides.

How to find a word fast: **ABC order, first letters first.** To find
*kindness*, open near K; compare second letters (*ki-* comes before
*kn-*… wait — *kindness* starts k-i; flip past *ka-*, *ke-*, land in *ki-*).
Guide words at the top of each page tell you the first and last word on the
page — if your word falls between them alphabetically, you are on the right
page.

For a glossary, it is easier: it is short and only covers the book you are
reading — always check the glossary first when a book has one.

Digital dictionaries work the same way (type, read the entry, check the
part of speech and meaning 1), but the adult supervises: no open web
searching by the learner, and the adult previews the site for advertising
(see the [resource pack](../resource-pack.md) for the vetted dictionary
route).

### Part 2 — Rule-breaker words (RF.3.3.d)

Some words refuse to follow the sound rules. *Said* is not "sayed."
*Have* does not rhyme with *cave*. *Of* does not sound like "off." These are
**irregularly spelled words** — the unit calls them **rule-breakers**.
You cannot sound them out; you have to *recognize* them, the way you
recognize a friend's face.

How to learn a rule-breaker: **see it, say it, spell it, use it.**
Look at *could* — notice the tricky part (the *oul*). Say it. Spell it
aloud (c-o-u-l-d). Write it. Use it in a sentence. Rule-breakers live in
your memory, not in the rules — and every reader, even adults, has a
collection of them.

The unit's rule-breaker set: *said, have, of, was, were, some, come, what,
where, again, laugh, would, could, should, friend, people, water, only,
because, enough, thought, through, bought, does, gone.*

### Modeled example 1 — a dictionary entry

*Adult writes the entry for* disagree *from a real beginning dictionary (or
writes a clean model entry):*
"D-I-S … found it. Entry: **dis·a·gree** (dis-uh-GREE) *verb* — **1.** to
have a different opinion. **2.** to not agree. Part of speech: verb — it is
something people do. Meaning 1 fits our sentence 'They disagree about the
game' — different opinions. Detective case closed — and notice: the
dictionary's meaning matches what our prefix tool predicted. Tools and
dictionaries should agree; when they do, you are almost certainly right."

### Modeled example 2 — a rule-breaker

*Adult writes* though. "Sound it out my way: t-h-o-u-g-h … 'thowg'? That is
not a word. This is a rule-breaker — the *ough* is the tricky part. See it
(*though*), say it (*though* — rhymes with *go*), spell it (t-h-o-u-g-h),
use it: 'Though it rained, we played.' Now *thought* — same tricky *ough*,
different sound: *thought* rhymes with *caught*. Rule-breakers are learned
in families when you can, one by one when you must."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 7 tasks)

1. "Find *rebuild* in the dictionary. What part of speech is it?" (Verb.)
   "Read meaning 1." (To build again.)
2. "Find *kindness*. Guide words say the page runs from *kindergarten* to
   *king*. Is *kindness* on this page?" (Yes — k-i-n-d-n comes after
   k-i-n-d-e-r and before k-i-n-g.) "Read the entry." (Noun — the quality
   of being kind.)
3. "Which meaning of *rebuild* fits 'They will rebuild the fence'?"
   (Meaning 1 — build again.)
4. **Glossary drill:** the adult makes a 5-word glossary for "The Puzzle
   Box" passage (session 8's text): *treacherous, frigid, weary, diligent,
   ancient* — with short definitions. The learner finds each word's
   meaning in under 30 seconds. (Glossaries are fast because they are
   short — that is the point.)
5. "Read these rule-breakers: *said, have, of, was, were, some*." (Adult
   corrects any sounding-out immediately — these are recognized, not
   decoded.)
6. **See-say-spell-use:** *could* and *would*. The learner does all four
   steps for each.
7. **Error-analysis:** the adult reads "I *wold* like some water" for
   *would*. "What did I do wrong?" (I sounded it out like a regular word —
   *would* is a rule-breaker; you recognize it whole.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 6 tasks)

1. Look up *uncomfortable* and *disappear*. Write each entry's part of
   speech and meaning 1.
2. "Which meaning of *light* fits 'the light of the lamp' vs. 'a light
   bag'?" (Meaning for brightness vs. meaning for not-heavy — context
   picks; the dictionary lists both.)
3. See-say-spell-use for *enough* and *through*.
4. Read all 12 of these rule-breakers without hesitation: *what, where,
   again, laugh, should, friend, people, water, only, because, does,
   gone*. (Adult times it gently — fluency with rule-breakers matters for
   Lesson 6.)
5. Write 4 sentences, each using a different rule-breaker correctly.
6. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why do you check meaning 1 first but not
   *always* choose it?" (Meanings are ordered most-common-first, so 1 is
   the best bet — but the sentence decides, like *light* above.)

## Application

"Dictionary detective agency." The learner picks 4 words from the unit's
word sets that they are still unsure about, looks each up, and writes a
proper mini-entry for the [project dictionary](../project.md): the word
with syllable dots, the stressed part in capitals, the part of speech, and
meaning 1 in the learner's own words — plus one rule-breaker card
(see-say-spell-use on the back). These become project entries.

## Exit check

The adult gives the learner a beginning dictionary and the word
*preheat*. The learner finds it, reads the part of speech and meaning 1
(to heat beforehand), and confirms it matches the Lesson 02 prediction.
Then the learner reads *thought, bought, enough* correctly on sight.
Success: dictionary entry found and read correctly; all three
rule-breakers recognized without sounding out.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult handles the ABC-order navigation
  while the learner reads the entry; entries may be read aloud by the
  adult.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate entries for the adult
  to scribe; rule-breakers may be answered by pointing to the correct word
  among three.
- **Extension:** compare two dictionaries' entries for one word (print vs.
  digital) — which gives more meanings? Which shows a picture? Write one
  sentence about which you would trust for schoolwork and why.

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

Learners treat the dictionary as the *first* tool instead of the last —
keep the order: context, affix, root, *then* look it up, so the dictionary
confirms thinking rather than replacing it. They also grab any numbered
meaning instead of meaning 1 / the context-fitting one; always ask "does
that meaning fit your sentence?" And with rule-breakers, the sounding-out
habit is strong — correct it the instant it appears: "That one is a
rule-breaker. Recognize it." See
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-05).
