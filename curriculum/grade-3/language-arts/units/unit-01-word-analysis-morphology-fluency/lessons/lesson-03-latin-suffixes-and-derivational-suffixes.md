# Lesson 03 — Suffixes: Latin Endings and Meaning-Changers

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can read words with Latin suffixes, and I can
use -ful, -less, and -ness to change what a word means."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the unit word sets; word cards;
the word-part puzzle image
([word-part-puzzle-pieces.png](../assets/word-part-puzzle-pieces.png)) —
**an AI-generated illustration**; blue, yellow, and red crayons or markers.
**Standards:** RF.3.3.a, RF.3.3.b.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult shows the puzzle image again. "Blue holds beginnings — we did
those. Today we fill the **red** piece: endings. A word part added to the
*end* of a word is called a **suffix**. Some suffixes you have to *read*
because they came from Latin and look tricky; others you have to *use*
because they change the meaning."

## Explicit teaching

### Part 1 — Latin suffixes you decode (RF.3.3.b)

Long ago, English borrowed thousands of words from Latin, and their endings
came along. Three groups to recognize by sight and sound:

- **-tion / -sion** says "shun": *ac-tion*, *sta-tion*, *na-tion*,
  *cel-e-bra-tion*; *mo-tion*, *di-vi-sion*, *vi-sion*, *de-ci-sion*. The
  spelling is strange, but the sound is always the same. Break first, then
  read: the *-tion* chunk is its own syllable.
- **-able / -ible** says "uh-bull": *read-a-ble* (can be read), *break-a-ble*
  (can be broken), *a-gree-a-ble*, *com-fort-a-ble*; *in-vis-i-ble* (cannot
  be seen), *ter-ri-ble*, *pos-si-ble*. Notice what *-able* and *-ible* mean:
  "can be" — *readable* = can be read.
- **-ment** says "ment": *pay-ment* (the result of paying), *move-ment*,
  *treat-ment*, *en-joy-ment*, *state-ment*, *gov-ern-ment*. It usually turns
  a verb into a noun — the *thing* that results from the action.

The adult's rule for all three: **they are their own syllables**. When you
break a word, give the Latin suffix its own piece — it always sounds the
same, so it is the easiest syllable in the word.

### Part 2 — Derivational suffixes you use (RF.3.3.a)

A **derivational suffix** is added to a word and makes a new word with a new
meaning (and sometimes a new part of speech). Three of them:

- **-ful** = "full of": *helpful* = full of help; *careful* = full of care;
  *thankful*, *painful*.
- **-less** = "without": *hopeless* = without hope; *careless* = without
  care; *fearless* = without fear; *helpless*.
- **-ness** = "the state or quality of": *kindness* = the quality of being
  kind; *sadness*, *darkness*, *happiness*.

Watch a word change twice: *care* → *careful* (full of care, an adjective)
→ *careless* (without care, also an adjective — the opposite!). And *happy*
→ *happiness* (the state of being happy — now a noun). The suffix is the red
puzzle piece: it always attaches at the end, and it always changes the
meaning.

### Modeled example 1 — celebration

*Adult writes* celebration *and draws yellow around* celebr- *… no — the
adult models honesty:* "First I break it: cel-e-bra-tion. The *-tion* chunk
gets its own syllable — 'shun.' Read each: cel … e … bra … tion … *celebration*.
The Latin suffix is the easiest part because it never changes its sound."
The adult points to the red puzzle piece: "*-tion* lives on the red piece.
Whenever I see it, I already know how that syllable sounds."

### Modeled example 2 — careless, then kindness

*Adult writes* careless. "Red piece: *-less* — 'without.' Yellow piece:
*care*. *Careless* = without care. Sentence check: 'He was careless with
his crayons and they rolled everywhere.' Without care — fits."
*Adult writes* kindness. "Red piece: *-ness* — 'the state or quality of.'
Yellow piece: *kind*. *Kindness* = the quality of being kind. 'Her kindness
made everyone smile.' Fits." The adult contrasts: "*careful* is the
opposite of *careless* — one suffix means 'full of,' the other 'without.'
The red piece decides."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 7 tasks)

1. "Read *action*. Which chunk is the Latin suffix?" (*-tion* — 'shun'.)
   "Break it." (ac-tion.)
2. "Read *division*." (di-vi-sion — the *-sion* chunk says 'zhun'/'shun'
   here; the adult notes that *-sion* can sound like "zhun" after vowels —
   *vision, decision* — and that is fine, it is still its own syllable.)
3. "What does *-able* mean in *breakable*?" ("Can be" — can be broken.)
   "And in *invisible*?" (Cannot be seen — the *in-* is a prefix meaning
   "not," plus *-ible* = "can be": not-can-be-seen. A two-prefix word!)
4. "Change *help* two ways with red-piece suffixes." (*helpful* = full of
   help; *helpless* = without help.) "Use both in one sentence." (Any
   sensible sentence; the adult checks both meanings fit.)
5. **Three-piece build:** using the puzzle image, build *un + comfort +
   able* — blue, yellow, red. "What does it mean?" (*uncomfortable* = not
   able to be comfortable — not comfortable. The adult confirms this matches
   the standard's example pair *comfortable/uncomfortable*.)
6. "Read *enjoyment*." (en-joy-ment.) "What part of speech did *-ment*
   make?" (A noun — the thing that results from enjoying.)
7. **Error-analysis:** the adult says "*terrible* means 'full of terror.'"
   "Am I right?" (Not exactly — *terrible* is *-ible* = "can be," and the
   root is an old Latin word; in modern English we treat *terrible* as
   "very bad," not literally "full of terror." Some Latin-suffix words you
   *read*; their exact part-meanings are history, not a rule.) "So what do
   we do with *terrible*?" (Read it with the *-ible* chunk, then learn its
   meaning as a whole word.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 7 tasks)

1. Break and read: *station, motion, readable, possible, payment,
   treatment*. (sta-tion, mo-tion, read-a-ble, pos-si-ble, pay-ment,
   treat-ment.)
2. Sort 12 suffix cards from the unit sets into three piles: Latin
   (-tion/-sion, -able/-ible, -ment), -ful/-less, -ness.
3. Meaning-match: *thankful, fearless, darkness, celebration* →
   "full of thanks" / "without fear" / "the state of being dark" / "the
   act of celebrating."
4. "Add *-ness* to *happy* and *sad*. What changed about the words?"
   (*happiness, sadness* — they became nouns naming a state or quality.)
5. "Add *-ful* to *pain* and *-less* to *hope*. Use both in one sentence."
6. Read *government* (gov-ern-ment) and *television* (tel-e-vi-sion);
   name the Latin suffix in each.
7. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why is *-tion* the easiest syllable in
   *celebration*?" (It always sounds the same — 'shun' — so once you know
   the chunk, you never have to guess it.)

## Application

"Suffix machine." The learner draws the three puzzle pieces (blue, yellow,
red) and runs 6 root words through the "machine": attach a red-piece suffix
to each (*help, care, kind, dark, hope, read* — learner's choice of
*-ful/-less/-ness/-able*), write the new word across the yellow and red
pieces, and write what the suffix did to the meaning. Then: build one
three-piece word (prefix + root + suffix, e.g., *unhelpful*) and explain
each piece's job.

## Exit check

The adult shows *decision* and *hopeless* (unpracticed today). The learner
breaks *decision* (de-ci-sion), names the Latin suffix and its sound, then
explains *hopeless* (*hope* + *-less* = without hope) and uses it in a
sentence. Success: both correct with the suffix job stated.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** Latin-suffix chunks pre-highlighted on
  cards; the learner reads the suffix chunk first, then the whole word.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort physical cards onto a printed copy of
  the puzzle image; dictate meanings for the adult to scribe.
- **Extension:** word families — *act, action, active, activity; decide,
  decision, decisive*. How does the spelling of the root change when the
  suffix arrives? (The adult notes this previews grade-4 morphology.)

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

Learners try to sound out *-tion* letter by letter ("t-i-o-n"). Teach it as
a single chunk from the start — "shun, one piece, always." They also treat
every *-ible* word as breakable into a modern root (*terrible* ≠ *terr* +
*ible* in any useful sense); distinguish suffixes you *use* (derivational)
from suffixes you *read* (Latin). Finally, *-ful* vs. *-less* get swapped —
anchor them as opposites with *careful/careless* on day one. See
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-03).
