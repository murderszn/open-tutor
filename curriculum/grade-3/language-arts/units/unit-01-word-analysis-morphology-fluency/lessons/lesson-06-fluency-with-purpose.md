# Lesson 06 — Fluency with Purpose: Accuracy, Rate, Expression, Self-Correction

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can read a story and a poem out loud so a
listener can follow and enjoy them — and fix myself when something sounds
wrong."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the two original passages below
(the adult prints them before session 8 — see the teacher note); a timer
or clock; paper and pencil.
**Standards:** RF.3.4.a, RF.3.4.b, RF.3.4.c, L.3.6.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult reads one sentence from "The Puzzle Box" twice: once flat and
robotic, once with expression. "Which reading would you rather listen to?
What did I change?" (Pace, voice, emphasis — the *expression*.) "Today we
practice reading like the second one. Fluency has three parts: **accuracy**
(the words are right), **rate** (the speed is comfortable — not racing, not
crawling), and **expression** (your voice shows the meaning)."

## Explicit teaching

Fluent reading is not fast reading. It is reading with **purpose and
understanding** (RF.3.4.a): the listener should hear the story, not the
struggle. The method is **repeated reading** — you read the same passage
three times, and each time has a job:

- **Read 1 — accuracy:** get the words right. Break multisyllable words
  (Lesson 01), use prefixes and suffixes (Lessons 02–03), recognize
  rule-breakers on sight (Lesson 05). It is fine to be slow.
- **Read 2 — rate:** smooth it out. Fewer stops and restarts. Aim for a
  storytelling pace — about as fast as talking.
- **Read 3 — expression:** perform it. Your voice rises for questions,
  gets loud for exclamations, pauses at commas and periods, and stresses
  the important words.

And the safety net for all three reads: **self-correction** (RF.3.4.c). When
something you read does not make sense or does not sound right, stop,
**reread** the sentence, and use context to fix it. Good readers fix
themselves constantly — catching your own error is a skill, not a failure.

While you read, notice the **temporal words** (*before, after, during*)
and **spatial words** (*between, beyond, across*) the author used — they
tell you *when* and *where* things happen (L.3.6). After reading, use two
of them in your own summary sentence.

### Modeled example 1 — self-correction in action

*Adult reads aloud from "The Puzzle Box," deliberately misreading:* "…They
packed a basket with sandwiches and *lemonade*." The adult stops. "Wait —
does that make sense? Lemonade in a basket — hmm, actually the word is
*lemonade*… let me reread: 'They packed a basket with sandwiches and
lemonade.' The sentence before said *picnic* — lemonade fits a picnic. But
did I read the word right? l-e-m-o-n-a-d-e — *lemonade*. Yes. That time the
check confirmed it. Now a real fix:" *Adult reads:* "…the bridge looked
*unsafe*…" *as "un-safe-uh."* "That did not sound right. Break it: un-safe.
*Unsafe* = not safe. Reread the sentence: 'the bridge looked unsafe.' Now
it makes sense — that is why they rebuilt it. I caught it because the
first try sounded wrong *and* did not fit the story."

### Modeled example 2 — expression marks in the poem

*Adult shows "Word Parts Parade."* "Poems give you expression instructions.
Exclamation points mean energy. The bold words are the stars — punch them a
little. Short lines mean: do not rush; let each line land." *Adult reads
the first four lines flat, then again with expression, and asks the learner
to name exactly what changed* (pauses at line ends, louder on *not!*,
slower on *slams the door*).

## The unit's original passages

Both passages below are original, written for this unit. The adult prints
them before session 8; the learner re-reads them in sessions 8, 13, and 16.

### "The Puzzle Box" (prose)

On Saturday, Maya found a small wooden box in the garden. It was locked,
and the key was gone.

"We will unlock it," said her brother Jon. He was careful with the rusty
latch. Inside the box sat a stack of paper, a magnet shaped like a
horseshoe, and a note.

The note said: "You are invited to a picnic across the river. Follow the
path between the tall trees. Look beyond the old bridge. The celebration
begins at noon."

Maya reread the note. "A celebration!" she said. "We must prepare!" They
packed a basket with sandwiches and lemonade. They were unhappy about one
problem: the bridge looked unsafe.

"We can rebuild the crossing with these boards," said Jon. He was
fearless. They worked until the job was finished, then crossed the river
together.

On the other side, their friends were waiting with games and music. It was
the best day of the summer — though Maya never learned who left the box.

### "Word Parts Parade" (poem)

Word parts are marching, hear them play —
**un-** says *"not!"* and marches away.
**re-** runs back to try once more,
**dis-** disagrees and slams the door!
**pre-** peeks in *before* the start,
yellow roots play the biggest part.
**-ful** is full and **-less** is bare,
**-ness** is floating in the air.
**-tion** goes *"shun!"* in celebration —
break each word, no hesitation!
Read it fast, then read it slow,
now the word parts steal the show!

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 6 tasks)

1. **Read 1 of "The Puzzle Box"** (paragraphs 1–2): accuracy only. The
   adult marks errors lightly; the learner breaks *garden, unlock,
   careful* as needed.
2. **Read 2 of the same paragraphs:** smoother, storytelling pace. "What
   got easier?" (Naming one improvement is the point.)
3. **Self-correction drill:** the adult covers *celebration* in paragraph
   3 and the learner reads the sentence with a blank, predicts from
   context (*a party word*), uncovers, breaks it (cel-e-bra-tion), and
   rereads the sentence fluently.
4. **Poem lines 1–4, twice:** first flat, then with expression. The learner
   names two expression choices they made (louder on *not!*, pause after
   *door!*).
5. **Temporal/spatial hunt:** "Find two temporal words and two spatial
   words in the story." (*before* — "reread the note" … actually: *until*,
   *then*, *never*; spatial: *across, between, beyond*.) The learner uses
   one of each in a summary sentence: "After they crossed the river,
   they celebrated beyond the bridge."
6. **Error-analysis:** the adult reads "He was *careful* with the rusty
   latch" as "He was *careless*…" and keeps going. "Should I have kept
   going?" (No — *careless* contradicts the gentle handling; stop,
   reread, self-correct to *careful*. Meaning monitors accuracy.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 6 tasks)

1. **Read 3 of "The Puzzle Box"** (paragraphs 1–4): full expression read.
   Adult notes: ≤4 uncorrected errors, visible pausing at punctuation.
2. Read "Word Parts Parade" aloud twice; the second time, honor every
   exclamation point and bold word.
3. **Self-correction log:** during the reads, the learner taps the table
   each time they catch and fix their own error; the adult counts taps —
   2+ taps is the success mark (catching errors is the skill).
4. Time the story paragraphs 1–4 on Read 3 (adult times, learner reads).
   Record words-correct — the number goes in the notebook as a baseline
   for session 16, never as a grade or a comparison.
5. Write a 2-sentence summary of the story using one temporal and one
   spatial word.
6. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why did we read the same passage three
   times instead of three different passages?" (Each read has a job —
   accuracy, then rate, then expression. New passages would restart the
   struggle; repeats build the smoothness.)

## Application

"Storyteller's chair." The learner performs "Word Parts Parade" for the
adult (or a family member) from the storyteller's chair — standing or
sitting tall, poem in hand, full expression. Afterwards the listener names
one thing the reader did well (a specific expression choice) and one
puzzle-piece word they heard (*unhappy? celebration? reread?*). The learner
may record the performance (adult-supervised device) and compare Read 1
vs. Read 3 — the recording stays in private storage.

## Exit check

The learner reads paragraph 5 of "The Puzzle Box" ("We can rebuild…")
aloud: accuracy (breaks *rebuild* correctly), rate (storytelling pace),
expression (energy on the dialogue), and at least one audible
self-correction *or* a stated "that sounded right" check. Success: ≤1
uncorrected error and the self-monitoring is visible.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult pre-marks breaks in multisyllable
  words; the learner may echo-read (adult reads a line, learner repeats
  with the same expression).
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may record instead of performing
  live; expression choices may be marked on the printed poem with arrows
  and stars instead of performed.
- **Extension:** write two more rhyming couplets for "Word Parts Parade"
  using *-sion* and *-ment* words (*television, enjoyment*), keeping the
  bold-word pattern.

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

Learners equate fluency with speed and start racing — redirect: "A
storyteller's pace, not a race." They also skip self-correction because it
feels like failing; praise every caught error explicitly ("Great catch —
that is exactly what good readers do"). Finally, expression without
accuracy is just noise — Read 1 accuracy gates the performance reads; if
accuracy is below ~90%, stay on Read 1–2 and postpone the performance.

both passages before **session 8**; see the teacher guide's session notes.
