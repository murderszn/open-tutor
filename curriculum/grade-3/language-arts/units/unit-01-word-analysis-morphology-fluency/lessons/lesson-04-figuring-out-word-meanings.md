# Lesson 04 — Word Detective: Context Clues, Affixes, and Roots

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can figure out a new word's meaning using
clues in the sentence, word parts I know, or a root word I know."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** the unit word sets; word cards;
paper and pencil.
**Standards:** L.3.4.a, L.3.4.b, L.3.4.c.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult reads: "The *gloopy* mud sucked at our boots." "What do you think
*gloopy* means?" (Sticky, gooey — something like that.) "How do you know?"
(The sentence tells you: mud that sucks at boots.) "You just used a
**context clue** — information in the sentence that hints at a word's
meaning. Today you become a word detective with three tools."

## Explicit teaching

When you meet a word you do not know, detectives try the tools in order:

**Tool 1 — Sentence-level context (L.3.4.a).** Read the whole sentence —
sometimes the sentence before and after, too. Look for:
- A *definition hiding in the sentence*: "The arboretum, a garden of trees,
  was quiet." (The commas tell you what an arboretum is.)
- An *example*: "Nocturnal animals, like owls and bats, sleep during the
  day." (The examples show you the meaning.)
- A *contrast*: "Unlike her timid brother, she was bold." (*Unlike* and
  *timid* point to bold's meaning.)

**Tool 2 — A known affix on a known word (L.3.4.b).** Strip the prefix or
suffix, read the root, and rebuild the meaning: *dis* + *agree* = not agree;
*care* + *less* = without care; *pre* + *heat* = heat before. (You practiced
this in Lessons 02–03; now it is a detective tool.)

**Tool 3 — A known root word (L.3.4.c).** Sometimes you know a *relative* of
the word — a word with the same root. If you know *company*, you can guess
*companion* (someone you keep company with). If you know *sign*, you can
guess *signature* (your sign — the mark you write). If you know *port* from
*transport* (carry across), you can guess *portable* (can be carried).

The detective's rule: **make a prediction, then check it.** Read the
sentence again with your predicted meaning in place. If it fits, keep it. If
not, try the next tool. And if all three tools fail, that is what
dictionaries are for (Lesson 05) — a good detective knows when to look it
up.

### Modeled example 1 — context (Tool 1)

*Adult writes:* "After the storm, the streets were **treacherous**, so we
stayed inside."
"Tool 1: the sentence. *After the storm* … *so we stayed inside*. Streets
you stay inside because of must be dangerous. Prediction: *treacherous* =
dangerous, unsafe. Check: 'the streets were dangerous, so we stayed inside'
— fits. Detective verdict: dangerous."
The adult points out the clue words: *so we stayed inside* is the evidence,
and the evidence goes in the notebook, not just the guess.

### Modeled example 2 — affix, then root (Tools 2 and 3)

*Adult writes:* "The hikers were **unprepared** for the sudden rain."
"Tool 1 first: the sentence — *sudden rain*, hikers caught by it. Maybe
'unready'? Tool 2 to confirm: strip the affixes — *un* + *pre* + *pare*…
*prepare* is the root I know, *pre-* = before, *un-* = not. Not prepared
before(hand) = not ready. Both tools agree: *unprepared* = not ready.
Check: 'The hikers were not ready for the sudden rain' — fits."
*Adult writes:* "She put her **signature** on the letter." "Tool 3: I know
*sign*. *Signature* has the same root (*sign*). A signature is your sign —
the way you write your name. Check: 'She put her name-mark on the letter'
— fits."

## Guided practice (adult does it with the learner; 7 tasks)

1. "The **frigid** wind made us zip our coats. Tool 1 — what is the
   evidence?" (*Made us zip our coats* → very cold.) "Prediction?" (Very
   cold.) "Check it in the sentence." (Fits.)
2. "Unlike the **timid** mouse, the **bold** lion roared. What does *bold*
   mean?" (The opposite of timid — brave, not shy.) "Which clue type?"
   (Contrast.)
3. "He was **fearless** on the high dive. Tool 2." (*fear* + *-less* =
   without fear.) "Check: 'He was without fear on the high dive.'" (Fits.)
4. "The **portable** radio went everywhere with us. Tool 3 — what root do
   you know?" (*Port* — carry — from *transport*.) "So *portable* = ?"
   (Able to be carried.) "Check it." (Fits.)
5. "The scientist made a careful **observation** of the ants. Tools in
   order." (Tool 1: *careful … of the ants* → a careful looking. Tool 2:
   *observe* + *-ation* → the act of observing. Verdict: watching closely
   and noticing.)
6. **Tool-choice:** "The **ancient** castle had stood for a thousand years.
   Which tool is fastest?" (Tool 1 — *stood for a thousand years* defines
   it: very old.) "Do we need the other tools?" (No — one strong clue is
   enough; detectives do not overwork a case.)
7. **Error-analysis:** the adult says, "'The **bark** of the tree was
   rough' — *bark* means the sound a dog makes, so the tree was making dog
   sounds!" "What is wrong with my detective work?" (I used the wrong
   meaning of a multiple-meaning word and ignored the sentence. Tool 1
   says *rough* describes the tree's covering — so *bark* here is the
   tree's skin. Context picks *which* meaning.)

## Independent practice (learner tries; adult observes, 7 tasks)

1. "The **weary** travelers sat down at once." Predict and cite evidence.
   (Tired — *sat down at once*.)
2. "She felt **relief** when the test ended." Predict and cite evidence.
   (The worried feeling going away — *when the test ended*.)
3. "The **prehistoric** cave paintings are very old. Tool 2." (*pre-* =
   before + *historic* → before written history; very ancient.)
4. "A **companion** sat beside him on the bus. Tool 3." (*Company* → one
   who keeps you company; a friend alongside.)
5. "Unlike his **generous** sister, he kept all the cookies. What does
   *generous* mean?" (Giving, sharing — the contrast with keeping.)
6. Write one sentence that *defines* a made-up word with a hidden
   definition, one with an example clue, and one with a contrast clue —
   three sentences, three clue types.
7. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Why do detectives check the prediction in
   the sentence instead of stopping at the first guess?" (Because a first
   guess can be wrong — especially with multiple-meaning words — and the
   check is what makes it evidence, not guessing.)

## Application

"Cold case files." The learner gets 5 "case cards" (adult-written sentences
with one bolded unfamiliar word each, drawn from the unit word sets and
lesson passages). For each: name the tool used, write the prediction, quote
the evidence, and write the checked sentence with the prediction swapped in.
One case must use Tool 3 (root). Cases are filed in the learner's notebook —
they become entries in the [project dictionary](../project.md).

## Exit check

The adult reads: "The **diligent** student finished her work before playing,
while her brother waited until the last minute." The learner predicts
*diligent* (hardworking, careful — the *before playing* and the contrast
with the brother are the evidence), names the tool (context: example +
contrast), and checks the prediction in the sentence. Success: prediction
correct with evidence quoted.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads each case sentence aloud;
  clue words may be underlined by the adult first.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may answer orally while the
  adult scribes the "case file."
- **Extension:** shades of meaning — *glad, happy, delighted, thrilled*.
  Put them in order from weakest to strongest feeling. (This previews
  L.3.5.c work in later units and deepens L.3.6 vocabulary.)

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

Learners guess from the word's "feel" instead of citing evidence — insist on
the quoted clue every time: "Show me the words that told you." They also
apply Tool 2 to non-affixed words (*under* again) — the removal test from
Lesson 02 applies here too. And they stop after one tool when the first
prediction fails; teach tool-switching explicitly: "Tool 1 gave nothing —
what is Tool 2?" See [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-04).
