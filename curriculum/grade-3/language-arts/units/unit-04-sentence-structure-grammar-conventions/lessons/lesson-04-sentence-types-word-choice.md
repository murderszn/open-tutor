# Lesson 04 — Simple, Compound, and Complex Sentences; Word Choice; Spoken vs. Written

**Goal (student-readable):** I can build simple, compound, and complex
sentences, choose stronger words, and turn spoken style into written
style.
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper, word cards (optional).
**Standards:** L.3.1.i, L.3.3.a–b.

## Readiness check (2 minutes)

The adult says: "Tell me about the park in one short sentence. Now
tell me about it in a longer sentence with two ideas." The learner
answers in their own words. The adult notes whether the longer
sentence joins its ideas or just runs them together.

## Explicit teaching

### Three sentence types

A **clause** is a group of words with a subject and a verb. An
**independent clause** can stand alone as a sentence. A **dependent
clause** cannot — it leaves the listener waiting ("Because the wind
blew… blew what?").

- **Simple sentence** — one independent clause.
  "Maya flies her kite."
- **Compound sentence** — two independent clauses joined by a
  coordinating conjunction (*and, but, or, so*), with a comma before
  the conjunction. "Maya flies her kite, **and** Sam watches the
  clouds."
- **Complex sentence** — one independent clause plus one dependent
  clause, joined by a subordinating conjunction (*because, when,
  before, after, if, while*). "Maya flies her kite **when** the wind
  blows."

Two errors to spot: a **fragment** is missing a subject or a verb, or
is only a dependent clause ("Because the wind blew."). A **run-on**
smashes two sentences together with no joining word or punctuation
("It was windy we flew kites.").

### Choosing words for effect

Strong words paint a clearer picture. "The dog went fast" tells little;
"The dog **dashed** across the grass" shows speed. "We had a nice day"
could mean almost anything; "We had a **sunny, breezy** day" shows the
weather. When revising, ask: "Does this word show the reader what I
mean, or just fill space?"

### Spoken vs. written standard English

Spoken English is looser: "We're gonna fly kites, yeah?" Written
standard English spells words fully and finishes the thought: "We are
going to fly kites." Neither way of speaking is wrong at home or with
friends — but school writing follows the written conventions, so the
learner practices switching on purpose.

## Worked example 1 — one idea, three shapes

Start: "Maya flies her kite."

- Simple: "Maya flies her kite." (One clause.)
- Compound: "Maya flies her kite, **and** Sam watches the clouds."
  (Two clauses that could each stand alone; comma + *and*.)
- Complex: "Maya flies her kite **when** the wind blows."
  (*When the wind blows* cannot stand alone — it is the dependent
  clause, and *when* is the subordinating conjunction.)

## Worked example 2 — stronger words, written style

- Weak: "The dog went fast." → Stronger: "The dog **dashed** across
  the grass." (A precise verb replaces a vague verb + adverb.)
- Weak: "It was really, really fun." → Stronger: "It was
  **thrilling**." (One exact word replaces repeated vague words.)
- Spoken: "We're gonna fly kites, yeah?" → Written: "We are going
  to fly kites." (Full forms, finished sentence, no filler word.)

## Guided practice (adult helps)

1. Label each sentence *simple*, *compound*, *complex*, or *not a
   sentence*: "The sun shines." · "The sun shines, but the wind is
   cold." · "We stay inside when it rains." ·
   "Because the wind blew."
2. Fix each error: "Runs across the park." ·
   "It was windy we flew kites."
3. Replace the weak word with a stronger one: "We had a nice day."
   (Sample stronger versions are accepted; see the answer key.)
4. Rewrite in written style: "We're gonna fly kites, yeah?"

## Independent practice

1. Label each sentence *simple*, *compound*, *complex*, or *not a
   sentence*: "Lin reads a book." · "Lin reads a book, and Maya
   draws." · "Maya draws while Lin reads." · "After lunch."
2. Combine into one sentence: "The kite rose. The wind blew."
   (Compound or complex — either is accepted if the joining is
   correct.)
3. Replace the weak word with a stronger one: "The dog is big."
4. Rewrite in written style: "I dunno where my kite is."

## Applied task — three shapes, one afternoon

Write three sentences about a park afternoon: one simple, one
compound, and one complex. Label each type. Then circle your
strongest word and tell the adult why you chose it.

## Exit check

Label: "Sam sings because he is happy." Then fix: "Went to the park."

## Supports and extension

- **Reading access:** the adult reads each sentence aloud with a
  pause at each clause boundary.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner builds sentences from
  clause cards (independent cards in one color, dependent cards in
  another) instead of writing them.
- **Extension:** take a four-sentence paragraph of short, choppy
  sentences and rewrite it using one compound and one complex
  sentence, keeping every fact.
- **Error-analysis note:** a comma alone cannot join two sentences
  ("It was windy, we flew kites."). That is a comma splice: add a
  conjunction or split the sentences.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: longer always means complex. Length is not the
test — clause type is. A long simple sentence ("The happy brown dog
with floppy ears runs quickly across the wide green park.") is still
simple: one clause. Full solutions are in the
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
