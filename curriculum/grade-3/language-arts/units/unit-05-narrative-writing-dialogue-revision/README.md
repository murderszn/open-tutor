# Unit 05 — Narrative Writing: Dialogue and Revision

**Grade:** 3 · **Subject:** Language Arts · **Track weeks:** 19–22
**Backlog:** [issue #20](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/20), section U05
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

A story is not a list of things that happened. It is a situation with
people in it, events that follow one another in an order the reader can
trust, characters who speak and think and feel, and an ending that answers
the question the beginning asked. In this unit the learner writes one —
from a plan, through a draft, through revision, to a finished story they
can read aloud. The model story, "The Kite in the Tree," is about Maya,
her dog Biscuit, her friend Leo, and a red kite that gets away from them
in the park. The learner first studies how that story is built, then
builds one of their own: a beginning that sets the scene, events joined
by time words, dialogue that shows what characters feel, descriptions
that show instead of tell, and a closing that lands. Unit 04 taught the
punctuation of dialogue; this unit puts that punctuation to work inside
real stories.

## Essential question

How do I turn something that happened — real or imagined — into a story
someone else can follow, feel, and finish?

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–04 and the diagnostic weeks (track weeks 1–18):

- Read grade-level text aloud with accuracy and expression on repeated
  readings; self-correct with context (RF.3.4.a–c)
- Recount a story and name its central message; describe characters
  (traits, motivations, feelings) and explain how their actions move
  events along (RL.3.2–3, Unit 02) — the same character work, now done
  as a writer instead of a reader
- Determine the main idea and key details of a passage (RI.3.2,
  Unit 03) — a story's events are its details, and they must all
  belong to the same story
- Produce simple, compound, and complex sentences; choose words and
  phrases for effect (L.3.1.i, L.3.3.a, Unit 04)
- Use commas and quotation marks in dialogue; capitalize titles;
  spell studied words and suffix additions conventionally; consult a
  beginning dictionary (L.3.2.a–g, Unit 04) — dialogue punctuation was
  taught in Unit 04, Lesson 05 (draft PR #299); this unit applies it
  and does not re-teach it from scratch
- Write short pieces with a beginning, middle, and end, and revise
  with adult guidance (W.3.3, W.3.5, from Unit 02 writing routines)

Nothing in this unit assumes the learner has written a multi-paragraph
story before. Lesson 01 re-teaches "narrative" from scratch: a narrative
tells about real or imagined events in an order, with people who do,
say, think, and feel things.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. Establish a situation and introduce a narrator and/or characters,
   and organize an event sequence that unfolds naturally — beginning,
   middle, end — with each event following from the one before it
   (W.3.3, W.3.3.a).
2. Use dialogue and descriptions of actions, thoughts, and feelings to
   develop experiences and events, or to show how characters respond
   to situations (W.3.3.b).
3. Use temporal words and phrases — *first, next, then, after that,
   later, suddenly, finally, in the morning, at last* — to signal
   event order (W.3.3.c).
4. Provide a sense of closure: an ending that resolves the story's
   problem and tells how the characters end up feeling (W.3.3.d).
5. Choose words and phrases for effect — strong verbs, exact feeling
   words, and specific details — and punctuate dialogue with commas
   and quotation marks, starting a new paragraph when the speaker
   changes (L.3.3.a, L.3.2.c).
6. Plan, revise, and edit a narrative with peer and adult guidance —
   adding, removing, moving, and swapping words and sentences — and
   produce a finished story over an extended time frame, reading it
   aloud at an understandable pace (W.3.5, W.3.10, SL.3.4).

## Unit stories (all original, written for this unit)

Every story and passage in this unit was written for it. No published
story, textbook passage, or worksheet is reproduced. The model story
uses a fictional girl, Maya, and her dog Biscuit — the same Maya whose
dialogue was punctuated in Unit 04, Lesson 05, so the learner meets a
familiar character and now writes her story.

- **"The Kite in the Tree"** (Lessons 01–06, practice, project model):
  Maya and Biscuit fly a red kite in the park; a gust of wind pulls
  the kite away and tangles it in the oak tree; Maya tries and fails
  to reach it; her friend Leo arrives and they make a plan; with Ms.
  Alvarez, the park keeper, watching from the bench, Maya pulls the
  string gently and the kite floats free. The story is told in full
  across Lessons 01–05, one part per lesson, so the learner sees each
  craft move in the same story.
- **"The Kite in the Tree" — weak draft** (Lesson 06, Practice F):
  the same story told badly — no dialogue, no time words, a flat
  ending — for the learner to revise.
- **"The Lost Library Book"** (culminating assessment): a new short
  narrative about a boy named Theo who loses a library book, finds it
  in the wrong place, and returns it — for reading and analysis only.
- **Story starters** (Practice A, project): the kite illustration and
  three one-line starters the learner may use or ignore.

## 16-session pacing (track weeks 19–22; sessions 25–30 minutes)

| Session | Activity | Success criterion |
|---|---|---|
| Week 19, S1 | Lesson 01 — Establish a situation; introduce characters | Learner names the who, where, when, and what of the kite story and orders its five events |
| Week 19, S2 | Practice A — Situation and event order | Learner writes a beginning that names characters and setting and lists events in order |
| Week 19, S3 | Story-map planning: learner chooses a project story and maps situation, characters, events, problem, ending | Story map has a situation, two characters, three events in order, and a problem |
| Week 19, S4 | Lesson 02 — Temporal words and event order | Learner joins four events with four different time words and fixes a scrambled sequence |
| Week 20, S5 | Lesson 03 — Dialogue that develops the story | Learner writes two dialogue exchanges that show feeling and move the story, punctuated correctly |
| Week 20, S6 | Practice C — Dialogue writing and fixing | Dialogue has quotation marks, commas, speaker tags, and a new paragraph per speaker |
| Week 20, S7 | Word workshop: feeling words and shades of meaning (worried/scared/terrified; happy/glad/thrilled) | Learner picks the exact feeling word for three situations and explains the difference |
| Week 20, S8 | Lesson 04 — Actions, thoughts, and feelings (show, don't tell) | Learner rewrites two "told" feelings as shown actions or thoughts |
| Week 21, S9 | Project drafting: first draft of "My Own Story" (beginning, middle, end) | Draft has a beginning, three events in order, one dialogue exchange, and an ending attempt |
| Week 21, S10 | Lesson 05 — Closure and word choice for effect | Learner writes a closure that resolves the problem and swaps three weak words for exact ones |
| Week 21, S11 | Practice E — Closures and exact words; revise own draft's ending | Draft ending answers "how did it end and how did the character feel?" |
| Week 21, S12 | Lesson 06 — Plan, revise, and edit with guidance | Learner makes at least three revisions to the weak draft and three to their own draft, using the checklist |
| Week 22, S13 | Project finish: final copy, title, illustration plan; share circle — read aloud | Finished story read aloud at an understandable pace; one listener question answered |
| Week 22, S14 | Formative quiz (week-22 quiz) | Adult scores with the answer key; reteach list written the same day |

## Differentiation and supports

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the model story aloud first; the
  learner follows along, then rereads. Any lesson passage may be
  chunked into paragraphs with a pause-and-retell after each.
- **Response modes:** answers may be spoken, pointed to, drawn, or
  written. A learner who cannot yet write a multi-paragraph story may
  dictate it while the adult scribes, then copy one paragraph
  themselves. The project allows a dictated first draft with a
  handwritten or typed final copy.
- **Writing support:** a personal word bank card for each lesson's new
  words (time words, said words, feeling words); a story-map template
  the learner fills with single words or short phrases, never whole
  sentences, so the plan stays a plan and not a first draft.
- **Extension:** fast finishers write a second ending for the kite
  story and argue which is stronger; add a second character's
  thoughts to a scene; or turn the kite story into a four-panel comic
  strip with dialogue in speech bubbles (see the ReadWriteThink
  lesson in the Resource Pack).
- **What to reteach:** if the learner's events do not follow from one
  another, return to Lesson 01's "what happens because of what"
  test before moving on; if dialogue is only "he said / she said,"
  return to Lesson 03's "dialogue must show a feeling or move the
  story" test. Do not let a shaky beginning ride into Lessons 05–06.
