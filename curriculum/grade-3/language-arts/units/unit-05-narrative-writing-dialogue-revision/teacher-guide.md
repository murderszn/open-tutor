# Teacher Guide — Unit 05: Narrative Writing: Dialogue and Revision

**Audience:** the guiding adult only. This guide explains the teaching
moves, the misconceptions behind the most common wrong answers, and what
to reteach when. It does not contain the learner's answers — those are in
the [answer key](answer-key.md), kept separate so a learner browsing the
unit does not stumble onto solutions.

## How to teach this unit

- **Read the model story aloud first, every time.** The kite story is
  told in five parts across Lessons 01–05. The adult reads each part
  aloud once while the learner follows, then the learner rereads. Never
  let decoding difficulty masquerade as a writing failure.
- **One craft move per lesson, revisited everywhere.** Lesson 01's
  beginning returns in every later lesson; Lesson 03's dialogue returns
  in the project and the assessment. If Lesson 01 is shaky, later
  lessons will look shaky too — fix Lesson 01, not Lesson 06.
- **Revision is a separate pass from editing — enforce the order.**
  Learners will want to fix spelling first. Do not let them: spelling
  fixes on sentences that get deleted are wasted work, and the habit is
  harder to break later than it is to build now.
- **Sessions are 25–30 minutes.** If a lesson runs long, split it at
  the guided-practice boundary and finish the next session; do not rush
  the modeled examples, which carry the teaching.
- **The same character returns.** Maya's dialogue was punctuated in
  Unit 04, Lesson 05 ("Maya said, 'Let's fly kites.'"). This unit gives
  that same Maya a whole story. If the learner remembers the kite line,
  name the connection — it shows that grammar was never an end in
  itself.

## The weak draft (Lesson 06, Practice F) — repeated here for the adult

> Maya went to the park. It was nice. She had a kite. The kite was red.
> The kite got stuck in a tree. The wind was strong. Maya was sad. Leo
> came. They got the kite down. They went home. It was a good day. The
> end.

Note the planted flaws: no dialogue; no time words; the wind sentence
sits *after* the tangle (cause after effect); "It was nice" belongs to
no story; "was sad" only names a feeling; tired words throughout
(*went, got, nice, good*); the ending is a stop sign.

## Lesson notes

### Lesson 01 — Establish a situation and introduce characters

- **Core move:** the four questions (Who? Where? When? What?) and the
  "because" test on event order.
- **Common wrong answer:** a list of facts as a beginning ("The park is
  big. Trees are green."). Response: ask "Who is *doing* something?"
  A situation needs people in motion.
- **Common wrong answer:** events in any order ("The kite tangled,
  then Maya flew it"). Response: run the "because" test on each pair;
  a kite cannot tangle before it flies.
- **Reteach path:** if the learner cannot order five events, drop to
  three (walk to park → fly kite → kite tangles), act them out in
  order, then rebuild to five.

### Lesson 02 — Temporal words and event order

- **Core move:** time words label order; they do not create it. Check
  the order first, then add the words.
- **Common wrong answer:** *then* at the start of every sentence.
  Response: praise the order, then ask for one different word per
  sentence, using the time-word card.
- **Common wrong answer:** *finally* for the first event. Response:
  *finally* means "last of all" — ask "Is this the last thing in your
  story?" If not, it cannot be *finally*.
- **Reteach path:** if time words are used randomly, remove them all
  and have the learner retell the events in order with no time words;
  then add one time word per sentence, checking each one against its
  place.

### Lesson 03 — Dialogue that develops the story

- **Core move:** the three-jobs test — dialogue must show a feeling,
  move the story, or show who a character is. Cut decoration.
- **Common wrong answer:** perfectly punctuated decoration ("Hi," said
  Maya. "Hi," said Leo.). Response: ask "What did that exchange
  change?" If nothing, it goes.
- **Common wrong answer:** dialogue as announcement ("I am worried,"
  said Maya.). Response: act it out — nobody talks like that. What
  would Maya *actually* say? ("Oh no! My kite!")
- **Reteach path:** if the learner cannot write dialogue that does a
  job, act out the scene first with the adult, then write down exactly
  what was said. Speech bubbles over the illustration work well here.

### Lesson 04 — Actions, thoughts, and feelings

- **Core move:** the "could I draw this?" test for showing vs. telling.
- **Common wrong answer:** thoughts in quotation marks. Response:
  quotation marks mean spoken aloud. Thoughts use italics or "she
  thought" — model both, and accept either.
- **Common wrong answer:** the strongest feeling word for every
  situation ("terrified" about a stuck kite). Response: ask "Is anyone
  in danger?" The word must match the situation, or the reader stops
  believing the story.
- **Reteach path:** if showing is hard, start with the body: "What do
  her hands do? What does her face do? What does the dog do?" Body
  first, feeling word second.

### Lesson 05 — Closure and word choice for effect

- **Core move:** the two closure questions — does it resolve the
  problem, and does it say how the character feels?
- **Common wrong answer:** "The end" as an ending. Response: run the
  two questions on it. "The end" answers neither.
- **Common wrong answer:** fancy words that do not fit ("Maya
  ejaculated"). Response: exact means *fitting*, not long. A
  third-grader who "yelled" is right; one who "ejaculated" is wrong.
- **Reteach path:** if closures are stop signs, finish this sentence
  first: "In the end, ___, and Maya felt ___." Then expand it to two
  sentences.

### Lesson 06 — Plan, revise, and edit

- **Core move:** three passes in order — plan, revise (ARMS), edit.
  Never edit before revising.
- **Common wrong answer:** revision = fixing spelling. Response: show
  the learner a sentence they spell-checked and then deleted. The
  wasted work makes the point better than any explanation.
- **Common wrong answer:** feedback as a list of everything wrong.
  Response: model Praise–Question–Polish on the learner's draft first,
  so they feel what useful feedback sounds like before they give it.
- **Reteach path:** if four ARMS moves at once overwhelm, do one move
  per read-through — Adds first, then Removes, then Moves, then Swaps.

## Readiness signals

- **Ready for Unit 06 (opinion writing):** the project story has a
  beginning answering all four questions, events in order with time
  words, at least one dialogue exchange doing a job, a closure
  answering both questions, and visible ARMS revision marks (project
  rubric 32–40 points). The revision routine transfers directly to
  opinion writing.
- **Reteach before Unit 06:** if the learner's events do not follow
  from one another, or dialogue is only "he said / she said," or the
  ending is a stop sign — revisit Lessons 01, 03, or 05 respectively
  with the reteach paths above. Do not carry a shaky narrative
  foundation into opinion writing, where the same revision routine
  will be needed for reasons and evidence.
- **What "ready" does not mean:** the story does not need to be long,
  and spelling does not need to be perfect. A three-paragraph story
  with a real beginning, a real problem, one good line of dialogue, and
  a real ending is ready. Length and polish come later.

## Answer-key use

The answer key gives sample wording for every item. Open responses
accept any wording that carries the same meaning, the same evidence,
and the same craft move; the samples show the meaning and the move
required. For dialogue and revision items, the punctuation and the job
the dialogue does both count — a correctly punctuated line that does no
job earns partial credit, and the key says so where it applies.
