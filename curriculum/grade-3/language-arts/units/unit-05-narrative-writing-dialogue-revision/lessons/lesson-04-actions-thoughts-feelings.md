# Lesson 04 — Actions, Thoughts, and Feelings

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can show how a character feels by what
they do and think — not just by naming the feeling."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper and pencil; the feeling-word
bank below (copy it onto a card for the learner to keep).
**Standards:** W.3.3.b, L.3.5.c.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "If I say 'Maya was scared,' do you *see* anything? If
I say 'Maya's hands shook, and she stepped back from the tree,' do you
see something now?" The learner answers. "Naming a feeling tells the
reader a fact. Showing what the character *does* with that feeling lets
the reader see it. Today we trade naming for showing."

## Explicit teaching

Writers develop a character three ways, and each one shows the reader
something different:

1. **Actions** — what the character *does*. (Maya jumped and grabbed at
   the lowest branch. Biscuit barked and ran in circles.) Actions are
   the most visible evidence of a feeling.
2. **Thoughts** — what the character *thinks*, in their own words.
   (Maya thought, *What if it never comes down?* / *Maybe Leo will
   know what to do.*) Thoughts are written in italics or as a sentence
   starting "Maya thought" — never in quotation marks, because the
   character is not speaking aloud.
3. **Feelings, named exactly** — sometimes you *do* name a feeling, but
   with the exact word, not a vague one. Feelings come in shades, like
   colors:

   - *worried → scared → terrified* (each one stronger than the last)
   - *glad → happy → thrilled*
   - *tired → sleepy → exhausted*
   - *mad → angry → furious*

   Choosing the exact shade is called **shades of meaning**: words that
   are related but not equal. "Maya was terrified" is not the same as
   "Maya was worried" — terrified is much stronger, and the reader must
   believe the situation earns it.

The **show, don't tell** test: read a sentence and ask, "Could I draw
this?" You can draw "Maya's shoulders sank" and "Biscuit ran in
circles." You cannot draw "Maya was sad" — sadness has no picture until
you show what it does to the body. If you cannot draw it, show it.

## Model story — "The Kite in the Tree," Part 4 (original, written for this unit)

*Adult reads aloud; the learner follows along.*

> Maya jumped and grabbed at the lowest branch, but the kite was far
> above her fingers. Her shoulders sank. *What if it never comes
> down?* she thought. *It was my birthday kite.* Biscuit barked and
> ran in circles around the tree, as if he could help by being loud.
> Then Maya took a deep breath. *Leo is coming,* she thought. *Leo
> always has a plan.*

### Modeled example 1 — One feeling, three ways

*Adult thinks aloud:* "The feeling here is worry. Watch it appear three
ways. Action: 'Maya jumped and grabbed at the lowest branch' — she is
trying, not giving up — and then 'Her shoulders sank' — the trying did
not work. Thought: '*What if it never comes down?*' — that question *is*
the worry, in her own words. And Biscuit's action — barking and running
in circles — shows the same worry in the dog, without anyone saying
'Biscuit was worried.' Three showings, zero namings, and you know exactly
how Maya feels."

### Modeled example 2 — Shades of meaning change the story

*Adult writes three versions of one sentence:*

- "Maya was worried about the kite."
- "Maya was scared about the kite."
- "Maya was terrified about the kite."

"Which one is true for our story? *Worried* fits — the kite is stuck,
but nobody is in danger. *Scared* is stronger, and could fit if Maya
thought the kite was lost forever. *Terrified* is wrong — terrified is
for real danger, and a stuck kite is not dangerous. If you pick a word
stronger than the situation, the reader stops believing you. Pick the
shade that matches what actually happened."

## Guided practice

The adult reads each item aloud; the learner answers orally or in writing.
Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04--actions-thoughts-and-feelings).

1. In Part 4, find one action, one thought, and one feeling-showing
   action by Biscuit. Quote or retell each one.
2. Rewrite this "telling" sentence so it shows the feeling with an
   action: "Leo was excited to help."
3. Rewrite this "telling" sentence so it shows the feeling with a
   thought: "Maya was hopeful."
4. Choose the exact feeling word, and explain why the other two are
   wrong for this situation: "When the kite floated down safely, Maya
   felt ___." (a) terrified (b) glad (c) thrilled
5. Which sentence shows a feeling, and which only names it?
   (a) "Maya was happy." (b) "Maya laughed and spun in a circle."
   Explain how you can tell.
6. Error analysis: Sam wrote, "Maya thought, 'I am worried about the
   kite.'" What is wrong with how Sam wrote the thought, and how
   should it look? (Hint: two problems — the quotation marks, and the
   announcing.)

## Independent practice

7. Show, don't tell: rewrite "Ms. Alvarez was proud of Maya" as one
   sentence about what Ms. Alvarez *does* (an action the reader can
   draw).
8. Order these feeling words from weakest to strongest: *furious,
   mad, angry.* Then use the strongest one in a sentence about a
   character who has lost something precious — and say why the
   strongest word fits.
9. Write one thought for Leo, in his own words, at the moment he sees
   the kite stuck in the tree. Remember: thoughts are not in quotation
   marks.

## Application

Choose one moment from the kite story — the wind yanking the string,
Maya reaching and missing, or the kite floating down. Write three
sentences about that moment: one action, one thought, one exact feeling
word. Read them to the adult, who checks: can each sentence be drawn,
and does the feeling word match the situation?

## Exit check

In your own words: what is the "show, don't tell" test? Name one action,
one thought, and one feeling word from today's part of the story.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** keep the feeling-word shades on a card (worried →
  scared → terrified; glad → happy → thrilled; tired → sleepy →
  exhausted; mad → angry → furious). If showing is hard, start with
  the body: "What do her hands do? What does her face do?"
- **Alternate response mode:** act out a feeling (shoulders sinking,
  jumping, running in circles) and let the learner guess it before
  writing; or draw the action and write the sentence under the drawing.
- **Extension:** rewrite Part 4 from Biscuit's point of view — what
  does the dog *do*, and what might the dog *think*? Argue with the
  adult about whether a dog can have thoughts in a story (yes — in a
  story, the writer decides).

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) thoughts go in quotation marks — they do
not; quotation marks mean spoken aloud. Teach italics or "she thought"
as the signal. (2) Stronger feeling words are always better — no; the
word must match the situation, or the reader stops believing the story.
(3) Showing means *never* naming a feeling — no; naming is fine when
the word is exact, and a mix of showing and naming is normal. The
error to fix is a story that *only* names feelings and never shows
them. See the [teacher
guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-04--actions-thoughts-and-feelings)
and [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04--actions-thoughts-and-feelings).
