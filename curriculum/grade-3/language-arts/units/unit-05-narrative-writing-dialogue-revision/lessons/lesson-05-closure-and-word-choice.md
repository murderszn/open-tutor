# Lesson 05 — Closure and Word Choice for Effect

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can end my story so the reader knows how
it turned out — and choose words that make the ending feel like an
ending."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper and pencil; the learner's
draft beginning and middle (from Sessions 3 and 9, if started) or the
kite-story parts.
**Standards:** W.3.3.d, L.3.3.a.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult reads two endings for the kite story. (a) "The kite came down.
The end." (b) "At last the kite floated down into Maya's hands. She
hugged it tight, Leo cheered, and Biscuit barked at the sky as if to
tell the wind: not this time." The adult asks: "Which ending feels like
an ending, and why?" The learner answers. "An ending is not a stop sign.
It is an answer."

## Explicit teaching

**Closure** is the sense of an ending: the reader knows how the story
turned out. A good closure does two things:

1. **Resolves the problem** — the kite comes down, the book is found,
   the choice is made. The question the beginning asked gets answered.
2. **Tells how the characters end up feeling** — relieved, proud, happy,
   wiser. The reader leaves the characters somewhere good (or at least
   somewhere settled).

A closure is *not* "The end," "And then they went home," or "It was a
good day." Those are stop signs, not answers. Test your ending with two
questions: "Does it answer what happened to the problem?" and "Does it
say how the character feels now?" If either answer is no, the ending is
not finished.

**Word choice for effect** is choosing the exact word instead of a tired
one. Tired words — *said, nice, good, big, went, got, happy, sad* — are
not wrong; they are vague. Exact words do more work:

- *said* → *whispered, shouted, begged, suggested, cheered, warned*
- *went* → *trotted, raced, tiptoed, marched, wandered*
- *big* → *towering, huge, wide, giant* (pick the one that fits)
- *happy* → *glad, thrilled, proud, relieved* (pick the shade — Lesson 04)
- *got* → *caught, grabbed, earned, found, pulled*

One exact word can replace a whole sentence of explaining. But exact
does not mean fancy: the word must fit the character and the moment. A
third-grader who "ejaculated" is wrong; a third-grader who "yelled" is
right.

## Model story — "The Kite in the Tree," Part 5 (original, written for this unit)

*Adult reads aloud; the learner follows along.*

> At last, the tail slipped off the branch, and the red kite floated
> down into Maya's hands. She hugged it tight. Leo cheered, Biscuit
> barked at the sky, and Ms. Alvarez clapped from the bench. "Next
> time," Maya said, laughing, "I will check the wind first." And the
> next Saturday, she did — with Leo holding the string beside her.

### Modeled example 1 — The closure answers both questions

*Adult thinks aloud:* "Problem resolved? Yes — the kite is down, in
Maya's hands. How do the characters feel? Maya hugs the kite (relieved
and proud), Leo cheers (glad), Biscuit barks at the sky (triumphant, in
a dog's way), Ms. Alvarez claps (pleased). And the last sentence does
one more job: it tells you what Maya *learned* — she checks the wind
first now. An ending that shows what changed is stronger than an ending
that only stops."

### Modeled example 2 — Exact words do the work

*Adult writes the tired version, then the exact version:*

- Tired: "The kite came down. Maya was happy. Leo said something good."
- Exact: "The kite floated down into Maya's hands. She hugged it
  tight. Leo cheered."

"'Floated' is better than 'came' — you can see a kite float. 'Hugged it
tight' is better than 'was happy' — you can draw a hug. 'Cheered' is
better than 'said something good' — you can hear a cheer. Three swaps,
and the ending went from a report to a picture."

## Guided practice

The adult reads each item aloud; the learner answers orally or in writing.
Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05--closure-and-word-choice-for-effect).

1. In Part 5, what happens to the problem? How does Maya feel at the
   end, and what shows it?
2. Which of these is a closure, and which is only a stop sign?
   (a) "The kite came down. The end." (b) "At last the kite floated
   down, and Maya hugged it tight, knowing she would check the wind
   next time." Explain your choice with the two closure questions.
3. Swap each tired word for an exact one, and say why your word fits:
   "Maya ___ (went) to the tree. She ___ (got) the string. Leo ___
   (said) something."
4. Choose the exact word for the shade of feeling: "When Maya caught
   the kite, she felt ___." (a) okay (b) relieved (c) furious. Why is
   your choice the exact one?
5. Write a one-sentence closure for a story about Theo and the lost
   library book (from Lesson 01): the book is found under the couch.
   Your closure must resolve the problem *and* say how Theo feels.
6. Error analysis: Sam ended his story, "And then they went home and it
   was a nice day and they were happy. The end." Name two things wrong
   with it, and rewrite it as a closure in two sentences.

## Independent practice

7. Read the ending. Does it pass the two closure questions? If not,
   fix it: "Maya put the kite away. Leo went home. Biscuit slept."
8. Replace the tired words with exact ones (one word each): "The dog
   ___ (went) fast after the kite. Maya ___ (said) loudly, 'Look!' The
   kite was very ___ (big) in the sky."
9. Write a closing sentence for the kite story that shows what Maya
   learned — without using the words "learned," "lesson," or "moral."

## Application

Take your own story plan (from Lesson 01's application or Session 3).
Write its closure in two or three sentences: resolve the problem, say
how the main character feels, and use at least two exact words. Read it
aloud to the adult, who checks it with the two closure questions.

## Exit check

In your own words: what two questions must a good ending answer? Name
one tired word and the exact word you would swap in for it.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** keep the exact-word bank on the table (said → whispered,
  shouted, begged, suggested, cheered, warned; went → trotted, raced,
  tiptoed, marched; got → caught, grabbed, earned, found, pulled). If
  a closure is hard, finish this sentence first: "In the end, ___,
  and Maya felt ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** tell the ending aloud before writing it;
  or draw the last scene and write the closure under the drawing.
- **Extension:** write a second, different closure for the kite story
  (for example, the wind changes and the kite flies away for good, and
  Maya learns a different lesson) and argue which closure is stronger
  and why — a good closure is not always a happy one.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) "The end" is an ending — it is a label, not
a closure; teach the two questions. (2) A longer ending is a better
ending — no; two sentences that answer both questions beat a paragraph
that answers neither. (3) Exact words mean long or fancy words — no;
*trotted* is exact and short; *ejaculated* is fancy and wrong. The
word must fit the character, the moment, and the reader. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-05--closure-and-word-choice-for-effect)
and [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05--closure-and-word-choice-for-effect).
