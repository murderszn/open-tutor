# Lesson 03 — Dialogue That Develops the Story

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can write what my characters say so a
reader hears how they feel — and so the talking moves the story
forward."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper and pencil; the kite
illustration; the dialogue punctuation rules from Unit 04, Lesson 05
(keep them on the table).
**Standards:** W.3.3.b, L.3.2.c.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Say the same sentence — 'My kite is stuck' — three
ways: like you are worried, like you are angry, and like you are
laughing." The learner tries all three. "Same words, three different
feelings — because of *how* you said them. In writing, there is no voice
to hear. Dialogue has to carry the feeling in the words themselves, and
in the words around them."

## Explicit teaching

**Dialogue** is the exact words a character says, inside quotation
marks. Good dialogue does at least one of three jobs:

1. **Shows a feeling** — the reader can tell how the character feels
   without being told. ("My kite!" Maya cried. "It's stuck!")
2. **Moves the story** — what a character says changes what happens
   next. ("If we pull the string slowly, the tail might slip free.")
3. **Shows who the character is** — a careful character speaks
   differently from a joking one.

Dialogue that does none of these jobs is decoration. "Hello," said Leo.
"Hello," said Maya. That exchange tells the reader nothing and moves
nothing. Cut it, or give it a job.

Four rules for writing dialogue (the first three are from Unit 04,
Lesson 05 — recall them, do not re-learn them):

1. **Punctuation:** a comma after the speaker tag before the quote
   (*Maya said, "Let's try."*); the comma or period goes *inside* the
   closing quotation mark (*"Let's try," said Maya.*); the first word
   inside the quote is capitalized.
2. **Speaker tags:** use a **said word** that fits the feeling — *asked,
   cried, whispered, laughed, shouted, begged, suggested* — instead of
   plain *said* every time. The tag is a clue to the feeling.
3. **New speaker, new paragraph:** every time a different character
   starts speaking, start a new paragraph (a new line, indented or with
   a blank line). This is the rule readers use to tell who is talking.
4. **Let the character speak in their own words:** dialogue is what the
   character would really say, not a summary. Not "Maya said that she
   was worried." Instead: "Oh no," Maya whispered. "My new kite!"

## Model story — "The Kite in the Tree," Part 3 (original, written for this unit)

*Adult reads aloud, using a different voice for each speaker; the
learner follows along.*

> Maya stared up at the tangled kite. Her shoulders sank.
>
> "Oh no," she whispered. "My new kite! It was my birthday kite!"
>
> Just then, Leo came running along the path. "What happened?" he asked.
>
> "The wind took it," Maya said. "It flew right into the tree, and now
> it's stuck."
>
> Leo looked up at the red kite. "Don't worry," he said. "We can get it
> down. If we pull the string slowly, the tail might slip off the
> branch."

### Modeled example 1 — Dialogue that shows a feeling

*Adult thinks aloud:* "'Oh no,' she whispered. 'My new kite! It was my
birthday kite!' Three things show Maya's feeling without anyone saying
'she was sad': the word *whispered* (quiet, worried voice), the words
*Oh no*, and the detail that it was her birthday kite — this kite
matters to her. If I wrote 'Maya was sad,' the reader knows the fact.
With dialogue, the reader *feels* it. That is the difference between
telling and showing, and dialogue is one of the best showing tools."

### Modeled example 2 — Dialogue that moves the story

*Adult points to Leo's last lines:* "'Don't worry,' he said. 'We can
get it down. If we pull the string slowly, the tail might slip off the
branch.' Before Leo speaks, Maya has no plan — she is only staring up.
After he speaks, the story has a plan: pull the string slowly. The
dialogue *caused* the next event. And notice the paragraphs: every time
the speaker changes — Maya, then Leo, then Maya, then Leo — a new
paragraph begins. If I put all of this in one paragraph, the reader
would lose track of who is talking."

## Guided practice

The adult reads each item aloud; the learner answers orally or in writing.
Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03--dialogue-that-develops-the-story).

1. In Part 3, which line of dialogue shows Maya's feeling? What two
   clues in the line tell you how she feels?
2. Which line of dialogue moves the story forward? What happens next
   because of it?
3. Fix the punctuation (Unit 04 rules) and the paragraphing: "what
   happened asked Leo the wind took it said Maya" — write it as two
   speakers, two paragraphs, correctly punctuated.
4. Choose the dialogue that does a job, and say which job it does:
   (a) "Hi," said Maya. "Hi," said Leo. (b) "Wait!" cried Leo. "Don't
   pull hard — you'll snap the string!"
5. Write one line of dialogue for Biscuit's owner, Maya, that shows she
   is *hopeful* (not sad, not angry). Use a said word that is not
   *said*.
6. Error analysis: Sam wrote this dialogue: "I am worried," said Maya.
   "I am worried because my kite is stuck in the tree and I am sad
   about it." Explain what is wrong with it, and rewrite Maya's line
   so it *shows* the worry instead of announcing it.

## Independent practice

7. Read the exchange. Rewrite it so the dialogue shows Leo is excited,
   not bored. Change the said words and the spoken words — not the
   punctuation rules: "We got it," said Leo. "The kite is down," said
   Maya. "That is good," said Leo.
8. Write a two-line dialogue between Maya and Ms. Alvarez, the park
   keeper, who is sitting on the bench. Ms. Alvarez asks what
   happened. Maya answers. Start a new paragraph for each speaker.
9. A new speaker must mean a new paragraph. Read this block and split
   it into paragraphs, adding the correct punctuation: Maya said I can
   reach it if I stand on the bench Leo said no the bench is too wobbly

## Application

Look at the kite illustration again. Imagine Maya says something to
Biscuit, and Leo (arriving on the path) says something to Maya. Write
three lines of dialogue — Maya to Biscuit, Leo to Maya, Maya to Leo —
each doing a job (show a feeling or move the story). Read them aloud in
different voices to the adult, who checks the punctuation and the
paragraphing.

## Exit check

In your own words: what are the three jobs good dialogue can do? And
what is the rule about a new speaker and paragraphs?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** keep a said-word bank on the table (*asked, cried,
  whispered, laughed, shouted, begged, suggested, warned, cheered*).
  The learner points to a said word before writing it. If writing
  dialogue is hard, act it out first with the adult, then write down
  what was said.
- **Alternate response mode:** act out the Part 3 exchange with the
  adult (one voice each) before writing anything; or draw speech
  bubbles over the illustration and write the dialogue inside them.
- **Extension:** rewrite Leo's plan as a line of dialogue that shows he
  is *nervous* instead of confident ("Don't worry" becomes what?);
  argue which version makes a better story and why.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) dialogue is "talking" — any talking counts;
teach the three-jobs test and cut decoration. (2) *Said* is the only
tag — the said-word bank fixes this, but warn against fancy tags that
do not fit (*"My kite!" Maya ejaculated* — no; the tag must match the
feeling). (3) Punctuation is the point of dialogue — it is not; it is
the tool. If the learner punctuates perfectly but the dialogue does no
job, the lesson is not learned. See the [teacher
guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-03--dialogue-that-develops-the-story)
and [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03--dialogue-that-develops-the-story).
