# Lesson 06 — Plan, Revise, and Edit

**Goal (learner hears):** "I can make my story better after I write it
— by adding, taking away, moving, and swapping — and then fix the
spelling and punctuation."
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** paper and pencil; the learner's
draft of their own story (from Session 9); a red or green pen for
revision marks.
**Standards:** W.3.5, W.3.10.

## Readiness check (1–2 min)

The adult asks: "Is a first draft a finished story?" The learner
answers. "A first draft is a story's *bones*. Revision puts muscle on
the bones. Today we do the part most writers say is the real writing:
going back and making it better."

## Explicit teaching

Writing has three passes, and each pass has a different job. Do them in
order — never edit spelling before you revise the story, or you will
polish sentences you are about to delete.

1. **Plan (before drafting).** A **story map**: situation, characters,
   events in order, problem, ending — in single words or short phrases,
   never whole sentences. A plan is a map, not a draft: if your plan
   has full sentences, you have already started drafting. (See the
   project for the map.)
2. **Revise (after drafting).** Revision changes the *story* — what it
   says and how it says it. Use four moves, and remember them as
   **ARMS** — Add, Remove, Move, Swap:
   - **Add:** add dialogue where a character only "said" nothing; add a
     time word where the order is unclear; add a showing detail where
     a feeling is only named.
   - **Remove:** remove a sentence that does not belong to this story;
     remove a repeated word; remove decoration dialogue that does no job.
   - **Move:** move an event into the right order; move the best
     sentence to the front of a paragraph.
   - **Swap:** swap a tired word for an exact one; swap a naming
     sentence for a showing one.
3. **Edit (last).** Editing changes the *surface* — spelling, capitals,
   commas, quotation marks, and periods. Use the Unit 04 conventions:
   dialogue punctuation, title capitals, possessives, studied
   spellings, and the beginning dictionary for any word you are unsure
   of.

**Feedback that helps** comes in three parts — **Praise, Question,
Polish** (after the Reading Rockets PQP revision strategy):

- **Praise:** name one specific thing that works ("Your dialogue shows
  Maya is worried — I felt it.").
- **Question:** ask about one confusing part ("I don't know why Leo
  came to the park — can you tell me?").
- **Polish:** suggest one concrete fix ("Add one line of dialogue when
  Leo arrives, so I know what he says.").

A good reader of your draft praises something real, asks one honest
question, and suggests one fix — never a list of everything wrong.

## Weak draft — "The Kite in the Tree," told badly (original, written for this unit)

*Adult reads aloud; the learner follows along. This is the SAME story as
Lessons 01–05, written without any of the craft moves — for revision
practice.*

> Maya went to the park. It was nice. She had a kite. The kite was red.
> The kite got stuck in a tree. The wind was strong. Maya was sad. Leo
> came. They got the kite down. They went home. It was a good day. The
> end.

### Modeled example 1 — Revise with ARMS (Add)

*Adult thinks aloud, marking the draft:* "This draft has no dialogue —
Maya and Leo never speak. **Add** one line when Leo arrives: 'What
happened?' he asked. And the events have no time words — **add** *first,
then, suddenly, at last* so the reader knows the order. And 'Maya was
sad' only names a feeling — **add** a showing action: 'Her shoulders
sank.' Three adds, and the story already breathes."

### Modeled example 2 — Revise with ARMS (Remove, Move, Swap) and then edit

*Adult continues:* "**Remove** 'It was nice' — it tells the reader
nothing about this story. **Move** the sentence about the wind before
the kite gets stuck, so the cause comes before the effect. **Swap** the
tired words: *went* becomes *ran*, *got* becomes *pulled*, *good day*
becomes a real closure — 'Maya hugged the kite tight.' Then, and only
then, **edit**: check the dialogue punctuation, the capitals, and the
spelling of *suddenly* in the dictionary if unsure. Revision first,
editing last — always."

## Guided practice

The adult reads each item aloud; the learner answers orally or in writing.
Answers are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06--plan-revise-and-edit).

1. Name the three passes of writing in order, and say what job each
   one does.
2. Which ARMS move fixes each problem? (a) The story says "Maya was
   sad" and nothing else. (b) A sentence about breakfast sits in the
   middle of the kite story. (c) The kite tangles before Maya flies
   it. (d) Every sentence says "said."
3. Give Praise–Question–Polish feedback for this draft sentence: "Maya
   went to the park and her kite got stuck and Leo came and they got
   it down." One praise, one question, one polish suggestion.
4. Fix the weak draft's ending: "They went home. It was a good day. The
   end." Rewrite it as a closure in two sentences.
5. Edit this sentence (Unit 04 rules): "maya said lets pull the string
   slowly Leo said ok"
6. Error analysis: Sam revised his story by fixing every spelling
   mistake first, then realized the middle event was in the wrong order
   and had to rewrite three sentences — including ones he had just
   spell-checked. What did Sam do wrong, and what should he do next
   time?

## Independent practice

7. Add one line of dialogue to the weak draft, at the moment Leo
   arrives. Punctuate it correctly and start a new paragraph for the
   new speaker.
8. Remove one sentence from the weak draft that does not belong to the
   story, and explain why it does not belong.
9. Swap three tired words in the weak draft for exact ones. Write the
   old word and the new word, and say why the new word is better.

## Application

Take your own story draft (Session 9). Do one full revision pass with
the ARMS checklist: make at least one Add, one Remove, one Move, and
one Swap, and mark each change with your red or green pen. Then do an
editing pass: read the draft aloud slowly, and fix spelling,
capitals, and dialogue punctuation. Read the revised draft to the adult,
who checks: can you point to each of your four ARMS changes?

## Exit check

In your own words: what does ARMS stand for, and what is one example of
each move? Why do we revise before we edit?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if four moves at once is too much, do one move per
  read-through: first read for Adds, then for Removes, then for Moves,
  then for Swaps. A revision checklist card (Add / Remove / Move /
  Swap, with one example each) stays on the table.
- **Alternate response mode:** revise by talking first — tell the adult
  what you would change and why, and let the adult scribe the marks;
  or revise by cutting the draft into sentence strips and physically
  moving them.
- **Extension:** give the revised weak draft to the adult as a "reader"
  and collect Praise–Question–Polish feedback; then decide which of the
  adult's suggestions to take and which to refuse — and justify the
  refusal in one sentence. (Writers may refuse a suggestion with a
  reason; that is part of revision, too.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: (1) revision means fixing spelling — no;
spelling is editing, the last pass. Keep the passes separate or the
learner will polish sentences that need deleting. (2) More changes mean
better revision — no; one good Add beats five random ones. Ask "does
this change help the reader?" for every mark. (3) Feedback means
finding mistakes — no; Praise–Question–Polish starts with what works,
and a writer may refuse a suggestion with a reason. See the [teacher
guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-06--plan-revise-and-edit) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06--plan-revise-and-edit).
