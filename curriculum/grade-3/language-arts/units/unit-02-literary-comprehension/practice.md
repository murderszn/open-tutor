# Practice Sets — Unit 02: Literary Comprehension

Four sets, one per week. Use them on Sessions 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 13, or as
flex-session work. Solutions live only in
[answer-key.md](answer-key.md#practices); this page contains no answers.

## Practice A — Evidence questions (RL.3.1; use in Week 7)

Read "The Kite Test" (Lesson 01) once more, then answer. Every answer must
end with *"I know this because the story says…"* plus quoted words.

1. What was wrong with the wind at the start?
2. How many times did Sam run down the hill?
3. What lifted the kite over the fence?
4. Why did Sam shout?
5. What did Sam do "all the way home"?
6. **Error analysis:** A classmate answered question 4 with "Because kites
   are fun." Explain what is wrong with that answer and fix it with text
   evidence.

## Practice B — Recount and message (RL.3.2; use in Weeks 7–8)

> **Story: "The Bundle of Sticks"** (original retelling of an Aesop fable)
>
> "A father had three sons who quarreled from morning until night. 'You
> will never get anywhere like this,' he told them. One evening he tied a
> bundle of sticks together and handed it to the oldest. 'Break it,' he
> said. The son strained and pulled, but the bundle would not break. The
> second son tried, then the third — none could break it.
>
> Then the father untied the bundle and gave each son a single stick.
> 'Break it,' he said. Snap, snap, snap — each stick broke easily. 'When
> you stand together, no one can break you,' said the father. 'Alone, you
> snap like twigs.' From that evening on, the brothers quarreled less — and
> worked together more."

1. Name the characters and the setting.
2. Write a three-sentence recount: beginning, middle, end.
3. What is the central message? Write it as advice for a friend.
4. List two key details that carry the message.
5. Is this story a fable, folktale, or myth? Name one clue.
6. **Explain your reasoning:** Could the message also be "Fathers are
   wise"? Why is your message from item 3 a *better* central message?

## Practice C — Characters and word meanings (RL.3.3, RL.3.4, L.3.5.a; use in Weeks 8–9)

> **Story: "Mina's Loud Morning"** (original)
>
> "Mina woke up **on the wrong side of the bed** — grumpy before breakfast.
> Her little brother had eaten the last banana, and Mina **saw red**. 'You
> always take everything!' she shouted.
>
> Mom poured the orange juice and said quietly, 'Mina, take a breath. Tell
> him how you feel with words, not volume.' Mina breathed in, breathed out,
> and said, 'I feel angry because I was looking forward to that banana.'
> Her brother blinked. 'You can have half of mine,' he said, pushing his
> bowl over. Mina smiled for the first time that morning. 'Thanks, squirt,'
> she said."

1. Name one **trait** Mina shows at the start and one **action** that proves
   it.
2. How does Mina **feel** at the start, and how does she feel at the end?
   Quote one detail for each.
3. What was Mina's **motivation** for shouting?
4. Build the chain: Mom's advice (action) → what happened next (event).
5. "On the wrong side of the bed" — literal or nonliteral? Real meaning?
6. "Saw red" — literal or nonliteral? What story clue helps?
7. **Error analysis:** A classmate says Mina's trait is "mean." Do you agree
   or disagree? Use the ending of the story as evidence.

## Practice D — Comparing two stories (RL.3.9; use in Week 10)

Read these two original Aesop-style retellings, then compare.

> **"The Fox and the Grapes"** (original retelling)
>
> "Fox saw a bunch of grapes hanging high on the vine, purple and round.
> He jumped — and missed. He jumped again — and missed. He jumped a third
> time, higher than ever — and missed. Panting, Fox sat down and said,
> 'Those grapes are probably sour anyway.' And he walked away, nose in
> the air."
>
> **"The Crow and the Pitcher"** (original retelling)
>
> "Crow was thirsty, and the pitcher had water — but only a little, far
> below the rim. Her beak could not reach it. Crow thought and thought.
> Then, one by one, she dropped pebbles into the pitcher — plink, plink,
> plink — until the water rose high enough to drink. She drank deeply and
> flew home happy."

1. Both stories are by "the same author" (the Aesop tradition). Name the
   **setting** of each.
2. What is the **central message** of "The Fox and the Grapes"? (One
   sentence, about people in general.)
3. What is the **central message** of "The Crow and the Pitcher"?
4. **Contrast** the two main characters: what trait does Fox show at the
   end? What trait does Crow show? Quote one action each.
5. **Compare** the plots: what is the same about the two problems?
6. **Compare** the themes: do both stories teach about giving up, or do
   they teach opposite lessons? Explain with details.
7. Which character would you rather be like — Fox or Crow? Give one story
   reason (your point of view, Lesson 06).
