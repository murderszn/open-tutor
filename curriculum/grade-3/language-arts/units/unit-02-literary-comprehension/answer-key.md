# Answer Key — Unit 02: Literary Comprehension (Character and Theme)

**Teacher eyes only.** Every item in the student materials is solved here
independently. Accept any answer that is correct **and** backed by the
required text evidence, even if the wording differs.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice** ("The Kite Test")
1. His new kite. *"I know this because the story says, 'pulling the kite
   behind him.'"*
2. Down the long hill. *"…the story says, 'He ran down the long hill three
   times.'"*
3. A gust lifted the kite over the fence. *"…the story says, 'On the third
   run, a gust lifted the kite over the fence.'"*
4. Because the kite was finally flying. *"…the story says, 'a gust lifted
   the kite over the fence.'"*
5. To keep the kite flying / so he wouldn't lose it after it finally flew.
   *"…the story says, 'He held the string tight all the way home.'"*
   (Accept any sensible reason tied to the kite flying.)
6. Sample: "Why did Sam run three times?" — Because the wind was too weak
   the first two times. *"…the story says, 'the wind was too weak'"* and
   *"'On the third run, a gust lifted the kite.'"*

**Independent practice**
1. Sam. 2. The wind was too weak to fly his new kite. 3. Three runs.
4. Happy/excited. *"…the story says, 'Sam shouted, "It flies! It flies!"'"*

**Exit check:** When the wind was too weak, Sam ran down the long hill
three times pulling the kite behind him. (*"…the story says, 'He ran down
the long hill three times, pulling the kite behind him.'"*)

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice** ("Anansi and the Talking Drum")
1. Characters: Anansi (a spider), the farmer, the village children.
2. Setting: a village at the edge of the forest; market day.
3. Beginning: Anansi finds a small drum that seems to answer,
   "Boom. Bring-me-yam. Boom."
4. Middle: (a) Anansi taps the drum near the farmer's fence; the farmer
   drops his basket of yams in fright. (b) Anansi laughs and fills his bag
   with yams.
5. End: The drum slips, rolls down the hill, and breaks on a rock; Anansi
   walks home with yams but no drum.
6. Folktale — clues: a clever trickster character, a retold-style story,
   an ending that explains why children still sing about it; no stated
   moral (so not a fable).

**Independent practice** — sample five-part recount of "The Tortoise and
the Hare": Characters: Hare (fast, boastful), Tortoise (slow, steady).
Setting: a meadow, from the big oak to the pond. Beginning: Hare brags;
Tortoise challenges him to a race. Middle: Hare sprints ahead then naps;
Tortoise keeps walking. End: Tortoise crosses the finish line first.
1. Accept: the nap (without it, Hare wins) or the challenge (without it,
   no race) — any event with a sound reason. 2. Learner's middle sentence
   underlined.

**Exit check:** characters, setting, beginning, middle, end — in order.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice** (sorting)
1. X — true about ants, but not in this story and carries no message.
2. M — the central message. 3. D. 4. D. 5. D. 6. D (carries the
   kindness/sharing layer). 7. M — a valid secondary message, carried by
   strip 6 (Ant sharing).
8. Best pair: strips 3 + 5 (the pattern + the result) — accept 3 + 4 with
   a good explanation (pattern + contrast).

**Independent practice**
1. Sample: "Work hard and prepare now so you are ready when hard times
   come."
2. Sample details (in order): Ant carried grain "grain by grain, day by
   day" all summer; Grasshopper played and laughed instead of working; when
   winter came, Ant had food and Grasshopper went hungry.
3. Yes — "Help others when you can," supported by "Ant shared her stored
   grain with him."
4. Sample: "The story has an ant and a grasshopper in it." — true, but it
   teaches nothing about life; a message must be "should"-shaped advice.

**Exit check:** A detail is a true fact or event from the story; a message
is the life lesson those details teach, stated about people in general.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice** ("The Fox and the Stork")
1. Trait: tricky (unkind/sly). Action: serving soup in a wide, flat dish he
   knew Stork couldn't use — "smiling his slyest smile."
2. Feeling: hungry and left out (puzzled/embarrassed accepted). Detail:
   "Stork's stomach rumbled"; the illustration shows her puzzled, left-out
   face.
3. Motivation: to play a mean trick on Stork and laugh at her — "smiling
   his slyest smile," "'Too bad you cannot enjoy it.'"
4. Motivation: to teach Fox how his trick felt. "She served the soup in a
   tall, narrow jar" + "'Too bad you cannot enjoy it.'"
5. Stork's action made Fox go hungry at the second dinner; the event chain
   ends with Fox learning — "he never played a mean trick on a dinner guest
   again."
6. Sample: the fox's smug, pleased face shows the trick was deliberate
   (the words say "slyest smile" but the picture shows the *feeling*);
   the stork's puzzled face shows the trick worked — feelings in the moment.

**Independent practice** — sample (Hare): Trait: boastful → Action: naps in
the grass mid-race, sure he'll win → Event: Tortoise crosses the finish
line first; Hare loses. Motivation: he wanted to show off and believed the
slow tortoise could never beat him.

**Exit check:** Sample — Action: shares his crayons with the new student →
Event: the new student feels welcome and they become friends. (Any logical
action→event pair.)

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice** ("The New School Play")
1. Nonliteral — she was excited/nervous. Clue: "She had wanted the lead
   role since second grade."
2. Discuss: hands can literally feel cold, so "literal description" is
   acceptable — but the phrase's *job* is showing nervousness. Clue: "but
   her voice was strong and clear" (contrast shows the cold hands = nerves).
3. Nonliteral — become too scared to go through with it. Clue: Jun is
   encouraging her about the lead role.
4. Nonliteral — give up/quit. Clue: "I have worked too hard to ___."
5. First it seems to mean "easy," but "until the final week, when the
   lights, costumes, and lines all had to fit together" revises it:
   rehearsals were easy *compared to auditions* until everything had to fit
   together. Context changed the meaning.
6. Nonliteral — nervous/excited. Clue: "peeked through the curtain at the
   full seats."

**Independent practice** — journal entries must show phrase, real meaning,
and a quoted story clue. Sample hardest: "a piece of cake," because the
next sentence changed its meaning.
Sample new sentences: "The spelling test was a piece of cake." /
"The bakery sold a piece of cake for two dollars." (literal)

**Exit check:** Nonliteral — it means she studied hard all weekend.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice** ("Why the Sky Is High")
1. Kofi's POV: the sky was a nuisance in his way; pushing it up was a good
   fix. "'The sky is always in my way,' he said." / "pleased with himself."
2. Narrator's POV: gently teasing — the fix didn't really work. "though his
   goats still wandered wherever they pleased."
3. Sample: I think Kofi was a bit selfish — the children lost something
   wonderful. "The children cried, 'Bring back the clouds!'" (Any POV with
   a story reason.)
4. Grandmothers: relieved — "sighed with relief." Children: sad — "cried."
5. Sample: a mischievous mood emphasizing Kofi poking with his long stick —
   because the funniest moment shows his point of view. (Any mood + subject
   with an explanation.)
6. Sample: the story never describes the sunset over the forest or the small
   wooden table and stools — the picture adds that the dinner happens
   outside at sunset.

**Independent practice**
1. Sample: "Kofi thought the sky was a nuisance. The narrator suggests the
   sky was fine where it was. I think the sky was a gift the children lost."
2–3. Two panels with one-word mood labels (e.g., "joyful" / "relieved");
   explanation names whose feelings each panel shows.

**Exit check:** The narrator's — the narrator knows what the character
doesn't: "She had no idea what waited around the corner."

## Practices

**Practice A** ("The Kite Test")
1. The wind was too weak. *"…the story says, 'but the wind was too weak.'"*
2. Three times. *"…the story says, 'He ran down the long hill three times.'"*
3. A gust (of wind). *"…the story says, 'a gust lifted the kite over the
   fence.'"*
4. Because the kite was finally flying. *"…the story says, 'a gust lifted
   the kite over the fence.'"*
5. He held the string tight. *"…the story says, 'He held the string tight
   all the way home.'"*
6. "Because kites are fun" uses no story words — the story never says that.
   Fixed: he shouted because the kite finally flew — *"…the story says, 'a
   gust lifted the kite over the fence.'"*

**Practice B** ("The Bundle of Sticks")
1. Characters: a father and his three sons (oldest, second, third).
   Setting: their home, one evening.
2. Sample: Beginning — three quarreling sons are given a tied bundle of
   sticks to break. Middle — none can break the bundle; untied, each single
   stick snaps easily. End — the father teaches "stand together," and the
   brothers quarrel less and work together more.
3. Sample: "Stick together instead of fighting — together you are
   stronger." (Any "should"-shaped advice about unity.)
4. None of the sons could break the tied bundle; each single stick broke
   easily ("Snap, snap, snap").
5. Fable — very short, animal-free but with a clear lesson stated at the
   end ("When you stand together, no one can break you").
6. "Fathers are wise" is about one character, not a life lesson from the
   pattern + result. The unity message is better because the bundle/single
   stick contrast *proves* it.

**Practice C** ("Mina's Loud Morning")
1. Trait: grumpy (irritable/short-tempered accepted). Action: shouting "You
   always take everything!"
2. Start: grumpy/angry — "grumpy before breakfast." End: happy/smiling —
   "Mina smiled for the first time that morning."
3. Motivation: she was angry that her brother took the last banana she had
   been looking forward to; she wanted him to know she was upset.
4. Chain: Mom's advice (action) → Mina breathes, uses words, brother
   shares half his banana, Mina smiles (event).
5. Nonliteral — woke up grumpy/in a bad mood. Clue: "grumpy before
   breakfast."
6. Nonliteral — became very angry. Clue: she shouted (the very next
   sentence).
7. Disagree. "Mean" would be a trait (most of the time), but the ending
   shows Mina apologizes with words, thanks her brother ("Thanks, squirt"),
   and smiles — grumpy was a passing feeling, not who she is.

**Practice D** (comparing two retellings)
1. "The Fox and the Grapes": a vineyard (grapevine), daytime. "The Crow
   and the Pitcher": wherever the pitcher stood, on a hot/thirsty day.
2. Sample: "Don't pretend you didn't want something just because you
   couldn't get it." / "Admit failure instead of making excuses."
3. Sample: "Use your brains to solve problems — keep trying and think
   creatively." / "Where there's a will, there's a way."
4. Fox: proud / a sore loser who makes excuses — "'Those grapes are
   probably sour anyway.'" Crow: clever and persistent — "one by one, she
   dropped pebbles into the pitcher."
5. Compare (plots): both face a problem they can't solve at first — grapes
   too high to reach; water too low to reach.
6. Compare (themes): opposite lessons about giving up — Fox quits and makes
   excuses ("walked away, nose in the air"); Crow keeps thinking and
   succeeds ("drank deeply and flew home happy").
7. Sample: Crow — because she solved her problem by thinking instead of
   quitting. (Any choice with a story reason.)

## Quiz

("The Lighthouse Cat" — 10 items, 1 point each)

1. Up the winding stairs to the lamp room. *"…the story says, 'climbed the
   winding stairs to the lamp room.'"*
2. Ships were coming and the rocks were waiting — ships could crash in the
   dark. *"…the story says, 'Ships were coming, and the rocks were
   waiting.'"*
3. None of the three exactly — it's an **original story** (fable-like
   animal character, but no stated moral and not retold). Accept "fable-like
   original story" with that reasoning.
4. Sample: brave (determined). Action: climbed the trembling stairs anyway
   / batted the loose wire to relight the lamp.
5. Scared/nervous. "Her paws trembled on the cold stone steps." (or "Her
   heart pounded like a drum.")
6. Sample: "Be brave even when you're scared — courage is doing the scary
   thing anyway."
7. "Her paws trembled… but she climbed anyway" / "She batted the loose
   wire with her paw, and the light blazed back on."
8. Nonliteral — her heart was beating very fast; she was scared (and
   determined).
9. The narrator's — the narrator tells us what Marisol truly felt
   ("Marisol was not brave at all. Her paws trembled"), while the gulls only
   saw the outside.
10. Disagree. "Cats like lighthouses" is a topic/detail, not a message
    about people. The pattern (scared but climbing anyway) and result (lamp
    relit, ships safe) carry courage, not cat preferences.

## Unit assessment

(10 items × 3 points; see rubric in the assessment)

**A1.** Sample: Beginning — a bored shepherd boy twice shouts "Wolf!" as a
joke and the villagers run up for nothing. Middle — the villagers grow
annoyed and warn him to stop. End — a real wolf comes, no one believes the
boy, and he learns too late that no one believes a liar.
**A2.** Trait: mischievous/dishonest (trick-loving). Action: shouting
"Wolf! Wolf!" when there was no wolf — "only the boy, laughing. 'Fooled
you!'"
**A3.** Annoyed/frustrated. "'Stop wasting our time,' they grumbled."
**A4.** Message: "No one believes a liar — even when he tells the truth."
Details: the villagers ran up the hill twice for nothing; when the real
wolf came, "no one came."
**A5.** Motivation: he was bored — "The days were long and quiet" — and
wanted excitement/fun.
**B1.** Nonliteral — it describes the *quality* of the laugh: hollow and
mocking, not joyful. It tells us the new pot was showing off / being cruel.
**B2.** New pot's POV: bumping is fun and harmless; he's tough — "'I am not
afraid of a little bump!'" Old pot's POV: bumping is dangerous for him —
"'You are strong and I am cracked. If we knock together, I will break.'"
**B3.** Sample: "The strong should be gentle with those more fragile than
themselves." / "Think about how your actions affect others."
**B4.** Shared trait: selfish/thoughtless (reckless). Boy: shouted "Wolf!"
as a joke, wasting the villagers' time. New pot: kept knocking against the
old pot to show off, despite the warning.
**B5.** Contrast: the boy *learns* his lesson (too late) — "The boy
learned, too late, that no one believes a liar." The new pot gets no stated
lesson; he's left alone and "the river felt very wide" — a sadder, quieter
ending about loneliness rather than learning.

## Project samples

**Meeting (10/15):** "The Squirrel and the River" — message "Share what you
have" stated; one proving action (Squirrel shares stored nuts in winter);
beginning/middle/end in order; illustration of the sharing moment with a
caption; expressive read-aloud; evidence question answered by quoting the
sharing sentence. Loses points on the second trait proof and a thin caption.

**Exceeding (14/15):** "The Peacock and the Sparrow" — message "Don't mock
others for what they can't change" with two proving details (the mocking,
the storm where plain feathers survive); two traits proven by actions;
clear cause-linked sequence; illustration of the storm moment captioned
with what the words don't say (the peacock's frightened face); confident
read-aloud; evidence question answered with two quoted lines.
