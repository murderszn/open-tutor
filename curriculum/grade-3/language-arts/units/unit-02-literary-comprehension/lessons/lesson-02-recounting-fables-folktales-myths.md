# Lesson 02 — Recounting Stories: Characters, Setting, Beginning, Middle, End

**Unit 02 · Grade 3 Language Arts · Session 3**
**Standard:** RL.3.2 (recount stories, including fables, folktales, and myths
from diverse cultures; determine central message — first pass on recounting)
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, five story-part
cards (characters / setting / beginning / middle / end)

## Student goal

Today I will retell a story in my own words, in order, naming the
characters, the setting, and what happens in the beginning, middle, and end.

## Readiness check (2 minutes, retrieval warm-up)

Learner reads Unit 01 words *fable, story, moral* from cards and says one
sentence about what each might mean before the lesson teaches them.

## Explicit teaching

To **recount** a story means to tell what happened **in your own words** and
**in order** — not to memorize it word for word. A complete recount has five
parts:

1. **Characters** — who the story is about.
2. **Setting** — where and when it happens.
3. **Beginning** — what starts the story (the problem or plan).
4. **Middle** — what happens next (the attempts, the surprises).
5. **End** — how it turns out (the solution or surprise).

Three kinds of stories appear in this unit:

- A **fable** is a very short story, often with animal characters, that
  teaches a lesson.
- A **folktale** is a story people have told and retold for a long time,
  often with clever characters or magic.
- A **myth** is a very old story that tries to explain something about the
  world.

The story below is an original retelling of an ancient fable plot ("The
Tortoise and the Hare") written for this unit.

### Worked example 1 — modeled five-part recount

> **Story: "The Tortoise and the Hare"** (original retelling)
>
> "Hare could run faster than anyone in the meadow, and he never let the
> other animals forget it. 'I am the fastest!' he bragged every morning.
> One day Tortoise said quietly, 'Let us race.' The animals laughed, but
> they marked a path from the big oak to the pond.
>
> At the start, Hare shot ahead like a brown arrow. Soon he was so far in
> front that he lay down in the soft grass. 'I have time for a nap,' he
> yawned. Tortoise kept walking — step, step, step — never stopping, never
> hurrying.
>
> Hare woke to cheering. He opened one eye and saw Tortoise's shell crossing
> the finish line at the pond. Slow and steady had won the race."

**Modeled recount:**

- **Characters:** Hare (fast, boastful) and Tortoise (slow, steady).
- **Setting:** a meadow, from the big oak to the pond; one morning.
- **Beginning:** Hare brags about being fastest; Tortoise challenges him to a race.
- **Middle:** Hare races ahead, then naps in the grass; Tortoise keeps walking without stopping.
- **End:** Tortoise crosses the finish line first; slow and steady wins.

Notice: every part is in my own words, and the events stay in story order.

### Worked example 2 — spotting a broken recount

> "Tortoise won because Hare took a nap. There was a race. Hare bragged."

This is true, but it is **not a complete recount**: no setting, no
characters named fully, and the order is scrambled (the bragging happened
first). A good recount keeps all five parts in order.

## Guided practice

Read this original folktale-style story. Then sort its parts.

> **Story: "Anansi and the Talking Drum"** (original story)
>
> "In a village at the edge of the forest lived Anansi, a spider who loved
> tricks more than breakfast. One market day he found a small drum that,
> when tapped, seemed to answer in a deep voice: 'Boom. Bring-me-yam.
> Boom.'
>
> Anansi had an idea. He carried the drum to the yam field and tapped it
> near the farmer's fence. 'Boom. Bring-me-yam. Boom.' The farmer heard the
> voice, looked around, saw no one — and dropped his basket of yams in
> fright.
>
> Anansi laughed and laughed, filling his bag with yams. But on the way
> home the drum slipped, rolled down the hill, and broke against a rock
> with a sad little 'thud.' Anansi walked home with yams but no drum, and
> the village children still sing about the day the drum talked back."

With the adult, place each story part on its card:

1. Characters: who? (2–3 words each)
2. Setting: where and when?
3. Beginning: what starts it?
4. Middle: what happens next? (two events in order)
5. End: how does it turn out?
6. What kind of story is this — fable, folktale, or myth? What clues tell
   you? (Hint: retold-style clever character, no clear moral stated yet.)

## Independent practice

Choose either story from this lesson. In your notebook, write a five-part
recount in your own words — one or two sentences per part. Then answer:

1. Which event could you **not** leave out without changing the ending?
2. Underline the sentence in your recount that tells the **middle**.

## Application

Tell your five-part recount of "Anansi and the Talking Drum" aloud to the
adult or a family member, without looking at the story. They may ask one
"what happened next?" question. This is a **storytelling rehearsal** — the
unit project (Session 12) will ask you to tell your own fable this way.

## Exit check

Name the five parts of a complete recount, in order, from memory.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Adult reads the story aloud; learner retells orally while the
  adult places the part cards. Draw instead of write for the end (one scene
  with a caption).
- **Alternate response mode:** Point-and-tell: learner points to story
  sentences in order while the adult reads them, then summarizes each in
  one word.
- **Extension:** Recount "The Tortoise and the Hare" from **Hare's point of
  view**, still covering all five parts (a preview of Lesson 06).

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

- Learners often retell the most exciting part only (the finish line) and
  skip the beginning. Ask: "What did the characters not know yet at the
  start?"
- "In my own words" is hard: if a learner recites sentences verbatim, cover
  the story and ask them to tell it to a stuffed animal — the audience
  shift breaks memorization.
- Folktale note: "Anansi and the Talking Drum" is an **original story in
  folktale style**, not a retelling of any specific culture's tale. Do not
  present it as an authentic traditional story.
