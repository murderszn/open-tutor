# Lesson 05 — Literal vs. Nonliteral Language: What Words Really Mean in a Story

**Unit 02 · Grade 3 Language Arts · Session 9**
**Standards:** RL.3.4, L.3.5.a (determine the meaning of words and phrases
as they are used in text, distinguishing literal from nonliteral language)
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, "Say what you
see" picture cards (a literal drawing vs. a nonliteral meaning, e.g., a cat
with a tongue in its mouth)

## Student goal

Today I will tell when story words mean exactly what they say (literal) and
when they mean something else (nonliteral), and I will explain the real
meaning from the story's clues.

## Readiness check (2 minutes, retrieval warm-up)

Adult says: "It is raining cats and dogs." Learner draws what the words
*literally* say (animals falling from the sky — funny!), then says what
people *really* mean (raining very hard). This gap is the whole lesson.

## Explicit teaching

- **Literal** language means exactly what the words say: "She opened the
  red door." A red door. She opened it. Done.
- **Nonliteral** language says one thing but means another: "She was on
  cloud nine." Nobody is standing on a cloud — it means she was **very
  happy**.

How to crack a nonliteral phrase in a story:

1. **Notice** it sounds strange or impossible if taken literally.
2. **Look** at the clues around it — what just happened? How does the
   character feel?
3. **Replace** it with the real meaning and re-read the sentence. Does it
   make sense now?

### Worked example 1 — impossible, then clear

> "After weeks of practice, Luis nailed his audition. He was walking on
> air all the way home."

*Literal check:* Was Luis floating? Impossible — people can't walk on air.
*Clues:* He nailed his audition (did great); walking home happy.
*Real meaning:* He was **extremely happy**. Re-read: "He was extremely
happy all the way home." Makes sense. ✅

### Worked example 2 — a trickier one with a story clue

> "The coach said the championship would be **a piece of cake**. Then our
> team lost by twenty points, and nobody was smiling."

First guess: "easy." But the **next sentence** contradicts it — they lost
badly and nobody smiled. The coach was **wrong** (or joking). Real meaning
here: the coach *thought* it would be easy. **Context can flip a phrase** —
always read the sentences around it.

## Guided practice

Read "The New School Play" (original story). For each bold phrase, decide
literal or nonliteral, then explain the real meaning with a story clue.

> **Story: "The New School Play"** (original)
>
> "On Monday, Ms. Patel announced the spring play, and Priya's heart
> **skipped a beat**. She had wanted the lead role since second grade.
> At auditions, her hands were **cold as ice**, but her voice was strong
> and clear.
>
> When the cast list went up, Priya scanned it twice. She had the lead!
> Her best friend Jun gave her a high five and said, 'You will be great.
> Just don't **get cold feet** now.' Priya laughed. 'I have worked too hard
> to **throw in the towel**,' she said.
>
> Rehearsals were **a piece of cake** compared to auditions — until the
> final week, when the lights, costumes, and lines all had to fit together.
> On opening night, Priya peeked through the curtain at the full seats and
> felt **butterflies in her stomach**. Then the music started, and she
> stepped into the light."

1. "her heart skipped a beat" — literal or nonliteral? Real meaning? Clue?
2. "her hands were cold as ice" — is this literal? (Careful: think about
   what the story is really telling you.)
3. "don't get cold feet"
4. "throw in the towel"
5. "a piece of cake" — then read the **next** sentence. Does the meaning
   stay simple? Explain.
6. "butterflies in her stomach"

## Independent practice

In your notebook, make an **Idiom Journal** table with three columns —
*Phrase | Real meaning | Story clue* — and fill it for any **three** phrases
from the story. Then answer:

1. Which phrase was hardest to crack, and what clue finally helped?
2. Write one new sentence using "a piece of cake" to mean something easy.
3. Write one new sentence where "a piece of cake" would be **literal**
   (about actual cake).

## Application

Listen for nonliteral language today — in conversation, on a sign, in a
book. Catch one, write the sentence, and explain the real meaning to the
adult using the three steps (notice → clues → replace-and-reread). Start an
ongoing **Idiom Journal** page for the unit.

## Exit check

"She **hit the books** all weekend." Literal or nonliteral? What does it
really mean?

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Act out each phrase literally first (miming cold feet,
  throwing a towel), then ask: "Could the story really mean *that*?" The
  silliness makes the nonliteral meaning stick.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner draws the literal meaning and the
  real meaning side by side for two phrases.
- **Extension:** Find the phrase in the story where context **changes** the
  meaning ("a piece of cake" + "until the final week"). Write a paragraph
  explaining how the author warns the reader not to trust the easy meaning.

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

- Learners often memorize idiom definitions and ignore context. This
  lesson's item 5 and the extension exist to break that habit: meaning
  lives in the surrounding sentences.
- "Cold as ice" (item 2): hands can literally feel cold — accept "literal
  describing how nervous she felt" as correct, since the phrase's *job* is
  showing nervousness. Discuss both readings.
- Idioms vary by family and culture; honor the learner's home phrases and
  invite them into the journal.
