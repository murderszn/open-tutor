# Lesson 06 — Points of View and Illustrations: Whose Eyes, Whose Story

**Unit 02 · Grade 3 Language Arts · Session 11**
**Standards:** RL.3.6 (distinguish the learner's own point of view from the
narrator's or characters'), RL.3.7 (explain how specific illustrations
contribute to what is conveyed by the words — e.g., create mood, emphasize
character or setting)
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, the fox-and-stork
illustration from Lesson 04 (revisited)

## Student goal

Today I will tell the difference between what **I** think, what the
**narrator** tells me, and what a **character** believes — and I will explain
what a picture adds that the words don't say.

## Readiness check (2 minutes, retrieval warm-up)

Learner reads Unit 01 cards *narrator, picture, mood* and defines each in one
sentence (approximate wording accepted; precision is today's work).

## Explicit teaching

Every story is told by **someone** — the **narrator**. Three points of view
to keep straight:

- **My point of view:** what *I*, the reader, think or feel about the story.
- **The narrator's point of view:** what the storyteller tells me and how
  they seem to feel about the characters.
- **A character's point of view:** what one character believes — which can
  be **wrong** or different from what I know.

They don't have to agree! A character can think a trick is funny while the
narrator shows it is unkind, while I feel sorry for the victim. Noticing the
difference is called **reading with perspective**.

Pictures do work, too. An **illustration** can:

- create a **mood** (scary, cozy, exciting),
- **emphasize** a character (make them look big, small, sneaky, kind),
- show the **setting** so the words don't have to describe everything.

### Worked example 1 — three points of view, one scene

> **Story: "Why the Sky Is High"** (original "why" story, myth-style)
>
> "Long ago, the sky hung low over the village — so low that the tallest
> grandmothers had to duck when they carried water. The children loved it:
> they could reach up and tickle the clouds.
>
> But Kofi the goat-herd grumbled. 'The sky is always in my way,' he said.
> 'My goats cannot see where they are going.' So Kofi took his longest
> herding stick and — poke, poke, poke — pushed the sky higher and higher
> until it rested far above the mountains.
>
> The grandmothers sighed with relief. The children cried, 'Bring back the
> clouds!' And Kofi smiled, pleased with himself, though his goats still
> wandered wherever they pleased."

- **Kofi's point of view:** the sky was a nuisance; pushing it up was a
  good fix. (He is "pleased with himself.")
- **The narrator's point of view:** the narrator shows Kofi's fix didn't
  really solve anything ("his goats still wandered wherever they pleased")
  — a gentle, teasing tone toward Kofi.
- **My point of view (example):** I think the children lost something
  wonderful, and Kofi was a bit selfish. (Yours may differ — that's fine,
  as long as you can point to words that shaped it.)

### Worked example 2 — what the picture adds

Revisit the Lesson 04 illustration (`../assets/fox-and-stork-dinner.png`):

- **Mood:** the warm sunset colors make the scene feel cozy — which
  *contrasts* with the unkind trick, making the trick feel sneakier.
- **Emphasis on character:** the fox is drawn big and smug in the front;
  the stork is tall but pushed to the side, looking small and left out. The
  picture *emphasizes* who has the power in this scene.
- **What words don't say:** the story never says "the fox looked smug."
  The picture tells us the trick was deliberate — new information, not just
  decoration.

## Guided practice

Read "Why the Sky Is High" again with the adult, then answer:

1. What is **Kofi's** point of view about pushing up the sky? Quote words
   that show it.
2. What is the **narrator's** point of view about Kofi's fix? Which sentence
   gives it away?
3. What is **your** point of view — was Kofi right? Give one story reason.
4. How do the **grandmothers** feel? How do the **children** feel? (Two
   points of view inside one story.)
5. Imagine an illustration of the moment Kofi pokes the sky: what **mood**
   would you draw, and what would it emphasize — Kofi, the sky, or the
   children? Explain.
6. Look at the fox-and-stork illustration once more: name **one** thing the
   picture shows about the **setting** that the story words do not describe.

## Independent practice

In your notebook:

1. Copy this sentence starter and finish it three ways: "Kofi thought the
   sky was ___. The narrator suggests the sky was ___. I think the sky
   was ___."
2. Draw a quick two-panel illustration: panel 1, the sky hanging low with
   happy children; panel 2, the sky high above with the grandmothers
   relieved. Label the **mood** of each panel in one word.
3. Answer: how do your two panels show different characters' points of view
   without using any words?

## Application

Open any picture book at home. Cover the words on one page and "read" only
the illustration: what mood, character, or setting information do you get?
Now read the words: what did the picture add? Report back in one paragraph.

## Exit check

A character says, "This is the best day ever!" but the narrator adds, "She
had no idea what waited around the corner." Whose point of view is more
trustworthy here — the character's or the narrator's? Why? (One sentence
with a reason.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use three paper puppets labeled ME / NARRATOR / CHARACTER;
  the learner holds up the puppet whose point of view each answer comes
  from.
- **Alternate response mode:** Draw each point of view as a face (happy,
  teasing, sad) and match faces to Kofi, the narrator, and the learner.
- **Extension:** Rewrite the last paragraph of "Why the Sky Is High" from
  **the sky's** point of view (first person, 3–4 sentences). Then compare:
  what changed besides the pronouns?

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

- Learners often merge all three viewpoints ("the story says Kofi is
  right"). Physically separate them — three columns, three voices — until
  the habit forms.
- "Why the Sky Is High" is an **original myth-style story**, not a
  retelling of any specific culture's myth. Name that distinction aloud.
- RL.3.7 is about illustrations *in the text*; since our texts are original,
  the generated fox-and-stork illustration is the unit's worked case. Label
  it as AI-generated whenever it is shown.
