# Lesson 01 — Asking and Answering Questions with Text Evidence

**Unit 02 · Grade 3 Language Arts · Session 1**
**Standard:** RL.3.1 (ask and answer questions to demonstrate understanding,
referring explicitly to the text as the basis for answers)
**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, this lesson,
"Text Evidence Markers" (two colors of crayon or sticky tabs)

## Student goal

Today I will ask and answer questions about a story, and I will point to
the exact words in the story that prove each answer.

## Readiness check (2 minutes, retrieval warm-up)

The adult shows four Unit 01 multisyllable word cards (*tortoise, decide,
happen, silent*). The learner reads each aloud, breaks the trickiest one
into syllables, and uses it in one sentence about a story.

## Explicit teaching

Strong readers don't answer from memory alone — they **show their proof**.
Proof in reading is called **text evidence**: the words in the story that
back up your answer.

Two question words need special care:

- **Who / What / Where / When** questions: the answer usually sits right in
  a sentence. Find the sentence, and you have found the proof.
- **Why / How** questions: the answer comes from putting two or more pieces
  together — what a character did *plus* what happened next. Your proof may
  be two sentences, not one.

Say it like this: *"I know this because the story says…"*, then read the
exact words.

### Worked example 1 — a right-there question

> **Story:** "Mara packed her yellow raincoat. She ran to the bus stop
> before the first drops fell."
>
> **Question:** What did Mara pack?
>
> **Answer:** A yellow raincoat. **I know this because the story says,
> "Mara packed her yellow raincoat."**

Notice the quotation marks: they show the words came straight from the
story, not from my head.

### Worked example 2 — a why question needing two pieces

> **Story:** "Jin practiced his drums every afternoon. On Friday the band
> leader handed him the solo."
>
> **Question:** Why did Jin get the solo?
>
> **Answer:** Because he practiced every afternoon. **I know this because
> the story says he "practiced his drums every afternoon," and the next
> sentence shows the reward: "the band leader handed him the solo."** Two
> pieces of evidence, one answer.

A weak answer says, "Because he is good." That might be true, but the story
never says it — so it is not text evidence.

## Guided practice (adult reads each item aloud; learner answers with proof)

Mark evidence with a yellow marker for **right-there** answers and a blue
marker for **put-together** answers.

> **Story: "The Kite Test"** (original)
>
> "Sam wanted to fly his new kite, but the wind was too weak. He ran down
> the long hill three times, pulling the kite behind him. On the third run,
> a gust lifted the kite over the fence. Sam shouted, 'It flies! It
> flies!' He held the string tight all the way home."

1. What did Sam pull behind him? (right-there)
2. Where did Sam run? (right-there)
3. What happened on the third run? (right-there)
4. Why did Sam shout "It flies!"? (put-together)
5. Why did Sam hold the string tight on the way home? (put-together)
6. Write one **why** question of your own about the story, then answer it
   with text evidence.

## Independent practice

Read "The Kite Test" again silently. Answer in your notebook, each answer
followed by "I know this because the story says…" with quoted words:

1. Who is this story about?
2. What was the problem at the start?
3. How many runs did Sam make before the kite flew?
4. How did Sam probably feel at the end? Use one story sentence as proof.

## Application

Find any book you are reading (or ask the adult for one). Ask the adult
three questions about it — one who/what/where and two why/how. The adult
answers each **with quoted text evidence**. You check: did they really use
the story's words? Switch roles if you like.

## Exit check

Answer with text evidence: *In "The Kite Test," what did Sam do when the
wind was too weak?* (One sentence of proof earns full marks.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** The adult reads the story aloud while the learner follows
  along, then answers orally; the adult scribes quoted evidence.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner points to the sentence in the text
  and the adult reads it back as the "proof sentence."
- **Extension:** Find one question about "The Kite Test" that the story does
  **not** answer (e.g., "What color is the kite?"). Explain how you know the
  story gives no evidence — a good reader notices missing proof, too.

## Teacher note (misconceptions)

- Learners often answer from prior knowledge ("kites need string") instead
  of the text. Require the words *"the story says"* before accepting an
  answer for the first two weeks of this unit.
- Watch for evidence that is only vaguely related ("He ran" for a *why*
  question). Ask: "Which words explain the *reason*?"
