# Unit 03 — Communities over Time and Historical Evidence

**Grade:** 3 (ages 8–9) · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#21](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/21)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 11–14 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, 30–35 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

How do we know what happened in a community long ago? This unit answers
that question with evidence. Learners **build and use chronological
sequences** to compare developments that happened at the same time
(D2.His.1.3-5); **compare life in the past to life today**
(D2.His.2.3-5); **summarize how different kinds of historical sources**
— artifacts, photographs, maps, documents, and oral accounts — **are used
to explain events in the past** (D2.His.9.3-5); **compare information
from different sources about the same event, infer a source's audience
and purpose from clues inside the source, and judge how useful a source
is for a question** (D2.His.10, 11, 13.3-5, adult-guided); **generate
questions about the people who shaped change** (D2.His.3.3-5);
**explain probable causes and effects** (D2.His.14.3-5); and **use
evidence to develop a claim about the past** (D2.His.16.3-5). The
unit's project — **Mini-Exhibit: One Change, Two Pieces of Evidence** —
asks the learner to pick one change in the fictional town of Cedar
Hollow, place it on a timeline, and defend a claim about it with two
pieces of evidence from two different kinds of sources.

## The fictional town — read this first

**Cedar Hollow is a made-up town created for this unit. It is not a
real place, and its "history" is practice material, not real history.**
Every date, newspaper story, and letter in this unit is labeled
*fictional practice source* wherever it appears. The adult says this out
loud at the start of Week 11 and again whenever a new source is
introduced: "We are practicing historian skills on a pretend town, so
that when we later study a real town, we already know how to ask good
questions." Real-world source work in this unit is limited to the
Library of Congress analysis routine (Observe–Reflect–Question) and the
Resource Pack references; no real town's history is invented or
rewritten here.

**Cedar Hollow timeline (fictional practice data):**

| Year | Event |
|---|---|
| 1880 | First families build homes near the river crossing. |
| 1895 | A water-powered mill opens on the river. |
| 1910 | The railroad station opens. |
| 1928 | The first brick school opens. |
| 1950 | A bridge replaces the ferry boat. |
| 1995 | The old mill opens as a museum. |

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Build** a chronological sequence of at least six related events
   and **use it** to tell which events happened before, after, or at
   the same time as each other (D2.His.1.3-5).
2. **Compare** life in a past time period to life today in at least
   two areas (for example travel and communication), using evidence
   from a source (D2.His.2.3-5).
3. **Summarize** how five kinds of historical sources (artifact,
   photograph, map, document, oral account) are used to explain the
   past, and **match** a question to the kind of source most likely to
   answer it (D2.His.9.3-5).
4. **Compare** two sources about the same event, **state** what they
   agree and disagree on, and **infer** each source's audience and
   purpose from clues inside the source (D2.His.10, 11.3-5).
5. **Explain** at least one probable cause and one probable effect of
   a community change, **generate** a question about the people who
   shaped that change, and **develop a claim** about the past
   supported by two pieces of evidence (D2.His.3, 14, 16.3-5).

## Essential question

*How do we know what happened long ago — and how do we tell when two
accounts of the same event disagree?*

## Vocabulary

**Timeline** — a line that puts events in the order they happened, with
earlier events on the left and later events on the right. ·
**Chronological order** — time order: first, next, then, last. ·
**Past / present** — the past is time that already happened; the
present is now. · **Source** — something we learn from: a thing, a
picture, a map, a paper, or a person's words. · **Artifact** — an
object made or used by people in the past (a tool, a dish, a toy). ·
**Document** — a paper with writing from the past (a letter, a
newspaper story, a list). · **Oral account** — history told out loud
by a person, often someone who remembers it. · **Primary source** — a
source made at the time of the event, by someone who was there or close
to it. · **Secondary source** — a source made later, by someone who
studied the event afterwards. · **Audience** — the people a source was
made for. · **Purpose** — the reason a source was made (to tell news,
to share feelings, to persuade, to remember). · **Cause** — why
something happened. · **Effect** — what happened as a result. ·
**Evidence** — facts from sources that support a claim. · **Claim** —
a statement about the past that you can support with evidence. ·
**Perspective** — one person's or group's way of seeing an event,
shaped by what they experienced.

## Accessibility and safety

- All image tasks have a text-only alternative; the assessed skill is
  reasoning, never eyesight or handwriting — the adult may scribe.
- The personal-timeline investigation (Week 11, S3) uses only
  adult-approved, private family events; nothing from it enters this
  repository.
- The two bridge sources are fictional practice texts. The adult
  names this every time, so learners never mistake practice material
  for a real town's history.
- No real learner names, photographs, schedules, or locations are
  published in this repository.
