# Answer Key — Unit 03: Communities over Time and Historical Evidence

**For the guiding adult only. Do not hand this key to the learner.**
Every practice item, quiz question, assessment task, and project
criterion is solved here. Open responses accept any answer matching
the stated reasoning; wording may differ. All Cedar Hollow material
is fictional practice material.

## Practice set A — Sequencing

- **A1.** 1880 first homes → 1895 mill opens → 1910 railroad station
  → 1950 bridge replaces ferry.
- **A2.** Just before the brick school (1928): the railroad station
  (1910). Just after it: the bridge (1950).
- **A3.** False. The mill became a museum in 1995; the station opened
  in 1910. 1995 is 85 years after 1910, so the museum came last, not
  first.
- **A4.** Sample: childhood event — the mill opens (1895, the year
  they were born); teenage event — the railroad station (1910, age
  15). In the overlapping-lives sense, yes: both events happened
  during this one person's childhood, even though they were 15 years
  apart — "at the same time" here means overlapping lives, not the
  same day.
- **A5.** The station (1910) and mill (1895) are swapped. Years prove
  it: 1895 comes before 1910. Cause also proves it: workers needed
  the mill's jobs before goods needed fast transport.
- **A6.** Any three with honest reasons. Sample set: 1880 homes
  (founding — without homes there is no town); 1910 station
  (connection — the town joins the wider world); 1950 bridge
  (crossing — daily travel changes for everyone). Accept any
  defended trio.

## Practice set B — Then and now

- **B1.** Sample chart:
  - Travel across the river: Then (1950 and before) — ferry boat,
    waiting in rain. Now — bridge, cars and buses, any weather.
  - School: Then (1928) — new brick school, shared books, board at
    the front. Now — many classrooms, own books, computers.
  - Sending news: Then (1950) — letter or town newspaper, arriving
    in days. Now — phone message in seconds.
- **B2.** Sentence 2 is best: it names a specific difference with
  evidence on both sides. Sentence 1 is a vague judgment ("bad /
  good") with no facts. Sentence 3 is false and insulting: people
  then knew plenty about travel — they built ferries, wagons, and
  railroads.
- **B3.** Any two: the dirt road (not pavement); the horse-drawn
  wagon (not a car or truck); barrels and crates for goods; the
  old-style lamp post; wooden storefronts with porches.
- **B4.** Any honest pair. Sample: better now — crossing in rain
  without waiting; good then — a wagon needed no gasoline and could
  stop anywhere. One-sided answers ("everything was worse") must be
  revised.
- **B5.** "Nobody" is false: some people then walked, rode wagons,
  or took trains, and some people still ride horses today.
  "Everybody" is false: many people today walk, bike, or take buses.
  Good comparisons avoid always/never words.
- **B6.** Changed sample: in 1880 there was no school, mill,
  station, or bridge; by 1950 all four existed. Same sample: the town
  still stood by the same river crossing, and people still needed
  water, food, and homes.

## Practice set C — Source kinds

- **C1.**
  1. Map — maps show where things were.
  2. Oral account (or document/letter) — feelings live in people's
     words; accept either with the feelings reason.
  3. Artifact — the ticket itself is the object.
  4. Document — a school record or list.
  5. Photograph — a picture shows what a place looked like.
- **C2.** (a) Primary — written the week of the event by someone
  close to it. (b) Secondary — written in 1995, 45 years later, by
  someone studying the event. (c) Primary — printed the week of the
  event, reporting what happened. (d) Secondary — written in 2020 by
  someone studying the event afterwards.
- **C3.** Sample: Observe — "I notice a horse pulling a wagon loaded
  with sacks on a dirt road." Reflect — "I think goods moved by horse
  wagon then." Question — "What was in the sacks?" Cannot tell —
  "The picture cannot tell me the town's name (signs are blank) or
  the year." Accept any honest entry per step.
- **C4.** Sample correction: "A photograph shows one street at one
  moment. It cannot show the year, people's thoughts, or what
  happened before or after — for those, we need other sources."
- **C5.** Try first: document (a mill record or list) or artifact
  (a mill product or tool) — accept either with a reason. Probably
  not helpful: a map (maps show places, not products) or an oral
  account about feelings (feelings do not name products). Accept
  any reasoned pair.
- **C6.** Primary. For the question "What did people in 1995 think
  about the mill?" the label was made in 1995 by people of that
  time, expressing their own view. The question changes the answer:
  the same label is secondary for 1895 facts.

## Practice set D — Two sources, one event

- **D1.** Agree (any two): the bridge opened on Saturday; the first
  wagon crossed; the ferry era ended. Only one source mentions (any
  two): Source A — the crowd, bells, flags, the mayor's "any
  weather" quote. Source B — the father's twenty-year job ending,
  his quiet sadness, the missed river mornings.
- **D2.** Source A audience: the whole town. Clue: printed in the
  Gazette newspaper / shouting headline / quotes the mayor.
  Source A purpose: to announce happy news. Clue: celebrating tone,
  crowd, bells, flags. Source B audience: one cousin (family).
  Clue: "Dear Cousin" / signed "Your cousin, Mara." Source B
  purpose: to share personal feelings. Clue: "I will miss..."
  / father's quiet sadness — things told to family, not a crowd.
- **D3.** Sample chain: Probable cause — people were tired of
  waiting for the ferry in rain, and the town had grown large enough
  to afford a bridge. Event — the bridge opens (1950). Happy effect
  — people cross in any weather. Sad effect — the ferry worker
  loses his twenty-year job.
- **D4.** Sample claim: "I claim the bridge changed daily life for
  everyone in Cedar Hollow. My evidence is the Gazette's report that
  people could now cross in any weather, and Mara's letter showing
  the ferry worker's job ended." Sample unanswerable question:
  "How much did the bridge cost?" (Neither source says.)
- **D5.** Sample: Source B shows one family's sadness, not the whole
  town's view. Source A shows many people celebrating. One source
  gives one perspective; a whole-town claim needs more than one
  source — and must weigh both feelings.
- **D6.** (1) Source A — it describes the crowd, bells, and flags.
  (2) Source B — only it mentions the twenty-year job ending.
  (3) Source A — only it quotes the mayor.

## Formative quiz — Week 13 (10 pts)

1. First homes (1880) → mill opens (1895) → bridge (1950).
2. Artifact, photograph, map, document, oral account (any order).
3. Map — maps show where things were. (Accept: "map, because a
   letter would not draw the dock.")
4. (a) Any two: dirt road; horse-drawn wagon with sacks; barrels and
   crates; old-style lamp; wooden storefronts. (b) Any honest gap:
   the town's name (signs are blank), the year, what people thought
   (no people shown). Reason must match the gap.
5. (a) Agree: the bridge opened on Saturday and the first wagon
   crossed. (b) Differ: the Gazette emphasizes celebration (crowd,
   bells, flags); the letter emphasizes loss and worry (the
   twenty-year job ending).
6. Secondary — written in 1995, 100 years after the event, by
   someone studying it afterwards.
7. Audience = the people a source was made for. Clue: it is printed
   in the Gazette newspaper / has a shouting headline / quotes the
   mayor speaking to the town.
8. False. Different sources can both be honest: they stood in
   different places and noticed different things (crowd vs. ferry
   family). Differences in emphasis are normal; check facts
   separately from feelings.

## Unit assessment (30 pts)

1. 1880 first homes · 1895 mill opens · 1910 railroad station ·
   1928 brick school · 1950 bridge replaces ferry · 1995 mill
   becomes a museum. (2 pts: 1 for order, 1 for years.)
2. Yes. 1950 − 1928 = 22 years earlier. (2 pts: 1 for yes, 1 for the
   subtraction.)
3. Childhood event: the railroad station (1910, the birth year).
   Age-18 event: the brick school (1928). Not the same year — 18
   years apart. One person's life connects them because this person
   lived through both: born the year the station opened, 18 the year
   the school opened. (2 pts.)
4. Sample chart (see B1). Each row: 1 pt for a correct then/now pair
   with a year on the then side.
5. Yes, it is a good comparison: it is specific (ferry vs. bridge),
   and it gives the then-side evidence (the ferry and rain-waiting
   from the sources; the illustration's wagon/dirt road also
   counts). Award 1 pt for the yes, 1 pt per reason (up to 2), with
   the evidence requirement folded into the reasons.
6. (a) Artifact — the ticket itself. (b) Map — where the dock was.
   (c) Photograph — what the street looked like. (d) Document — a
   school record/list. (e) Oral account — what the daughter
   remembers. (2 pts: award partial credit per correct match;
   accept document for (e) if reasoned as "her letter.")
7. (a) Primary — written in 1895, at the time, by the worker himself.
   (b) Audience: himself (a diary is private). Clue: first-person
   "I start work" / "my family" with no addressee — things written
   for oneself, not a crowd. (c) Can tell: the mill opened that day,
   the worker started at sunrise, work was tiring, pay meant bread
   for his family. Cannot tell: how many workers there were, what
   the mill made, or what the owner thought.
8. (a) Any two agreements: bridge opened Saturday; first wagon
   crossed; ferry stopped/ended. (b) Gazette audience: the whole
   town (newspaper/headline/mayor quote); purpose: announce happy
   news (celebrating tone). Letter audience: one cousin (Dear
   Cousin/signed Mara); purpose: share personal feelings (father's
   sadness, missed mornings).
9. Probable cause sample: the town made goods to sell and needed
   faster transport than wagons. Probable effect sample: goods
   traveled faster/farther; shops opened near the station. Question
   sample: "Who wanted the station built, and who paid for it?"
   (1 pt each.)
10. Score per the assessment's own rubric: clear bridge claim (1);
    first evidence matches (1); second evidence matches and comes
    from a different source (2); an honestly unanswerable question
    (1). Sample full answer: "I claim the bridge changed daily
    travel for everyone. My evidence is the Gazette's 'cross in any
    weather' report and Mara's letter showing the ferry stopped.
    I still wonder how much the bridge cost."

## Project — Mini-Exhibit (12 pts)

Score with the rubric in [project.md](project.md). Notes:

- **Timeline order:** the chosen event must sit between its true
  neighbors with correct years (for example, the bridge (1950)
  between the school (1928) and the museum (1995)).
- **Claim:** "The bridge was important" alone earns at most 2 — a
  strong claim names the change's effect ("changed daily travel").
- **Evidence match:** a mill fact cannot support a bridge claim;
  send mismatched exhibits back for revision before scoring.
- **Honest limits:** "What did the bridge cost?" and "Who built the
  bridge?" are strong wonder cards — neither source answers them.
  A wonder card answerable from the sources (for example, "When did
  the bridge open?") earns at most 2.
