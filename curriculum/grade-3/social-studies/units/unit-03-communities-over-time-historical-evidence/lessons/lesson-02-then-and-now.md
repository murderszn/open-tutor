# Lesson 02 — Then and Now: Comparing Life in the Past to Life Today

**Week 12 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 03**

## Student goal

*I can compare life long ago to life today in travel, school, and
communication — and point to the evidence for each difference.*

## Materials

- [`../assets/cedar-hollow-main-street.png`](../assets/cedar-hollow-main-street.png)
  (AI-generated teaching illustration; the lesson reads its
  AI-illustration label aloud).
- Two-column chart: THEN | NOW (paper or whiteboard; adult scribes
  if writing is not the assessed skill).

## Readiness check (2 min)

"How did you get to school today? How do you think a child in Cedar
Hollow got to school in 1928, when the brick school had just opened?"
(Accept: walked, wagon, horse, car today; walked or wagon then.)
"Today we compare carefully — not 'everything was worse' or
'everything was better,' but what exactly was different, and how do
we know?"

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "Remember: Cedar Hollow is pretend.
> The picture is a pretend picture too. We use it to practice
> comparing, not to learn facts about a real town."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

To **compare** the past and the present, historians pick one area of
life at a time and ask two questions: *What was it like then? What is
it like now?* Then they ask a third: *What evidence shows it?*
"Long ago" is not one single time — 1880 and 1950 are both "the past,"
but they are 70 years apart, and life changed a lot between them.
Always name the year you mean.

Three areas for this lesson, with Cedar Hollow evidence:

- **Travel.** Then (around 1910): people crossed the river by ferry
  boat, and goods traveled by horse-drawn wagon on dirt roads (see
  the illustration: a wagon, a dirt road, no cars). Now: people cross
  by bridge in cars and buses, in any weather.
- **School.** Then (1928): the first brick school opens — before
  that, children learned in smaller, simpler rooms. Lessons were
  written on a board at the front, and books were shared because
  there were fewer of them. Now: many classrooms, each learner often
  has their own books, and computers help with lessons.
- **Communication.** Then: news traveled by letter and by the town
  newspaper, which came out on certain days — a letter could take
  days. Now: a message can arrive in seconds by phone.

**Vocabulary in context:** *compare* — tell how two things are alike
and different. *Evidence* — facts from sources that support what you
say. *Present* — now.

> **AI-illustration label (read aloud):** "This is an AI-generated
> teaching picture — a made-up main street, not a real place and not
> a photograph. We are using it to practice looking for clues, the
> same way a historian studies a real old picture."

## Worked example 1 — Travel, then and now (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult shows the illustration and thinks aloud:

"I see a dirt road, not a paved street. I see one brown horse pulling
a wooden wagon loaded with large sacks — no car, no bus, no truck.
On the store porch there are barrels and crates, which are how goods
were stored before cardboard boxes and trucks. So my 'then' claim is:
around 1910, goods in a town like this traveled by horse wagon on
dirt roads. My evidence is the wagon, the horse, and the dirt road
in the picture. My 'now' claim is: goods travel by truck on paved
roads. My evidence is what I see outside today. The difference is
speed and weather: a wagon is slow and a dirt road turns to mud in
rain, while a truck on pavement is faster in any weather. I am not
saying wagons were 'bad' — horses did not need gasoline, and a wagon
could go where no road existed yet."

## Worked example 2 — Communication, then and now (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Then: if you wanted to tell your cousin in another town that the
bridge had opened, you wrote a letter. The letter traveled by wagon,
then by train after 1910, and arrived days later. Evidence: Lesson
04's fictional letter is exactly that kind of source — a person
writing to family about the bridge. Now: you could call or send a
message and your cousin would know in seconds. The difference is not
that people then did not care about news — they did, and that is why
the town had a newspaper. The difference is how fast the news could
travel. A common mistake is to say 'people then knew nothing.' They
knew plenty — they just learned it more slowly."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner fill two rows of the THEN | NOW chart together:

1. School: "Then (1928) — one new brick school, shared books, board
   at the front. Now — ?" (Many rooms, own books, computers.)
2. Travel: "Then — ferry and wagon. Now — ?" (Bridge, cars, buses.)
3. Error-analysis: the adult writes "Then, everyone rode horses;
   now, nobody does" and asks, "What is wrong with this sentence?"
   ("Everyone" and "nobody" are too strong — some people walk, some
   rode wagons, and some people still ride horses today. Good
   comparisons avoid always/never words.)

## Independent practice (4 min)

Learner completes practice set B, items B1–B2 (see

## Application (3 min)

"Pick one area — travel, school, or communication. Tell me one way
it is better now, and one way it was good then." (Sample: travel —
now is faster in rain; then, a wagon needed no gasoline and could
stop anywhere. Any honest two-sided answer counts; one-sided answers
are revised with the adult.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Name one then/now difference in travel, and the evidence for the
'then' side." (Sample: then, goods moved by horse wagon — evidence:
the wagon and dirt road in the illustration.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** the learner may answer orally or by pointing at the
  illustration; the adult scribes the chart.
- **Reading/language access:** sentence starters: "Then, ___.
  Now, ___. I know because ___."
- **Extension:** the learner compares a second pair of years
  (1880 vs. 1950) instead of past vs. present, and explains why
  "the past" needs a year attached.

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Everything long ago was worse."** Re-teach with the
  application: every change trades something away.
- **"The past was one time."** Always attach a year: 1880 ≠ 1950.
- **"A picture proves everything about the past."** A picture shows
  what the artist chose to show; Lesson 03 teaches what pictures
  cannot tell us.
