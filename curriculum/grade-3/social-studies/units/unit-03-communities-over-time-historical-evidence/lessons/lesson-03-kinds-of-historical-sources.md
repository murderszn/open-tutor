# Lesson 03 — Kinds of Historical Sources

**Week 13 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 03**

## Student goal

*I can name five kinds of historical sources, tell what each kind is
good for, and use Observe–Reflect–Question on a picture.*

## Materials

- [`../assets/cedar-hollow-main-street.png`](../assets/cedar-hollow-main-street.png)
  (AI-generated teaching illustration; caption and text-only
  alternative below).
- Five source-kind cards: artifact, photograph, map, document, oral
  account (adult prepares; one example on each card).

## Readiness check (2 min)

"If you wanted to know what your town looked like 100 years ago,
where would you look? What if you wanted to know what people
*thought* about it?" (Accept: old pictures, the library, ask an older
person, a museum.) "Today we sort those places into five kinds — and
learn that each kind answers some questions well and others badly."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **source** is something we learn from. Historians sort sources into
kinds, because the kind tells you what the source can and cannot show:

1. **Artifact** — an object made or used by people in the past: a
   tool, a dish, a toy, a ferry ticket. Good for: what people used,
   made, and touched. Bad for: what people thought.
2. **Photograph** — a picture taken at the time. Good for: what a
   place or event looked like. Bad for: sounds, smells, or what
   happened before or after the photo.
3. **Map** — a drawing of a place. Good for: where things were, how
   far apart they were, what was near what. Bad for: people's
   feelings.
4. **Document** — a paper with writing from the past: a letter, a
   newspaper story, a list, a school record. Good for: what people
   said, decided, and reported. Bad for: things the writer did not
   know or chose not to write.
5. **Oral account** — history told out loud by a person, often
   someone who remembers it. Good for: personal memories and feelings.
   Bad for: exact dates — memory can blur numbers over time.

Two more words historians use:

- A **primary source** was made at the time of the event, by someone
  who was there or close to it (the ferry worker's letter in Lesson
  04). A **secondary source** was made later, by someone who studied
  the event afterwards (a museum label written in 1995 about the
  1895 mill). The same object can be primary for one question and
  secondary for another — it depends on the question you ask.
- The Library of Congress routine for studying any source has three
  steps: **Observe** (what do you notice?), **Reflect** (what do you
  think it means?), **Question** (what do you wonder?). We add a
  fourth step for this unit: **Cannot tell** (what can this source
  *not* tell us?).

**Vocabulary in context:** *artifact* — an old object. *Document* —
an old paper with writing. *Oral account* — history told out loud.
*Primary source* — made at the time, by someone close to it.
*Secondary source* — made later, by someone studying it.

## Worked example 1 — Observe–Reflect–Question on the illustration (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult thinks aloud, pointing:

- **Observe:** "I notice a dirt road, not pavement. I notice one
  brown horse pulling a wooden wagon with large sacks. I notice
  wooden stores with porches, barrels and crates on a porch, and a
  lamp post. I notice no cars, no people, and the store signs are
  blank."
- **Reflect:** "I think this shows a time when goods moved by horse
  wagon, because the wagon is loaded and on the road. I think the
  town was small, because the street is short and the houses are
  small."
- **Question:** "I wonder what was in the sacks. I wonder what year
  this is meant to show. I wonder who drove the wagon."
- **Cannot tell:** "This picture cannot tell me the town's name —
  the signs are blank. It cannot tell me the year. It cannot tell me
  what people thought about the wagons, because no people are shown.
  A picture that cannot answer a question is not a bad source — it
  just means I need another kind of source for that question."

## Worked example 2 — Matching a question to a source kind (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Question 1: 'Where was the ferry dock?' Best kind: a **map** — maps
show where things were. A letter would not help much; a letter
rarely draws the dock. Question 2: 'How did the ferry worker feel
when the ferry stopped?' Best kind: an **oral account** or a
**document** (a letter) — feelings live in people's words, not in
maps or dishes. Question 3: 'What did a ferry ticket look like?'
Best kind: an **artifact** — the ticket itself. Notice: the question
chooses the source, not the other way around. A good historian asks
the question first, then picks the kind."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the five kind cards:

1. "Which kind would tell us what the mill's machines looked like?"
   (Photograph or artifact — accept either with a reason.)
2. "Which kind would tell us how many children went to the brick
   school in 1928?" (Document — a school record/list.)
3. Error-analysis: the adult says, "This old photograph tells us
   everything about Cedar Hollow in 1910." The learner corrects it:
   "It tells us what one street looked like at one moment. It cannot
   tell us the year, people's thoughts, or what happened before or
   after."

## Independent practice (4 min)

Learner completes practice set C, items C1–C2 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)).

## Application (3 min)

"Your museum label must answer one question: 'What did children
carry to school in 1928?' Pick the best source kind and tell me what
it would show — and what it could not show." (Sample: artifact — an
old school satchel shows size and material, but not what was inside
it every day.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Name the five kinds, and tell me one question each kind answers
best." (Artifact — what people used. Photograph — what a place
looked like. Map — where things were. Document — what people said or
decided. Oral account — what people remember and feel.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** the five kind cards stay visible all lesson; the
  learner may point instead of naming from memory.
- **Reading/language access:** sentence starters: "This source can
  tell me ___. It cannot tell me ___."
- **Extension:** the learner classifies the museum label from 1995
  (about the 1895 mill) as primary or secondary for two different
  questions: for "What did the mill look like in 1895?" it is
  secondary (made 100 years later); for "What did people in 1995
  think about the mill?" it is primary (made in 1995 by people of
  that time).

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Old means true."** Old sources can be wrong, incomplete, or
  one-sided — that is why Lesson 04 compares two sources.
- **"A picture tells everything."** Re-teach the Cannot-tell step.
- **"Primary is always better."** A later summary can be clearer
  and check more sources; "best" depends on the question.
