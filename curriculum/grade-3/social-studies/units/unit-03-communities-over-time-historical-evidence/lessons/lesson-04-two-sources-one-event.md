# Lesson 04 — Two Sources, One Event: The 1950 Bridge

**Week 13 · S3 (investigation session) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 03**

> **Fiction label (read aloud first):** "Both sources below are
> pretend. We wrote them for practice. They are not real newspaper
> stories or real letters. Real historians do this same job with real
> sources — comparing what agrees and what differs."

## Student goal

*I can compare two sources about the same event, tell what they
agree and disagree on, and infer each source's audience and purpose
from clues inside the source.*

## Materials

- Source A and Source B below (adult reads aloud; learner follows
  along or listens).

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Two children both watched the same school assembly. One says, 'It
was fun!' The other says, 'It was too loud.' Can both be right?"
(Yes — same event, different experiences.) "Today two pretend
sources describe the same day in Cedar Hollow: the day the bridge
opened in 1950. They will not say the same things. That is normal —
and it is the historian's job to notice."

## The event

In 1950, Cedar Hollow's new bridge opened and the ferry boat stopped
running. Before the bridge, people crossed the river by ferry. After
the bridge, people crossed on foot, by wagon, and later by car, in any
weather.

## Source A — Newspaper story (fictional practice source)

*The Cedar Hollow Gazette, Saturday, the week the bridge opened
(fictional). Written by a town reporter for the people of the town.*

> "THE BRIDGE IS OPEN! A great crowd gathered on Saturday morning as
> the first wagon crossed the new bridge over the river. Bells rang
> and children waved flags. 'Now we can cross in any weather,' said
> the mayor. 'No more waiting for the ferry in the rain.' The bridge
> was built with strong steel and wood, and it will carry wagons,
> horses, and people safely across the river for many years to come."

## Source B — Letter (fictional practice source)

*A letter from Mara, the daughter of the ferry worker, to her cousin
in another town, written the week the bridge opened (fictional).*

> "Dear Cousin, The bridge opened on Saturday. Father drove the ferry
> for twenty years, and now the ferry will stop. We stood on the bank
> and watched the first wagon cross. Father smiled, but later he was
> quiet. He does not know what work he will do now. I will miss the
> river mornings, when the mist sat on the water and Father called
> out to the passengers he knew by name. Your cousin, Mara"

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 8 min)

When two sources describe the same event, historians do three jobs:

1. **Find agreements.** What do both sources say? (Both say the
   bridge opened on Saturday, the first wagon crossed, and the ferry
   era ended.)
2. **Find disagreements — or different emphases.** What does one
   say that the other does not? (Source A celebrates the crowd, the
   bells, and crossing in any weather. Source B worries about the
   ferry worker's lost job and misses the river mornings. They do
   not contradict each other on facts — but they feel very
   different, because the writers stood in different places.)
3. **Infer audience and purpose from clues inside the source.**
   **Audience** is who the source was made for; **purpose** is why
   it was made. Clues: Source A is printed in a newspaper, uses a big
   excited headline, and quotes the mayor — clues that its audience
   is the whole town and its purpose is to share happy news. Source
   B begins "Dear Cousin," uses "I" and "Father," and talks about
   feelings — clues that its audience is one family member and its
   purpose is to share personal news and feelings.

A source's **perspective** — how its maker saw the event — shapes
what it includes and leaves out. The reporter stood with the crowd;
Mara stood with her father. Neither is lying. A historian who reads
only one would get half the story.

**Vocabulary in context:** *audience* — who a source was made for.
*Purpose* — why it was made. *Perspective* — one person's way of
seeing an event. *Agree* — both sources say the same thing.
*Emphasis* — what a source spends the most time on.

## Worked example 1 — Agreements and differences (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult builds the chart aloud:

| | Source A (newspaper) | Source B (letter) |
|---|---|---|
| What happened? | Bridge opened Saturday; first wagon crossed | Bridge opened Saturday; first wagon crossed |
| How do people feel? | Happy, celebrating — bells, flags, crowd | Sad and worried — quiet father, missed mornings |
| What ends? | Waiting for the ferry in the rain | The ferry worker's job of twenty years |

"Agreement: the bridge opened on Saturday and a wagon crossed first.
Difference: Source A emphasizes the town's gain (cross in any
weather); Source B emphasizes one family's loss (a job ending).
Neither source is wrong. Together they give a fuller picture than
either alone — and that is why historians use more than one source."

## Worked example 2 — Audience and purpose from clues (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"How do I know Source A's audience is the whole town? Clues inside
the source: it is printed in a newspaper called the Gazette, it has a
shouting headline, and it quotes the mayor — a public person
speaking to everyone. How do I know Source B's audience is one
cousin? Clues: it starts 'Dear Cousin,' it signs 'Your cousin,
Mara,' and it talks about Father's feelings — things you tell family,
not a crowd. Purpose follows audience: the newspaper's purpose is to
announce good news to the town; the letter's purpose is to share
personal feelings with family. If I swapped them — if the letter had
been written to the whole town — Mara would probably not have
written about her father's quiet sadness. Audience shapes purpose,
and purpose shapes what gets written."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together:

1. "Which source would you use to answer: 'How did the crowd feel
   on opening day?' Why?" (Source A — it describes the crowd, bells,
   and flags.)
2. "Which source would you use to answer: 'What happened to the
   ferry worker's job?' Why?" (Source B — only it mentions the
   twenty-year job ending.)
3. Error-analysis: the adult says, "Source B proves the bridge was a
   bad idea." The learner corrects: "It proves one family was sad
   about the ferry ending. It does not prove the bridge was bad for
   the whole town — Source A shows many people were glad. One source
   cannot settle a whole-town question."

## Application (3 min)

"You are the museum curator. You may display only one of these two
sources on the wall. Which one do you pick, and what important thing
would visitors miss?" (Either pick with an honest "miss" counts: pick
A, miss the ferry family's feelings; pick B, miss the town's
celebration. The adult-guided answer: display both — that is why
museums use more than one source.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Name one thing both sources agree on, and one way they differ."
(Agree: bridge opened Saturday, first wagon crossed. Differ: feelings
— celebration vs. sadness/worry — or content: crowd/weather vs. job
ending.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** the adult reads both sources aloud; the learner
  answers by pointing to lines in the chart.
- **Reading/language access:** sentence starters: "Both sources say
  ___. Only Source ___ says ___."
- **Extension (adult-guided, D2.His.13.3-5):** "Is Source B useful
  for studying how the whole town felt? Only partly — it shows one
  family's feelings, not everyone's. It is very useful for studying
  how the ferry ending affected workers' families. Usefulness depends
  on the question."

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Different means one is lying."** Different perspectives are
  normal; check facts separately from feelings.
- **"The newspaper is neutral because it is printed."** Newspapers
  choose what to celebrate and quote; audience and purpose always
  shape a source.
- **"One good source is enough."** One source gives one perspective;
  claims about a whole town need more than one.
