# Lesson 01 — Timelines: Putting Events in Order

**Week 11 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 03**

## Student goal

*I can put events in time order on a timeline and tell which happened
first, next, and last — and which happened at the same time.*

## Materials

- The Cedar Hollow timeline (unit README, or
  [`../assets/timeline-cedar-hollow.svg`](../assets/timeline-cedar-hollow.svg)
  printed or shown on screen).
- Paper strip and pencil (the adult may scribe); six event cards
  (adult prepares: one card per Cedar Hollow event, year on the back).

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Tell me three things you did yesterday, in the order you did them."
(Accept any sensible order: woke up, ate breakfast, went to school.)
"Today we do the same job for a whole town — but the 'yesterday' is
more than 100 years long."

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "Cedar Hollow is a made-up town we
> invented for practice. Its dates are pretend dates. We practice on a
> pretend town so we know how to read a real timeline later."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **timeline** is a line that puts events in the order they happened.
Earlier events go on the left. Later events go on the right. The order
is called **chronological order** — first, next, then, last. Historians
say "chronological" the way you say "in order."

Three rules for reading a timeline:

1. **Read left to right.** The left end is the earliest time. The
   right end is the latest time.
2. **Compare positions, not distances.** Two events close together on
   the line did not necessarily happen close together in years — check
   the dates. (1880 and 1895 look close on our strip, but they are 15
   years apart.)
3. **"At the same time" means overlapping lives, not the same day.**
   Two events in the same decade happened while some of the same
   people were alive, even if they were years apart.

**Vocabulary in context:** *timeline* — a line of events in time
order. *Chronological order* — first, next, then, last. *Past* — time
that already happened.

Cedar Hollow's six events, in order: 1880 first homes · 1895 mill
opens · 1910 railroad station · 1928 brick school · 1950 bridge
replaces ferry · 1995 mill becomes a museum.

## Worked example 1 — Ordering six cards (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult shuffles the six event cards (years hidden) and thinks
aloud:

"I know a town needs homes before it needs a mill, because workers
need somewhere to live — so 'first families build homes' must be
early. A mill needs workers too, so the mill comes after the homes.
A railroad station comes when the town is already growing, because
trains carry goods the town makes — so the station comes after the
mill. A brick school comes when many families live here — after the
station. The bridge replaces the ferry, and a ferry only stops when
something better arrives — so the bridge is late. The mill becomes a
museum only after the mill is old and no longer working — so the
museum is last. Now I turn the cards over to check the years: 1880,
1895, 1910, 1928, 1950, 1995. My order matches the years. When I was
unsure, I asked: 'What had to exist first?'"

## Worked example 2 — Before, after, at the same time (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Was the brick school open when the bridge replaced the ferry? The
school opened in 1928. The bridge came in 1950. 1928 is before 1950,
so yes — the school was already open for 22 years. Did the mill and
the railroad station open at the same time? The mill opened in 1895
and the station in 1910 — 15 years apart, so no, but a person born in
1895 would have been 15 when the station opened, so one person's
childhood can span both events. That is what 'at the same time' can
mean in history: overlapping lives, not the same day."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the timeline strip:

1. Point to the earliest event. Point to the latest event.
2. "The railroad station opened in 1910. What happened just before
   it?" (The mill, 1895.)
3. "What happened just after it?" (The brick school, 1928.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult deliberately places the 1950 bridge card
   before the 1910 station card and asks, "Is this right? How do you
   know?" (No — 1950 is later than 1910; a bridge cannot replace a
   ferry before the town has grown around the station, and the year
   itself proves the order.)

## Independent practice (4 min)

Learner completes practice set A, items A1–A2 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)). The adult does not correct mid-task;
errors are discussed in the exit check.

## Application (3 min)

"Your town's library is making a timeline wall. They have room for
only three Cedar Hollow events. Which three would you pick to show
how the town changed most — and why?" (Any three with a reason
connecting them count; the key gives a sample set: 1880 homes, 1910
station, 1950 bridge — founding, connection, crossing.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Put these in order: mill opens (1895), first homes (1880), bridge
(1950)." (Answer: homes, mill, bridge.) If the learner hesitates,
re-teach with the "what had to exist first?" question.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep years visible on the cards; let the learner
  answer by pointing and ordering cards instead of writing dates.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads every card aloud;
  "before" and "after" are shown with left/right hand gestures.
- **Extension:** the learner adds a seventh, clearly labeled
  *pretend* event of their own invention between two real timeline
  events and explains where it fits and why (for example, "1900: the
  town's first market opens — after the mill, before the station,
  because workers would want a market").

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Close on the line means close in years."** Re-teach rule 2:
  always check the numbers, not the gaps.
- **"The first event I learned about is the earliest."** Order comes
  from dates and causes, not from the order the adult told the story.
- **"At the same time means the same day."** Use the 1895/1910
  childhood example: overlapping lives count.
