# Lesson 05 — Causes, Effects, and Claims from Evidence

**Week 14 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 03**

## Student goal

*I can explain a probable cause and a probable effect of a community
change, ask a question about the people who shaped the change, and
make a claim supported by two pieces of evidence.*

## Materials

- The Cedar Hollow timeline (unit README or
  [`../assets/timeline-cedar-hollow.svg`](../assets/timeline-cedar-hollow.svg)).
- Claim frame (on the board): "I claim ___. My evidence is ___ and
  ___."

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Why did the ferry stop in 1950?" (Because the bridge opened.)
"Was that the only cause? What else had to be true?" (The town had
grown; people wanted to cross in any weather; the town could pay for
a bridge.) "Today we learn that most changes have more than one
cause — and that a claim is only as strong as its evidence."

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "Cedar Hollow is still pretend.
> The causes and claims we practice today are practice claims about
> a pretend town."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **cause** is why something happened. An **effect** is what
happened as a result. Historians say **probable** cause and
**probable** effect because we cannot always prove a single reason —
we weigh the evidence and pick the most likely explanation. Most
events have several causes, and one cause can have several effects.
Chains help: cause → event → effect → next effect.

Three Cedar Hollow chains (from earlier lessons):

1. **Mill chain (1895):** the river could power a mill (cause) →
   the mill opens (event) → workers move to town for jobs (effect) →
   more homes are built (next effect).
2. **Railroad chain (1910):** the town makes goods people elsewhere
   want (cause) → the station opens (event) → goods travel faster
   and farther (effect) → more shops open near the station (next
   effect).
3. **Bridge chain (1950):** people are tired of waiting for the
   ferry in rain (cause) → the bridge opens (event) → people cross
   in any weather (effect) and the ferry worker loses his job (a
   second, sadder effect).

Next, **questions about changemakers.** A changemaker is a person or
group whose actions shaped a change. Good historian questions about
them start with who, why, and how: "Who wanted the bridge built, and
why? Who built it? Who was helped, and who was hurt?" (D2.His.3.3-5.)
Questions come before claims: you cannot claim what you have not
asked about.

Finally, a **claim** is a statement about the past that you support
with evidence. A claim needs at least two pieces of evidence from
sources — one source alone is too thin for a town-wide claim. The
claim frame is: "I claim ___. My evidence is ___ and ___." Evidence
must actually support the claim: a fact about the mill does not
support a claim about the bridge.

**Vocabulary in context:** *probable* — likely, based on evidence,
but not certain. *Changemaker* — a person or group whose actions
shaped a change. *Claim* — a statement about the past backed by
evidence.

## Worked example 1 — Building a cause/effect chain (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Event: the railroad station opens in 1910. What is a probable
cause? The mill had been running since 1895, so the town made goods
— flour, lumber — that people in other towns wanted. Goods need a
fast way to travel, and trains are faster than wagons. So: goods to
sell + need for fast travel → station opens. What is a probable
effect? Goods now leave town faster, so the mill can sell more, and
more shops open near the station for travelers. A second effect:
people can now visit other towns easily, so ideas and news travel
faster too. Notice I said 'probable' — I was not there in 1910, and
our pretend sources do not give the station's exact reason. Probable
is honest; certain would be pretending to know more than the
evidence shows."

## Worked example 2 — From question to claim (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Question: 'Who shaped the bridge change most — the town leaders
who ordered the bridge, or the workers who built it?' Hmm — our two
sources do not name the builders, so I cannot answer that question
yet. A good historian notices missing evidence instead of guessing.
So I narrow my question: 'Did the bridge change daily life more than
the railroad did?' Now I can build a claim: 'I claim the bridge
changed daily life more than the railroad, because (1) the newspaper
says people could now cross in any weather instead of waiting for
the ferry, and (2) Mara's letter shows the change ended a twenty-year
job — daily life changed for travelers and for workers.' Both pieces
of evidence come from our two sources, and both are about daily life.
If my evidence were about the mill instead, my claim would fail —
evidence must match the claim."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together:

1. "Event: the mill opens in 1895. Name one probable cause and one
   probable effect." (Cause: river power + workers nearby. Effect:
   jobs bring families; more homes built.)
2. "Question check: 'What color was the mayor's hat on bridge day?'
   Is this a good historian question? Why not?" (It is not — the
   sources do not say, and the answer would not explain the change.
   Good questions ask about causes, effects, and people.)
3. Error-analysis: the adult offers the claim "The bridge was the
   most important event in Cedar Hollow history" with evidence "the
   mill opened in 1895." The learner rejects it: the evidence is
   about the mill, not the bridge — evidence must match the claim.

## Application (3 min)

"Finish this claim frame about any Cedar Hollow change you choose:
'I claim ___. My evidence is ___ and ___.' Then tell me one
question your evidence cannot answer." (Any well-matched claim
counts; the 'cannot answer' part practices honesty about limits.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Give me a claim about the bridge with two pieces of evidence."
(Sample: "The bridge changed daily travel because the newspaper says
people could cross in any weather, and Mara's letter shows the ferry
stopped running.")

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** the claim frame stays on the board; the learner may
  answer orally with adult scribing.
- **Reading/language access:** sentence starters: "A probable cause
  is ___. A probable effect is ___."
- **Extension (adult-guided, D2.His.17.3-5):** the adult reads a
  one-paragraph pretend museum label (a secondary source) that
  claims "The railroad was Cedar Hollow's greatest change," and the
  learner summarizes its central claim in one sentence and checks
  whether its evidence matches — practicing summary of a secondary
  work's claim.
- **Extension (D2.His.12.3-5):** the learner writes two questions
  that would require *both* bridge sources plus a third kind of
  source (for example, a map showing where the bridge and ferry dock
  stood).

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"One cause, one effect."** Real changes have chains and
  branches; the bridge had a happy effect and a sad effect.
- **"A claim is an opinion."** A claim without evidence is an
  opinion; a claim with matching evidence is a historian's argument.
- **"Probable means unsure, so it is weak."** Probable is honest
  history — it says "this is what the evidence best supports."
