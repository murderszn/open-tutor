# Unit 04 — Local Government, Participation, and Public Services

**Grade:** 3 (ages 8–9) · **Subject:** Social Studies · **Track issue:** [#21](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/21)
**Status:** draft — awaiting educator review and merge. **Weeks:** 15–18 of the 36-week track (see [scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md)).
**Session model:** 4 sessions per week, 30–35 minutes each (16 sessions total).

## What this unit is about

Who fixes the pothole? Who keeps the library open? Who decides where
the new swing set goes? This unit answers those questions close to
home. Learners **distinguish the responsibilities and powers of
government officials at different levels and branches**
(D2.Civ.1.3-5); **explain the origins, functions, and structure of
systems of government, including those created by the U.S. and state
constitutions** (D2.Civ.5.3-5); **examine the origins and purposes of
rules, laws, and key U.S. constitutional provisions** (D2.Civ.3.3-5);
**explain how groups of people make rules to create responsibilities
and protect freedoms** (D2.Civ.4.3-5); **explain how a democracy relies
on people's responsible participation, and what that implies for
individuals** (D2.Civ.2.3-5); **describe ways people benefit from and
are challenged by working together, including through government**
(D2.Civ.6.3-5); **apply civic virtues and democratic principles in
school settings** (D2.Civ.7.3-5); **use deliberative processes, compare
decision-making procedures, explain how rules and laws change society
and how people change rules and laws, and explain how policies are
developed to address public problems** (D2.Civ.9, 11–13.3-5);
**explain how government pays for the goods and services it provides**
(D2.Eco.12.3-5); and **use deliberative and democratic procedures to
decide on and act on a classroom or school civic problem**
(D4.8.3-5). The unit's project — **Our Class Council Proposal** — asks
the learner to name a real classroom or school problem, propose a fair
policy for it, and defend it in a short presentation.

## The fictional town — read this first

**Cedar Hollow is a made-up town created for this track. It is not a
real place, and its mayor, council, and services are practice
material, not a real town's government.** The adult says this out loud
at the start of Week 15 and again whenever a new Cedar Hollow detail
is introduced: "We are practicing on a pretend town, so that when we
look at our real town, we already know what questions to ask."
Real local governments vary: some towns have a mayor, some have a
town supervisor or a city manager, and some services are run by a
county or a school district instead of a town. The teacher guide tells
the adult to name the real local setup before Lesson 02, and to swap
job titles if the real town uses different ones — the jobs themselves
(streets, fire, library, parks) stay the same. No learner ever
contacts a real official alone; any real-world contact is
adult-supervised, and no names, addresses, or schedules enter this
repository.

**Cedar Hollow's local government (fictional practice material):**

| Role | Job in Cedar Hollow |
|---|---|
| Mayor Ana Ruiz | Leads town meetings; carries out the council's decisions; speaks for the town. |
| Town council (4 members: Ben Carter, Priya Patel, Tom Okafor, Grace Liu) | Discusses and votes on local rules (called ordinances) and the town's spending plan (the budget). |
| Road crew | Fixes potholes, plows snow, keeps street signs up. |
| Fire department | Puts out fires, rescues people, teaches fire safety. |
| Library | Lends books, runs story hour, helps people find information. |
| Parks crew | Mows, plants, fixes playground equipment, keeps the park safe. |
| Town clerk | Keeps town records and meeting notes. |

## Prerequisites (developmental expectations; re-taught here as needed)

- Explain why a familiar rule exists and what happens when people
  follow or break it (grade-2 entry expectations; diagnostic Weeks
  1–2).
- Make a group decision by listening, discussing, and voting (Unit 02
  deliberation routine; grade-2 citizenship expectations).
- Weigh benefits and costs of a simple choice (diagnostic Week 2;
  Unit 02).
- Sequence events and explain a probable cause and effect (Unit 03).
- Sort objects or jobs by one or two attributes and explain a choice
  with one or two reasons in speech or 2–3 written sentences.

## Objectives (measurable, adult-assessed)

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to:

1. **Distinguish** local, state, and national government by sorting
   at least eight community needs or jobs to the level that handles
   each, and **name** one responsibility of officials at each level
   (D2.Civ.1.3-5).
2. **Describe** the jobs of a mayor, a town council, and at least
   three public-service departments, and **explain** the three
   branches of the national government in one sentence each — makes
   laws, carries out laws, decides what laws mean (D2.Civ.1, 5.3-5).
3. **Explain** the difference between a rule and a law, **state**
   why groups make rules (to create responsibilities and protect
   freedoms), and **write** a fair classroom rule with a reason
   (D2.Civ.3, 4.3-5).
4. **Trace** how taxes pay for public services, using a fictional
   town budget: given a $100 pretend budget, **state** what share
   goes to each service and **explain** what would be lost if one
   service were cut (D2.Eco.12.3-5).
5. **Compare** three ways of deciding — consensus, vote, and leader
   decides — and **use** a deliberative process (state the problem,
   listen, share reasons, decide, check) to reach a group decision
   (D2.Civ.9, 11.3-5; D4.8.3-5).
6. **Describe** at least four ways people participate in a democracy
   (including voting when old enough, speaking up, helping, and
   serving), and **propose** a classroom or school policy for a real
   problem, with steps for making it happen and a way to check
   whether it worked (D2.Civ.2, 6, 7, 12, 13.3-5).

## Essential question

*Who takes care of the things we share — and how do we get a say in
how they are taken care of?*

## Vocabulary

**Government** — the people and groups that make, carry out, and
enforce rules for a community, state, or country. · **Local
government** — the government closest to home: a town, city, or
county. · **State government** — the government of one state, led by
a governor. · **National (federal) government** — the government of
the whole country, in Washington, D.C. · **Mayor** — the leader of a
town or city government. · **Council** — a group of elected people
who discuss and vote on local rules and spending. · **Ordinance** —
a local law made by a town or city council. · **Budget** — a plan for
how money will be collected and spent. · **Tax** — money people and
businesses pay to a government so it can provide services. ·
**Public service** — work a government does for everyone, such as
fire protection, libraries, parks, and road repair. · **Rule** — an
instruction a group agrees to follow (at home, at school, in a
club). · **Law** — a rule made by a government, written down, that
everyone in that place must follow. · **Constitution** — a country's
or state's most important set of rules, written more than 200 years
ago for the United States, that says how government works and
protects people's freedoms. · **Branch** — one of the three parts of
government: legislative (makes laws), executive (carries out laws),
judicial (decides what laws mean). · **Citizen participation** —
taking part in community life: voting when old enough, speaking up,
helping, serving. · **Deliberation** — deciding together by stating
the problem, listening, sharing reasons, and then deciding. ·
**Consensus** — a decision everyone in the group can accept, even if
it is not everyone's first choice. · **Vote** — a decision made by
counting choices. · **Policy** — a plan or rule a group adopts to
handle a problem. · **Civic virtue** — a good habit of community
life, such as honesty, respect, responsibility, and listening fairly.

## Accessibility and safety

- All image tasks have a text-only alternative; the assessed skill is
  reasoning, never eyesight or handwriting — the adult may scribe.
- The deliberation and proposal work stays in the classroom or home:
  learners do not contact real officials, attend real meetings alone,
  or share personal information. Any real-world look-up (for example,
  finding a town's real mayor on USA.gov) is done by the adult.
- Cedar Hollow's mayor, council, and budget are fictional practice
  material. The adult names this every time, so learners never
  mistake practice material for their real town's government.
- No real learner names, photographs, schedules, or locations are
  published in this repository.
