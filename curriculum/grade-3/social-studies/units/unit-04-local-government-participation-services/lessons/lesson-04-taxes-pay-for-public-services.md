# Lesson 04 — Taxes Pay for Public Services

**Week 17 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 04**

## Student goal

*I can explain how taxes pay for public services, and I can trace
one pretend tax dollar to one service.*

## Materials

- Ten play coins or counters (or ten drawn circles) to represent
  $10 of pretend tax money.
- Cedar Hollow's pretend budget strip (adult prepares, labeled
  FICTIONAL PRACTICE NUMBERS):
  - Roads: $30 · Fire department: $25 · Library: $15 · Parks: $15 ·
    Town hall and records: $15 — total $100.

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Who pays the firefighter? Not the family whose house is on fire —
the firefighter is already paid, before any fire starts. Where does
that money come from?" (Accept guesses; do not correct yet.) "Today
we follow the money, step by step."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **tax** is money people and businesses pay to a government so it
can provide services. The chain has four links:

1. **People and businesses pay taxes.** Two common kinds: a
   *property tax* — paid by people who own land or a building, based
   on its value — and a *sales tax* — a small extra amount added
   when you buy something. In a fictional example: a toy costs $10
   and the sales tax is $1, so the buyer pays $11. The shop keeps
   $10 and sends the $1 tax to the government.
2. **The government collects the money** into one pot.
3. **The council votes a budget** — a plan that says how much of the
   pot goes to each service.
4. **Departments spend it on services** — roads, fire protection,
   the library, parks, and keeping records.

Cedar Hollow's pretend budget (fictional practice numbers, not a real
town's budget) shows the idea with $100:

| Service | Share of $100 |
|---|---|
| Roads | $30 |
| Fire department | $25 |
| Library | $15 |
| Parks | $15 |
| Town hall and records | $15 |
| **Total** | **$100** |

Check the arithmetic: 30 + 25 = 55; 55 + 15 = 70; 70 + 15 = 85;
85 + 15 = 100. Every dollar is assigned; nothing is left over, and
nothing is spent twice.

Two things follow. First, **services are not free** — they are paid
for in advance by everyone together, so nobody has to pay the whole
fire truck alone. Second, **choosing a budget means choosing**: if
the council gives more to roads, it must give less to something else
— unless it collects more taxes, which is itself a decision voters
and councils argue about honestly.

**Vocabulary in context:** *tax* — money paid to a government for
services. *Property tax* — a tax on land or buildings you own.
*Sales tax* — a small extra amount added to a purchase. *Budget* — a
plan for collecting and spending money.

## Worked example 1 — Tracing one tax dollar (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult thinks aloud with the play coins: "Here are 10 pretend
dollars from Cedar Hollow's taxes. The budget says $30 of every $100
goes to roads. That is 30 out of 100 — about one-third. So of my 10
dollars, about 3 go to roads. I will move 3 coins to the 'roads' pile.
Fire gets $25 of $100 — one-quarter — so about 2 or 3 of my 10 coins
go to fire. The library gets $15 of $100, so 1 or 2 of my 10 coins.
Parks: 1 or 2. Town hall: 1 or 2. The exact split of 10 coins is a
rounding problem — 30% of 10 is exactly 3, 25% is 2.5, so we round —
but the idea is exact: **every coin lands in exactly one pile, and
the piles add back up to the whole.** That is what a budget is: a
promise about where every dollar goes."

## Worked example 2 — What a cut costs (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"The council is asked: 'Cut the library from $15 to $5, and give the
$10 saved to roads. Roads go from $30 to $40.' What is gained? Roads
get $10 more — maybe one more pothole crew for a month. What is
lost? The library loses $10 of its $15 — two-thirds of its money.
Story hour might end; new books might stop. So the question is never
just 'do we want better roads?' Everyone wants better roads. The
real question is: **'Is $10 of road work worth losing story hour and
new books?'** Budget choices always have two sides: what you gain
and what you give up. That is why councils argue about budgets in
public — and why residents come to meetings to speak."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the $100 pretend budget:

1. "How much goes to the fire department?" ($25.)
2. "Roads plus parks together?" ($30 + $15 = $45.)
3. "If the council moves $5 from parks to the library, what are the
   new amounts?" (Parks $15 − $5 = $10; library $15 + $5 = $20;
   total still $100.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "Roads $30, fire $25, library $15,
   parks $15, town hall $15 — that adds up to $110, right?" (No:
   30 + 25 + 15 + 15 + 15 = 100. The adult added an extra 10. Always
   re-add a budget before voting on it.)

## Independent practice (4 min)

Learner completes practice set C, items C1–C4 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)). The adult does not correct mid-task;
errors are discussed in the exit check.

## Application (3 min)

"A Cedar Hollow resident says, 'I never use the library, so I should
not pay library tax.' How do you answer, kindly and honestly?" (Sample
answer: taxes pay for services for everyone together — you may not
use the library, but you probably use roads and fire protection, and
your neighbors use the library; the town shares the cost so every
service is there when anyone needs it. Any answer that names shared
payment and a service the speaker does use counts.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Finish the chain: people pay taxes, the government collects the
money, the council votes a ____, and departments spend it on ____."
(Answers: budget; services.) If the learner hesitates, re-teach the
four links in order.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the budget strip visible; let the learner move
  coins or counters instead of doing written arithmetic.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the budget table
  aloud; "tax" is glossed as "shared money for shared jobs."
- **Extension:** the learner proposes their own Cedar Hollow budget
  for $100 (five services, must total exactly $100), and explains in
  two sentences which service they funded most and why.

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Public services are free."** They are free at the moment of use
  because they were paid for in advance, together, through taxes.
- **"Tax money disappears."** A budget assigns every dollar; the
  learner can always ask "where did it go?" and the budget strip
  answers.
- **"More money for one service is free."** Unless new taxes are
  collected, more for one service means less for another. Both sides
  of a budget choice must be named.
