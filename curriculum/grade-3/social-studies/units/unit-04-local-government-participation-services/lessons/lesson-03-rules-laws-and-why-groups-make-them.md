# Lesson 03 — Rules, Laws, and Why Groups Make Them

**Week 16 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 04**

## Student goal

*I can tell a rule from a law, I can say why groups make rules, and I
can write a fair classroom rule with a reason.*

## Materials

- Two sorting labels: RULE and LAW, and six statement cards (adult
  prepares): "Raise your hand before speaking in class," "Stop at a
  red light," "Put your shoes by the door at home," "Do not take
  something that belongs to someone else" (a law against stealing),
  "Return library books on time," "Wear a seat belt in a car."

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Name one rule in our classroom and tell me why it exists." (Accept
any rule with a reason: listening so everyone can learn, walking so
nobody gets hurt.) "Today we learn how a rule is different from a
law — and who gets to make each one."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **rule** is an instruction a group agrees to follow. Your family
has rules. Your classroom has rules. A club has rules. Rules are made
by the group they belong to, and the group can change them.

A **law** is different in three ways:

1. **A law is made by a government** — a town council, a state
   legislature, or Congress — not by one family or classroom.
2. **A law is written down** so everyone can read it and check it.
3. **A law applies to everyone** in that place, and the government
   enforces it — police, courts, and other officials make sure it is
   followed, and there are consequences for breaking it.

So: every law is a kind of rule, but not every rule is a law. "Raise
your hand" is a classroom rule. "Stop at a red light" is a law — it
was made by a government, it is written down, and it applies to
every driver in that place.

Why do groups make rules at all? The C3 Framework puts it this way:
groups make rules **to create responsibilities and protect
freedoms**. That means two jobs at once:

- **Responsibilities** — things each person must do so the group
  works (listen while others speak, return what you borrow).
- **Freedoms** — things each person is free to do because the rules
  protect them (speak your mind, play safely in the park, borrow a
  book and know it will be there).

A fair rule does both: it asks something of everyone and it protects
something for everyone.

Where do a country's most important rules come from? The United
States has a **Constitution** — the country's most important set of
rules, written more than 200 years ago. It says how the government
is set up (the three branches from Lesson 01) and it protects
people's freedoms, such as freedom of speech. States have their own
constitutions too. A town council cannot make a local law that breaks
the Constitution — the basic rules stand above the smaller ones.

**Vocabulary in context:** *rule* — a group's agreed instruction.
*Law* — a rule made and enforced by a government, written down,
applying to everyone in that place. *Constitution* — a country's or
state's most important rules. *Responsibility* — something you are
expected to do for the group. *Freedom* — something you are free to
do because rules protect it.

## Worked example 1 — Sorting rules and laws (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult thinks aloud with two cards: "'Raise your hand before
speaking.' Who made it? Our class. Is it written in a government's
law book? No. Does it apply to the whole town? No, just our class.
So it is a RULE. Now: 'Stop at a red light.' Who made it? A
government, for drivers everywhere in the state. Is it written down?
Yes, in traffic laws. Does it apply to everyone driving? Yes. And
police enforce it. So it is a LAW. The test is three questions: who
made it, is it written in law, and does it apply to everyone in that
place?"

## Worked example 2 — A fair rule does two jobs (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Here is a rule: 'In Cedar Hollow Park, dogs must stay on a leash.'
What responsibility does it create? Dog owners must hold the leash
and control their dog. What freedom does it protect? Other people's
freedom to walk, play, and picnic without being chased or knocked
over. Now try a bad rule: 'Only children with red shoes may use the
swings.' Does it create a fair responsibility? No — shoe color has
nothing to do with safety. Does it protect a freedom? No — it takes
one away for no good reason. A fair rule has a reason that connects
to safety, fairness, or learning. When you write your own rule in
practice, check: does it ask something fair, and does it protect
something real?"

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together:

1. "Wear a seat belt in a car." (Law — made by government, written
   down, enforced, applies to everyone riding.)
2. "Put your shoes by the door at home." (Rule — made by a family,
   applies at home only.)
3. "Return library books on time." (Rule — made by the library for
   its borrowers. Note: a library fine is a library rule, not a
   criminal law; the adult says this plainly if asked.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "A law is just a really big
   rule," and asks the learner to fix it. (Not quite — a law is a
   rule made by a government, written down, and enforced for
   everyone in that place. Size is not the test; who made it is.)

## Independent practice (4 min)

Learner completes practice set B, items B1–B4 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)). The adult does not correct mid-task;
errors are discussed in the exit check.

## Application (3 min)

"Your class wants a new rule: 'Everyone gets a turn as line leader,
in alphabetical order.' Is it fair? Does it create a responsibility
and protect a freedom?" (Sample answer: yes — the responsibility is
to wait for your turn and let others lead; the freedom it protects
is that everyone, not just the loudest or fastest, gets a turn. Any
answer that names both a responsibility and a protected freedom
counts.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Tell me one rule and one law — and the one question that tells them
apart." (Sample: rule — raise your hand; law — stop at a red light.
The question: "Who made it — our group, or a government?") If the
learner hesitates, re-teach the three-question test.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the three-question test on a card (Who made it?
  Is it written in law? Does it apply to everyone here?); let the
  learner answer by sorting cards instead of writing.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads every statement card
  aloud; "responsibility" is glossed as "your job for the group"
  and "freedom" as "something you are free to do."
- **Extension:** the learner rewrites an unfair rule from Worked
  example 2 ("only red shoes on the swings") into a fair one (for
  example, "take turns on the swings, five minutes each, so everyone
  gets a turn") and names the responsibility and freedom it covers.

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"A law is just a big rule."** Who made it is the test, not size.
  A school rule can be strict and still not be a law.
- **"Rules only take things away."** Fair rules also protect
  freedoms — the leash rule protects everyone's freedom to enjoy the
  park.
- **"The Constitution is a law about one thing."** It is the basic
  set of rules for how government works and which freedoms are
  protected; ordinary laws must fit inside it.
