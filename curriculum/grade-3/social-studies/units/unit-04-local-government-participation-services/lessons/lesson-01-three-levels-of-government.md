# Lesson 01 — Three Levels of Government

**Week 15 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 04**

## Student goal

*I can tell local, state, and national government apart, and I can
say one job each level does.*

## Materials

- The levels diagram
  ([`../assets/levels-of-government.svg`](../assets/levels-of-government.svg),
  printed or shown on screen) or the same three boxes drawn on paper.
- Three sorting cards labeled LOCAL, STATE, NATIONAL, and six need
  cards (adult prepares): "pothole on Oak Street," "fix the state
  highway," "keep the country safe," "new books for the town
  library," "driver's license rules," "money used in every state."

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Name three things grown-ups take care of in our town — things no one
person could do alone." (Accept: roads, the library, the park, trash
pickup, the fire station.) "Today we learn that three different
groups share that work — and each group has its own jobs."

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "Cedar Hollow is a made-up town we
> invented for practice. Its mayor and council are pretend. We
> practice on a pretend town so we know what to look for in a real
> town."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **government** is the people and groups that make, carry out, and
enforce rules for a place. In the United States, that work is shared
across **three levels**:

1. **Local government** — the government closest to home: a town,
   city, or county. It handles everyday things you can see: streets,
   parks, libraries, fire protection, trash pickup, and local rules.
   Cedar Hollow's town council is a local government.
2. **State government** — the government of one whole state, led by
   a governor. It handles things for the whole state: state highways,
   state parks, driver's licenses, and state laws. A state law applies
   everywhere in that state.
3. **National government** — also called the **federal**
   government — the government of the whole country, in Washington,
   D.C. It handles things for all 50 states: keeping the country
   safe, printing money, making agreements with other countries, and
   national laws.

A useful test: **ask how big the place is.** One town? Local. One
state? State. The whole country? National.

**Vocabulary in context:** *government* — the groups that make and
carry out rules. *Local* — closest to home. *State* — one of the 50
states. *National (federal)* — the whole country.

The national government itself has **three branches**, and each
branch has one main job:

- **Legislative** (Congress: the House and the Senate) — **makes
  laws**.
- **Executive** (the President) — **carries out laws**.
- **Judicial** (the courts, including the Supreme Court) — **decides
  what laws mean**.

Remember it as: *make, carry out, decide.* Most states copy this same
three-branch shape, with a governor leading the executive branch.

## Worked example 1 — Sorting six needs (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult thinks aloud, placing each need card:

"'Pothole on Oak Street' — Oak Street is one street in one town, so
that is LOCAL. 'Fix the state highway' — a highway runs across a
whole state, so STATE. 'Keep the country safe' — the whole country,
so NATIONAL. 'New books for the town library' — one town's library,
so LOCAL. 'Driver's license rules' — licenses are given by each
state, so STATE. 'Money used in every state' — every state means the
whole country, so NATIONAL. Notice the pattern: I did not ask who is
famous or important. I asked: how big is the place this job
covers?"

## Worked example 2 — "Who fixes the broken swing?" (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"A swing breaks in Cedar Hollow Park. Who fixes it? First ask: where
is the park? In one town. So the job starts LOCAL — the town's parks
crew. Could the state help? Maybe, if the state gave the town money
for parks — but the crew that shows up with tools is local. Could the
national government fix it? No — it does not run town parks. So the
answer is: local government fixes it, and the town council decides
whether the park gets money for the repair. One job can have helpers
at more than one level, but one level is in charge."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the three sorting cards:

1. "A new stop sign on your street." (Local — one street, one town.)
2. "A law about schools that applies in every town in your state."
   (State — it covers the whole state.)
3. "Coins and paper money used everywhere in the country."
   (National — the whole country.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult places "the town library's story hour"
   under NATIONAL and asks, "Is this right? How do you know?" (No —
   a town library serves one town, so it is local, even though people
   all over the country also have libraries.)

## Independent practice (4 min)

Learner completes practice set A, items A1–A4 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)). The adult does not correct mid-task;
errors are discussed in the exit check.

## Application (3 min)

"Your cousin moves to a new town and asks, 'Who do I call about the
trash not being picked up?' What do you tell her, and how do you
know?" (Call the town or city — trash pickup is a local service —
because it is one town's job, not a state or national job.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Name the three levels, closest to home first." (Answer: local,
state, national.) If the learner hesitates, re-teach with the
"how big is the place?" test.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the three level cards visible all lesson; let
  the learner answer by pointing and placing cards instead of
  writing.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads every card aloud;
  "local" is shown by pointing to the learner's own chest ("close to
  you"), "national" by sweeping both arms wide ("the whole
  country").
- **Extension:** the learner names one job for each level that was
  not in the lesson (for example: local — snow plowing; state —
  state police on highways; national — mail delivery across states)
  and defends each placement with the "how big is the place?" test.

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"The President is in charge of everything."** The President
  leads the national government, not towns or states. Towns and
  states have their own leaders and jobs.
- **"Bigger means more important."** Each level matters for its own
  jobs. A pothole fixed locally matters as much to a street as a
  national law matters to the country.
- **"State and national are the same thing."** A state is one of 50
  parts; the national government covers all of them.
