# Lesson 05 — Deciding Together

**Week 17 · S3 (investigation) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 04**

## Student goal

*I can use the deliberation steps — state the problem, listen, share
reasons, decide, check — and I can tell consensus, vote, and
leader-decides apart.*

## Materials

- Deliberation-steps card (adult prepares): 1. State the problem.
  2. Listen to everyone. 3. Share reasons. 4. Decide. 5. Check how it
  worked.
- A real classroom decision to make in this session (adult picks one
  in advance): for example, how to spend 30 minutes of Friday choice
  time, or which of two books to read aloud next. The decision must
  be one the adult can actually carry out.

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Have you ever been in a group that could not decide? What went
wrong?" (Accept: people talked over each other, nobody listened, one
person decided for everyone.) "Today we practice a way of deciding
that gives everyone a fair hearing — and then still reaches a
decision."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

When a group must decide together, it can use **deliberation**: a
careful way of talking before deciding. The steps are:

1. **State the problem** in one sentence. ("We have $10 of pretend
   park money. Should it buy a new bench or new balls?")
2. **Listen to everyone.** Each person speaks once before anyone
   speaks twice. No interrupting.
3. **Share reasons, not just wishes.** "I want the bench" is a wish.
   "I want the bench because grandparents need a place to sit while
   children play" is a reason. Reasons can be answered; wishes
   mostly cannot.
4. **Decide** — by one of three procedures (below).
5. **Check how it worked** after trying it. If the decision fails,
   the group can deliberate again. A decision is not forever.

Three procedures for step 4:

| Procedure | How it works | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Consensus** | Talk until everyone can accept the decision, even if it is not their first choice. | Small groups; decisions everyone must live with. Slow but strong. |
| **Vote** | Everyone chooses; the choice with the most votes wins. | Larger groups; fair when the group cannot agree. Fast, but some people lose. |
| **Leader decides** | One person (a teacher, a mayor, a captain) decides after hearing the group — or, in an emergency, without hearing it. | Emergencies; jobs where one person is responsible. Fast, but the group must trust the leader. |

A **civic virtue** in deliberation is listening fairly: you can
disagree with an idea while still respecting the person who offered
it. Deliberation is also how real councils work: residents speak
(Lesson 02's picture), the council discusses, and then it votes.
That is why the resident with the raised hand matters — speaking up
is part of deciding, not an interruption of it.

**Vocabulary in context:** *deliberation* — deciding together by
talking carefully first. *Consensus* — a decision everyone can
accept. *Vote* — deciding by counting choices. *Reason* — a why
behind a choice. *Civic virtue* — a good habit of community life,
such as listening fairly.

## Worked example 1 — The bench or the balls (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult thinks aloud: "Problem: $10 of pretend park money, bench
or balls? First, listen. Maya says: 'Bench — grandparents need to
sit.' Jon says: 'Balls — ours are flat, and everyone plays with
balls.' Both are reasons, not just wishes. Now weigh them. The bench
helps a few people rest; the balls help many people play, but balls
wear out and will need replacing. Neither reason is wrong. If we vote
and balls win 6–4, the bench people lose this time — but they were
heard, and the group can check later: did the new balls get used?
Could the next $10 buy the bench? A good deliberation does not make
everyone happy. It makes everyone heard, gives reasons, and leaves a
way to try again."

## Worked example 2 — Choosing the procedure (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Three situations. One: a fire alarm rings. Procedure? Leader
decides — the adult says 'walk out now,' and nobody votes, because
safety cannot wait. Two: the class picks a name for its reading
corner. Procedure? Vote or consensus — nobody is in danger, and
everyone will live with the name, so fairness matters more than
speed. Three: the council must set the town budget by Friday, and
residents disagree. Procedure? Deliberation, then a council vote —
listen first, then decide by counting, because the town cannot wait
forever and the decision is written down. The lesson: **match the
procedure to the situation.** Emergencies need speed; shared
decisions need fairness; big public decisions need both listening
and a recorded vote."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together:

1. "The class must choose a Friday game, and two games tie 5–5.
   What is a fair next step?" (Talk once more with reasons, then
   re-vote; or reach consensus on taking turns — one game this
   week, the other next week. Any answer that re-opens reasons
   instead of letting one person overrule counts.)
2. "A rule says: 'No talking during the fire drill.' Is that a
   leader-decides moment?" (Yes — safety, speed, one responsible
   adult.)
3. Error-analysis: the adult says, "Deliberation means everyone must
   agree before we do anything," and asks the learner to correct it.
   (No — that is consensus only. Deliberation means talking
   carefully first; the group may then vote, and the losers were
   still heard.)

## Independent practice (4 min)

In this session, the learner's independent practice **is** the real
deliberation: the class (or the learner and adult) runs the five
steps on the pre-chosen classroom decision, and the learner states
one reason of their own and repeats one reason someone else gave, to
show listening. Written practice on decision procedures follows in
practice set D (Week 18).

## Application (3 min)

"Your friend says, 'Voting is unfair because someone always loses.'
What do you say?" (Sample answer: losing a vote is not the same as
being ignored — in a fair vote everyone was heard, the count is
open, and the group checks the result and can vote again later. Any
answer that separates "losing" from "not being heard" counts.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Name the five deliberation steps in order." (State the problem,
listen, share reasons, decide, check.) If the learner hesitates,
re-teach with the steps card, one finger per step.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the steps card visible; let the learner answer
  by pointing to a step or by speaking instead of writing.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the steps card aloud;
  "reason" is glossed as "your why" and shown with the sentence
  frame "I think ___ because ___."
- **Extension:** the learner re-runs the bench-or-balls decision by
  consensus instead of by vote, and reports whether the outcome
  changed and what the consensus cost (more time, a compromise such
  as cheaper balls plus saving toward a bench).

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Deliberation means everyone agrees."** Consensus is one
  procedure; deliberation is the careful talk that comes before any
  procedure, including a vote.
- **"If I lose, the process was unfair."** Fairness is about being
  heard, an open count, and a chance to check and retry — not about
  always winning.
- **"Leaders should never decide alone."** In emergencies and for
  jobs one person is responsible for, leader-decides is the right
  procedure — provided the leader is accountable afterwards.
