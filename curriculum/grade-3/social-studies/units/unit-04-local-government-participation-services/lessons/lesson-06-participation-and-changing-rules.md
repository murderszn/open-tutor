# Lesson 06 — Participation and Changing Rules

**Week 18 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 04**

## Student goal

*I can name four ways people take part in a democracy, and I can
explain the steps for changing a rule or making a new policy.*

## Materials

- Participation cards (adult prepares): VOTE, SPEAK UP, HELP, SERVE,
  plus one blank card for the learner's own idea.
- The "how a policy is made" strip (adult prepares): 1. Notice a
  problem. 2. Talk and listen. 3. Propose a policy. 4. Decide (vote).
  5. Try it. 6. Check whether it worked.

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Last week your class decided something together. Who, outside our
classroom, gets to decide things for a whole town?" (The council,
after listening to residents.) "Today we learn all the ways ordinary
people — including children, in age-fitting ways — take part."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A democracy relies on people's **responsible participation**: it
only works if people take part, not just watch. "Responsible" matters
— participation means listening, telling the truth about what you
need, and accepting a fair decision even when you lose the vote.
Four forms of participation, from simplest to most involved:

1. **Vote** — when old enough (18 in the United States), choose the
   people who will make decisions. Children cannot vote yet, but
   they can practice voting in class, and they can learn how voting
   works before they are old enough to do it.
2. **Speak up** — tell leaders what you think: speak at a meeting
   (with an adult), write a letter (with an adult's help), or join a
   petition. In Cedar Hollow's picture (Lesson 02), the resident
   with the raised hand is speaking up.
3. **Help** — volunteer: pick up litter in the park, read to younger
   children at the library, help a neighbor. Helping fixes small
   problems without any law at all.
4. **Serve** — take a job in community life: run for student
   council, join a library board when old enough, become a
   firefighter, a librarian, a council member. Serving means
   accepting responsibility for other people.

How do rules and laws **change**? A rule changes society when it
changes what people do every day: a new leash rule changes how dogs
are walked; a new library-hours rule changes when families can read.
And people change rules and laws through a **policy process**:

1. **Notice a problem.** ("Books are left on the floor and get
   ruined.")
2. **Talk and listen.** Who is affected? What do they need?
3. **Propose a policy** — a plan or rule to handle the problem.
   ("Each class gets a return box, emptied every Friday.")
4. **Decide** — deliberate, then vote (class vote, or council vote
   for a town).
5. **Try it** for a set time.
6. **Check whether it worked.** Count the ruined books. If the number
   fell, keep the policy. If not, deliberate again.

Notice what this process protects: nobody changes a rule alone in
secret. A problem is named in public, reasons are heard, a decision
is recorded, and the result is checked. That is the difference
between a rule that serves a community and a rule that serves one
person.

**Vocabulary in context:** *participation* — taking part in
community life. *Petition* — a written request signed by many people.
*Volunteer* — someone who helps without being paid. *Policy* — a plan
or rule a group adopts to handle a problem.

## Worked example 1 — Matching participation to a problem (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult thinks aloud: "Problem: Cedar Hollow Park has litter after
weekends. Which form of participation fits? Voting? There is no
election this weekend, and voting picks people, not litter. Speaking
up? Yes — a resident can tell the council the park needs more trash
cans. Helping? Yes — volunteers can pick up litter on Saturday, and
that helps right away. Serving? Yes, later — someone could join the
parks board. So one problem can take several forms at once: help now,
speak up for more cans, serve later. The mistake would be to pick
only one form and call the others useless. Participation is a set of
tools, and a good citizen picks the tool that fits the job."

## Worked example 2 — Changing a rule, step by step (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Cedar Hollow's library rule used to be: 'No talking at all.' Then
children stopped coming for homework help, because homework help
needs quiet talking. Follow the policy steps. 1. Notice: fewer
children visit after school. 2. Talk and listen: the librarian asks
children and parents; they say they need quiet talk, not silence.
3. Propose: 'Quiet voices for homework help, silent reading in the
back room.' 4. Decide: the library board votes yes. 5. Try it for a
month. 6. Check: visits rise, and complaints about noise do not.
The rule changed — and because the steps were followed, the new rule
is fairer than the old one and everyone knows why it changed. If
visits had not risen, the board would deliberate again. A changed
rule is not a broken promise; it is the system working."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the participation cards:

1. "You are 9 years old and you want a safer crosswalk near school.
   Which forms can you use now?" (Speak up — with an adult; help —
   for example, join a supervised walk-to-school group. Not voting
   yet, and not serving on the council yet. The adult names this
   honestly: children participate differently from adults, and both
   count.)
2. "A town wants a new park rule. What is step 1?" (Notice a
   problem — name it in one sentence.)
3. "The new rule was tried for a month and the problem got worse.
   What happens next?" (Check, then deliberate again — change or
   drop the policy.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "Once a rule is made, it can never
   change," and asks the learner to correct it. (Rules and laws
   change all the time, through the policy steps — that is how a
   community fixes mistakes.)

## Independent practice (4 min)

Learner completes practice set D, items D1–D4 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)). The adult does not correct mid-task;
errors are discussed in the exit check.

## Application (3 min)

"Name one problem in our classroom or school, and tell me which
participation form you would use first — and why." (Any problem with
a matching form and a reason counts; the key gives a sample: messy
cubbies → help first, by tidying and labeling, because the problem
is small and fixable without a new rule.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Name two ways to participate that a 9-year-old can use right now."
(Sample: speak up with an adult's help; help by volunteering. Also
accepted: practice voting in class; serve on student council.) If
the learner names only voting, re-teach that children participate
through speaking up, helping, and serving in school roles — voting
in elections waits until 18.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the participation cards and the policy strip
  visible; let the learner answer by pointing to a card or step
  instead of writing.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads every card aloud;
  "petition" is glossed as "a letter many people sign together."
- **Extension:** the learner writes (or dictates) a two-sentence
  "speak up" note for a real classroom problem — problem in sentence
  one, a fair request in sentence two — addressed to the class, not
  to a real official. The adult keeps it in the classroom; nothing is
  sent to a real official from this lesson.

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Children cannot participate."** Children speak up (with adult
  help), help, serve in school roles, and practice voting. Only
  election voting waits until 18.
- **"Speaking up means complaining."** Speaking up means naming a
  problem and proposing something fair — with reasons, as in Lesson
  05.
- **"A rule that changes was a failure."** Changing a rule after
  checking the result is the system working, not failing — provided
  the change follows the steps in public.
