# Lesson 03 — Three Kinds of Resources

**Week 9 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 02**

## Student goal

*I can name the three kinds of resources — natural, human, and physical
capital — and find each kind in a production chain.*

## Materials

- Three labeled boxes or columns: **FROM NATURE** · **PEOPLE'S WORK & SKILLS**
  · **TOOLS & BUILDINGS**.
- Paper, pencil.

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Think of a loaf of bread at the store. What had to exist *before* that loaf
— name as many things as you can." (Wheat, a farm, a farmer, an oven, a
baker, a bag, a truck…) "Today we sort all those ingredients into three
kinds of resources."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **resource** is anything people use to make what they need. Geographers
and economists sort resources into three kinds:

1. **Natural resources** — things from nature that people use: water, soil,
   trees (timber), stone, fish, sunlight, wild animals. Nobody *made* them;
   nature provided them.

2. **Human resources** (also called *human capital*) — the **work and
   skills** people bring: the farmer who knows when to plant, the baker who
   knows how to knead, the builder who knows how to frame a house.

3. **Physical capital** — the **tools, machines, and buildings** people use
   to make things: an oven, a tractor, a fishing boat, a bridge, a school
   building. People *made* these, and then they use them to make *other*
   things.

A **production chain** shows how the three kinds work together to make one
product. Follow a loaf of bread:

- **Natural:** wheat seeds, soil, rain, sunlight → wheat grows.
- **Human:** the farmer's skill in planting and harvesting; the miller's
  skill in grinding; the baker's skill in baking.
- **Physical capital:** the tractor, the mill, the oven, the delivery truck.

Take away *any* kind and the chain breaks: no wheat (natural) → nothing to
grind; no baker (human) → flour never becomes bread; no oven (physical
capital) → dough never bakes.

Now connect to settlements (Lesson 01): **settlements grow where the three
kinds meet.** The valley town has natural resources (river, forest, stone,
soil), human resources (farmers, builders, boat-fishers), and physical
capital (the bridge, the water wheel, the boats, the fenced field). A town
with only one kind struggles — imagine a forest with no people, or skilled
people with no tools.

**Vocabulary in context:** *natural resource* — from nature. *Human
resource* — people's work and skills. *Physical capital* — tools, machines,
buildings used to make things. *Production chain* — the steps and resources
that make one product.

## Worked example 1 — The bread chain (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

Adult thinks aloud, placing word cards into the three boxes:

"Wheat — from nature: **natural**. Soil and rain — **natural**. The farmer's
planting skill — that's a person's skill: **human**. The tractor — a machine
people built to farm: **physical capital**. The miller's grinding skill —
**human**. The mill building — **physical capital**. The baker — **human**.
The oven — **physical capital**. The delivery truck — **physical capital**.
The driver's knowledge of the route — **human**."

"Notice the pattern: if it grew or flowed in nature → natural. If it is what
a person *knows or does* → human. If people *built it to make other things*
→ physical capital."

Tricky-note (say it out loud): "Is a *farmed field* natural or physical
capital? The soil is natural; the fence and the plowed rows are things
people built — so the improved field is a mix. Real chains are mixed like
that."

## Worked example 2 — A wooden chair (5 min)

"A carpenter makes a wooden chair. Sort with me: the tree → **natural**.
The carpenter's measuring and sawing skill → **human**. The saw and the
workbench → **physical capital**. The chair itself — is it a resource? It is
a *product*; but if the carpenter sells chairs to buy food, the chairs
become a resource for the family. Resources and products trade places
depending on the story."

Then the learner's turn: "A fisher catches fish from a boat and sells them
at the town dock. Name one natural, one human, and one physical-capital
resource." *(Natural: fish/water. Human: the fisher's skill/knowledge of
good spots. Physical capital: the boat, the dock, nets.)*

## Guided practice (with prompts/hints, 6 min)

**G1.** Sort into the three boxes: *sunlight · teacher · school building ·
river water · bridge · nurse's medical skill · timber · hammer · truck
driver*. *(Natural: sunlight, river water, timber. Human: teacher, nurse's
skill, truck driver. Physical capital: school building, bridge, hammer.)*

**G2.** Error hunt: "A classmate sorted the *oven* as a natural resource
'because fire is natural.' Fix the thinking." *(The oven was built by people
to bake things — physical capital. Fire occurring in nature doesn't make a
built oven natural.)*

**G3.** Finish the chain: "A town builds houses from its forest." List one
natural, one human, and one physical-capital resource in that chain.
*(Natural: timber/trees. Human: builders' skills. Physical capital: saws,
hammers, / the houses themselves as built shelter.)*

## Independent practice (6 min)

**I1.** Pick a product in our home (a shirt, a pencil, a jar of jam). Write
or dictate its three-box chain: one natural, one human, one physical-capital
resource. *(Adult checks the sort logic, not the specific product facts.)*

**I2.** "Our valley town (Lesson 01 picture) wants to build *one more
bridge*. Name one resource of each kind the builders will need." *(Natural:
timber/stone. Human: builders' skills. Physical capital: tools, ropes,
scaffolding.)*

## Application — Why towns need all three (4 min)

"Imagine a valley with a river and forest but *no people*. Is it a
settlement? Now imagine skilled builders with tools but *no trees, no stone,
no river*. Can they build a town? So which kind matters most?" *(All three —
the point is that settlements need the meeting of all three, which is why
towns cluster where nature provides and people bring skills and tools.)*

## Exit check

"Give me one example of each kind of resource — and tell me which kind a
*baker's skill* is." (Success: three correct examples; baker's skill =
human.)

## Supports

- **Reading/language access:** adult reads aloud; the three boxes stay
  posted all week with one picture cue each (tree / person / hammer).
- **Alternate response mode:** word-card sorting by pointing/placing;
  dictated chains.
- **Extension:** "Trace the classroom's pencils: wood (natural, from trees),
  the factory workers' skills (human), the saws and paint machines (physical
  capital). Where might each step happen — and why would a pencil factory
  settle near a forest?" (Links to Unit 05 trade.)

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Money is a resource kind."** Money *buys* resources; it is not one of
  the three kinds. (Unit 05 will give money its proper job.)
- **"Natural = untouched."** A farmed field mixes natural soil with built
  improvements — the three kinds blend in real life.
- **"Human resources means humans as objects."** It means people's *work
  and skills*, treated with respect — the unit names workers' knowledge as
  valuable.
