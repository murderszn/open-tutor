# Lesson 01 — Where People Settle

**Week 7 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 02**

## Student goal

*I can explain why people build settlements near fresh water, flat land,
and natural resources — using evidence I can point to.*

## Materials

- [`../assets/river-valley-settlement.png`](../assets/river-valley-settlement.png)
  (AI-generated teaching illustration — a river valley with a small town;
  the lesson reads its AI-illustration label aloud).
- Paper and pencil (the adult may scribe).
- A familiar map or photograph of the learner's own community (optional, for
  the application).

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Name three things a person needs every day to live." (Accept: water, food,
shelter/a home, warmth — anything pointing at daily needs.) "Today we ask:
if *you* got to choose where to build a brand-new town, where would you put
it — and what would you need to be close to?"

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **settlement** is a place where people live and build homes together — a
village, a town, or a city. Settlements are not scattered randomly across
the map. For thousands of years, people have built new settlements near the
same few things, because people have the same few needs everywhere.

**Need 1: Fresh water.** People need water to drink, cook, wash, and water
crops. Long ago, before pipes and trucks, water had to be *close* — so towns
grew along rivers and lakes. Water also gives food (fish) and a road for
boats.

**Need 2: Flat land.** Houses are hard to build on steep, rocky slopes, and
farming needs level soil. Flat land near a river is also usually the best
soil, because rivers leave behind rich dirt when they flood gently.

**Need 3: Natural resources.** A **natural resource** is something from
nature that people use — trees for wood, stone for building, good soil for
farming, animals for food. A settlement near a forest has building wood and
firewood; a settlement near stone has building material.

So a geographer looking at a map asks: *Where is the water? Where is the
flat land? What resources are near?* The answers usually predict where the
towns are.

**Vocabulary in context:** *environment* — everything around a place (water,
land, air, weather, plants, animals). *Settlement* — where people live and
build together.

## Worked example 1 — Reading the valley (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult shows the illustration and thinks aloud, pointing:

"I am looking for the three things. First, water: I see a wide blue river
curving through the middle, and there are two small wooden boats on it — so
people can drink, fish, and travel by boat. Second, flat land: I see the
cluster of six houses with red roofs sitting on flat green grass right by
the river, and next to them a fenced field with neat rows of crops — flat
soil good for building and farming. Third, resources: on the right hill
there is a tall dark-green forest — that is timber for building and
firewood; in the distance there are gray rocky mountains — that is stone;
and the river itself gives fish. So this is a *good settlement spot*: the
town has water, flat land, timber, stone, and farm soil all close by."

The adult then asks: "What is the bridge for?" (It lets people cross the
river — the river is useful, but it also divides the land, so people built a
way across.) "What is the water wheel for?" (It uses the river's moving
water to do work — people put their town where the environment can *work*
for them.)

## Worked example 2 — A bad spot vs. a good spot (5 min)

"Now imagine two empty places. **Place A:** a flat meadow next to a slow
river, with a forest on one side. **Place B:** the rocky top of a steep
mountain, far from any river. Where do we build our town?"

Think-aloud: "Place A has all three things: water (river), flat land
(meadow), resources (forest). Place B has none — carrying water uphill every
day would be miserable, and you cannot farm a rocky peak. We choose Place A.
Notice I did not say Place B is *bad land* — it is just a bad spot *for a
town*. Mountains are fine for other things, like looking at."

Then the turn: "Could *anyone* ever settle at Place B? What if they found
something valuable there?" (Accept: a mine — people do settle near valuable
resources even in hard places. This foreshadows Lesson 03.)

## Guided practice (with prompts/hints, 5 min)

**G1.** Point to each of these in the illustration and say what need it
meets: the river · the fenced field · the forest · the mountains.
*(River: water/drink/fish/travel. Field: flat farm soil/food. Forest:
timber. Mountains: stone.)*

**G2.** "The town in the picture has six houses. Why didn't the people build
their houses *inside* the forest instead?" *(Trees are a resource to use,
not a place to clear away; also: building among tall trees blocks light and
is harder. Accept any reason tied to flat open land near water.)*

**G3.** Error hunt: "A classmate says, 'Towns grow wherever people feel like
building — the environment doesn't matter.' Give two pieces of evidence from
the illustration that prove the environment mattered here."
*(The houses sit on the flat land by the river, not on the mountains; the
field is on flat soil by the water; the bridge and water wheel show people
using the river.)*

## Independent practice (5 min)

**I1.** Draw a quick sketch of YOUR ideal settlement spot: include water,
flat land, and one natural resource. Label the three with arrows. *(Adult
checks the three labels are present and sensible.)*

**I2.** Write or dictate two sentences: "I put my town here because…"
*(Must name at least two environmental reasons.)*

## Application — Our own community (5 min)

With the adult: "Where is our community's water? (river, lake, reservoir,
wells, city pipes) Is the land flat or hilly here? What natural resource is
nearby — farm soil, forest, stone, something else?" The adult records the
learner's answers; this feeds the Week 7 S3 local inquiry.

## Exit check

"Name the three things people look for when they choose a settlement spot,
and point to one of them in the illustration." (Water, flat land, natural
resources. Success: all three named; at least one located in the picture.)

## Supports

- **Alternate response mode:** pointing, drawing, or dictated sentences count
  fully — the assessed skill is the reasoning, not handwriting.

## Extension

Compare with Unit 01's desert/rainforest illustration: "Would a town grow
easily in the desert half of that picture? What is missing?" (Water and farm
soil — then note that desert towns *do* exist near oases and rivers like the
Nile, so 'hard' does not mean 'impossible.')

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"People can live anywhere now, so environment doesn't matter."** True
  that pipes and trucks loosen the link — but water, food, and materials
  still come *from* environments, and most big cities still sit on rivers,
  coasts, or lakes. The link changed shape; it did not vanish.
- **"Flat land is always best."** Flat floodplains flood. Good settlement
  thinking weighs trade-offs — Lesson 02 builds on this.
