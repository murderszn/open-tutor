# Lesson 02 — Map Scale: The Same Place, Three Sizes

**Time:** three sessions, 30–35 minutes each · **Materials:** printed maps of
the *same familiar place* at three scales (adult prepares: e.g., a street map
of the neighborhood, a state map, a country map — or draws them), a ruler,
the unit vocabulary word cards (map scale, scale bar)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how maps show big real
places in small drawings — and how to use a map's scale to estimate real
distances. By the end, you will draw your own scaled map.

**Readiness check:** Adult holds up two drawings of the same room, one big
and one tiny. "Which drawing is easier to carry? Which one can show the
whole house?" Accept: the small one is easier to carry; a big-place map must
be small to fit the paper. If stuck: "A map of the whole country cannot be
country-sized. It must shrink."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

**Map scale** is what tells us a map is much smaller than the real place.
Look for a **scale bar** — the little ruler-like drawing on a map that
shows distance. A scale bar might say "1 centimeter = 10 kilometers." That
means: every centimeter you measure on the map stands for 10 kilometers in
the real world.

Now the key idea: the **same place looks different at different scales.**
At a big scale (zoomed in), a neighborhood map shows streets, houses, and
the park. At a small scale (zoomed out), a country map shows whole states,
but the streets disappear — there is no room. Nothing is wrong with either
map; each one is right for its job. **Large-scale maps show small areas with
lots of detail. Small-scale maps show large areas with less detail.**

**Worked example 1 — reading a scale bar:**

> Adult measures on the neighborhood map: "The scale bar says 1 centimeter
> = 1 block. From my house to the park measures 3 centimeters on the map.
> 3 × 1 block = 3 blocks. The park is about 3 blocks from my house in real
> life. Steps: (1) read the scale bar, (2) measure on the map, (3)
> multiply."

**Worked example 2 — same place, three scales:**

> Adult lays out the three maps. "Here is our neighborhood map: I can see
> Maple Street and the library. Here is the state map: our town is a tiny
> dot — I can see the capital city and the big lake instead. Here is the
> country map: our state is one shape among fifty. As the scale gets
> smaller, we see MORE land but FEWER details. The library vanishes by the
> state map — not because it moved, but because the map has no room for
> it."

Key idea to say plainly: **Scale tells you how much smaller the map is than
the real place. Large-scale maps show a small area with big detail;
small-scale maps show a big area with little detail. A scale bar lets you
turn map measurements into real distances.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. Point to the scale bar. "What does it say? What does that mean in your
   own words?" *(learner reads it, e.g., "1 cm = 5 km — each centimeter
   stands for 5 kilometers")*
2. "Measure from the school to the park on the neighborhood map. How many
   centimeters? So how far in real life?" *(measure, then multiply by the
   scale — adult checks the arithmetic)*
3. "Which map shows our street — the neighborhood, state, or country map?
   Why does the country map leave it out?" *(neighborhood; no room at that
   scale)*
4. "I want to plan a trip between two cities in our state. Which scale is
   best — the neighborhood map or the state map? Why?" *(state map — it
   shows both cities and the roads between them)*
5. Error-analysis: "A friend says, 'This country map is wrong — it doesn't
   show my house!' What would you tell them?" *(maps at small scale cannot
   show every small thing; the house is too small to fit at that scale)*

## Independent practice

- The learner picks two places on the state map, measures the map distance
  in centimeters, and estimates the real distance with the scale bar.
  (Adult supplies the map's scale and checks the multiplication.)
- Session 3: the learner **constructs a scaled map** of a familiar place
  (bedroom, classroom, or block): title, key, compass rose, labels, AND a
  scale bar (e.g., "1 finger-width = 1 step," then a drawn bar). Success:
  all five map parts present, scale bar readable, roughly proportional
  drawing.

## Application

"Your cousin lives far away and wants to visit the park near us. Which of
our three maps would you send first, and why? What is one thing the map
tells them, and one thing it cannot tell them?" (Answer: the neighborhood
map; it shows the route and nearby landmarks, but not the drive between
cities.)

## Exit check

Prompt: "A map's scale bar says 1 cm = 2 km. Two towns are 4 cm apart on the
map. How far apart are they really? And which shows more detail — a map of
our block or a map of our whole country?" Accept: 8 km; the block map
(large scale, small area, big detail).

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Use nonstandard units the learner can feel ("1 hand = 1
  block") before centimeters; adult does the multiplying while the learner
  measures and decides.
- **Reading/language access:** "Scale" is a hard word — anchor it with a
  gesture: arms wide = real place, hands close = the map. Word card shows a
  tiny map next to a big landscape.
- **Alternate response mode:** Point-and-measure; dictate the estimate; draw
  the final map instead of writing sentences about it.
- **Extension:** The learner explains why a *globe* also has a scale
  problem: "How many miles does one inch on this globe stand for?" (Adult
  looks up the globe's scale together with the learner.)

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** "bigger map = bigger place" — learners often think
a physically larger piece of paper shows a larger area. Re-anchor with the
three-map laydown: the *smallest* paper can show the *largest* area if its
scale is small. A second slip: confusing "large-scale" and "small-scale"
vocabulary — repeat the mantra "large scale = small area, big detail" and
let the learner chant it. Keep multiplication at the ×1, ×2, ×5, ×10 level;
the assessed skill is reading the scale and knowing to multiply, not
multi-digit arithmetic.
