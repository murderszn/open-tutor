# Lesson 01 — The Seven Continents and Five Oceans

**Time:** two sessions, 30–35 minutes each · **Materials:** a globe (or a ball
you draw on), a printed blank world outline map (the adult provides), crayons,
the unit vocabulary word cards (continent, ocean, equator)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will name Earth's seven continents
and five oceans, find them on a map, and compare a globe with a flat map to
see what each one shows well.

**Readiness check:** Adult points at a globe or world map: "What is this big
picture called? What do you think the blue parts are?" Accept "a map of the
world" and "water." If the learner is stuck: "This is a map of our whole
Earth. The blue parts are water."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Earth has two big kinds of surface: **land** and **water**. A **continent**
is one of Earth's seven very large pieces of land. An **ocean** is one of
Earth's five very large areas of water.

Name the seven continents with the learner, touching each on the globe:
**Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia, Europe, North America, South America.**
A memory trick the class can chant: "Africa, Antarctica, Asia, Australia,
Europe, North America, South America." Point out that we live on North
America.

Name the five oceans: **Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic, Southern.** The
Pacific is the biggest ocean — so big it covers more of Earth's surface than
all the land put together. The Southern Ocean is the ocean around Antarctica.

Now the imaginary line: the **equator** is the imaginary line around Earth's
middle, halfway between the North Pole and the South Pole. Places near the
equator get strong, direct sun all year, so they are usually warm. (This idea
returns in Lesson 03.)

**Worked example 1 — finding a continent by its neighbors:**

> Adult: "I want to find Africa. I know Antarctica is at the bottom of the
> map, around the South Pole. Above Antarctica is the Atlantic Ocean. East of
> the Atlantic Ocean, across from South America, is a big continent — that is
> Africa. I used the ocean and the South Pole as landmarks: pole, then
> ocean, then continent."

**Worked example 2 — globe vs. flat map (the investigation setup):**

> Adult holds the globe and a flat map side by side. "The **globe** is round
> like Earth, so shapes and sizes are more true. But I cannot see the whole
> Earth at once — the other side is hidden. The **flat map** shows the whole
> Earth at once, but something must stretch or squish to flatten a ball. So
> the flat map's continent shapes are a little off. Each one is useful:
> the globe for true shape, the flat map for seeing everything."

Key idea to say plainly: **A continent is one of Earth's seven very large
pieces of land; an ocean is one of Earth's five very large areas of water.
A globe shows Earth's true round shape, and a flat map shows the whole Earth
at once — but flattening a ball always stretches something.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Touch North America on the globe. Now touch the ocean east of it —
   what is its name?" *(Atlantic)*
2. "Which continent is at the very bottom of the map?" *(Antarctica)* "Which
   ocean surrounds it?" *(Southern)*
3. "Point to the equator. Is the equator a real line painted on Earth?"
   *(No — imaginary.)* "What does it tell us?" *(the middle of Earth;
   places near it get strong, direct sun)*
4. Hold up the globe: "Where is Asia? How do you know?" *(the learner names
   landmarks used — Europe to the west, Pacific to the east)*
5. "Look at the flat map, then the globe. Which one lets you see all seven
   continents at once?" *(the flat map)* "Which shows Earth's true shape?"
   *(the globe)*

## Independent practice

On the blank outline map, the learner labels the seven continents (adult
spells names on request; invented spelling accepted at this stage, corrected
in review) and colors the five oceans one shared blue. Success: all 12 names
in approximately the right places.

## Application

The learner gives the adult a "tour": "Start at our continent and travel to
three other continents, naming each ocean you cross." (e.g., "From North
America, cross the Atlantic to Africa, cross the Indian Ocean area to Asia,
cross the Pacific back toward home.")

## Exit check

Prompt: "Name the seven continents. Why can't a flat map show Earth exactly
like it really is?" Accept: the seven names in any order; because Earth is
round and flattening a ball stretches or squishes something.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Pre-label three continents; the learner adds the rest. Let
  the learner point-and-name instead of writing.
- **Reading/language access:** Each word card shows the word plus a tiny
  sketch (continent = land blob, ocean = wave). Welcome home-language names
  beside English ones.
- **Alternate response mode:** The learner may answer every check by pointing
  on the globe and chanting, or record an audio naming tour.
- **Extension:** The learner finds one country on each of two continents and
  writes (or dictates) one sentence about where it sits ("Egypt is in the
  northeast of Africa, by the Mediterranean Sea").

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners often treat the equator as a real painted
line, or think "Australia" and "Oceania" are two different continents. Re-
anchor: the equator is imaginary — it is a tool humans drew; and the unit
teaches **Australia** as the continent (Britannica Kids), while some videos
say "Oceania" for the same region — name the difference out loud. A second
common slip: thinking the Southern Ocean is "the ocean at the bottom of the
map" rather than the ocean around Antarctica — the name and the location
together.
