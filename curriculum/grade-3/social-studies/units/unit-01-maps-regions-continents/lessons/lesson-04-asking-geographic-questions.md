# Lesson 04 — Asking Geographic Questions

**Time:** one session, 30–35 minutes · **Materials:** the learner's evidence
chart from Lesson 03, a world map, the unit vocabulary word cards
(compelling question, supporting question)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will ask a big geographic question
and the smaller questions that help answer it — the same skill geographers
use to start every investigation.

**Readiness check:** Adult: "What is something about the Sahara or the
Amazon you still wonder about?" Accept any genuine wonder ("Do people live
in the Sahara?" "How do macaws find food?"). If stuck: "I wonder how
people get water in a desert. What do *you* wonder?"

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **compelling question** is a big, interesting question that needs evidence
to answer — it cannot be answered with one word, and smart people might
disagree about the answer. A **supporting question** is a smaller question
that helps answer the big one.

Example: *"Why do people live in the Sahara if it is so dry?"* is compelling
— it needs evidence, and the answer has layers (oases, trade routes, adapted
animals, modern towns). Supporting questions help: "Where do Saharan towns
get water?" "What work do people do there?" "How do they travel?" Each
supporting question is one step toward the big answer.

Not every question is compelling. "Is the Sahara a desert?" is a yes/no
check — useful, but not compelling. "How many countries does the Sahara
touch?" is a fact lookup. Compelling questions start with **why** or **how**
and ask about reasons, choices, and connections.

**Worked example 1 — building up:**

> Adult thinks aloud: "My wonder: do children in the Amazon go to school by
> boat? That is a good wonder, but it is small. Let me grow it: *How does
> living in a rainforest change daily life for the people there?* That is
> compelling — it needs evidence about homes, food, travel, and school.
> Supporting questions: 'How do people travel when the forest floods?'
> 'What do families eat from the forest?' 'Where do children go to school?'
> Each one feeds the big question."

**Worked example 2 — fixing a weak question:**

> Adult: "Here is a weak one: 'What color is a camel?' It has one answer
> and teaches us nothing about geography. Fix it: *'How are camels built for
> desert life?'* Now it asks about connection — body features to a dry
> environment. That is geographic thinking."

Key idea to say plainly: **A compelling question is big and needs evidence;
supporting questions are the smaller steps that help answer it. Geographers
start every investigation with questions, not answers.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner decides)

1. "Which of these is compelling? (a) 'Is the Amazon wet?' (b) 'Why is the
   Amazon wetter than the Sahara?'" *(b — needs evidence and explanation)*
2. "Turn this wonder into a compelling question: 'I wonder what people eat
   in the desert.'" *("How do people in the Sahara get food in such a dry
   place?")*
3. "Give me one supporting question for 'How does the rainforest shape daily
   life?'" *(accept any on-task smaller question: travel, food, homes,
   school, work)*
4. Error-analysis: "A friend's 'compelling question' is 'What is the
   biggest desert?' How would you improve it?" *(it is a fact lookup;
   improve to "Why did the Sahara become a desert while the Amazon stayed
   wet?" — asks for reasons)*
5. "Why do geographers ask questions *before* they look at maps and data?"
   *(questions tell you what evidence to look for)*

## Independent practice

The learner writes (or dictates) **one compelling question** about either
region and **two supporting questions** beneath it. Success: the compelling
question starts with why/how, needs evidence, and is about the region; the
supporting questions each connect to it. The learner also names **one kind
of source** that could help (map, photograph, video, book, a person who
knows).

## Application

"Your compelling question is the engine of your Region Explorer project
(see [project.md](../project.md)). Read your questions aloud. Does your
compelling question need evidence from more than one source? If not, grow
it one step."

## Exit check

Prompt: "What makes a question 'compelling' instead of just a fact check?
Give one supporting question for: 'Why do deserts and rainforests have such
different animals?'" Accept: it needs evidence and has no one-word answer;
supporting question on-task (e.g., "What do desert animals drink?").

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Offer two pre-written questions; the learner picks the more
  compelling one and says why, then adds one supporting question with a
  sentence starter ("One smaller question is…").
- **Reading/language access:** Anchor "compelling" as "a question that
  *compels* you — it pulls you in." Word card shows a big magnifying glass
  over a small one.
- **Alternate response mode:** Oral questions recorded by the adult; the
  learner draws the "big question" as a large circle and supporting
  questions as smaller circles inside it.
- **Extension:** The learner predicts what *two different people* (a desert
  trader, a rainforest scientist) might answer to their compelling question
  — noticing that questions can be open to interpretation.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** every question feels "compelling" to the asker —
learners defend fact lookups as big questions. Re-anchor with the test:
"Can it be answered in one word or one number? Then it is a check, not a
compelling question." Second slip: supporting questions that wander off
topic — check each one against the big question: "Does answering this help
answer the big one?" This lesson feeds the project directly; keep the
learner's final questions on file.
