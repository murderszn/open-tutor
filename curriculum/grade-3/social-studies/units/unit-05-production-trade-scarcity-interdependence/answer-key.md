## Practice set A — Scarcity, choice, incentives

- **A1.** (a) Book benefit: a story June can read again and again
  (or: a story she has waited for). Book cost: all $5.00 is gone;
  she has no money left. (b) Jump-rope benefit: exercise and play
  with friends, plus $2.00 kept for later. Jump-rope cost: no new
  book. (c) Opportunity cost: the storybook — the best thing she
  gave up.
- **A2.** (a) Scarcity: 10 customers want loaves but only 6 loaves
  exist — not enough for all wants. (b) Four customers must go
  without (or wait), or Ana must bake more tomorrow; Ana may also
  raise the price or decide who gets a loaf first. Accept any
  answer that names someone going without or waiting.
- **A3.** (a) Positive incentive — nudges learners to read more
  (read five books to earn the sticker). (b) Negative incentive —
  nudges learners to return books on time (avoid the fine). (c)
  Positive incentive — nudges people to bring cups back (bring
  five cups, get a free one).
- **A4.** (a) 2 × $5.00 = $10.00. (b) Choice B keeps $4.00.
  (c) Opportunity cost: the 2 soccer balls.
- **A5.** Sam is wrong. Picking the book means giving up the jump
  rope (and the $2.00 kept). Having the book does not mean nothing
  was given up — every choice has an opportunity cost, and here it
  is the jump rope.
- **A6.** Open. Sample: positive — "My teacher gives extra recess
  to the table that cleans up first; it nudges us to clean up."
  Negative — "If I leave my bike in the rain it rusts; that nudges
  me to put it away." Accept any real pair where the incentive and
  the nudged choice match.

## Practice set B — Resources, specialization, trade, money

- **B1.** Natural resources: wheat, wool, water. Human resources:
  baker, weaver. Physical resources: clay oven, loom, flour mill.
  Sample reasons: wheat is natural because it grows from soil, sun,
  and rain; the loom is physical because it is a tool the weaver
  uses to make cloth.
- **B2.** (a) Bakery stall: bread. One resource: wheat (natural),
  the clay oven (physical), or the baker (human). (b) Farm stand:
  apples (and carrots, cabbage, peas). One resource: soil/sun/rain
  (natural), the farmer (human), or baskets/tools (physical). (c)
  Weaving stall: cloth. One resource: wool (natural), the weaver
  (human), or the loom (physical).
- **B3.** (a) 3 × 25¢ = 75¢. (b) $1.00 − $0.75 = $0.25 change.
- **B4.** (a) $5.00 − $2.00 = $3.00 change. (b) Check:
  $2.00 + $3.00 = $5.00. Correct.
- **B5.** (a) The barter problem: the basket-maker wants apples,
  not bread, and Ana has bread — so Ana must first trade bread to
  Ben for apples, then trade the apples to the basket-maker for
  the basket. Two trades, and she must find someone who wants what
  she has. (b) Money fixes it: Ana sells her bread for money, then
  pays money for the basket — one sale and one purchase, with no
  matching of wants needed.
- **B6.** (a) 2 × $5.00 = $10.00 taken in. (b) 8 × 25¢ = 200¢ =
  $2.00 for the apples. (c) $10.00 − $2.00 = $8.00 left.

## Practice set C — Profit

- **C1.** (a) Money in: 10 × $1.00 = $10.00. (b) Total cost:
  10 × 40¢ = 400¢ = $4.00. (c) Profit: $10.00 − $4.00 = $6.00.
- **C2.** (a) Money in: 20 × $1.00 = $20.00. (b) Total cost:
  20 × 40¢ = 800¢ = $8.00. (c) Profit: $20.00 − $8.00 = $12.00.
- **C3.** (a) Money in: 8 × $1.50 = $12.00. (b) Total cost:
  8 × 40¢ = 320¢ = $3.20. (c) Profit: $12.00 − $3.20 = $8.80.
  (d) No — $8.80 is less than the hot day's $12.00. The higher
  price earned more per cup ($1.10) but sold far fewer cups, so
  the day's profit fell.
- **C4.** (a) New cost per cup: 25¢ + 20¢ + 5¢ = 50¢. (b) New
  profit per cup: $1.00 − $0.50 = $0.50. (c) Profit for 10 cups:
  10 × $0.50 = $5.00 (down from $6.00).
- **C5.** Sam is wrong. $20.00 is the *revenue* — the money taken
  in. Profit is revenue minus cost: $20.00 − $8.00 = $12.00.
- **C6.** 16-cup plan: money in 16 × $0.75 = $12.00; total cost
  16 × 40¢ = 640¢ = $6.40; profit $12.00 − $6.40 = $5.60. The
  10-cup plan at $1.00 earns more: $6.00 > $5.60.

## Practice set D — Outside effects and interdependence

- **D1.** (a) External benefit. (b) Buyer/seller (the deal itself).
  (c) External benefit. (d) External cost. (e) External cost.
  (f) Buyer/seller (the deal itself). Sample reasons: (a) children
  did not buy or sell the park but are helped by it. (d) the
  neighbors did not buy or sell the park but hear the noise.
- **D2.** (a) External benefit: neighbors get jobs at the shop, or
  the school gets donated wooden benches. (b) External cost:
  delivery trucks make noise during lessons, or sawdust smells
  drift over the playground. (c) Fix: deliver trucks after school
  hours, or put a filter on the sawdust, or move deliveries to a
  back entrance. Accept any fix that shrinks the named cost.
- **D3.** (a) Cedar Hollow sends bread and apples to Sunnyvale.
  (b) Hilltop sends wool to Cedar Hollow. (c) Any two sensible
  effects, for example: shoppers in Cedar Hollow get fewer oranges
  (and oranges may cost more); Ana sells less bread to Sunnyvale
  because Sunnyvale earned less money; Ben sells more apples as
  families switch from oranges to apples.
- **D4.** (a) One person would need a forest, a mine, a rubber
  tree, and a metal works — plus the skill to cut, dig, shape, and
  assemble all four materials. No one person has all of that.
  (b) The pencil shows interdependence: an ordinary thing needs
  many places and many workers, each doing their part and trading.
- **D5.** (a) Goods moved faster and farther by train instead of
  slowly by wagon and ferry; more goods arrived and left, and new
  shops opened near the station. (b) Helped: shops near the
  station, or farmers who could sell farther away. Hurt: ferry
  workers, who lost work when the bridge and railroad came.
- **D6.** (a) Yes — the next-door class bought and sold nothing,
  but the squeak affects their reading time, so it is an external
  cost. (b) Fix: a quiet wheel, oiling the wheel, or moving the
  cage to the far corner — the squeak stops or moves away from the
  shared wall.

## Project — Cedar Hollow Trade Fair (12 pts)

Score against the rubric in [project.md](project.md). The worked
profit example in the project (paper bracelets: beads 30¢ + string
10¢ = cost 40¢; price $1.00; profit per bracelet $1.00 − $0.40 =
$0.60; profit for 10 bracelets 10 × $0.60 = $6.00) is checked
arithmetic: 30 + 10 = 40; 100 − 40 = 60; 10 × 60 = 600¢ = $6.00.
Sample trade: the learner trades 1 bracelet ($1.00) to Ben for 4
apples ($1.00) — both sides gain: the learner gets apples they
cannot grow, Ben gets a bracelet he cannot make. Sample map
sentence: "If Hilltop stopped trading wool, Rosa would have no
cloth to sell, and I could not trade for cloth." Accept any stall
plan whose good is makeable with simple resources, any price/profit
card whose arithmetic checks out, and any map whose arrows and
"if trading stopped" sentence follow from the learner's own trade.

## Lesson exit checks (quick reference)

- **Lesson 01:** Scarcity = not enough for all wants. Choosing the
  book gives up the tower (opportunity cost).
- **Lesson 02:** Ana trades because specializing and trading gives
  her bread *and* apples *and* cloth.
- **Lesson 03:** Money is easier than barter because everyone
  accepts it. Change from $1.00 for 75¢ is 25¢.
- **Lesson 04:** Profit = money in − cost. 10 cups: $6.00 profit.
- **Lesson 05:** External cost = a bad effect on people outside
  the deal. Park benefit: a place to play (or neighbors meeting,
  or more visitors for nearby shops).
- **Lesson 06:** Places depend on each other because each has
  something the others need. The 1910 railroad moved goods faster
  and farther, and shops opened near the station.
