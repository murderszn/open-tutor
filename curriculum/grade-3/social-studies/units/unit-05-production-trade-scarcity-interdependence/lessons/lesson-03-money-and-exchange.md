# Lesson 03 — Money Makes Trading Easier

**Week 21 · S3 (investigation session) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 05**

## Student goal

*I can explain why money makes trading easier than barter, count
money to $5.00, and make change.*

## Materials

- Play coins and bills (or paper tokens): pennies (1¢), nickels
  (5¢), dimes (10¢), quarters (25¢), $1.00 bills. No real money is
  used.
- Three "want cards" (adult prepares): Ana wants a basket · the
  basket-maker wants apples · Ben wants bread.
- Price tags (fictional practice data): apple 25¢ · loaf of bread
  $2.00 · piece of cloth $5.00 · cup of lemonade $1.00.

## Readiness check (2 min)

"How many cents are in one dollar?" (100¢.) "How many quarters
make one dollar?" (Four — 25¢ + 25¢ + 25¢ + 25¢ = 100¢.) "How
many dimes make one dollar?" (Ten.)

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

**Barter** is trading a good or service directly for another good
or service, with no money. Barter works when both people want what
the other has. But it has a problem: what if the other person does
not want what you have?

Ana wants a basket. The basket-maker wants apples, not bread. Ana
has bread, not apples. So Ana must trade bread to Ben for apples,
and then trade the apples to the basket-maker for the basket. Two
trades, and she must find Ben first, and hope Ben wants bread
today. If Ben already has bread, Ana is stuck.

**Money** solves this. Money is coins and bills that everyone
agrees to accept in exchange. Ana sells her bread for money, then
pays money for the basket. One step instead of two, and she does
not need to find someone who wants bread — she only needs someone
who will take money, and everyone takes money.

Money has three jobs, and the learner only needs the first two by
name:

1. **Money is a go-between for trade.** It lets you sell what you
   make and buy what you want, without matching wants person by
   person.
2. **Money measures prices.** A loaf is $2.00 and an apple is 25¢,
   so you can see at once that a loaf costs the same as eight
   apples (8 × 25¢ = $2.00).
3. (Adult-side note) Money also stores value — you can keep it
   and spend it later. The learner meets this idea as "saving" in
   Lesson 01's jump-rope choice, not as a definition to memorize.

Counting money: add the coins by value. A quarter is 25¢, a dime
is 10¢, a nickel is 5¢, a penny is 1¢. One dollar is 100¢. To make
change, subtract the price from the money paid: pay $1.00 for a
75¢ purchase, and the change is 25¢ ($1.00 − $0.75 = $0.25).

**Vocabulary in context:** *barter* — trading one thing directly
for another, no money. *Money* — coins and bills everyone accepts
in exchange. *Price* — what a good or service costs in money.

## Worked example 1 — Three apples and change (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult works with play coins, thinking aloud:

"Ben sells apples for 25¢ each. Milo buys 3 apples. First, the
price: 25¢ + 25¢ + 25¢ = 75¢. Three quarters make 75¢, and so does
one quarter + one quarter + one quarter — same coins, same total.
Milo pays with a $1.00 bill. Change is what is left over:
$1.00 − $0.75 = $0.25. So Milo gets one quarter back. Check it
backwards: 75¢ + 25¢ = 100¢ = $1.00. The check works, so the
change is right. A common error is to forget that $1.00 is 100¢,
not 10¢ — always count in cents first."

## Worked example 2 — The basket, two ways (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Ana wants a basket. The basket-maker wants apples. Ana has bread.

*By barter:* Ana trades 1 loaf to Ben for 8 apples. That trade is
fair, because 1 loaf is $2.00 and 8 apples are $2.00 (8 × 25¢ =
$2.00). Then she trades 4 of those apples to the basket-maker for
the basket — 4 apples are $1.00, and the basket is $1.00, so that
trade is fair too. Two trades, and she had to carry apples around
and find two people who wanted what she had.

*With money:* Ana sells 1 loaf for $2.00. She pays $1.00 for the
basket and keeps $1.00. One sale, one purchase, done. Money did
not change what anything is worth — the loaf is still $2.00 and
the basket is still $1.00. Money only made the exchange easy.
That is the whole lesson: *money is a tool for trading, not a
thing that makes bread or baskets.*"

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, with play coins:

1. "A loaf costs $2.00. Milo pays with a $5.00 bill. What is his
   change?" ($5.00 − $2.00 = $3.00.)
2. "A piece of cloth costs $5.00. Rosa is paid with five $1.00
   bills. Is that enough? How do you know?" (Yes — 5 × $1.00 =
   $5.00, exactly the price, so no change.)
3. "Milo has 2 quarters, 1 dime, and 1 nickel. How much money is
   that?" (25¢ + 25¢ + 10¢ + 5¢ = 65¢.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "Milo paid $1.00 for a 75¢
   apple bag and got 15¢ change — that is right, because 100 minus
   75 is 15." Ask, "Is that right? How do you know?" (No —
   100 − 75 = 25, not 15. The change is 25¢. Check: 75¢ + 25¢ =
   100¢.)

## Independent practice (4 min)

Learner completes practice set B, items B3–B4 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)). The adult does not correct mid-task;
errors are discussed in the exit check.

## Application (3 min)

**Classroom trade simulation.** The adult gives the learner three
play "goods" cards (bread, apples, cloth) and a classmate role (or
the adult plays the other trader). Round 1 — barter only: the
learner must get cloth, starting with bread, when the cloth trader
wants apples, not bread. Round 2 — with play money: the learner
sells bread for $2.00 and buys cloth. "Which round was easier, and
why?" (Round 2 — money removed the matching problem.) If the
learner is working alone with the adult, the adult plays both other
traders.

## Exit check (1 min)

"Why is money easier than barter?" (Because everyone accepts
money, so you do not need to find someone who wants exactly what
you have.) "And how much change from $1.00 for a 75¢ purchase?"
(25¢.) If the learner hesitates, re-teach with the apple example:
3 × 25¢ = 75¢, $1.00 − $0.75 = $0.25.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** use real play coins the learner can hold and count;
  keep the price tags visible; the adult scribes all numbers.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads every card aloud;
  "barter" is acted out with a hand-swap gesture, "money" with a
  coin tap.
- **Extension:** the learner prices three new market goods in cents
  (for example, a basket $1.00, a candle 50¢, a pie $3.00) and
  works out the change from a $5.00 bill for each. (Answers: $4.00,
  $4.50, $2.00.)

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Money makes things."** Money buys things; workers, tools,
  and nature make things. The oven makes bread, not the coins.
- **"$1.00 is 10 cents."** A dollar is 100 cents. Always count in
  cents first, then convert.
- **"Barter is bad."** Barter is not bad — it is just harder when
  wants do not match. People still barter today (trading toys, for
  example). Money is easier, not "correct."
- **"Change is extra money."** Change is *your own money back* —
  the part of what you paid that is more than the price.
