# Lesson 05 — Outside Effects: Who Else Is Affected?

**Week 23 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 05**

## Student goal

*I can tell who is affected by a change besides the buyer and the
seller, and name the good and bad outside effects.*

## Materials

- Two scenario cards (adult prepares; fictional practice material):
  Card 1 — "The town builds a new park on an empty lot." Card 2 —
  "A woodworking shop opens next to the school."
- A three-column chart: "Helps the buyer/seller" · "Helps other
  people (outside benefit)" · "Hurts other people (outside cost)".

## Readiness check (2 min)

"When Milo sells lemonade, who is helped? Who is hurt?" (The
buyer gets lemonade; Milo gets profit. Nobody is hurt — that is
why it is a good trade.) "But what if Milo's stand played loud
music all day — who would be affected then?" (The neighbors, who
did not buy anything and did not sell anything. That is today's
lesson.)

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "The park and the woodworking
> shop are pretend Cedar Hollow plans, not real town news."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

Most lessons so far were about two people: a buyer and a seller.
But choices often affect a third group: **other people** — people
who did not buy anything and did not sell anything, but feel the
effects anyway.

- An **external benefit** is a good effect on people who are not
  the buyer or the seller.
- An **external cost** is a bad effect on people who are not the
  buyer or the seller.

The word "external" means "outside" — outside the deal between
buyer and seller.

Take the new park. The town (the "seller" of the idea, paid for
with taxes — see Unit 04) builds it on an empty lot. Who is helped
outside the deal? Children get a place to play; neighbors meet
each other; shops near the park get more visitors. Those are
external benefits. Who is hurt outside the deal? The house right
next to the park hears weekend noise; the town must pay to keep
the park clean and safe, and that money comes from taxes — so
everyone in town pays a little, even people who never visit the
park. Those are external costs. A wise decision weighs *both*
sides, not only the people who wanted the park.

Now the woodworking shop next to the school. The shop owner
(the seller) makes wooden toys and earns a profit; buyers get
toys. Outside the deal: neighbors get jobs at the shop (external
benefit), and the school gets new wooden benches the shop donates
(external benefit). But delivery trucks rumble past the school all
morning (external cost: noise during lessons), and sawdust smells
drift over the playground (external cost). The shop did not *mean*
to make noise — but the noise still affects the school. Intention
does not matter; effects do.

How to sort an effect:

1. Name the change.
2. Ask: "Who is the buyer or seller here?" Put their gains and
   losses in the first column.
3. Ask: "Who else is affected, even though they bought and sold
   nothing?" Those effects go in the outside columns.
4. A fair judgment counts all three columns.

**Vocabulary in context:** *external benefit* — a good effect on
people outside the deal. *External cost* — a bad effect on people
outside the deal.

## Worked example 1 — The new park (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult sorts the park's effects aloud into the three columns:

"Buyer/seller column: the town pays to build the park, and park
visitors enjoy it. That is the deal itself.
External benefits: children have a safe place to play; grandparents
meet friends on the benches; the bakery across the street sells
more bread to park visitors. None of those people bought or sold
the park — they are helped anyway.
External costs: the house next door hears children shouting on
Saturday mornings; the town pays workers to mow, clean, and fix
the park, and that money comes from taxes, so everyone pays a
little. Also, the empty lot used to be a quiet shortcut — walkers
must now go around.

So the park is not 'all good' or 'all bad.' It helps many people,
and it costs some people quiet and tax money. The town must decide
whether the outside benefits are worth the outside costs. Notice
the tax link: Unit 04 taught that government pays for services
with taxes. The park's upkeep is one of those services."

## Worked example 2 — The woodworking shop (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Sort with me. The shop sells wooden toys. Buyer/seller column:
the shop owner earns profit; buyers get toys. External benefits:
three neighbors get jobs at the shop — they earn money without
buying or selling a toy. The shop gives the school two wooden
benches. External costs: delivery trucks make noise during morning
lessons; sawdust smells drift over the playground at recess.

Now compare: the jobs and benches help people; the noise and smell
hurt people. A fair decision would ask: can the shop deliver
trucks after school hours? Can it put a filter on the sawdust?
*Fixing an outside cost is often cheaper than living with it.*
That is a grown-up idea worth remembering: when a choice hurts
people outside the deal, look for a way to shrink the hurt instead
of pretending it is not there."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the scenario cards:

1. "Milo's lemonade stand plays loud music. Who is the buyer?
   The seller? Who is outside the deal?" (Buyer: thirsty people.
   Seller: Milo. Outside: the neighbors who hear the music.)
2. "Is the music an external benefit or an external cost — and
   for whom?" (External cost for the neighbors. It could be an
   external benefit for a neighbor who *likes* the music — one
   effect can be good for one person and bad for another.)
3. "Name one way to shrink the outside cost." (Turn the music
   down, play it only for one hour, or use no music.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "The park's noise is not a
   real cost, because the town did not pay for the noise." Ask,
   "Is that right? How do you know?" (No — a cost is not only
   money. The neighbors' lost quiet is a real cost, even though
   nobody was paid for it.)

## Application (3 min)

"Your class wants a class pet — a hamster in a cage by the window.
Name one external benefit and one external cost for the class next
door." (Benefit: the next-door class can visit and learn about the
hamster. Cost: the hamster's wheel squeaks during their quiet
reading time. Any honest pair counts; the adult checks that the
effect is on people outside the deal.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"What is an external cost?" (A bad effect on people who are not
the buyer or the seller.) "Name one external benefit of the new
park." (A place to play, neighbors meeting, more visitors for
nearby shops — any one.) If the learner hesitates, re-teach with
the question: "Who is affected, even though they bought and sold
nothing?"

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the three-column chart visible; let the learner
  sort effects by pointing to a column; the adult scribes.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads both scenario cards
  aloud; "external" is shown with hands pointing *outward*, away
  from the buyer and seller.
- **Extension:** the learner takes the hamster example and proposes
  a fix for its external cost (a quiet wheel, a different corner),
  then re-sorts the effects with the fix in place — showing that
  outside costs can shrink.

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Only money counts as a cost."** Lost quiet, lost time, and
  bad smells are real costs, even when no money changes hands.
- **"If the seller did not mean it, it does not count."** Effects
  count whether or not they were intended. The shop did not mean
  to make noise, but the school still hears it.
- **"External means far away."** External means *outside the
  deal*, not far away — the house next to the park is very close,
  and its noise is an external cost.
- **"Every change is all good or all bad."** Most changes have
  both outside benefits and outside costs. The job is to weigh
  them, not to pick a side first.
