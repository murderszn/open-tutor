# Lesson 04 — Profit: What the Seller Keeps

**Week 22 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 05**

## Student goal

*I can work out a seller's profit, and explain how price and cost
changes change what a seller does.*

## Materials

- Milo's lemonade-stand card (adult prepares; fictional practice
  data): price $1.00 per cup · cost per cup 40¢ (lemon 25¢ + sugar
  10¢ + cup 5¢) · profit per cup 60¢.
- Play coins and bills, or a simple three-row table (money in /
  cost / profit).

## Readiness check (2 min)

"If you sell a cup of lemonade for $1.00, is all of that $1.00
yours to keep? What did you have to buy first?" (No — the lemon,
the sugar, and the cup cost money. The seller keeps only what is
left after paying those costs.)

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "Milo's lemonade stand is
> pretend, and its prices are pretend practice numbers. We use them
> to learn how profit works."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A seller takes in money and pays costs. The money a seller takes
in from sales is called **revenue**. The money a seller spends to
make the good is the **cost**. What is left over is the **profit**:

**Profit = money in − cost.**

For one cup of Milo's lemonade: the price is $1.00, so one cup
brings in $1.00. The cost of one cup is 40¢: lemon 25¢ + sugar 10¢
+ cup 5¢ = 40¢. The profit on one cup is $1.00 − $0.40 = $0.60.
Sixty cents of every dollar stays with Milo; forty cents pays for
the next cup's ingredients.

For a whole day, multiply: if Milo sells 10 cups, the money in is
10 × $1.00 = $10.00. The cost is 10 × $0.40 = $4.00. The profit
is $10.00 − $4.00 = $6.00. Or, quicker: 10 cups × 60¢ profit per
cup = $6.00. Both ways give the same answer — check one way
against the other.

**Profit is why sellers sell.** If the profit is good, a seller
keeps selling, makes more, or opens a second stand. If the profit
is tiny or negative (costs more than the money in), the seller
stops, raises the price, or finds cheaper ingredients. Profit is
also a signal: when many buyers want lemonade and few sellers
sell it, sellers earn good profit — and new sellers join in,
which gives buyers more choice.

Two things change profit: **price** and **cost**. Raise the price
and each cup earns more — *but* fewer people may buy, so the day's
profit can fall. Raise the cost (lemons get expensive) and each cup
earns less — so the seller may raise the price or sell fewer cups.

**Vocabulary in context:** *revenue* — all the money taken in
from sales. *Cost* — what the seller pays to make the good.
*Profit* — money in minus cost; what the seller keeps.

## Worked example 1 — A cool day: 10 cups (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult fills the table aloud:

"Milo sells 10 cups at $1.00 each. Money in: 10 × $1.00 = $10.00.
Cost: 10 cups × 40¢ = 400¢ = $4.00. Profit: $10.00 − $4.00 =
$6.00. Check the quick way: profit per cup is 60¢, and 10 × 60¢ =
600¢ = $6.00. Both ways agree, so $6.00 is right. Milo keeps
$6.00 and has $4.00 of cost already paid — the $4.00 is gone, spent
on lemons, sugar, and cups. A common error is to call the $10.00
the profit. It is not: $10.00 is the revenue. Profit is what is
left *after* the $4.00 cost."

## Worked example 2 — A hot day, then a higher price (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"On a hot day Milo sells 20 cups at $1.00. Money in: 20 × $1.00 =
$20.00. Cost: 20 × 40¢ = 800¢ = $8.00. Profit: $20.00 − $8.00 =
$12.00. Twice the cups, twice the profit — $12.00 instead of
$6.00.

Now Milo tries a higher price: $1.50 a cup. Only 8 people buy.
Money in: 8 × $1.50 = $12.00. Cost: 8 × 40¢ = 320¢ = $3.20.
Profit: $12.00 − $3.20 = $8.80. Compare: $8.80 is *less* than the
$12.00 he made at $1.00 on the hot day. The higher price earned
more per cup ($1.10 profit per cup: $1.50 − $0.40) but sold far
fewer cups, so the day's profit fell. The lesson: *a higher price
does not always mean more profit.* Profit depends on price *and*
on how many people buy."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the stand card:

1. "Sugar gets expensive: 20¢ a cup instead of 10¢. What is the
   new cost per cup?" (Lemon 25¢ + sugar 20¢ + cup 5¢ = 50¢.)
2. "At $1.00 a cup, what is the new profit per cup?" ($1.00 −
   $0.50 = $0.50.)
3. "Milo sells 10 cups at the new cost. What is his profit for the
   day?" (10 × 50¢ = $5.00, down from $6.00.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "Milo took in $10.00 today, so
   his profit is $10.00." Ask, "Is that right? How do you know?"
   (No — $10.00 is revenue. Profit is revenue minus cost: $10.00
   − $4.00 = $6.00 at the old cost, or $10.00 − $5.00 = $5.00 at
   the new cost.)

Learner completes practice set C, items C1–C2 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)). The adult does not correct mid-task;
errors are discussed in the exit check.

## Application (3 min)

"Another child opens a lemonade stand across the street and
charges 75¢ a cup. What could Milo do — and what would each
choice do to his profit?" (He could lower his price to 75¢, make
his lemonade better (for example, add a mint leaf — still a cost
to count), or keep his price and sell to people who like his stand
best. Lowering the price earns less per cup but may keep his
buyers; keeping the price earns more per cup but may lose buyers.
Any answer that names a choice *and* its effect on profit counts.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Profit is…?" (Money in minus cost.) "Milo sells 10 cups at
$1.00 and each cup costs 40¢. His profit is…?" ($6.00.) If the
learner hesitates, re-teach with one cup first: $1.00 − $0.40 =
$0.60, then multiply by 10.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the stand card visible with price, cost, and
  profit per cup; use play coins to act out "money in" and "cost"
  piles; the adult scribes all arithmetic.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the card aloud;
  "revenue" is shown as coins coming *in*, "cost" as coins going
  *out*, "profit" as the coins that stay.
- **Extension:** the learner works out Milo's profit if he sells
  15 cups at $1.00 (revenue $15.00, cost $6.00, profit $9.00) and
  explains why it sits exactly between the 10-cup and 20-cup days.
  (Answer: 15 × 60¢ = $9.00; each extra cup adds 60¢ of profit.)

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Profit is the money taken in."** Profit is money in *minus*
  cost. Revenue is the money taken in; profit is what stays.
- **"A higher price always means more profit."** Fewer buyers can
  shrink the day's profit, as the $1.50 example shows.
- **"If the seller makes a profit, the buyer loses."** The buyer
  gets lemonade they value at $1.00 or more; the seller gets
  profit. A fair trade helps both sides (Lesson 02).
- **"Costs don't matter once the stand is built."** Every cup has
  a cost — lemon, sugar, cup — and the cost must be paid again for
  every cup sold.
