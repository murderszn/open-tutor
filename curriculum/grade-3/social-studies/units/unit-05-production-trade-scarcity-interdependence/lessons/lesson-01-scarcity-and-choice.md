# Lesson 01 — Scarcity and Choice: We Can't Have Everything

**Week 20 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 05**

## Student goal

*I can explain what scarcity is, compare the benefits and costs of
a choice, and tell what I give up when I choose.*

## Materials

- Two "choice cards" (adult prepares): Card 1 — "One Saturday
  morning: build a tall block tower OR read a new library book."
  Card 2 — "The class has $10.00: buy new soccer balls OR buy seeds
  and soil for the school garden."
- A blank benefit/cost chart (two columns: "Good things about this
  choice" / "What it costs or gives up").

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Name three things you want right now." (Accept any answers.)
"Now name three things you want that you cannot have right now —
because there is not enough time, not enough money, or not enough
of the thing itself." (Accept any honest answer: "a puppy — my
building does not allow pets," "every book in the shop — I have
$5.00.")

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "Cedar Hollow is a made-up town
> we invented for practice. Its market, its lemonade stand, and its
> prices are pretend. We practice on a pretend town so we know how
> to think about choices in a real town later."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

**Scarcity** means there is not enough of something for everyone to
have all they want. Wants are almost endless — more toys, more time,
more books, more snacks. But time, money, and things are limited.
That gap — unlimited wants, limited resources — is scarcity, and it
is the reason we must choose.

A **want** is something you would like to have. A **need** is
something you must have to live, learn, and stay healthy: food,
water, shelter, clothing. Most choices in this unit are about wants,
but needs come first — a family buys food before it buys a toy.

When you choose, every choice has **benefits** (good things that
come from it) and **costs** (what you pay, lose, or give up). The
best thing you give up is called the **opportunity cost** — a big
name for a simple idea: *choosing one thing means not choosing
another thing.*

How to compare two choices:

1. Name the two choices clearly.
2. List the benefits of each — what good comes from it?
3. List the costs of each — money, time, and what you give up.
4. Pick the one whose benefits matter most to you, and say what
   you gave up. There is often no single right answer — but there
   is always something given up.

An **incentive** is something that nudges a person toward or away
from a choice. A *positive* incentive makes a choice more
attractive: a sale price, a reward, a sticker for reading. A
*negative* incentive makes a choice less attractive: a fine, a
higher price, a long wait. Incentives do not force anyone — they
change what a choice is worth to the chooser.

**Vocabulary in context:** *scarcity* — not enough for everyone to
have all they want. *Benefit* — a good thing from a choice. *Cost*
— what a choice takes: money, time, or something given up.
*Opportunity cost* — the best thing you give up. *Incentive* —
something that nudges a choice.

## Worked example 1 — One Saturday morning (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult thinks aloud with choice card 1:

"Milo has one free Saturday morning. He can build a tall block
tower OR read a new library book. He cannot do both — that is
scarcity of time.

Benefits of the tower: he builds something tall, he practices
balancing, and his little sister can help. Cost: he does not read
the book, and the tower will fall down at cleanup time.
Benefits of the book: he finds out what happens in the story, and
he can tell his friend about it. Cost: he does not build the tower,
and the book is due back on Monday.

Milo picks the book, because he has waited a week for it. His
opportunity cost is the block tower — the best thing he gave up.
If he had picked the tower, his opportunity cost would be the book.
Either way, something is given up. That is the rule: *every choice
has an opportunity cost.*"

## Worked example 2 — The class's $10.00 (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Our class has $10.00 from a bake sale. Choice A: buy new soccer
balls for $10.00. Choice B: buy seeds and soil for the school
garden for $6.00, and keep $4.00.

Benefits of the soccer balls: everyone plays at recess right away,
and the old flat ball is replaced. Cost: all $10.00 is gone, and
there is no money left for the garden.
Benefits of the garden: we grow food we can eat and watch grow for
months, and we keep $4.00 for later. Cost: recess still uses the
old flat ball, and the garden needs watering every week.

The class picks the garden, because the garden lasts months and the
$4.00 stays for later. The opportunity cost is the new soccer balls.
Notice the money math: $6.00 + $4.00 = $10.00 — the two parts add
up to the whole $10.00, so nothing is missing. A common error is
to forget the money left over: the $4.00 kept is part of the
choice's benefits, not money that vanished."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the benefit/cost chart:

1. "June has $5.00. She can buy a storybook for $5.00 OR a jump
   rope for $3.00 and keep $2.00. What is one benefit of the book?
   One cost?" (Benefit: a story to read again and again. Cost:
   all $5.00 is gone and she has no money left.)
2. "What is one benefit of the jump rope? One cost?" (Benefit:
   exercise and play with friends, plus $2.00 kept. Cost: no new
   book.)
3. "If June picks the jump rope, what is her opportunity cost?"
   (The storybook — the best thing she gave up.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "June picked the jump rope, so
   she gave up nothing — she still has $2.00!" Ask, "Is that right?
   How do you know?" (No — she gave up the book. Keeping $2.00 is
   a benefit of her choice, not proof that nothing was given up.)

Learner completes practice set A, items A1–A2 (see

## Application (3 min)

"The library will give a sticker to every learner who reads five
books this month. Is that sticker an incentive? Is it positive or
negative — and what choice does it nudge?" (Yes, an incentive;
positive — it nudges learners to read more, because reading now
earns something extra. Accept any answer that names the sticker as
the nudge and reading as the nudged choice.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Scarcity means…?" (There is not enough of something for everyone
to have all they want.) "And when you choose the book instead of
the tower, what did you give up?" (The tower — the opportunity
cost.) If the learner hesitates, re-teach with "choosing one thing
means not choosing another."

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the benefit/cost chart visible; let the learner
  answer by pointing to a column or by speaking, with the adult
  scribing.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads every card aloud;
  "benefit" and "cost" are shown with thumbs-up / thumbs-down
  gestures.
- **Extension:** the learner finds a real choice from their own
  week (a snack choice, a game choice), builds its benefit/cost
  chart, and names its opportunity cost. The adult checks that the
  opportunity cost is the *best* thing given up, not just any
  thing.

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Scarcity means there is none left."** Scarcity means *not
  enough for all wants* — there can be plenty of apples and still
  scarcity, if more people want apples than there are apples.
- **"The best choice has no cost."** Every choice gives something
  up. A choice with no cost is a sign the costs were not listed.
- **"Opportunity cost is the money I spent."** The money is a
  cost, but the opportunity cost is the *best thing given up* — the
  book, not the $5.00.
- **"An incentive forces you."** Incentives nudge; people still
  choose. A sticker does not force anyone to read.
