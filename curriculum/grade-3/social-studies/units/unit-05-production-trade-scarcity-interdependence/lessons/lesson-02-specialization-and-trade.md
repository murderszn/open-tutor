# Lesson 02 — Specialization and Trade: Everyone Makes Something

**Week 21 · S1 (concept launch) · 30–35 minutes · Unit 05**

## Student goal

*I can name the resources used to make a good, and explain why
people specialize in one kind of work and trade for the rest.*

## Materials

- The market illustration
  ([`../assets/cedar-hollow-market-day.png`](../assets/cedar-hollow-market-day.png))
  printed or shown on screen, or its text-only alternative below.
- The Cedar Hollow market table (unit README).
- Three "resource cards" (adult prepares): wheat, clay oven, baker.

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Name one thing you used today that someone else made." (Accept
any answer: bread, shoes, a book, a chair.) "Could you have made
it yourself? What would you have needed?" (Usually: tools, skills,
and materials the learner does not have — that gap is today's
lesson.)

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "Remember: Cedar Hollow is a
> made-up town. Its market is pretend, and its prices are pretend
> practice numbers."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 10 min)

A **good** is a thing you can buy: bread, an apple, a cloth. A
**service** is work someone does for you: baking, fixing a roof,
teaching. The people who make goods or provide services are
**producers**. The people who buy or use them are **consumers**.
Most people are both: Ana the baker produces bread and consumes
apples.

Everything a producer makes needs **resources** — things used to
make a good or provide a service. There are three kinds:

1. **Natural resources** — things from nature: wheat, wool, water,
   wood, sunshine, soil.
2. **Human resources** — workers and their skills: the farmer who
   grows wheat, the miller who grinds it, the baker who bakes it.
3. **Physical resources** — tools, machines, and buildings: the
   mill, the clay oven, the loom, the baskets.

Follow one loaf of Ana's bread backwards: bread needs flour; flour
needs wheat and a mill; wheat needs soil, sun, rain, and a farmer.
No one person does all of that. So people **specialize** — they
focus on making one kind of good or doing one kind of work, and
they **trade** for the rest. Ana bakes bread. She does not grow
wheat, grind flour, or build ovens. She trades bread for apples,
cloth, and everything else she needs. Specialization works because
each person gets good at one job, and trade lets everyone have
more kinds of things than any one person could make alone.

Trade also crosses places. Cedar Hollow cannot grow oranges — its
summers are too short. Sunnyvale, to the south, grows oranges
easily but cannot grow enough wheat. So Cedar Hollow trades bread
and apples for Sunnyvale oranges. Hilltop raises sheep for wool;
Rosa trades cloth for wool. When places trade, each place uses
what its land and people do best.

**Vocabulary in context:** *good* — a thing you buy. *Service* —
work done for you. *Producer* — one who makes or does.
*Consumer* — one who buys or uses. *Natural resource* — from
nature. *Human resource* — workers and skills. *Physical
resource* — tools and buildings. *Specialize* — focus on one kind
of work. *Trade* — exchange with someone.

## Worked example 1 — The loaf of bread (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

The adult traces the bread chain on the table, pointing at the
market illustration's bakery stall:

"Ana sells a loaf for $2.00. What did it take to make? Wheat —
a natural resource, grown in soil with sun and rain. A farmer — a
human resource, who planted and harvested it. A mill — a physical
resource, that ground the wheat into flour. A miller — a human
resource, who ran the mill. A clay oven — a physical resource.
And Ana — a human resource, who mixed, kneaded, and baked.

Now ask: why doesn't Ana do it all herself? She would need a
field, a mill, and an oven, plus the time to farm, mill, and bake.
By specializing — baking only — she bakes many loaves every day,
and she trades loaves for apples from Ben and cloth from Rosa.
Specialization plus trade gives Ana bread *and* apples *and*
cloth. Doing everything alone would give her less of everything."

## Worked example 2 — Two loaves for one basket (modeled reasoning, 5 min)

"Ana trades 2 loaves of bread for 1 basket of apples from Ben.
Check both sides. Ana gives 2 loaves she baked and gets apples
she cannot grow — she is better off, because she wanted apples.
Ben gives apples he grew and gets bread he did not bake — he is
better off, because he wanted bread. A good trade makes *both*
sides better off. If Ben already had plenty of bread, he would say
no — and that is fine. Trade only happens when both sides want
what the other has. That is why trade is a choice, not a rule."

## Guided practice (5 min)

Adult and learner together, using the market illustration:

1. "Point to the bakery stall. What is the baker's good? What are
   two resources she used?" (Bread; wheat and the clay oven — or
   flour and the baker's skill.)
2. "Point to the farm stand. What is the farmer's good? Name one
   natural, one human, and one physical resource for it." (Apples
   and vegetables; soil/sun/rain; the farmer; baskets and tools.)
3. "Point to the weaving stall. What does Rosa make, and what does
   she need from Hilltop?" (Cloth; wool from Hilltop sheep.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "Rosa should grow her own wool,
   bake her own bread, and grow her own apples — then she would
   never need to trade!" Ask, "Is that a good plan? Why not?"
   (No — she would have little time left to weave, and her cloth
   would be worse; specializing and trading gives her more and
   better things.)

## Application (3 min)

Use the Observe–Reflect–Question routine from Unit 03 on the
market illustration (or its text-only alternative):

- **Observe:** "Name three things you notice in the picture."
- **Reflect:** "What do the three different stalls tell you about
  how work is divided in Cedar Hollow?"
- **Question:** "What do you wonder about where the wool or the
  wheat comes from?"
- **Cannot tell:** "Name one thing this picture cannot tell you."
  (For example: the prices, the town's real name, how much each
  seller earns, or what happens to goods that do not sell.)

## Exit check (1 min)

"Why does Ana the baker trade instead of making everything
herself?" (Because she is good at baking, and trading lets her
have apples, cloth, and other things she cannot make as well or as
fast.) If the learner hesitates, re-teach with the bread chain:
wheat → flour → bread, and who does each step.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the market table visible; let the learner
  answer by pointing at stalls in the illustration; the adult
  scribes resource lists.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the table aloud;
  "natural / human / physical" are shown with three hand signals
  (ground, person, tool).
- **Extension:** the learner traces a second good backwards —
  Rosa's cloth (wool → yarn → cloth) or Ben's apples (seed → tree
  → basket) — naming one resource of each kind at each step.

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"The baker made the bread all by herself."** Bread needs a
  chain of workers and resources. One producer is the last step,
  not the only step.
- **"Trade means someone loses."** A voluntary trade makes both
  sides better off — each gives what they have and gets what they
  want. If one side would lose, they say no.
- **"Money is a resource for making bread."** Money is a tool for
  *trading*, not an ingredient: wheat, the oven, and the baker
  make bread; money only buys it. (Lesson 03 makes this explicit.)
- **"Specializing means doing only one thing forever."** People
  can learn new work. Specializing means *focusing* for now, not
  being locked in.
