# Lesson 06 — Depending on Each Other: The Trade Web

**Week 23 · S3 (investigation session, first half) · 15 minutes teaching + Trade Fair share · Unit 05**

## Student goal

*I can explain how trade makes places depend on each other, and
how a new road or railroad changes a place's economy.*

## Materials

- The trade map
  ([`../assets/trade-map-cedar-hollow.svg`](../assets/trade-map-cedar-hollow.svg))
  printed or shown on screen, or its text-only alternative below.
- A pencil (for the pencil trace) and the market illustration.
- The Trade Fair project materials (see [project.md](../project.md)).

## Readiness check (2 min)

"Look around the room. Name one thing that was made in a different
place from where we sit." (Accept any answer: a book printed
elsewhere, shoes made far away, an apple grown in another town.)
"How did it get here?" (Someone traded it here — by truck, train,
or ship. That journey is today's lesson.)

> **Fiction label (read aloud):** "The trade map is a pretend map
> of pretend towns. It shows how trade connects them — it is not a
> real map and not to scale."

## Teaching (explicit explanation, 8 min)

When places trade, they become **interdependent** — they depend on
each other. Cedar Hollow depends on Sunnyvale for oranges: no
Sunnyvale oranges, no orange juice in Cedar Hollow. Sunnyvale
depends on Cedar Hollow for bread and apples. Hilltop depends on
Cedar Hollow for cloth, and Cedar Hollow depends on Hilltop for
wool. Each place has something the others need, so each place
needs the others. That web of needs is interdependence, and it is
why trade matters: it is not just swapping things, it is how
places take care of each other.

Interdependence goes further than towns. Trace one pencil: the
wood comes from trees, the black middle (graphite) comes from a
mine, the rubber eraser comes from rubber trees, and the metal band
comes from metal dug from the ground. Four different materials,
from different places, put together in one pencil. Your sandwich
is the same: bread from Ana, cheese from a dairy farm, lettuce
from Ben. Almost everything you use is a team effort by people and
places you will never meet.

Trade patterns also **change over time**. In Cedar Hollow, the
railroad station opened in 1910 (Unit 03's timeline). Before the
railroad, goods moved slowly by wagon and ferry. After the
railroad, goods moved fast and far: more oranges arrived, more
bread left town, and new shops opened near the station. A new road,
a new railroad, or a new market changes *where* goods go and *how
much* moves — and that changes what work people do. That is the
pattern: **new connections change old patterns.**

What happens when one thread of the web breaks? If Sunnyvale has
a bad year for oranges, Cedar Hollow gets fewer oranges, and the
price rises — so people buy apples instead. If the river road
closes, the wool carts take the long way, and Rosa's cloth costs
more. Interdependence is a strength (everyone gets more) and a
risk (one place's trouble touches the others). Wise traders keep
more than one partner, so one broken thread does not break the web.

**Vocabulary in context:** *interdependence* — places and people
depending on each other. *Trade web* — the pattern of who trades
what with whom.

## Worked example 1 — The pencil trace (modeled reasoning, 4 min)

The adult holds up a pencil and traces it aloud:

"Wood: from trees, cut and shaped in a sawmill. Graphite: the
black middle, dug from a mine as a mineral. Rubber: the eraser,
from rubber trees far away. Metal: the little band, from metal
dug from the ground and shaped in a factory. Four materials, four
different places, plus the workers who cut, dug, shaped, and put
them together. Now ask: could one person make a whole pencil
alone? They would need a forest, a mine, a rubber tree, and a
metal works — and the skill to do all four jobs. No. The pencil
exists because many places and many workers each did their part
and traded. *Every ordinary thing is an interdependence story.*"

## Worked example 2 — The bad orange year (modeled reasoning, 4 min)

"Sunnyvale has a bad year: frost kills half its oranges. Trace
the web. Sunnyvale has fewer oranges to trade, so Cedar Hollow
gets fewer oranges. With fewer oranges, the price in Cedar Hollow
rises — sellers charge more for what is scarce (Lesson 01). Some
Cedar Hollow families buy apples instead, so Ben sells more
apples. Sunnyvale earns less money, so it buys less bread from
Ana. One frost in Sunnyvale touches the baker, the farmer, and
the shoppers in Cedar Hollow. That is interdependence: *one
place's trouble travels along the web.* And the fix is also a web
fix: Cedar Hollow buys oranges from a second town as well, so one
bad year in one town does not leave Cedar Hollow with no oranges."

## Guided practice (4 min)

Adult and learner together, using the trade map:

1. "Point to Sunnyvale on the map. What does Cedar Hollow get
   from Sunnyvale? What does Sunnyvale get back?" (Oranges in;
   bread and apples out.)
2. "Point to Hilltop. What travels each way?" (Wool comes down to
   Cedar Hollow; cloth goes up to Hilltop.)
3. "If the river road to Hilltop closed, what would happen to
   Rosa's cloth?" (Less wool arrives, so less cloth is made, and
   cloth may cost more — or Rosa buys wool from another town.)
4. Error-analysis: the adult says, "Cedar Hollow does not need
   anyone — it has bread, apples, and cloth." Ask, "Is that right?
   How do you know?" (No — it has no oranges and no wool of its
   own. Without trade it would have no oranges and no new cloth.)

## Independent practice (3 min)

Learner completes practice set D, items D3–D4 (see
[practice.md](../practice.md)). The adult does not correct mid-task;
errors are discussed in the exit check.

## Application — Trade Fair share (10 min)

The learner presents their Trade Fair stall (see
[project.md](../project.md)): the good, its resources, its price
and profit, the trade they made, and their interdependence map.
The adult asks one question: "What would happen to your stall if
your trade partner stopped trading?" The learner answers from
their own map.

## Exit check (1 min)

"Why do places depend on each other?" (Because each place has
something the others need — oranges, wool, bread — and trade is
how they share.) "And what changed in Cedar Hollow when the
railroad came in 1910?" (Goods moved faster and farther; more
shops opened near the station.) If the learner hesitates, re-teach
with the pencil: four materials, four places, one pencil.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** keep the trade map visible; let the learner trace
  arrows with a finger; the adult scribes the web.
- **Reading/language access:** the adult reads the map labels
  aloud; "interdependence" is shown with linked hands.
- **Extension:** the learner adds a fourth trade partner to the
  map — a fishing town that trades fish for bread — draws its
  arrows, and explains one new outside effect of the fish trade
  (for example, fish smell near the market is an external cost;
  fresh fish for everyone is an external benefit).

## Misconceptions to watch

- **"Trade is only about things."** Trade also moves ideas and
  skills: Rosa learns a new weaving pattern from a Hilltop trader,
  and the idea travels with the wool.
- **"Depending on others is a weakness."** Interdependence gives
  everyone more than they could make alone. The risk is real, but
  the answer is more partners, not no partners.
- **"The railroad only helped Cedar Hollow."** New connections
  help and hurt: shops near the station grew, but the ferry
  workers (Unit 03) lost work when the bridge and railroad came.
  Patterns change — winners and losers both count.
- **"One town makes everything in my home."** Almost nothing in a
  home comes from one place. The pencil trace proves it.
