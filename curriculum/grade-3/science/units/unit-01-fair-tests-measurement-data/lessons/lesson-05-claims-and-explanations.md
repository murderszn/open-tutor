# Lesson 05 — Claims with Evidence (and Honest Limitations)

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, the
learner's data table from Lesson 03's practice plan (or the fictional ramp
table from Lesson 04)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how scientists turn
data into a **claim** — a statement supported by **evidence** — and why
honest scientists also name their test's **limitations**.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "What is the difference between 'I think
the high ramp rolls farther' and 'The high ramp rolled 39 cm farther in our
3 trials'?" The second uses measured data — that is the beginning of a
claim.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **claim** says what you believe is true *because of your evidence*.
**Evidence** is your data, used to support the claim. And a **limitation**
is something your test cannot prove — honest science names it.

A strong grade-3 claim has three parts:

1. **What you claim** (answers the testable question).
2. **At least two pieces of evidence** (real numbers from your table).
3. **One limitation** (what the test did not prove, or could not rule out).

**Worked example 1 — a full claim, built slowly:**

> Adult: "Question: *Does ramp height change how far the car rolls?*
> Evidence from my fictional practice table: low-ramp trials were 28, 30,
> 29 cm (total 87); high-ramp trials were 41, 43, 42 cm (total 126).
> My claim: **The higher ramp made the car roll farther.** My evidence:
> **the high-ramp total was 126 cm and the low-ramp total was 87 cm — 39
> cm more.** My limitation: **we only tested one car on one ramp, so this
> may not be true for every car and ramp.**"

**Worked example 2 — a weak claim, strengthened:**

> Adult: "Weak claim: 'The towel ramp was slower.' What's missing? No
> numbers! Strengthened: 'The car rolled farther on bare wood than on the
> towel. Evidence: bare-wood trials totaled 120 cm and towel trials totaled
> 96 cm. Limitation: we did only 3 trials each, so one bumpy roll could
> still change the result.' Now the claim can be checked — that is the
> point."

Key idea to say plainly: **A claim without numbers is just an opinion.
Name your evidence, then name what your test cannot prove.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner builds)

Use this fictional practice dataset (magnet paper-clip pickups, 3 trials):

| Magnet | Trial 1 | Trial 2 | Trial 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| One magnet | 6 clips | 7 clips | 6 clips |
| Two magnets | 11 clips | 12 clips | 11 clips |

1. Add each row. *(One: 6 + 7 + 6 = 19; Two: 11 + 12 + 11 = 34)*
2. How many more clips did two magnets pick up in total? *(34 − 19 = 15)*
3. Draft a claim answering "Do two magnets pick up more paper clips than
   one?" *(Accept: "Yes — two magnets picked up more.")*
4. Add two pieces of evidence with numbers. *(Accept: totals 34 vs. 19;
   every two-magnet trial beat every one-magnet trial.)*
5. Add one limitation. *(Accept: only 3 trials; only paper clips tested;
   only these two magnets.)*
6. Your friend claims "Two magnets are always stronger than one." What is
   wrong with "always"? *(The test only tried these magnets and paper
   clips — "always" goes beyond the evidence.)*

## Independent practice

The learner writes a three-part claim for a dataset of their choice (their
own Lesson 02 measurements, the Lesson 04 bean-bag data, or the magnet
data): claim + two evidence numbers + one limitation. Sentence frame:
"I claim ___. My evidence is ___ and ___. One limitation is ___."

## Application

Error-analysis: the adult reads a flawed claim — "The blue car is the
fastest car in the world because it won our race." The learner names two
problems (no evidence numbers; "in the world" goes far beyond one race)
and rewrites it as an honest claim.

## Exit check

Prompt: "What three parts does a strong science claim need?" Accept: the
claim itself, at least two pieces of evidence (numbers), and one limitation.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Provide the three-part frame with blanks; let the learner
  dictate while the adult scribes.
- **Extension:** The learner evaluates a real-world style claim ("Our towel
  absorbs twice as much!") and lists what evidence they would demand before
  believing it — connecting to advertising and media literacy.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** confusing "limitation" with "mistake." A
limitation is not an apology — it is honesty about what the evidence
covers. Praise limitations; they are what separate science claims from
bragging. Also watch for claims that sneak in a second changed variable
("the high ramp AND the smooth surface won") — send the learner back to
the Lesson 03 step cards. **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) (Lesson 05). All datasets here are
fictional practice data, labeled as such.
