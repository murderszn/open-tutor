# Lesson 02 — Observing Carefully and Measuring with Standard Tools

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, ruler with
cm/mm, balance scale, measuring cup or graduated cylinder, hand lens,
3–5 everyday objects (pencil, eraser, spoon, small rock, crayon)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how scientists observe
with tools and how to measure length, mass, and volume with standard units —
so every measurement you record can be trusted and repeated.

**Readiness check:** Adult holds up the ruler: "What is this for?" Any answer
about measuring length shows readiness. Show the balance and cup: "These
measure how heavy and how much liquid — we'll learn both today."

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

An **observation** is something you notice with your senses — or with a tool
that extends them. A hand lens lets you see tiny details. But observations
like "it's kind of long" are not very useful, because "kind of long" means
different things to different people. Scientists **measure** so everyone
means the same thing.

**Length.** We measure length in **centimeters** and **millimeters** with a
ruler. Two rules that matter: (1) start at the **0 mark**, not the end of
the ruler — the end may be worn or extra; (2) always write the **unit**
(cm or mm), because "12" alone means nothing. Ten millimeters make one
centimeter.

**Mass.** We measure how heavy something is in **grams** with a balance
scale. Put the object on one side and gram weights (or known masses) on the
other until it balances.

**Volume.** We measure liquids in **milliliters** with a measuring cup.
Read the scale at eye level, at the bottom of the curve of the liquid.

**Estimate first.** Before measuring, make an **estimate** — a careful
guess. Estimating trains your eyes; then the measurement checks you.

**Worked example 1 — measuring length, out loud:**

> Adult: "I estimate this pencil is about 15 cm. Now I measure: I line up
> the 0 mark with one end — not the ruler's edge, the 0. The other end
> reaches past 17 cm to the 5th small mark after 17. Each small mark is
> 1 millimeter, so this is 17 cm and 5 mm. My estimate (15 cm) was close
> but short. The measurement wins."

**Worked example 2 — measuring mass and volume, out loud:**

> Adult: "I estimate this eraser is about 20 grams. On the balance it takes
> 25 grams to balance — so 25 g. Now volume: I pour water to the 100 mL
> line, reading at eye level. Not 100-ish — exactly at the line: 100 mL.
> Units every time: 25 g, 100 mL."

Key idea to say plainly: **Start at zero, read carefully, and always write
the unit. An estimate comes first; the measurement decides.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner measures)

1. Estimate, then measure, the length of the spoon in centimeters. *(Start
   at 0; record "___ cm.")*
2. Measure the same spoon in millimeters. *(Should be 10× the cm number —
   check: does it match?)*
3. Estimate, then weigh, the small rock in grams.
4. Pour water to exactly the 50 mL line; read at eye level.
5. Measure the crayon's length. Which is longer — the crayon or the spoon?
   By about how many centimeters?

## Independent practice

The learner picks any two classroom objects, estimates length and mass for
each, measures, and records all four numbers with units in the journal in a
two-column table (Object | Estimate | Measurement).

## Application

The bean-seed extension (from the scope-and-sequence enrichment path):
estimate how many bean seeds fit in a 10 mL spoon, then test by filling it.
Record estimate vs. count — this is the same estimate-then-measure habit
Unit 05's seedling work will need.

## Exit check

Prompt: "Show me where 0 is on the ruler, and tell me why we don't start at
the ruler's edge." Accept: the edge can be worn or have extra space, so the
0 mark is the true start; starting at the edge makes every measurement wrong.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Mark the 0 on the ruler with a sticker. Use pre-drawn table
  frames. Let the learner measure with paper strips marked in whole
  centimeters first.
- **Extension:** Convert: a 4 cm eraser is how many millimeters? (40 mm.)
  Measure an object longer than the ruler by marking and moving the ruler —
  and explain why the marks must not overlap or gap.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** reading from the ruler's end instead of 0, and
dropping units ("it's 12"). Both are treated as incomplete measurements,
not wrong numbers — re-teach the ritual, don't just correct the value.
