# Lesson 04 — Data Tables, Bar Graphs, Pictographs, and Line Plots

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, crayons
(3 colors), ruler

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn how to put measurements
into tables and turn tables into three pictures of data — bar graphs,
pictographs, and line plots — so patterns are easy to see.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "If you rolled a car 3 times and got 28,
30, and 29 cm, how would you keep those numbers so you don't forget?"
Any answer about writing them down shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

**Data** belongs in a **table** first: neat rows and columns with labels and
units. Then we draw the data so our eyes can find **patterns**.

- A **bar graph** uses bars. The labels go along the bottom, numbers up the
  side, and the title says what the graph shows. Longer bar = bigger number.
- A **pictograph** uses pictures. Each picture stands for a set number —
  the key tells you how many (for example, each car picture = 5 cm).
- A **line plot** uses X marks above a number line. Each X is one trial.
  Stacks of X's show where results cluster — a pattern you can see.

**Worked example 1 — from table to bar graph, built together:**

> Adult: "Here is my ramp data — a **fictional practice table**, not our
> real measurements:"
>
> | Ramp height | Trial 1 | Trial 2 | Trial 3 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Low | 28 cm | 30 cm | 29 cm |
> | High | 41 cm | 43 cm | 42 cm |
>
> "First I add each row: low = 28 + 30 + 29 = 87 cm; high = 41 + 43 + 42 =
> 126 cm. Now the bar graph: title 'Total roll distance by ramp height',
> two bars labeled Low and High, number line 0–140. The High bar is much
> longer. I can also ask 'how many more': 126 − 87 = 39 cm more for the
> high ramp."

**Worked example 2 — pictograph and line plot, read together:**

> Adult: "Pictograph key: each 🚗 = 5 cm. Ramp A shows 6 cars (6 × 5 =
> 30 cm); Ramp B shows 8 cars (8 × 5 = 40 cm). How many more for B? 2 more
> pictures × 5 = 10 cm more. Now a line plot of six practice rolls: 40,
> 42, 41, 43, 42, 44 cm. I mark X's: 40 (one X), 41 (one), 42 (two X's),
> 43 (one), 44 (one). The stack at 42 is the tallest — 42 cm happened most.
> The values cluster between 40 and 44: a pattern."

Key idea to say plainly: **Tables hold data; graphs picture it. Always
label, always include units, and always say what each picture stands for.**

## Guided practice (adult leads, learner builds and reads)

Use this fictional practice table (bean-bag toss distances, 3 trials):

| Thrower | Trial 1 | Trial 2 | Trial 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sam | 120 cm | 125 cm | 121 cm |
| Alex | 140 cm | 138 cm | 142 cm |

1. Add each row: Sam's total? *(120 + 125 + 121 = 366 cm)* Alex's total?
   *(140 + 138 + 142 = 420 cm)*
2. Sketch a bar graph with a title, labels, and units. Which bar is longer?
   *(Alex's)*
3. How many more centimeters did Alex's throws total than Sam's?
   *(420 − 366 = 54 cm)*
4. Pictograph check: key says each ⭐ = 10 cm. Sam's total needs how many
   stars? *(366 ÷ 10 = 36.6 — not a whole number! Discuss: pictographs work
   best with friendly numbers. Round to about 370 cm → 37 stars, and note
   that the picture is approximate.)*
5. Line plot: plot Sam's three trials (120, 125, 121) as X's. Where do they
   cluster? *(120–125 cm; close together — a pattern, trustworthy)*
6. Error spot: a classmate's graph has no numbers on the side. What is
   missing, and why does it matter? *(The scale — without it you cannot
   tell how big each bar is.)*

## Independent practice

The learner makes a line plot of these fictional roll distances (cm):
35, 37, 36, 35, 38, 36 — then writes two sentences: where the X's cluster,
and which value happened most.

## Application

Look back at your own measurement table from Lesson 02 (the two objects).
Choose bar graph or pictograph, draw it with labels and units, and write
one "how many more" question about it for the adult to answer.

## Exit check

Prompt: "You have a table of 3 trials. Your friend says 'just tell me the
numbers.' Why is a graph sometimes better?" Accept: a graph shows the
pattern (which is bigger, where results cluster) at a glance.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** Provide pre-drawn axes with the number line filled in; the
  learner only draws the bars. Use stickers for X marks and picture symbols.
- **Extension:** The learner explains when a pictograph is a *bad* choice
  (item 4 above) and converts the bean-bag data to a scaled bar graph with
  a scale of 50 cm per grid line.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** treating the tallest bar as "the winner" without
checking what was measured or whether the test was fair — always ask "what
did they measure, and was it a fair test?" Also watch for pictographs drawn
without a key. **Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md)
(Lesson 04). All datasets in this lesson are fictional practice data and
are labeled as such.
