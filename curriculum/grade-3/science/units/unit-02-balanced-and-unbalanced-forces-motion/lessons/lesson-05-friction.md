# Lesson 05 — Friction: The Force That Opposes Motion

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, toy car,
book ramp, two surface cloths (smooth paper/cardboard and a small towel),
tape measure or ruler

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will meet **friction** — the
force that **opposes** sliding and rolling when surfaces touch — and you
will compare motion on a smooth surface and a rough surface.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "In Unit 01's ramp race, the car rolled
farther on the bare board than on the towel. Why do you think that is?"
Any answer about the towel "grabbing" or "slowing" the car shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

When two surfaces touch and one slides or rolls across the other, a force
pushes back **against** the motion. That force is **friction**. Friction
always points **opposite** the direction of motion — it tries to slow the
object down.

Rub your hands together fast. Feel the warmth? That is friction at work —
your hands push against each other, and friction pushes back. Now slide one
hand across the table: easy. Press hard and slide: harder. The same idea
applies to the car:

- **Smooth surface** (bare board) — little friction — the car rolls far.
- **Rough surface** (towel) — more friction — the car slows sooner and
  rolls a shorter distance.

Important: in grade 3 we compare friction with words — *more* friction,
*less* friction — never with numbers. And friction is only one force among
many: gravity still pulls the car down the ramp, and the ramp still pushes
it along. Friction is the force that *opposes* that motion.

Engineers use friction on purpose. Shoe soles and tires are bumpy on
purpose — more grip means more friction, so feet and cars do not slip.
Playground slides are smooth on purpose — less friction, so children slide
fast. Same force, opposite designs.

**Worked example 1 — the two-surface race, narrated:**

> Adult (ramp set by the adult, same height both times): "Same car, same
> ramp, same height — I change only the surface. On the **bare board**, the
> car rolls 80 centimeters. On the **towel**, it rolls 40 centimeters.
> The towel has **more friction** — a stronger force opposing the motion —
> so the car slowed sooner. One variable changed; everything else the
> same. That is a fair test." *(Distances are the adult's demonstration
> values, recorded as observed data.)*

**Worked example 2 — friction by design:**

> Adult shows a shoe sole and a slide (or pictures): "The shoe sole is
> bumpy. Why? **More friction** — your foot grips instead of slipping.
> The slide is smooth. Why? **Less friction** — you slide fast. Engineers
> choose friction on purpose."

Key idea to say plainly: **Friction is a force that opposes motion. Rougher
surfaces make more friction; smoother surfaces make less.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Hands-on: learner slides a block across the bare table, then across the
   towel, and describes which had more friction and how they could tell.
   *(Key: towel = more friction; the block was harder to push / stopped
   sooner.)*
2. Adult names four designs; learner says "more friction" or "less
   friction" and why: bumpy bike tires / a polished slide / sandpaper /
   an ice rink. *(Key: more: tires, sandpaper — grip; less: slide,
   ice — glide.)*
3. **Error-analysis:** "Friction always helps things move faster." Learner
   explains the error. *(Key: friction opposes motion — it slows things
   down; it helps only in the sense of grip/stopping, never by speeding
   motion up.)*

## Independent practice

4. Complete the comparison table in the journal:

   | Surface | Friction (more/less) | Car rolls (farther/shorter) |
   |---|---|---|
   | Bare board | ? | ? |
   | Towel | ? | ? |

   *(Key: board = less / farther; towel = more / shorter.)*
5. Draw the car on the ramp with two arrows: motion direction and
   friction's direction. Label both. *(Key: motion arrow down the ramp;
   friction arrow opposite, labeled "friction.")*
6. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "A designer wants a toy car to roll as far
   as possible down a ramp. Should the ramp be smooth or rough? Use
   friction in your answer." *(Key: smooth — less friction opposing the
   motion, so the car keeps rolling farther.)*

## Application

Friction detectives: the learner finds two "more friction on purpose"
designs and two "less friction on purpose" designs at home (doormat,
rug, socks on a smooth floor, a sled, a drawer that sticks) and journals
each with a one-sentence reason.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "What is friction, and which way does it point compared to the
motion?" *(Answer: a force that opposes sliding/rolling; opposite the
direction of motion.)*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** "friction = the slow-down force" chant card;
  learner may demonstrate with hands instead of writing.
- **Extension:** The learner predicts, then tests, a third surface (e.g.,
  sandpaper vs. board) — writing the prediction with the Lesson 04 frame
  ("I predict ___ because ___").

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: friction is "only on rough things." Correct: friction
acts whenever surfaces touch — even smooth ones have *some* friction, just
less. Keep all friction talk qualitative; the standard's boundary excludes
quantitative force sizes, and grade-3 friction stays descriptive.
