# Lesson 03 — Unbalanced Forces Change Motion

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, a ball,
the tug rope/towel, the generated image
[assets/tug-of-war-balanced-unbalanced.png](../assets/tug-of-war-balanced-unbalanced.png)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn that when forces are
**not** balanced — when one side is stronger, or pushes come from only one
direction — the forces are **unbalanced**, and unbalanced forces **change
the object's motion**: it starts, stops, speeds up, slows down, or changes
direction.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Yesterday you learned that equal and
opposite forces are balanced and nothing changes. What do you think happens
when the forces are NOT equal?" Any prediction about movement shows
readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

When forces on an object do **not** cancel out, they are **unbalanced**.
Unbalanced forces **change the object's motion**. There are exactly five
ways motion can change — learn them like a checklist:

1. **Start** — a still ball begins to roll (you pushed it).
2. **Stop** — a rolling ball comes to rest (you caught it).
3. **Speed up** — the swing goes higher each push.
4. **Slow down** — the car rolls slower on the towel.
5. **Change direction** — the ball bounces off the wall and heads back.

In every case, the motion changed because the forces were unbalanced. A
push on only one side of a ball makes it start moving. Balanced forces
pushing on a box from both sides will not produce any motion at all — but
let one side push harder, and the box slides toward the weaker side.

**Text-only alternative** (the same information, for anyone who cannot see
the picture): The left scene shows a tug-of-war tie. Three kids pull toward
the left and three kids pull toward the right, all pulling equally hard. A
small red flag is tied at the center of the rope, and the flag sits exactly
at a dashed white center line painted on the grass. The rope is straight and
level; nothing is moving. The right scene shows an unbalanced tug-of-war.
Four kids on the left lean back and pull hard toward the left; only two kids
on the right pull toward the right, and they are being dragged forward, off
balance. The rope is visibly sliding toward the left side.

Questions to answer together:

- Which scene shows **balanced** forces? How can you tell? *(Key: left —
  equal teams, flag on the center line, rope not moving.)*
- Which scene shows **unbalanced** forces? What is the motion change?
  *(Key: right — more pullers on the left, the rope slides left; the motion
  changes: the right-side kids start moving / the rope speeds toward the
  left.)*
- What **one thing** is different between the two scenes? *(Key: the
  number of pullers on each side — the number of forces changed, while the
  rope and the ground stayed the same. This is the 3-PS2-1 one-variable
  idea in picture form.)*

**Worked example 1 — one side only:**

> Adult places the ball on the table: "I push from the left only — one
> force, one direction." (Ball rolls right.) "The forces are unbalanced.
> The motion **changed**: the ball **started** moving and headed **right**."

**Worked example 2 — stronger side wins:**

> Adult: "Now I push from the left AND you push back from the right — but
> I push **harder**." (Ball rolls right.) "Two forces, opposite directions,
> but NOT equal. Unbalanced. The ball moves toward the **weaker** side —
> the stronger force wins."

Key idea to say plainly: **Unbalanced forces change motion. Look for the
change: start, stop, speed up, slow down, or change direction — and the
object moves toward the stronger side.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Adult demonstrates five mini-scenes; the learner names the motion
   change from the checklist: (a) still ball pushed; (b) rolling ball
   caught; (c) car given a second push while rolling; (d) ball bounced off
   a wall; (e) car rolling onto a towel and slowing. *(Key: start, stop,
   speed up, change direction, slow down.)*
2. **Error-analysis:** "The ball was rolling and it stopped all by itself,
   so no force acted on it." Learner explains the error. *(Key: something
   stopped it — a hand, a wall, or friction. A change in motion always
   means an unbalanced force acted.)*

## Independent practice

3. Sort six scene cards into **Balanced** and **Unbalanced** piles (adult
   reads if needed): *tug-of-war tie / one team winning / book on a table /
   ball rolling after a kick / two equal pushes on a box / car slowing on
   grass.* *(Key: balanced: tie, book, equal pushes; unbalanced: winning
   team, rolling ball, slowing car.)*
4. For each unbalanced card in item 3, write the motion change: start,
   stop, speed up, slow down, or change direction. *(Key: winning team =
   rope/rope-side starts moving toward the stronger side; rolling ball =
   start; slowing car = slow down.)*
5. Draw and label: a ball with two equal opposite arrows (balanced — write
   what the ball does) and the same ball with one long arrow and one short
   opposite arrow (unbalanced — write what the ball does). *(Key: balanced
   = stays still; unbalanced = moves toward the longer/stronger arrow.)*

## Application

Motion-change detective: over the next hour, the learner spots and
journals three real motion changes (a door stopping, a sibling starting to
run, a ball changing direction) and names the unbalanced force behind each.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Name the five motion changes, and finish this sentence: 'When
I see a motion change, I know ___.'" *(Answer: start, stop, speed up, slow
down, change direction; "...the forces were unbalanced.")*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** the five motion changes on a chant card
  ("start, stop, speed up, slow down, turn around"); learner may point,
  act, or draw.
- **Extension:** The learner designs a fair test question for the project:
  "Does a *stronger* launch push make the car roll farther?" — naming the
  one variable and two controls.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners may think "the object moved toward the
stronger push" is always about people. Reinforce with non-human examples:
wind vs. a kite string, a slope vs. a brake. Keep all comparisons
qualitative — no force numbers (3-PS2-1 boundary). Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
