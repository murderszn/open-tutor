# Lesson 01 — Pushes and Pulls: The Language of Force

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, a toy car
or ball, a piece of string, a drawer or door the learner may open safely,
force word cards (push / pull)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn the word scientists use
for every push and every pull — **force** — and you will practice naming a
force's **direction** and comparing its **strength**.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Yesterday in Unit 01 you changed one
thing at a time in a fair test. Today we learn about the *thing that makes
objects move*. What moves a toy car when you let go of it on a ramp?" Any
answer like "you push it," "it rolls down," or "the ramp" shows readiness.
(Answer: a push from your hand, then the ramp gives it direction.)

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **force** is a **push** or a **pull** acting on an object. A **push** moves
something *away* from you — like pushing a door closed. A **pull** moves
something *closer* to you — like pulling a wagon. Every force has two
features scientists always describe:

1. **Direction** — which way the force points. Push the car toward the
   window, and the force's direction is "toward the window."
2. **Strength** — how strong the force is. In grade 3 we compare strength
   with words, never numbers: a **stronger** push, a **weaker** pull.

Try this with the adult: put the ball on the table. Give it a *gentle* push
— it rolls a little. Now give it a *firm* push — it rolls farther. The
**direction** was the same both times, but the **strength** changed, and
the stronger push changed the motion more. That is the whole idea scientists
use: force = push or pull, with a direction and a strength.

Even things you cannot see are forces. **Gravity** is the force that pulls
objects *down* toward Earth — it is why the ball always rolls down the
ramp and never up. You cannot see gravity, but you can see what it does.

**Worked example 1 — naming a force out loud:**

> Adult pushes the toy car and narrates: "The force is a **push**. Its
> **direction** is toward the door. It was a **weak** push — the car rolled
> only a short way." Then the adult pulls the car back with the string:
> "Now the force is a **pull**. Its **direction** is toward me. The pull
> was **stronger** than my first push."

**Worked example 2 — strength changes the result:**

> Adult: "I will push the car *gently*. Watch how far it goes." (Car rolls
> about 30 cm.) "Now I push *firmly*, in the **same direction**." (Car rolls
> about 90 cm.) "Direction stayed the same; **strength** changed. A stronger
> force changed the motion more."

Key idea to say plainly: **A force is a push or a pull. Every force has a
direction and a strength. Stronger forces change motion more.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Adult points to five everyday moments (opening a door, pulling a
   drawer, kicking a ball, lifting a backpack, pushing a swing). The
   learner names each as push or pull and points the direction with a
   finger. *(Key: door = push, drawer = pull, kick = push, lift = pull
   against gravity, swing push = push.)*
2. Adult rolls the car with a weak push and a strong push. Learner uses the
   frame: "The force was a ___; the direction was ___; the strength was
   ___." *(Key: push; direction as observed; weaker/stronger.)*
3. **Error-analysis:** Adult says, "Gravity pushes objects up into the
   sky." Learner explains the error. *(Key: gravity **pulls** objects
   **down** toward Earth, not up.)*

## Independent practice

4. Draw three arrows in the journal: a push to the right, a pull to the
   left, and gravity's pull. Label each. *(Key: arrows match; gravity's
   arrow points down, labeled "pull.")*
5. Sort six force cards (adult reads if needed): *opening a lunchbox,
   dragging a chair, pressing a doorbell, pulling a sled, pushing a
   stroller, lifting a bucket.* Write P for push, L for pull (or sort the
   cards into two piles). *(Key: push: lunchbox, doorbell, stroller;
   pull: chair, sled, bucket.)*
6. Write or dictate one sentence: "A stronger force changes motion more
   because ___." *(Acceptable: "...because the ball/car rolled farther" or
   any observation from the demos.)*

## Application

Force walk: the learner finds five forces in the room (or on a short walk
with the adult) and records each in a three-column journal table —
*what moved / push or pull / direction*. The adult models the first row.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Is gravity a push or a pull, and which direction does it
pull?" *(Answer: a pull; down toward Earth.)*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** force word cards with pictures (arrow
  showing direction); learner may answer by pointing, drawing arrows, or
  acting the force with the body instead of writing.
- **Extension:** The learner invents two forces that act at the same time
  on one object (e.g., pushing a box while gravity pulls it down) and draws
  both arrows — a preview of Lesson 02.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: learners may say "strength" means "how big the
object is" rather than how strong the push/pull is. Redirect: compare two
pushes on the *same* object. Keep force size qualitative — no numbers, no
newtons (3-PS2-1 assessment boundary). Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
