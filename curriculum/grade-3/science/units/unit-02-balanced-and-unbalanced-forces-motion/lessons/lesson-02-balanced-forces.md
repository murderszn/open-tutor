# Lesson 02 — Balanced Forces: Equal and Opposite, Nothing Changes

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** science journal, pencil, a soft
rope or towel for a team tug, a ball, a small box

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn what happens when two
forces are **equal in size and opposite in direction** — they are
**balanced** — and why a balanced force means **no change in motion**.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Yesterday you named forces as pushes or
pulls with a direction and a strength. Show me a push with your hand, then
a pull." Correct gestures show readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Sometimes two forces push or pull on the same object at the same time. When
the two forces are **equal in strength** and point in **opposite directions**,
they **cancel each other out**. Scientists call that **balanced forces**.
The rule is simple: **balanced forces cause no change in motion.** If the
object was still, it stays still. (A moving object with balanced forces
keeps going the same way — you will see this in older grades; in grade 3 we
watch still objects stay still.)

The classic example: a tug-of-war **tie**. Two teams pull with equal
strength in opposite directions. The rope does not move. The forces are
balanced, so the rope's motion does not change.

Another example: push a box from the left and push it equally hard from the
right. The box stays put. The two pushes are balanced — equal and opposite
— so nothing moves.

A third example is sitting right in front of you: a book lying still on a
table. **Gravity pulls the book down**, and the table holds it up with an
equal push back. The two forces balance, so the book does not move. (Notice
we only name gravity's part as a pull downward — that is all the standard
asks us to say.)

**Worked example 1 — the tie, narrated:**

> Adult (with the learner holding the towel/rope ends, adult supervised):
> "Pull toward you — I will pull toward me, just as hard. Is the rope
> moving? No. The forces are **equal** and **opposite**, so they are
> **balanced**. The rope keeps doing what it was doing: staying still."

**Worked example 2 — the box, narrated:**

> Adult places the ball on the table. "I push it gently from the left
> toward the right." (Adult's other hand blocks from the right with an
> equal push.) "It does not move. My two pushes are **equal and opposite** —
> balanced forces. Now I remove this hand." (Ball rolls.) "Only one push
> remains. The forces are no longer balanced — and the motion changed."

Key idea to say plainly: **Balanced forces are equal in strength and
opposite in direction. They cancel out, so the object's motion does not
change.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Play the tug game twice: once with equal effort (a tie), once with the
   adult pulling clearly harder. After each, the learner names whether the
   forces were balanced and what the rope did. *(Key: tie = balanced, rope
   still; harder pull = not balanced, rope moves.)*
2. Adult shows four quick scenes; learner calls "balanced" or "not
   balanced" and gives the reason: (a) book sitting on a table; (b) two
   kids pushing a box from opposite sides equally; (c) one kid pushing a
   box alone; (d) tug-of-war where one team is winning. *(Key: a and b
   balanced — equal and opposite, no motion change; c and d not
   balanced.)*
3. **Error-analysis:** "The two teams are pulling equally, and the rope is
   moving." Learner explains why this cannot be right. *(Key: if the forces
   are truly equal and opposite, the rope cannot move — balanced forces
   mean no change in motion.)*

## Independent practice

4. Draw two force arrows on a ball: one pushing left, one pushing right,
   the same length. Write one sentence: "The forces are balanced because
   ___." *(Key: arrows equal length, opposite directions; "...because
   they are equal and opposite.")*
5. Complete the frame for three situations: *tug-of-war tie / book on a
   table / two equal pushes on a box* — "The forces are balanced. The
   object ___." *(Key: each ends with "stays still" / "does not move.")*
6. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "Your friend says the rope in a tied
   tug-of-war is not moving because nobody is pulling. Is your friend
   right? Explain." *(Key: No — both teams ARE pulling, equally and in
   opposite directions; the pulls cancel, so the rope stays still.)*

## Application

Balanced-force hunt: the learner finds two still objects in the room and
names the balancing forces for each (e.g., a lamp on a desk: gravity pulls
down, the desk holds it up). Record in the journal as labeled drawings.

## Exit check

Adult asks: "What two things must be true for forces to be balanced, and
what happens to the motion?" *(Answer: equal strength and opposite
direction; no change in motion.)*

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading/language access:** arrow-length cards — equal-length arrows
  mean "balanced"; learner may act scenes out or point instead of writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the tug scenes with toy figures and a
  string; move the string to show still vs. moving.
- **Extension:** The learner predicts what happens if a third friend joins
  one side of a tied tug-of-war — a preview of Lesson 03 (unbalanced).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "nothing is moving, so no forces are acting." Counter
with the book-on-table example: gravity is pulling down *right now*, and
the table is pushing back. Still does not mean force-free; it means
balanced. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
