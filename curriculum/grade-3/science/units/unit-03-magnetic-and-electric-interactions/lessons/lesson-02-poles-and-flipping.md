# Lesson 02 — Poles: Opposite Attract, Like Repel

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** two bar magnets with one end
of each marked with a dot sticker (the adult marks them before the
lesson), science journal, pencil, two small paper labels "N" and "S"
(optional, adult-written)

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn why magnets
sometimes snap together and sometimes push apart: every magnet has
two ends called **poles**, and which poles face each other decides
whether they **attract** or **repel**.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Yesterday one way round, two
magnets snapped together, and the other way round they pushed apart.
What do you think was different?" Any answer about the ends, the way
round, or flipping shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Every magnet has two ends, and scientists call each end a **pole**.
One end is the **north pole** and the other end is the **south pole**.
You can think of them as the magnet's two faces. The rule for poles is
short, and it explains everything you saw yesterday:

- **Opposite poles attract.** North facing south: pull together.
- **Like poles repel.** North facing north, or south facing south:
  push apart.

Say it as a rhyme if it helps: *opposites attract, likes repel.*
Flipping a magnet swaps which pole faces the other magnet, so flipping
**changes the direction** of the force — from a pull to a push, or
from a push to a pull. Nothing about the magnet changed except which
end is facing forward.

How do you know which end is which? On many classroom magnets the ends
are painted different colors or marked N and S. If your magnets are
not marked, the adult's dot sticker marks one end of each magnet
before the lesson — call the dotted end "the dot end" and the plain
end "the plain end" for today's tests, and the rule still works: dot
facing plain attracts, dot facing dot repels, plain facing plain
repels. When a compass is nearby, its needle is itself a tiny magnet:
the end that points north is its north-seeking end, which is why a
compass can find direction.

**Worked example 1 — predicting before testing:**

> Adult holds two magnets with the dotted ends facing each other and
> asks for a prediction first. "Dotted end facing dotted end — like
> poles. I predict **repel**." The adult brings them close: they push
> apart. "The prediction was right. Like poles repel." Then the adult
> flips one magnet so a dotted end faces a plain end. "Now opposite
> poles face. I predict **attract**." They snap together.

**Worked example 2 — flipping changes the direction:**

> Adult holds two magnets so they attract, then
> slowly flips one magnet end over end while the learner watches the
> pull weaken, stop, and turn into a push. Adult narrates: "I changed
> nothing but the direction the magnet faces. The force changed from
> a pull to a push. **Orientation changes the direction of the
> magnetic force.** That is a cause-and-effect relationship: the
> cause is flipping the magnet; the effect is the force changing
> direction."

Key idea to say plainly: **Opposite poles attract; like poles repel.
Flipping a magnet changes which pole faces forward, and so changes
the force from a pull to a push or back.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Adult sets four pole pairs (dot–dot, plain–plain, dot–plain,
   plain–dot). For each, the learner predicts attract or repel
   **before** testing, then tests and records whether the prediction
   was right.
2. Adult holds two magnets attracting. The learner tells the adult
   exactly what to flip so they will repel, then watches it happen.
3. **Error-analysis:** Adult says, "These two magnets repel, so they
   must be broken." Learner explains the error.

## Independent practice

4. Complete the pole-pair table in the journal: N–N, S–S, N–S, S–N —
   write "attract" or "repel" for each, then check each one with the
   real magnets (using the dot/plain code if the magnets are
   unmarked).
5. Draw two magnets in two panels: panel 1, ends facing that attract,
   with arrows pulling together; panel 2, ends facing that repel, with
   arrows pushing apart. Label each panel.
6. A friend says, "My two magnets used to stick together, but I
   flipped one of them and now they push apart." Write or dictate
   what you would tell the friend, in two sentences.
7. Answer: "If two magnets push apart, what is one thing you can do
   to make them pull together? Why does it work?"

## Application

Magnet-pair puzzle: the adult places two bar magnets end to end so
they repel and will not meet. The learner's job is to flip exactly
one magnet so the pair snaps together, then explain the rule used
(like poles repelled; flipping made opposite poles face, so now they
attract).

## Exit check

Adult holds two magnets end to end, ends facing, without letting them
touch. Learner says "attract" or "repel" and names the rule. Then the
adult flips one magnet and the learner says what changed and what the
new result will be.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** use only the dot/plain code and two pairs at a time;
  the adult holds the magnets while the learner points and predicts.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort picture cards of pole pairs into
  "attract" and "repel" piles instead of writing the table.
- **Extension:** bring the compass near each end of a bar magnet, one
  end at a time. Which way does the needle swing for each end? Record
  it — the compass needle is a magnet too, so it is pushed and pulled
  by the bar magnet's poles.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "the red end always attracts and the blue end
always repels." Color is a paint choice, not a pole: what matters is
which pole faces which. If the set's colors differ from the book's
examples, teach from the marks and the tests, not the colors. Another
common slip: thinking a magnet has only one pole that "does the
pulling." Both ends act; the pair decides. See
[answer key](../answer-key.md) for Lesson 02 solutions.
