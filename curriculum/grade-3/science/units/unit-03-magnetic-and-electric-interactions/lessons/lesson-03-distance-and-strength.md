# Lesson 03 — Distance and Strength: Closer Is Stronger, More Magnets Are Stronger

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** two bar magnets, steel paper
clips (at least 30), ruler with centimeters, a strip of cardboard to
mark distances, science journal, pencil

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will test two
cause-and-effect questions: does a magnet pull harder when it is
closer, and does it pull harder when you use two magnets instead of
one? You will run a fair test for each, with one thing changed at a
time.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Yesterday flipping a magnet changed
the direction of the force. Today we ask about strength. If a magnet
is far away from a paper clip, will it pull harder or weaker than when
it is close?" Any reasoned guess shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A magnetic force gets **weaker with distance**. Close up, the pull is
strong. Far away, the pull is weak — and far enough away, there is no
pull you can notice at all. That is a cause-and-effect relationship:
**cause** — the distance gets bigger; **effect** — the pull gets
weaker. Say it plainly: *closer is stronger, farther is weaker.*

A second cause-and-effect relationship: **more magnets, stronger
pull**. Stack two magnets together and the pair pulls harder than one
magnet alone. It holds a longer chain of paper clips, and it can start
pulling a paper clip from farther away.

Both questions need a **fair test**: change one thing, keep the rest
the same, and repeat three times. In the distance test, the one thing
changed is the distance; the magnet, the paper clip, and the table stay
the same. In the magnet-number test, the one thing changed is the
number of magnets; the distance, the paper clip, and the table stay the
same. If you change two things at once, you cannot tell which one
caused the result.

**Worked example 1 — the distance test (practice data):**

> A learner slides a magnet along a ruler toward a paper clip lying
> on the table and notes the distance at which the clip first starts
> to slide. Trial results: 4 cm, 4 cm, and 5 cm. Adult narrates: "The
> clip started moving at about 4 cm, three times in a row. Now slide
> the magnet from 10 cm away instead. The clip does not move at all.
> The distance changed, the pull changed: closer pulled, farther did
> not. That is evidence for the rule *closer is stronger.* These are
> fictional practice numbers — your own trials are the real evidence
> in the guided practice below."

**Worked example 2 — one magnet versus two (practice data):**

> A learner hangs a chain of paper clips from one magnet: the chain
> holds 6 clips before the last one drops. Then the learner stacks a
> second magnet on top of the first and tries again: the chain now
> holds 11 clips. Adult narrates: "Only the number of magnets changed.
> 11 clips is 5 more than 6 clips, because 11 − 6 = 5. Two magnets
> pull stronger than one. And notice: we did not also change the clips
> or the table — that is what makes it a fair test. These are fictional
> practice numbers; test your own magnets and compare."

Key idea to say plainly: **Closer is stronger; farther is weaker. Two
magnets pull stronger than one. A fair test changes one thing at a
time.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. **Distance test (real trials):** Learner places a paper clip on
   the table, slides one magnet toward it along the ruler, and records
   the distance where the clip first moves, three times. Adult checks
   the ruler is read from zero.
2. **Magnet-number test (real trials):** Learner counts the longest
   paper-clip chain one magnet holds, then repeats with two magnets
   stacked, three tries each. Learner records both counts and the
   difference.
3. **Error-analysis:** Adult says, "I tested one magnet at 2 cm and
   two magnets at 8 cm, and the two magnets won, so two magnets are
   always stronger." Learner explains the error.

## Independent practice

4. Use the practice data in Worked example 1: the clip moved at 4 cm,
   4 cm, and 5 cm. What distance would you report, and why is three
   trials better than one?
5. Use the practice data in Worked example 2: one magnet holds 6
   clips, two hold 11. How many more clips do two magnets hold? Show
   the subtraction.
6. Write two cause-and-effect questions of your own about magnets,
   each in the form "If I change ___, what happens to ___?" One must
   be about distance and one about magnet number.
7. A magnet pulls a clip from 3 cm away. Predict: will it pull the
   same clip from 9 cm away? Explain with the distance rule.
8. Order these pulls from strongest to weakest: a magnet 1 cm from a
   clip, a magnet 6 cm from a clip, two magnets 1 cm from a clip.

## Application

Through-a-barrier test: the learner predicts whether a magnet can
attract a paper clip through a piece of paper, then through cardboard,
then through a thin book, testing each in turn. The question to
answer: does the barrier change the distance rule, or does the magnet
still pull — just weaker? Record the result for each barrier.

## Exit check

Learner answers without the magnets in hand: "A clip starts moving
when the magnet is 4 cm away. You move the magnet to 12 cm away.
Stronger or weaker — and will the clip move?" Then the learner tests
it to confirm.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** the adult slides the magnet while the learner calls
  out "moved!" and reads the ruler; chain counts may be recorded as
  drawings of clips instead of numbers.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer by pointing to "closer" or
  "farther" cards, or by ordering three distance cards from strongest
  to weakest pull.
- **Extension (adult-only demonstration):** an electromagnet — a nail
  wrapped in insulated wire, connected briefly to one AA battery by
  the adult — picks up paper clips while connected and drops them when
  disconnected. Adult rules: adult assembles and holds everything, 30
  seconds at most, stop at once if anything feels warm, never a wall
  outlet. Learner question: how is this like two magnets, and how is
  it different? (Answer frame: it can be switched on and off.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "a bigger magnet is always stronger." Size
helps sometimes, but this unit's tested rules are distance and magnet
number — a small magnet close up can out-pull a big magnet far away.
Keep force comparisons qualitative: "stronger/weaker," never invented
force numbers. See [answer key](../answer-key.md) for Lesson 03
solutions.
