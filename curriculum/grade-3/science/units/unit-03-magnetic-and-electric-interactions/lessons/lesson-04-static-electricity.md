# Lesson 04 — Static Electricity: The Balloon That Pulls Without Touching

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** balloons (adult-inflated;
check for latex allergy first), a wool sweater or fleece cloth, small
torn paper bits, science journal, pencil; no-balloon alternative: a
plastic comb and the same cloth

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will meet a second
noncontact force: **static electricity**. A rubbed balloon can lift
hair and pick up paper without touching them — and you will test what
makes that pull stronger or weaker.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Magnets pull without touching. Do
you think anything else can pull without touching? What have you seen
stick to a wall or jump toward something?" Any example (balloon on a
wall, hair standing up, clothes clinging) shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Rub a balloon on a sweater, again and again. Now the balloon is
**charged** — it carries **static electricity**. Hold the charged
balloon near your hair, not touching it: strands of hair lift toward
the balloon. Hold it near tiny bits of paper: the bits jump up to the
balloon. Nothing touched them first. Static electricity is a
**noncontact force**, just like magnetism — but it is not magnetism.
No magnet is involved, and it works on hair and paper, which magnets
do not attract at all.

What causes it? Rubbing moves tiny electric charges onto the balloon.
Opposite charges attract, the way opposite magnet poles attract: the
charged balloon pulls the light objects toward it. The pull is a
**pull**, never a push you can feel in this lesson, and it is weak:
it can lift hair and paper bits, not books.

What changes the effect? That is today's cause-and-effect question.
Rub more times, and the pull usually gets stronger — more charge, more
pull. Let the balloon touch a wall or your hand, and the charge drains
away: the pull gets weaker or stops. Wet or damp air also weakens it.
So: **more rubbing, stronger pull; charge drained away, weaker pull.**
Test it, do not just believe it — that is the fair-test habit from
Unit 01, used again.

Safety in this lesson: the adult inflates the balloon and keeps it
away from faces. If anyone in the house has a latex allergy, skip
balloons entirely and use the comb alternative: rub a plastic comb on
wool and hold it near paper bits — the same effect, no balloon.

**Worked example 1 — the balloon and the paper:**

> Adult rubs a balloon on a sweater 10 times, holds it just above
> torn paper bits, and the bits jump up and stick. Adult narrates:
> "The balloon is **charged**. The force is a **pull**, and it worked
> **without touching**. The paper bits are light, so this weak force
> can lift them. A book would be far too heavy — the force would not
> move it." Then the adult presses the balloon against a wall and
> tries again: far fewer bits jump. "Touching the wall drained some
> charge away. The pull got **weaker**. Cause: charge drained away.
> Effect: weaker pull."

**Worked example 2 — the balloon and the hair:**

> Adult rubs the balloon again, 20 times this time, and holds it near
> the learner's hair (with permission, not touching the head). Strands
> lift toward the balloon. Adult narrates: "More rubbing, more charge,
> stronger pull — more hair lifts than after 10 rubs. Compare: after 10
> rubs, a few bits of paper jumped; after 20 rubs, more hair lifted.
> The number of rubs changed, the strength changed. That is a
> cause-and-effect relationship, tested fairly because the balloon,
> the sweater, and the distance stayed the same."

Key idea to say plainly: **Rubbing charges a balloon with static
electricity. The charged balloon pulls light objects without touching
them. More rubbing makes a stronger pull; draining the charge makes a
weaker pull.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Adult rubs the balloon (or comb) 10 times; learner holds it near
   paper bits and counts how many jump, three tries.
2. Adult rubs 20 times; learner repeats the count and compares: which
   count is bigger, and what changed?
3. **Error-analysis:** Adult says, "The balloon is a magnet, so it
   attracts the paper." Learner explains the error.

## Independent practice

4. Draw two panels: balloon after 10 rubs (a few paper bits lifted)
   and balloon after 20 rubs (more bits lifted). Label what changed
   and what happened.
5. Write or dictate a cause-and-effect sentence: "When I rubbed the
   balloon ___ times, ___ happened, because ___."
6. Predict and then test: will the charged balloon lift a paper clip?
   A pencil? Explain each result with "heavy/light" and "charged."
7. Answer: "A charged balloon sticks to a wall. Is the wall magnetic?
   How do you know the force is static electricity and not magnetism?"

## Application

Static hunt: the learner, with the adult, finds one more static
example at home (clothes clinging from the dryer, hair lifting after
removing a hat, a comb picking up paper bits). For each, name the
cause (rubbing) and the effect (pull without touching), and say
whether a magnet would do the same thing.

## Exit check

Adult asks, without the balloon in hand: "You rub a balloon 5 times
and it lifts 2 paper bits. You rub it 25 times. More bits, fewer bits,
or the same? Why?" Learner answers, then tests to confirm.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** the adult does all rubbing and holding; the learner
  counts and points. Paper-bit counts may be recorded as tallies.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw the result instead of counting;
  answer "more" or "fewer" by pointing to cards.
- **Extension:** test whether the charged balloon still works after
  touching it to a metal object, a wooden table, and a wall, one at a
  time. Which drains the charge fastest? Record and explain in one
  sentence each. (Adult note: results vary with humidity — accept the
  observed ordering, not a textbook ordering.)

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "static electricity is magnetism" and "the
balloon is sticky." Neither is right: there is no glue and no magnet
— rubbing moved charges, and opposite charges attract. Also correct
the idea that static pulls everything: it only moves very light
objects. This unit stays with **static electricity only**: no
batteries, circuits, or wall outlets in learner work. See
[answer key](../answer-key.md) for Lesson 04 solutions.
