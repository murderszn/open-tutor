# Lesson 01 — Magnets Attract and Repel

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** two bar magnets, steel paper
clips, a plastic spoon, a wooden block, a rubber eraser, an
aluminum-foil square, a coin, a crayon, a cotton ball, science journal,
pencil

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will test which objects a
magnet pulls toward it, and you will learn two new force words:
**attract** (pull toward) and **repel** (push away). You will find out
that a magnet can push or pull **without touching**.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "In Unit 02 you learned that every
push and pull is a force. Hold a magnet near a paper clip — but do
not let them touch yet. What do you think will happen when the magnet
gets close?" Any prediction about pulling, sticking, or moving shows
readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

A **magnet** is an object that pulls some materials toward it. When a
magnet pulls something toward it, scientists say the magnet
**attracts** it. **Attract** means *pull toward*. When a magnet
pushes something away, scientists say the magnet **repels** it.
**Repel** means *push away*. Attract and repel are the two things a
magnetic force can do.

Here is the surprising part: a magnetic force works **without
touching**. Hold a magnet close to a paper clip — not touching — and
the clip jumps to the magnet. The force crossed the little gap of air.
A force that acts without touching is called a **noncontact force**.
Gravity is another noncontact force you already know: it pulls things
down without touching them first.

Not everything is attracted to a magnet. Objects a magnet pulls are
called **magnetic**. Steel paper clips are magnetic. Objects a magnet
does not pull are called **nonmagnetic**. A plastic spoon and a
wooden block are nonmagnetic. You cannot tell by looking, and you
cannot tell by weight alone — you have to **test** it. That is the
rule for this lesson: no guessing, only testing.

**Worked example 1 — testing one object:**

> Adult holds a magnet near a steel paper clip, keeping a small gap.
> The clip jumps to the magnet. Adult narrates: "The magnet
> **attracted** the paper clip. The force was a **pull**, and it
> worked **without touching**. So the paper clip is **magnetic**."
> Then the adult holds the same magnet near a plastic spoon. Nothing
> happens. "The magnet did **not** attract the spoon. The spoon is
> **nonmagnetic**."

**Worked example 2 — attract or repel between two magnets:**

> Adult holds two bar magnets end to end. One way round, they snap
> together with a click. "These ends **attract** — they pull toward
> each other." The adult turns one magnet around. Now the magnets push
> apart and will not meet. "Now these ends **repel** — they push away
> from each other. Same magnets, different ends facing, different
> result. We will learn why in Lesson 02."

Key idea to say plainly: **A magnet attracts some objects and does
not attract others. Its force can pull or push without touching.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Adult lays out the eight test objects. The learner tests each one
   with the magnet and sorts them into magnetic and nonmagnetic piles,
   saying "attract" or "not attracted" for each.
2. Adult holds a magnet near a paper clip through a thin piece of
   cardboard. The learner watches the clip move and explains whether
   the force needed contact.
3. **Error-analysis:** Adult says, "This wooden block is heavy, so
   the magnet will attract it." Learner explains the error.

## Independent practice

4. Draw the sort in the journal: two labeled boxes, "Magnetic" and
   "Nonmagnetic," with each tested object drawn or named in the right
   box.
5. Write or dictate two sentences: one about an object the magnet
   attracted, one about an object it did not, each using the word
   **attract** correctly.
6. Find three more objects at home. Predict magnetic or nonmagnetic
   for each, then test. Record which predictions were right.
7. Answer in a full sentence: "Can a magnet pull something it is not
   touching? How do you know?"

## Application

Magnet hunt: walk around one room with the adult and find three
places magnets are already at work (for example, a fridge door, a
cabinet latch, a magnetic toy). For each, tell the adult whether the
magnet is attracting something, repelling something, or holding
something still, and what would happen if the magnet were removed.

## Exit check

Adult holds up a paper clip and a crayon. Learner says which one the
magnet will attract and what the force is called when it pulls
something toward it — without testing first, then tests to confirm.

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** fewer objects (four instead of eight); the adult reads
  labels aloud; sorting by placing objects in piles instead of
  writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the pile, draw the objects,
  or act out attract (arms pulling in) and repel (arms pushing out).
- **Extension:** test the same objects through paper, cardboard, and
  a thin book. Does the magnet still attract through them? Record
  what changes and what does not.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "all metals are magnetic" and "heavy things are
magnetic." Both fail on the coin and the foil square in this sort
(most coins and aluminum foil are nonmagnetic — if the household coin
happens to be steel, say so and swap in a brass key or a copper coin
instead; the point is the test, not the object). See
[answer key](../answer-key.md) for Lesson 01 solutions and the
misconception notes.
