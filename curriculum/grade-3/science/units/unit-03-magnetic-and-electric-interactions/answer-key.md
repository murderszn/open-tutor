**For the guiding adult only.** Every lesson item, practice item,

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. Magnetic: steel paper clip. Nonmagnetic: plastic spoon, wooden
   block, rubber eraser, aluminum-foil square, crayon, cotton ball.
   Coin (if tested): most coins are nonmagnetic; if the household
   coin is steel and is attracted, accept the tested result — the
   test decides, not the list.
2. No contact needed. The force crossed the cardboard gap: a
   noncontact force.
3. Error: weight does not decide magnetism. Correction: only testing
   decides — a heavy wooden block is nonmagnetic, and a light foil
   square is nonmagnetic too.

**Independent practice**

4. "Magnetic" box: paper clip (and coin, if steel and tested as
   attracted). "Nonmagnetic" box: all other tested objects.
5. Acceptable: "The magnet attracted the paper clip." / "The magnet
   did not attract the plastic spoon." The word **attract** must be
   used correctly in the first sentence.
6. Accept the learner's tested results for the three home objects;
   predictions that were wrong are still good science if the test
   was done.
7. Yes. The clip jumped to the magnet before touching it, so the
   force is a noncontact force.

**Application / exit check**

- Magnet hunt: accept any three observed household uses (fridge
  door, cabinet latch, magnetic toy, compass), each with attract,
  repel, or hold-still labeled and the removal consequence stated.
- Exit: the paper clip will be attracted; the force is called
  **attract** (a pull).

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice**

1. Dot–dot: repel. Plain–plain: repel. Dot–plain: attract.
   Plain–dot: attract.
2. Flip one magnet so opposite poles face: the pair will attract.
3. Error: the magnets are not broken. Correction: like poles repel —
   that is the normal rule, not damage.

**Independent practice**

4. N–N: repel. S–S: repel. N–S: attract. S–N: attract.
5. Panel 1 (attract): arrows pulling together, opposite poles
   facing. Panel 2 (repel): arrows pushing apart, like poles facing.
6. Acceptable: "Flipping one magnet changed which poles face each
   other, so now like poles face and they repel. Flip one magnet
   back and opposite poles will face, so they will attract again."
7. Flip one magnet. It works because flipping swaps which pole faces
   forward, turning like poles facing into opposite poles facing.

**Application / exit check**

- Puzzle: flip exactly one magnet; the pair snaps together because
  opposite poles now face.
- Exit: learner names attract or repel for the shown pair, then after
  the flip names the changed result and the rule.

**Extension note:** the compass needle is a tiny magnet, so each bar
magnet end pushes or pulls it; accept observed needle swings, not a
fixed direction, since sets differ.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. Distances where the clip first moves, three trials (learner's own
   numbers; ruler read from zero).
2. Chain counts, three tries each: one magnet, then two stacked;
   record both counts and the difference.
3. Error: two things changed at once (magnet number and distance),
   so the result cannot prove "two magnets are always stronger."
   Correction: test one variable at a time — same distance for both
   magnet numbers.

**Independent practice**

4. Report about 4 cm (the repeated value; 4, 4, 5). Three trials are
   better than one because they show a pattern and rule out a fluke
   or a misread.
5. 11 − 6 = 5. Two magnets hold 5 more clips.
6. Acceptable distance question: "If I change the distance, what
   happens to the pull?" Acceptable magnet-number question: "If I
   change the number of magnets, what happens to the pull?"
7. Probably not: the pull is weaker at 9 cm than at 3 cm (distance
   rule), so the clip will probably not move.
8. Strongest to weakest: two magnets 1 cm from a clip; one magnet
   1 cm from a clip; one magnet 6 cm from a clip.

**Application / exit check**

- Barrier test: accept the learner's tested results; the usual
  pattern is paper lets the most pull through, then cardboard, then
  a thin book — but the tested result, not the prediction, is the
  evidence.
- Exit: weaker at 12 cm; the clip probably will not move. Test to
  confirm.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**

1. Counts after 10 rubs, three tries (learner's own tallies).
2. Counts after 20 rubs are bigger: more rubbing, more charge,
   stronger pull.
3. Error: the balloon is not a magnet. Correction: rubbing gave the
   balloon a static-electricity charge; opposite charges attract, so
   the charged balloon pulls light objects without touching.

**Independent practice**

4. Panel 1 (10 rubs): a few bits lifted. Panel 2 (20 rubs): more bits
   lifted. Labels: what changed (rubs) and what happened (more bits).
5. Acceptable: "When I rubbed the balloon 20 times, more paper bits
   jumped, because more rubbing made a stronger charge/pull."
6. Paper clip: predict it will not lift — too heavy for the weak
   static pull. Pencil: predict it will not lift — too heavy, and
   not pulled by the weak charge. Accept tested results with
   heavy/light reasoning.
7. No, the wall is not magnetic. The force is static electricity,
   not magnetism: a magnet would not attract paper bits at all
   (paper is nonmagnetic), and the balloon has no magnet in it.

**Application / exit check**

- Static hunt: accept one observed example with cause (rubbing) and
  effect (pull without touching) named, plus "a magnet would not do
  this" for paper/hair cases.
- Exit: more bits — more rubbing, more charge, stronger pull. Test
  to confirm.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. Acceptable problem sentence: "Design a magnet latch that keeps
   this box shut when tipped, but lets an adult open it with one
   hand." It must name the job and the magnet tool.
2. Testable: "stays shut upside down," "opens with one hand."
   Untestable: "be nice" — it cannot be checked by a test.
3. Errors: "use magnets" states a material (a constraint), not a
   success criterion; "be good" cannot be tested; "be fast" needs a
   number (for example, "opens in under 5 seconds with one hand")
   before it can be tested.

**Independent practice**

4. Acceptable keeper definition: "Design a device that keeps two toy
   cars from touching when they roll toward each other, using
   magnets." Criteria (any three testable, for example): the cars
   never touch; the gap stays at least 2 cm; an adult can reset it
   with one hand. Constraints (any two, for example): at most two
   magnets; tape and cardboard only.
5. Latch sketch: one magnet on the lid, one on the box, opposite
   poles facing (attract).
6. Fair latch test: tip the box upside down twice; "pass" means the
   lid stays shut both times (and, for the opening criterion, an
   adult opens it with one hand both times).
7. Change one thing. Changing several at once hides which change
   caused the new result — the same one-variable rule as every fair
   test in this unit.

**Application / exit check**

- Keeper sketch: magnets positioned so like poles face each other
  (repel), with the gap between the cars labeled.
- Exit: criteria are what a good solution must do, and they must be
  testable so a test can pass or fail them; example: "the lid stays
  shut when the box is tipped upside down."

## Practice set C (Week 13)

1. Cause: more rubbing (20 rubs instead of 10). Effect: more paper
   bits lifted — a stronger pull.
2. (a) Paper bits jump to the balloon: light objects pulled by the
   static charge. (b) The steel paper clip does not move: too heavy
   for the weak static pull (a magnet, not the balloon, would pull
   the steel clip).
3. (a) Testable. (b) Not testable — rewrite, for example, as "the
   lid stays shut when the box is tipped upside down." (c) Testable.
4. Wrong: paper is nonmagnetic, so a magnet would not lift paper
   bits at all; the balloon lifts them because rubbing charged it
   with static electricity, and opposite charges attract.
5. Repel: like poles must face each other so the cars are pushed
   apart and kept from touching.
6. Fewer bits lift (or none): farther away, weaker pull — the
   distance idea applies to static pulls too.
7. The pull gets weaker or stops: touching the wall drained the
   charge away.
8. Sketch: one magnet on the lid, one on the box, opposite poles
   facing; one test per criterion (tip test, one-hand open test,
   materials check).

## Unit assessment

1. Magnetic: steel paper clip, iron nail. Nonmagnetic: plastic
   spoon, wooden block, rubber eraser, aluminum-foil square.
2. **Attract** = pull toward. **Repel** = push away.
3. N–N: repel. S–S: repel. N–S: attract. S–N: attract. (All four
   needed for the item.)
4. Flip one magnet: opposite poles then face, so the magnets
   attract.
5. Strongest to weakest: (b) two magnets 1 cm, (c) one magnet 1 cm,
   (a) one magnet 6 cm.
6. Report about 4 cm. Three trials show a pattern and rule out a
   fluke or misread.
7. 5 more clips (11 − 6 = 5). The one thing changed was the number
   of magnets.
8. Acceptable: "When I rubbed the balloon more times, it lifted more
   paper bits, because more rubbing made a stronger charge/pull."
9. (a) Paper bits jump to the balloon: light, pulled by the static
   charge. (b) The steel clip does not move: too heavy for the weak
   static pull; a magnet would pull the steel clip, the balloon
   does not.
10. (a) Stuck: the chain of steel paper clips on the red end of the
    left magnet. (b) Loose: three paper clips, the compass, the
    wooden block, the plastic spoon. (c) Magnetic force works
    without touching — a noncontact force.
11. Acceptable: problem sentence naming the latch job and magnets;
    two testable criteria (for example, stays shut upside down;
    opens with one hand); one constraint (for example, at most two
    magnets; tape only).
12. (a) False: aluminum foil is shiny and nonmagnetic — shininess
    does not decide; testing does. (b) False: distance matters — a
    small magnet close up can out-pull a big magnet far away, by
    the rule "closer is stronger."

## Project sample responses

- Defined problem (Option A): "Design a magnet latch that keeps this
  box shut when tipped, but lets an adult open it with one hand."
- Criteria: stays shut upside down; opens with one hand; uses only
  allowed materials. Constraints: at most two magnets; tape and
  string only.
- Sample test table: tip test — fail on hard tip before improvement
  (lid popped on the second hard tip), pass twice after moving the
  magnets closer to the lid's edge; one-hand open — pass twice
  before and after.
- Sample claim: "My latch works because it stayed shut on both
  upside-down tips and opened with one hand both times, after I
  moved the magnets to the lid's edge and retested."
