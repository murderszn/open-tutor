# Lesson 02 — Variation in Our Bean Plants

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** bean plants and growth log
from Unit 04, ruler with centimeters, science journal, pencil,
crayons, bar-graph paper or a blank table

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will measure your bean
plants again and find out how much they **vary** — how different
they are from each other. You will learn the words **variation**,
**range**, **most common**, and **fair-share height**.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Open your growth log. What was the
last height you wrote down, and how did you measure it — from where
to where?" Answers naming the soil line as the zero point and the
tallest tip as the reading show readiness. Re-teach the measurement
ritual from Unit 04 if the learner measures from the table instead.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Plants from the same kind of seed, grown in the same tray, do not all
grow to the same height. Some grow taller, some stay shorter, some
have more leaves, some have fewer. Those differences are called
**variation**. **Variation** means *differences among members of a
group of similar living things*. No two bean plants are exactly alike
— just like no two puppies are exactly alike.

To describe variation, scientists use three tools:

- The **range**: the smallest value and the largest value, and how
  far apart they are. If the shortest plant is 12 cm and the tallest
  is 17 cm, the range is 12 cm to 17 cm, and they are 5 cm apart
  (17 − 12 = 5).
- The **most common** value: the measurement that shows up most
  often. If two plants are 12 cm tall and no other height appears
  twice, then 12 cm is the most common height.
- The **fair-share height**: add up all the heights, then share the
  total out equally among the plants. If five plants measure 12, 15,
  14, 17, and 12 cm, the total is 70 cm, and 70 ÷ 5 = 14 cm. So the
  fair-share height is 14 cm. (Adults call this the mean. The learner
  only needs the fair-share idea: add, then share equally.)

**Worked example 1 — the Practice Tray (fictional practice data):**

> Adult shows this table, labeled **fictional — not our plants**:
>
> | Plant | Height (cm) | Leaves |
> |---|---|---|
> | Plant 1 | 12 | 6 |
> | Plant 2 | 15 | 8 |
> | Plant 3 | 14 | 7 |
> | Plant 4 | 17 | 9 |
> | Plant 5 | 12 | 6 |
>
> Adult thinks aloud: "Shortest height: 12 cm. Tallest height:
> 17 cm. Range: 12 to 17 cm, which is 17 − 12 = **5 cm** apart. Most
> common height: 12 cm — it appears twice, and no other height does.
> Fair-share height: 12 + 15 + 14 + 17 + 12 = **70** cm; 70 ÷ 5 =
> **14** cm. Leaves: fewest 6, most 9, most common 6 leaves — it
> appears twice. The plants vary: they are the same kind, grown the
> same way, and still different from each other."

**Worked example 2 — Practice Bean 1's growth pattern (fictional practice data):**

> Adult shows the Unit 04 practice log, labeled **fictional — not
> our plants**: "Practice Bean 1: Day 1 — 0 cm, 0 leaves. Day 7 —
> 3 cm, 2 leaves. Day 14 — 8 cm, 4 leaves. Day 21 — 13 cm, 6 leaves."
> Adult thinks aloud: "From Day 7 to Day 14 it grew 8 − 3 = **5 cm**.
> From Day 14 to Day 21 it grew 13 − 8 = **5 cm**. Leaves went up by
> 2 each week. The pattern is steady: 5 cm and 2 leaves every week.
> So on Day 28 I would predict 13 + 5 = **18 cm** and 6 + 2 =
> **8 leaves**. But a prediction is not a measurement — I would still
> have to measure the real plant to know."

Key idea to say plainly: **Plants of the same kind vary. Measure
them, and the range, the most common value, and the fair-share height
tell you how much.**

## Measuring activity

Measure every bean plant in the main tray now (Day 28 if planted in
Unit 04, Week 16 — adjust the day count to the learner's real log).
Use the Unit 04 ritual: ruler zero at the soil line, read to the
nearest centimeter at the tallest tip, count every leaf out loud,
draw and label, record date, height, and leaves at once. Then build
a bar graph: one bar per plant, height in centimeters, every bar
labeled with its number. If the learner's plants failed or the log is
missing, use the Practice Tray table above as the data and say so —
it is labeled fictional practice data, and it carries the same tasks.

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Adult and learner measure one plant together, then the learner
   measures the rest, with the adult checking the ruler zero each
   time.
2. Adult asks: "What is the shortest plant? The tallest? How many
   centimeters apart are they?" Learner reads the numbers from the
   table and does the subtraction out loud.
3. **Error-analysis:** Adult says, "Two plants are both 12 cm tall,
   so there is no variation in this tray." Learner explains the
   error: variation is about the whole group — the plants range from
   12 to 17 cm, so they do vary, even though two share one height.

## Independent practice

4. Complete the Day 28 log entry for every plant: date, height,
   leaves, labeled drawing.
5. Draw the bar graph of the heights. Title it, label the bars with
   their numbers, and write one sentence under it: "The plants vary
   from ___ cm to ___ cm."
6. Using the Practice Tray table: (a) What is the most common height?
   (b) What is the fair-share height? Show the addition and the
   division.
7. Answer in a full sentence: "What is variation, and what did our
   bean plants show about it?"

## Application

Compare the bean plants with the dog family from Lesson 01: the
puppies vary in fur color, ear shape, and chest patch; the bean
plants vary in height and leaf count. Write or dictate one sentence
that is true for both groups: "Living things of the same kind vary
because ___."

## Exit check

Adult asks: "Our shortest plant is 12 cm and our tallest is 17 cm.
What is the range, and how many centimeters apart are they?" Then:
"What does variation mean?" (Range 12 to 17 cm, 5 cm apart;
variation means differences among members of a group.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** fewer plants to measure (three instead of five); the
  adult holds the ruler's zero at the soil line while the learner
  reads; leaf counts shown by pointing and counting aloud; the
  fair-share division done with counters (70 counters shared into 5
  equal piles of 14).
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the tallest and shortest
  bars on the graph, draw the plants at their heights, or sort plant
  cards from shortest to tallest.
- **Extension:** measure the plants again in one week and compare the
  new range with today's range. Did the range grow, shrink, or stay
  the same? What does that tell you?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "variation means something is wrong with the
short plants" and "the fair-share height is the height of a real
plant." Both need correcting: variation is normal — same seeds, same
tray, different heights — and the fair-share height is a computed
summary, not a plant you can point to: it happens to match Plant 3's
14 cm in the Practice Tray, but it would still be 14 cm even if no
plant measured 14 cm. Say so plainly. If the
learner's real heights differ from the Practice Tray, use the real
numbers everywhere and treat the Practice Tray as the worked example
only. See [answer key](../answer-key.md) for Lesson 02 solutions and
the misconception notes.
