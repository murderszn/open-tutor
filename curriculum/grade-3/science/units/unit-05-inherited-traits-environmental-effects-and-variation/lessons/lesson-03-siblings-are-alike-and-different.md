# Lesson 03 — Siblings Are Alike and Different

**Time:** 25–30 minutes · **Materials:** cat-family table (below),
science journal, pencil, crayons

**Goal (read to the learner):** Today you will learn the word
**sibling** and find out why brothers and sisters from the same
parents can look different from each other — even though they share
the same mother and father.

**Readiness check:** Adult asks, "Yesterday you saw three puppies
with the same mother. Were they all the same? What was different
about them?" Any answer naming one difference (fur color, ear shape,
chest patch) shows readiness.

## Teaching (adult reads and models)

Offspring of the same parents are called **siblings**. Puppies from
the same mother are siblings. Kittens from the same mother are
siblings. Bean plants grown from seeds of the same parent plant are
like siblings too.

Siblings share the same parents — but they do not inherit the same
mix. Each offspring gets some traits from one parent and some traits
from the other parent, and each one gets a different mix. That is why
siblings can look alike in some ways and different in others. It is
like shuffling a deck of cards and dealing them out: the same deck,
but every player gets a different hand.

A table helps you trace the mix. Write each parent's traits at the
top. Then, for each offspring, check which parent each trait came
from. A trait can only come from a parent who has it — or, if the
trait is a mix of the two parents, from both of them together. (In
this unit we only trace traits that clearly match one parent, and we
say "we cannot tell from this table" when a trait matches neither
parent — that is honest science.)

**Worked example 1 — the cat family:**

> Adult shows this table:
>
> | Cat | Fur color | Eye color |
> |---|---|---|
> | Mother cat | orange | green |
> | Father cat | gray | blue |
> | Kitten A | orange | blue |
> | Kitten B | gray | green |
> | Kitten C | orange | green |
>
> Adult thinks aloud: "Kitten A has orange fur — that came from the
> mother. Kitten A has blue eyes — that came from the father. So
> Kitten A is a mix: fur from the mother, eyes from the father.
> Kitten B has gray fur — from the father — and green eyes — from the
> mother. Kitten C has orange fur and green eyes — both from the
> mother. Three kittens, same parents, three different mixes. None of
> them looks exactly like either parent."

**Worked example 2 — bean-plant siblings:**

> Adult says: "Imagine one parent bean plant makes five seeds, and we
> plant all five in the same tray with the same water and light. At
> Day 28 the plants are 12, 15, 14, 17, and 12 cm tall — the Practice
> Tray from Lesson 02. They are siblings: same parent, same tray, same
> care. Yet they vary. The variation is not a mistake and not a
> different kind of plant. It is what siblings do: same parents,
> different mixes, different heights."

Key idea to say plainly: **Siblings share the same parents, but each
one inherits a different mix of traits — so siblings are alike in
some ways and different in others.**

## Guided practice (adult coaches)

1. Adult and learner trace Kitten B together: gray fur from the
   father, green eyes from the mother. Learner says the full sentence
   for Kitten B.
2. Adult asks: "Kitten C looks exactly like its mother — orange fur,
   green eyes. Does that mean Kitten C inherited everything from its
   mother and nothing from its father?" Learner explains: no — Kitten
   C still has a father, and it may carry traits we cannot see in
   this table; looking like one parent does not mean the other parent
   gave nothing.
3. **Error-analysis:** Adult says, "Siblings have the same parents,
   so they must all look the same." Learner explains the error using
   the cat table: same parents, three different mixes.

## Independent practice

4. Complete the cat-family table in the journal: for each kitten,
   write which parent each trait came from.
5. Draw Kitten A, B, and C as small cats, each labeled with fur color
   and eye color.
6. Write or dictate two sentences: one about how two siblings are
   alike, one about how they are different, each using the word
   **sibling**.
7. Answer in a full sentence: "Why can two puppies from the same
   mother have different fur colors?"

## Application

Sibling hunt: think of an animal family the learner knows (a pet's
litter, birds in a garden, the bean plants). Name the parents'
traits, then name one way two siblings are alike and one way they
are different. If no real family is at hand, invent a parent pair
(one trait each) and draw two siblings with different mixes,
labeling where each trait came from.

## Exit check

Adult shows the cat table and asks: "Kitten A has orange fur and blue
eyes. Which parent gave the fur color, and which gave the eye
color?" (Fur: mother. Eyes: father.)

## Supports and extensions

- **Support:** trace one trait at a time (fur first, then eyes); use
  colored pencils to match each kitten's fur to its parent's fur
  color; the adult reads the table aloud.
- **Alternate response mode:** point to the parent who gave each
  trait, draw lines from each kitten's trait to the matching parent,
  or sort kitten cards under "like mother," "like father," and "mix."
- **Extension:** add a fourth kitten with gray fur and blue eyes and
  trace both traits to the father — then explain why that kitten
  still has a mother, and what the table cannot show (traits we
  cannot see, such as the shape of its paws' pads).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "looking like one parent means the other parent
gave nothing" and "siblings should look the same because they have
the same parents." Both fail on the cat table. Also watch for
"the puppies got their traits from the mother only because the father
is not in the picture" (Lesson 01) — repeat that a missing father in
a picture is not a missing father in real life. Keep the language at
"traits passed from parents" — no genes, no DNA, no trait prediction
(outside the 3-LS3-1 assessment boundary). No human siblings are
used as examples. See [answer key](../answer-key.md) for Lesson 03
solutions and the misconception notes.
