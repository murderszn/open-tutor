# Lesson 06 — Multiply by Multiples of 10; Estimate to Check

**Session:** 12 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can multiply a one-digit
number by a multiple of 10 using tens-as-units thinking, and use a rounded
estimate to judge whether an exact answer is reasonable.

**Materials:** base-ten rods, paper and pencil.

## Readiness check (3 min)

Quick facts: 9 × 8 = ? (72.) 4 × 3 = ? (12.) 6 × 2 = ? (12.) Then: "12 tens is
what number?" (120.) If "tens as units" is shaky, count rods aloud: 10, 20,
…, 120.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

A multiple of 10 like 30 or 80 is just **some number of tens**: 30 is 3 tens,
80 is 8 tens. So multiplying by a multiple of 10 is multiplying **groups of
tens**:

- **4 × 30** = 4 groups of 3 tens = 12 tens = **120**.
- **9 × 80** = 9 groups of 8 tens = 72 tens = **720**.

Strategy: multiply the one-digit numbers first (4 × 3 = 12), then remember the
answer counts **tens**, so put the zero back on: 120. You are not "adding a
zero" — you are counting tens.

**Estimation** is rounding with a job: round each number, compute with the
easy numbers, and compare to the exact answer. If the exact answer and the
estimate are close, the exact answer is probably **reasonable**; if they are
far apart, something went wrong and you should re-check. Example: for
487 + 305, estimate 490 + 310 = 800. The exact sum is 792 — close to 800, so it
is reasonable.

## Worked example 1 — 4 × 30

1. Think of 30 as 3 tens: 4 × 30 = 4 groups of 3 tens.
2. 4 × 3 = 12 → 12 tens.
3. 12 tens = **120**.
4. Block check: 4 rows of 3 ten-rods is 12 rods, and 12 rods is 120. **4 × 30
   = 120.**

## Worked example 2 — 9 × 80

1. 80 is 8 tens: 9 × 80 = 9 groups of 8 tens.
2. 9 × 8 = 72 → 72 tens.
3. 72 tens = **720**. **9 × 80 = 720.**

## Worked example 3 — Estimate to check 487 + 305

1. Exact: 487 + 305 = 792. (Ones 7+5=12 write 2 carry 1; tens 8+0+1=9;
   hundreds 4+3=7.)
2. Estimate: round to the nearest ten → 490 + 310 = 800.
3. Compare: 792 and 800 are close (8 apart) → the exact answer is
   **reasonable**.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. **6 × 20** *(Hint: 6 groups of how many tens?)*
2. **5 × 60** *(Hint: 5 × 6 first, then count the tens.)*
3. **7 × 40** *(Hint: build it with rods if you want.)*
4. **358 + 194**: first estimate by rounding to the nearest ten, then find the
   exact sum, then judge: reasonable? *(Hint: estimate first, cover the exact
   work.)*

## Independent practice

5. **3 × 90**
6. **8 × 50**
7. **9 × 80**
8. **623 − 289**: estimate by rounding to the nearest ten, find the exact
   difference, judge reasonableness.
9. Error analysis: Tom says **4 × 70 = 2800**. What did Tom do wrong? What is
   the correct answer?

## Application

A pack holds **30** stickers. The club buys **6** packs for prizes. How many
stickers is that? Write the multiplication and solve.

## Exit check

1. **5 × 80 = ?**
2. Round **746** to the nearest 10 and to the nearest 100.

*Success criterion:* the product is correct with tens-as-units reasoning, and
both roundings are right. If the learner "adds a zero" mechanically without
naming the tens, ask: "28 of what?" until the unit (tens) is spoken.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** the adult says the tens version aloud ("nine groups of
  eight tens"); the learner supplies the one-digit fact and the final number.
- **Alternate response mode:** build each product with ten-rods on a place-
  value mat, then count the rods by tens.
- **Extension:** explain whether 4 × 70 and 7 × 40 give the same answer, using
  tens-as-units reasoning for both. (4 × 7 tens = 28 tens = 280; 7 × 4 tens =
  28 tens = 280 — same, and this is commutativity at work.)

## Teacher note

Two separate skills live in this lesson; learners can nail the ×10 products
and still estimate sloppily. Keep estimation honest: the estimate must be
written **before** the exact computation, or the "check" is just decoration.
