# Lesson 05 — Patterns in the Multiplication Table

**Session:** 10 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can find a
pattern in the multiplication table, predict the next value, and explain
the pattern using properties of operations.

**Materials:** blank 10 × 10 multiplication chart, crayons (two colors
for marking even products and 9s products), counters.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "4 × 5 = ?" (20.) "Is 20 even or odd?"
(Even — it can be split into two equal groups of 10.) "9 × 3 = ?" (27.)
"What do 2 + 7 make?" (9.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "List the first five multiples of 4." (4, 8, 12, 16, 20.)
"Even or odd, each one?" (All even.) "List the first three multiples of
9." (9, 18, 27.) If skip counting by 4s and 9s is shaky, chant the
multiples with counters in rows today before hunting for explanations.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

A **pattern** is something that keeps happening for a reason, not a
coincidence. In the multiplication table, three patterns are worth
knowing — and each has an explanation, not just a trick:

**Pattern 1 — 4 times a number is always even.** Why? 4 × n means 4
groups of n, which can be re-sorted into two equal addends: 4 × 7 =
14 + 14 = 28. Anything that splits into two equal whole-number parts
is even. So 4 × *anything* is even — even when the "anything" is odd:
4 × 7 = 28, 4 × 9 = 36. (This is the official example in 3.OA.D.9.)

**Pattern 2 — The 9s pattern.** The products 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54, 63,
72, 81, 90 have digits that add to 9 (1 + 8 = 9, 2 + 7 = 9, 5 + 4 = 9).
Why? Because 9 × n = 10 × n − n: 9 × 6 = 60 − 6 = 54. Each step up the
9s row adds 10 and takes away 1, so the tens digit climbs by 1 while
the ones digit falls by 1 — and their sum stays 9. (90 is the exception
that still fits: 9 + 0 = 9.)

**Pattern 3 — Doubling.** If you know the 4s, you know the 8s: 8 × n is
double 4 × n, because 8 groups are two sets of 4 groups. 4 × 8 = 32, so
8 × 8 = 64. Similarly, the table is symmetric: 6 × 7 = 7 × 6 = 42
(turn-around fact), so you only ever need to learn half the table.

Rules for pattern claims: a pattern must be checked on at least three
examples, and a good explanation says *why* it must always work — not
just "it worked these times." A pattern that fails on one example is
not a pattern; it is a coincidence. (Example of a false pattern: "all
multiples of 3 are odd" — 6 and 12 are multiples of 3 and even.)

## Worked example 1 — Why 4 × 7 is even

1. Compute: 4 × 7 = 28.
2. Split into two equal addends: 28 = 14 + 14. (Two groups of 14:
   take the 4 groups of 7 and pair them up — two pairs of 7s make
   14 + 14.)
3. Conclude: 28 splits evenly into two equal parts, so 28 is even.
4. Generalize: any 4 × n splits the same way (n + n, twice), so every
   4s product is even. Test: 4 × 9 = 36 = 18 + 18. ✓

## Worked example 2 — The 9s shortcut that explains itself

Find 9 × 6 two ways:

1. Skip count: 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54 — the sixth multiple is **54**.
2. Ten-minus-one: 10 × 6 = 60; 60 − 6 = **54**. Same answer, and this
   way shows *why* the digits add to 9: 60 − 6 borrows across the tens,
   leaving 5 in the tens place and 4 in the ones place, and 5 + 4 = 9.
3. Predict and check: 9 × 7 should have digits adding to 9 and equal
   70 − 7 = 63. Digits: 6 + 3 = 9. ✓

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Predict: is 4 × 8 even or odd? Compute it, then split it into two
   equal addends to prove your prediction.
   *(Hint: 4 × 8 = 2 × 8 + 2 × 8. What are the two equal addends?)*
2. Use the 9s shortcut: find 9 × 4 as 10 × 4 − 4. Check that the
   digits of your answer add to 9.
   *(Hint: 10 × 4 = 40. Take away one group of 4.)*
3. Doubling: if 4 × 6 = 24, what is 8 × 6? Explain with groups.
   *(Hint: 8 groups are two sets of 4 groups.)*
4. Error analysis: Jay claims "every multiple of 3 is odd, because
   3, 9, and 15 are odd." Give one counterexample and explain why
   Jay's pattern fails.
   *(Hint: check 3 × 2 and 3 × 4.)*

## Independent practice

5. Find 4 × 6 and 4 × 10. Are both even? Split each answer into two
   equal addends.
6. Find 9 × 5 and 9 × 8 with the ten-minus-one method. Do the digits
   of each answer add to 9?
7. Fill and explain: 3, 6, 9, 12, ___, ___. What is the pattern, and
   what is the next value after your two fills? Is every value even,
   odd, or mixed? Explain.
8. True or false, with an explanation: "If a number is even, then
   3 times that number is even." Test with 3 × 4 and 3 × 6, then
   explain with equal addends.
9. Challenge: 6 × 7 = 42. Use a break-apart to explain why 6 × 7 is
   even without computing 6 × 7 a second way. (Hint: 6 groups of 7
   can be re-sorted into two groups of 21.)

## Application

Pattern poster: choose one pattern from this lesson, show three
examples, write the explanation in your own words, and add one
prediction the pattern lets you make about a fact you have not computed
yet (for example, "4 × 12 must be even, and it must be double 2 × 12").
The adult checks the prediction by computing.

## Exit check

Find 4 × 8. Is the answer even? Explain with two equal addends, in one
sentence.

**Teacher note:** likely misconception — treating a pattern as a magic
trick ("9s digits always add to 9 because that's the rule") without the
why. Answers: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 05.
