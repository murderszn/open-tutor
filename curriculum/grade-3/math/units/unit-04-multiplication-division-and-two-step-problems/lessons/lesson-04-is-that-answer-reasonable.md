# Lesson 04 — Is That Answer Reasonable?

**Session:** 8 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can estimate a
two-step answer by rounding and mental computation, judge whether an
answer is reasonable, and find the faulty step when it is not.

**Materials:** counters, number-line strip 0–120, multiplication chart.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "Round 47 to the nearest 10." (50.)
"Round 6 × 7 to an easy estimate." (6 × 7 = 42; round the 7 to 10 for
6 × 10 = 60 — a rough, deliberately high estimate, or keep 42 exactly
since 6 × 7 is known.) "Why estimate at all if we can compute exactly?"
(To catch big mistakes before trusting an answer.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "Round 28 to the nearest 10." (30.) "Estimate 4 × 9." (About
40 — 4 × 10 = 40, and 4 × 9 is a little less.) "Estimate 36 + 28."
(About 70 — 40 + 30.) If rounding to the nearest 10 is shaky, revisit
Unit 01's rounding lessons with the number line before estimating
two-step answers today.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

**Reasonable** means "close enough to what I expected that I can trust
it." An estimate does not need to equal the answer — it needs to be in
the same neighborhood. If your estimate is about 60 and your answer is
57, that is reasonable. If your estimate is about 60 and your answer is
94, something went wrong — go back and check each step.

Three checking tools, in order:

1. **Estimate first (or right after).** Round numbers to friendly ones
   (nearest 10, or a known fact) and compute roughly. For 6 packs of 7
   plus 15: 6 × 7 is about 40, plus about 20, so expect about 60.
2. **Check with the inverse operation.** Multiplication undoes division
   and division undoes multiplication; addition undoes subtraction.
   If you found 72 ÷ 8 = 9, check 9 × 8 = 72. If you found 30 − 14 = 16,
   check 16 + 14 = 30.
3. **Re-do the faulty step, not the whole problem.** When an answer is
   unreasonable, test Step 1 alone, then Step 2 alone. Usually only one
   step is wrong — often the operation, not the arithmetic.

What "close" means: an estimate made by rounding each number to the
nearest 10 can easily be 10–15 away from the exact answer, and that is
fine. An answer that is *twice* the estimate, or in the wrong tens
(30s instead of 60s), is not fine. The adult should accept any
estimate the learner can defend ("I rounded 7 up to 10, so my estimate
is a bit high") over a memorized rule about exact gaps.

A note on order: in a combined equation with no parentheses,
multiplication and division come before addition and subtraction
(3.OA.D.8's order-of-operations note). That is why 5 + 3 × 4 = 17, not
32 — and why a wrong order is a common source of unreasonable answers.

## Worked example 1 — Spotting an impossible answer

Problem: A shop has 6 packs with 7 pencils in each pack, plus 15 loose
pencils. How many pencils in all? A learner answers 94. Reasonable?

1. Step 1: 6 × 7 = 42 pencils in packs.
2. Step 2: 42 + 15 = 57 pencils in all. (Correct answer: 57.)
3. Estimate check: 6 × 7 is about 40 (6 × 7 = 42 exactly, or round to
   6 × 10 = 60 at the roughest); 15 is about 20. Expect about 60.
4. Verdict: 94 is **not reasonable** — it is far above 60, in the
   wrong tens. The learner probably added before multiplying, or
   multiplied 6 × 7 as 79, or added 42 + 52. Re-do Step 1 (42 ✓),
   then Step 2 (42 + 15 = 57, not 94). The fault was in Step 2's
   addition.
5. Lesson: the estimate does not give the answer, but it tells you
   *where to look* for the mistake.

## Worked example 2 — Checking with the inverse

Problem: A teacher shares 72 stickers equally among 8 students, then
each student gets 3 more stickers. How many stickers does each student
have now?

1. Step 1: 72 ÷ 8 = 9 stickers each.
2. Step 2: 9 + 3 = 12 stickers each.
3. Estimate: 72 ÷ 8 is about 9 (8 × 9 = 72 exactly); 9 + 3 is about 12.
   Expect about 12. ✓
4. Inverse check, step by step: Step 1 undone — 9 × 8 = 72. ✓ Step 2
   undone — 12 − 3 = 9. ✓ Both steps check out, so **12** is trusted.
5. Answer: **12 stickers** per student.

## Guided practice (with hints)

For each, give an estimate, a verdict (reasonable / not reasonable),
and the corrected answer when needed.

1. Claim: 8 × 6 − 19 = 29. Estimate first, then judge.
   *(Hint: 8 × 6 is about 50; 19 is about 20. Expect about 30.)*
2. Claim: 5 × 9 + 12 = 67. Estimate first, then judge.
   *(Hint: 5 × 9 = 45 exactly; 12 is about 10. Expect about 55.)*
3. Claim: 63 ÷ 9 + 5 = 12. Check with the inverse, then judge.
   *(Hint: undo Step 2 first — 12 − 5 should give the Step 1 answer.)*
4. Error analysis: Ana solved "4 packs of 8 pencils, shared equally
   between 2 friends" as 4 × 8 = 32, then 32 ÷ 2 = 16, and wrote the
   answer as "16 packs per friend." What is wrong with Ana's *unit*,
   and what should the answer sentence say?
   *(Hint: the answer counts what each friend receives — pencils or
   packs? Step 1 counted pencils; Step 2 shares those pencils.)*

## Independent practice

5. Estimate, then solve exactly: 7 × 8 + 13. Is 69 reasonable? Show
   the estimate and the exact answer.
6. A learner claims 9 × 4 − 11 = 47. Give an estimate, a verdict, and
   the correct answer.
7. A learner claims 54 ÷ 6 × 2 = 18. Check each step (left to right:
   54 ÷ 6 = 9, then 9 × 2 = 18) and judge. Is the claim reasonable?
   Explain why the order of the two steps matters here.
8. Solve, then check both steps with inverse operations: A class has
   5 boxes with 6 balls in each box. Two balls are lost in total.
   How many balls are left? Show both inverse checks.
9. Explain in your own words: why is an estimate allowed to be
   different from the exact answer, and how different is "too different"?

## Application

Be the checker: the adult solves three two-step problems, making one
deliberate mistake in one of them (wrong operation, wrong order, or a
slipped fact). Your job is to estimate each answer, find the faulty
problem, name the faulty step, and correct it. Swap roles if time
allows.

## Exit check

A learner claims 6 × 5 + 18 = 58. Estimate, judge, and give the correct
answer with its check.

**Teacher note:** likely misconception — treating "close" as "any
number in the same hundred" or demanding the estimate equal the answer.
