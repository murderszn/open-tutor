# Lesson 02 — Letters for the Unknown

**Session:** 5 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can write a
two-step problem as equations with a letter for the unknown, find the
letter's value, and prove it by putting the value back in.

**Materials:** counters, multiplication chart, index cards (one letter
card: n, p, q).

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "8 × ? = 48" (6.) "5 = ? ÷ 3" (15, because
5 × 3 = 15.) "What does the ? stand for in each equation?" (The number
we do not know yet.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "Find the missing number: 6 × ? = 42." (7.) "Write 42 ÷ 6
using a letter instead of ?." (42 ÷ 6 = n, n = 7.) If finding a missing
factor is shaky, use the Unit 03 unknown-factor question — "what times
6 makes 42?" — before any two-step equations today.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

A **letter for the unknown** is a letter that stands for a number we
have not found yet. Mathematicians often use **n** (for "number"). The
letter is not the answer — it is a *placeholder* for the answer until we
find it.

For a two-step problem, write **one equation per step**, and let the
first step's answer become the second step's starting number:

- Step 1: a = 5 × 4 (a stands for the first answer)
- Step 2: n = a − 6 (n stands for the final answer)

Then solve top to bottom: a = 20, so n = 20 − 6 = 14.

Rules that keep letters honest:

1. **One letter, one meaning, inside one problem.** If n means "markers
   now," it cannot also mean "new markers" in the same problem. Use a
   second letter (a, p, q) for the middle answer.
2. **Prove it by putting the value back.** If you claim n = 14, check:
   does 14 make every equation true? 5 × 4 = 20, 20 − 6 = 14. ✓
3. **Multiplication before addition or subtraction** when there are no
   parentheses to say otherwise. In n = 9 + 4 × 6, multiply first:
   4 × 6 = 24, then 9 + 24 = 33. Writing (9 + 4) × 6 would mean something
   different — 13 × 6 = 78 — so parentheses change the story. In this
   unit we mostly write stepwise equations, where the order is obvious,
   and use one combined equation only as a challenge.

## Worked example 1 — Two equations, one story

A class has 5 bags with 4 oranges in each bag. The class eats 6
oranges. How many oranges are left? Let a be the starting oranges and
n be the oranges left.

1. Step 1 equation: a = 5 × 4 = **20 oranges** to start.
2. Step 2 equation: n = a − 6 = 20 − 6 = **14 oranges** left.
3. Prove it: put a = 20 back into Step 2: 20 − 6 = 14. ✓ Put 14 back
   into the story: start with 20, eat 6, have 14. ✓
4. Answer: **14 oranges** are left.

## Worked example 2 — The unknown in the middle

Solve 48 = 6 × n.

1. Read it as a question: 6 times what number makes 48?
2. Use the unknown-factor strategy from Unit 03: 6 × 8 = 48, so
   **n = 8**.
3. Prove it by substitution: 6 × 8 = 48. ✓ Also by division: 48 ÷ 6 = 8. ✓
4. Notice the letter can sit anywhere — at the end (6 × n = 48), at
   the start (n = 48 ÷ 6), or in the middle (48 = 6 × n). The meaning
   does not change: n is the number that makes the equation true.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Find the unknown: 7 × ? = 42. Write the equation with n, then solve.
   *(Hint: what times 7 makes 42? Check with the multiplication chart.)*
2. Find the unknown: ? ÷ 5 = 9. Write it with n, then solve.
   *(Hint: undo division with multiplication — 9 × 5 = ?)*
3. Two steps, two equations: a shop makes 3 trays of 7 muffins, then
   sells 8 muffins. Let p be the muffins made and n the muffins left.
   Write both equations and solve.
   *(Hint: Step 1 is p = 3 × 7. Step 2 uses p.)*
4. Error analysis: Sara computed n = 5 + 3 × 4 as (5 + 3) × 4 = 32.
   What rule did Sara break, and what is n really?
   *(Hint: with no parentheses, which operation goes first?)*

## Independent practice

5. Find n: 9 × n = 63.
6. Find n: n − 14 = 25. Prove it by putting your value back in.
7. Two steps: q = 6 × 5; n = q ÷ 5. Find q, then n.
8. Story to equations: A teacher buys 4 packs of 8 pencils, then gives
   11 pencils away. Let p be the pencils bought and n the pencils left.
   Write both equations and solve.
9. Write letter equations for this story, then solve: A class has
   6 tables with 5 chairs at each table. Four chairs are broken. How
   many good chairs are there? Let c be all chairs and n be good chairs.

## Application

Swap equations with the adult: you write a two-step problem as two
equations (with a letter), the adult solves only from your equations —
no story allowed. If the adult cannot solve it, your equations are
missing a step. Fix them together.

## Exit check

Find n: 8 × n = 56. Show the multiplication fact and the division check.
