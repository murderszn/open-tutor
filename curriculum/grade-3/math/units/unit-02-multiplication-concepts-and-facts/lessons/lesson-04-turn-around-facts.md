# Lesson 04 — Turn-Around Facts

**Session:** 9 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can use the turnaround
(commutative) property to find a product from its partner fact, and explain
why the two facts have the same product.

**Materials:** paper and pencil, counters, 10 × 10 multiplication chart.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "Write the equation for 6 groups of 3." (6 × 3
= 18.) "Round 64 to the nearest 10." (60.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask: "Build 3 × 4 with counters as 3 groups of 4. Now build 4 × 3 as 4 groups
of 3. Count both." (Both are 12.) If the learner resists — "but they look
different!" — that resistance is the lesson. Build both, count both, and let
the counters settle it before any explanation.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

Turn an array sideways and something wonderful happens: the **count does not
change**. A 3-by-4 array (3 rows of 4) turned sideways becomes a 4-by-3 array
(4 rows of 3) — but it is still the same 12 dots, just viewed from the other
side.

This is the **commutative property of multiplication**: the order of the
factors does not change the product. Grown-ups call it "commutative"; we call
it **turn-around facts**. If you know 6 × 4 = 24, you automatically know
4 × 6 = 24. Every hard fact has an easier partner:

- 9 × 3 is hard? Turn it around: 3 × 9 — count by 3s nine times, or think of
  the 3s you already know.
- 7 × 6 is hard? Turn it around: 6 × 7.

Learning turnarounds **cuts the facts you must memorize nearly in half**: on
the 10 × 10 chart, everything below the diagonal is a mirror of everything
above it. (The adult may name "commutative property" — the standard says
students need not use the formal term, so the learner's word is "turnaround.")

## Worked example 1 — 3 × 7 and 7 × 3

1. Draw a 3 × 7 array: 3 rows, 7 dots each. Product: skip count "7, 14, 21."
   **3 × 7 = 21.**
2. Rotate the paper a quarter turn. Now read it: 7 rows of 3. Count the dots
   again — still 21 dots. Nobody added or removed a dot.
3. Equation pair: **3 × 7 = 21** and **7 × 3 = 21.** Same dots, same product.
4. The story changed (3 groups of 7 vs. 7 groups of 3) but the total did not.
   That is the whole property: turn it around, keep the product.
5. Common slip: thinking this works for subtraction too ("7 − 3 = 3 − 7"?).
   No — turnarounds are a **multiplication** (and addition) privilege. 7 − 3 =
   4 but 3 − 7 is not 4.

## Worked example 2 — Using a known fact

*You know 6 × 8 = 48. What is 8 × 6?*

1. Do not rebuild. The turnaround rule says: same factors, flipped order,
   same product.
2. **8 × 6 = 48.** Done — one known fact just earned you a second fact free.
3. Prove it once with an array (6 rows of 8, rotated to 8 rows of 6, count 48
   dots), then trust the rule forever.
4. Memory strategy: when a fact feels hard, ask "do I know its turnaround?"
   8 × 6 feels long; 6 × 8 = 48 is sitting right there.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner draws or
writes.

1. Draw a **2 × 9** array. Write the equation. Then rotate the paper and write
   the turnaround equation.
   *(Hint: the second equation starts with 9.)*
2. You know **5 × 7 = 35**. What is **7 × 5**? Explain without rebuilding.
   *(Hint: same dots, turned sideways.)*
3. Find **4 × 8** by turning it around to a fact you know.
   *(Hint: 8 × 4 = 32 — doubles of 4s, or 4 + 4 + ... eight times.)*
4. On the 10 × 10 chart, find **6 × 9** and **9 × 6**. What do you notice?
   *(Hint: they sit in mirror positions across the diagonal.)*
5. Error analysis: Zoe says **"9 − 4 = 4 − 9 because of turnarounds."**
   Is she right? Show with numbers.
   *(Hint: 9 − 4 = 5. What is 4 − 9? It is not 5.)*

## Independent practice

6. Write the turnaround fact for **3 × 8 = 24**. Draw both arrays.
7. You know **7 × 7 = 49**. What is the turnaround? (Trick question —
   explain.)
8. Complete: 6 × __ = 42 and __ × 6 = 42. (Same missing number — why?)
9. Which is easier to solve: **9 × 4** or **4 × 9**? Solve both and explain
   your pick.
10. List three fact pairs (a × b and b × a) that both equal 18.
11. Error analysis: Max wrote **5 × 6 = 30** and **6 × 5 = 35**. What went
    wrong, and which turnaround rule did he forget?

## Application

The Array Market manager says: "I need 8 rows of 3 apple trays for the front,
and the back display needs 3 rows of 8." Her helper worries these are two
different orders. Write both equations, find both totals, and write the
manager a one-sentence note explaining whether the helper should worry.

## Exit check

1. Write two turnaround equations for the fact **4 × 7 = 28**.
2. You know **8 × 3 = 24**. What is **3 × 8**? Explain in one sentence why you
   do not need to rebuild the array.

*Success criterion:* learner states the same-product reason (same dots, turned)
rather than "the teacher said so." If they apply turnarounds to subtraction,
re-teach with the 9 − 4 counterexample before Session 10.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** "turn it around" said while physically rotating the
  paper — the gesture carries the meaning before the words do.
- **Alternate response mode:** build one array with counters, rotate the tray,
  count again, and answer orally.
- **Extension:** on the 10 × 10 chart, shade every square fact (1 × 1, 2 × 2,
  … 10 × 10). What pattern do the shaded squares make? Why are square numbers
  their own turnarounds? (49 dots in a square — rotating a square changes
  nothing.)

## Teacher note

Two things to protect: (1) learners must **prove it once, then trust it** —
endless rebuilding defeats the purpose of a shortcut; (2) the
subtraction-trap must be addressed head-on now, or it resurfaces in Unit 04.
Note the standard's footnote: formal terms are optional — "turnaround" is a
complete and correct working vocabulary for grade 3. Answer key:
