# Lesson 02 — Arrays: Rows and Columns

**Session:** 5 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can draw an array in rows
and columns, label the rows and columns, and write the matching multiplication
equation.

**Materials:** paper and pencil, counters, the generated illustration
[`../assets/array-market-stand.png`](../assets/array-market-stand.png).

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "Show 3 groups of 4 with counters. Write the
equation." (3 × 4 = 12.) "Round 52 to the nearest 10." (50.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask: "Draw a rectangle. Put 2 rows of 3 dots inside it." Check that the rows
go **across** (like rows of seats in a classroom) and the columns go **down**.
If rows and columns are mixed up, practice pointing: sweep your finger across
for a row, down for a column, three times before the lesson.

![An AI-generated illustration of a market stall with trays of fruit arranged in neat rows and columns](../assets/array-market-stand.png)
*Caption: An AI-generated illustration of the Array Market stall. The trays
hold fruit in neat rows and columns — the apple tray shows 5 rows of 5 apples.*

**Activity with the image:** Look at the apple tray in the picture. Count the
rows across (5 rows) and the apples down each column (5 apples). Write the
equation: **5 × 5 = 25**. Now the orange crate: count its rows and columns and
write its equation. (**4 × 4 = 16.**)

*Text-only alternative:* the apple tray holds 5 rows of 5 apples (5 × 5 = 25
apples); the orange crate holds 4 rows of 4 oranges (4 × 4 = 16 oranges). You
can answer every question from these counts without seeing the picture.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

An **array** is a neat rectangle of things lined up in **rows** and **columns**.
A **row** goes across (side to side). A **column** goes up and down. An array
is just equal groups standing in formation: every row is one equal group, and
every column is one equal group too.

To read an array as multiplication:

1. Count the **rows** — that is the first factor (how many groups).
2. Count the **columns** — that is the second factor (how many in each group).
3. Multiply: rows × columns = total.

So "3 rows of 6" is 3 × 6 = 18. Arrays are the bridge from Lesson 01's piles
to real multiplication: a 3-by-6 rectangle of chairs, egg cartons, muffin
tins, and seed trays are all arrays hiding in everyday life.

Grade-2 review, now bigger: you already built arrays up to 5 × 5. Today we read
and draw arrays up to 10 × 10.

## Worked example 1 — 3 rows of 6 chairs

1. Draw 3 rows; put 6 dots in each row. Check: is every row the same length?
   Yes — 6 dots each.
2. Count rows: 3 (first factor). Count columns: 6 (second factor).
3. Equation: **3 × 6 = 18.**
4. Verify by skip counting down the rows: "6, 12, 18." Or add the columns:
   6 + 6 + 6 = 18. All three ways agree.
5. Common slip: counting 3 × 6 as 3 + 6 = 9, or counting dots one by one and
   losing track. The array's rows do the grouping for you — trust them.

## Worked example 2 — Draw an array for 4 × 7

1. First factor 4 = 4 rows. Second factor 7 = 7 columns.
2. Draw 4 rows with 7 dots each. Line them up so the columns are straight —
   sloppy columns hide the structure.
3. Find the product: skip count "7, 14, 21, 28." Equation: **4 × 7 = 28.**
4. Turn the paper sideways: now it reads 7 rows of 4 — same 28 dots.
   (Lesson 04 will name this trick.)
5. Check: 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 = 28. ✓

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner draws.

1. Draw a **2 × 8** array. Write the equation and product.
   *(Hint: 2 rows, 8 in each row — make the two rows the same length.)*
2. Draw a **5 × 3** array. Write the equation and product.
   *(Hint: count the rows first — that number goes before the ×.)*
3. An array has **6 rows and 4 columns**. Write the equation without drawing,
   then draw it to check.
   *(Hint: rows × columns: 6 × 4.)*
4. Look at the plum basket in the illustration. The plums sit in **3 rows of
   3**. Write the equation and product.
   *(Hint: use the text-only alternative if the picture is hard to see:
   3 × 3 = 9.)*
5. Error analysis: Sam drew **4 rows of 5** but wrote **4 × 4 = 16**. What
   did Sam mix up?
   *(Hint: count the dots in one row — is it 4 or 5?)*

## Independent practice

6. Draw a **3 × 9** array. Write the equation and product.
7. Draw a **7 × 2** array. Write the equation and product.
8. Write the equation for an array with **8 rows and 5 columns**. Find the
   product by skip counting.
9. A muffin tin has **4 rows with 6 muffins in each row**. Draw it as an
   array, write the equation, and find how many muffins.
10. How many dots are in a **10 × 3** array? Explain using the 3s row
    (skip count) rather than counting one by one.
11. Challenge: draw two *different* arrays that both hold 12 dots. Write both
    equations.

## Application

You are setting up chairs for a class play: **5 rows with 7 chairs in each
row**. Draw the array, write the equation, and find the total number of
chairs. Then answer: if 2 more rows are added, what is the new total? Show two
ways to find it (draw the new rows, or add 2 × 7 to your first total).

## Exit check

1. Draw a **4 × 6** array, label the rows and the columns, and write the
   equation with the product.
2. An array has 3 rows and 8 columns. Write the multiplication equation.

*Success criterion:* arrays drawn with straight, equal rows; rows counted
before the ×. If rows and columns are swapped in the equation, re-teach the
finger sweep (across = row) before Session 6.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** the learner dictates "4 rows of 6" while the adult
  writes **4 × 6**; the learner then points to each row while skip counting.
- **Alternate response mode:** build arrays with counters on grid paper
  instead of drawing dots; answer orally.
- **Extension:** find all the ways to arrange 24 counters into an array with
  at least 2 rows and 2 columns. List each as rows × columns = 24.
  (2 × 12, 3 × 8, 4 × 6, 6 × 4, 8 × 3, 12 × 2.)

## Teacher note

Two traps: (1) **rows/columns swapped** — harmless for the product but fatal for
understanding later (area, coordinates); always have the learner say "rows"
while sweeping across. (2) **Drawing 12 dots scattered** and calling it an
array — insist on the rectangle; the rectangle *is* the equal-group guarantee.
The image activity works because all counts are also supplied in text; never
ask a learner to settle a disputed count from the AI illustration alone.
