# Answer Key — Unit 05: Fractions on Models and Number Lines

**Audience:** the guiding adult only. Every answer below was solved
independently from the lesson, practice, project, quiz, and assessment
pages and reconciled with them. Open responses accept any answer that
states the same reasoning; sample wording is given.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. Fold into 2 equal parts; one part is **1/2** (one half).
2. One part is **1/3** (one third).
3. One part is **1/8** (one eighth).
4. No. The parts are not equal, so none of them is 1/4 — they are just

5. Leo is wrong because the 6 parts are not the same size. Before any

**Independent practice**

## Lesson 02

**Picture question (generated illustration):** The tray was cut into 8
equal pieces. **3/8** of the tray is set aside (3 pieces of size 1/8);
**5/8** of the tray is still in the tray (5 pieces of size 1/8).
Check: 3/8 + 5/8 = 8/8 = the whole tray.

**Guided practice**

1. Shade 2 of 3 equal parts: **2/3** ("two thirds").
2. **4/6** ("four sixths").
3. **1/8, 2/8, 3/8, 4/8**.
4. **5/6** ("five sixths"). The 5 counts the parts; the 6 says the
   whole has 6 equal parts.
5. Ben is wrong: the whole has 4 equal parts, so the denominator is 4.
   He should write **2/4**.

**Independent practice**

6. **3/4**.
7. **6/8**.
8. **"Four sixths."** The 4 means 4 parts are counted; the 6 means the
   whole has 6 equal parts.
9. **1/8, 2/8, 3/8, 4/8, 5/8, 6/8, 7/8**.
10. True. 3/4 is three parts, each of size 1/4.
11. Rosa is wrong: the denominator is the total number of equal parts
    in the whole, which is 6, not 5. She should write **5/6**. Counting
    check: 1/6, 2/6, 3/6, 4/6, 5/6 — five parts of size 1/6.

**Application:** Rectangle split into 6 equal parts, 5 shaded: **5/6**.
Sample sentence: "5/6 means five parts, each of size one sixth."

**Exit check**

1. **2/4**.
2. **3/8**.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. **1/4** at the first tick after 0.
2. **1/6** at the first tick after 0.
3. **1/2** — the middle mark, halfway between 0 and 1.
4. **8** equal parts (8 intervals).
5. Jay is wrong: the second tick is the end of **2** parts, so it is
   2/4. 1/4 is at the **first** tick after 0.

**Independent practice**

6. **1/3** at the first tick after 0.
7. **1/8** at the first tick after 0.
8. **1/6** at the first tick after 0.
9. The **first** tick after 0 — it is the end of exactly one part of
    size 1/4.
10. True. 1/4 is the endpoint of the first of 4 equal parts.
11. Kim is wrong: there are **4** equal parts (4 intervals), not 5.
    The 5 marks are 0, 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, 1 — tick marks, not parts.
    Count the intervals between the marks: 4.

**Application:** Number line 0 to 1 in fourths, ticks labeled 1/4, 2/4,
3/4, 1. Sample sentence: "The first tick after 0 is 1/4 because it is
the end of the first part, and the interval from 0 to 1 is split into 4
equal parts."

**Exit check**

1. **1/3**.
2. **1/6**.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**

1. **2/3** — 2 jumps of 1/3 from 0.
2. **1/2** — halfway between 0 and 1.
3. **6/8** — 6 jumps of 1/8; yes, it is past the halfway point (4/8).
4. **4/4** lands on **1**.
5. Leo is wrong: he counted the tick at 0 as a jump. The third tick
   after 0 is the end of 3 jumps, so it is 3/3 = 1. **2/3** is the
   **second** tick after 0.

**Independent practice**

6. **5/8**.
7. **2/4**.
8. **3/3**; the whole number at that point is **1**.
9. **7/8**; it is **1** jump of 1/8 short of 1.
10. True. 4 jumps of 1/4 from 0 land on 1, so 4/4 and 1 are the same
    point.
11. Kim is wrong: counting backward from 1 miscounts the jumps.
    4/6 is **4 jumps from 0** — the second tick before 1, not the
    fourth. (Counting backward from 1, the fourth tick is 2/6.)

**Application:** 3/8 and 6/8 marked; **6/8 is farther from 0** (6 jumps
of 1/8 against 3 jumps of 1/8). **8/8** is marked; the whole number
there is **1**.

**Exit check**

1. **3/6**.
2. **8/8**; the whole number at that point is **1**.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. **2/6 = 1/3** — shaded lengths match.
2. **3/4 = 6/8** — each fourth cut in half becomes two eighths.
3. **2/2**; the whole number at that point is **1**.
4. **3 = 3/1**; **6/1 = 6**.
5. Mia is wrong: 2/3 and 4/6 are the **same size** (2/3 = 4/6). Cut
   each third in half: 2 thirds become 4 sixths.

**Independent practice**

6. **3/6**.
7. **4/6**.
8. **5 = 5/1**; **4/1 = 4**.
9. **1 = 8/8**; **3/3 = 1**.
10. True. 2/8 and 1/4 are the same size: cut each fourth in half and
    1 fourth becomes 2 eighths, so 2/8 lands on the same point as 1/4.
11. Tom is wrong: **3/6 = 1/2**. Three sixths cover the same length
    as one half: cut the whole into 6 equal parts and take 3, and that
    is half the whole. Tom's mistake is judging by the size of the
    numerals instead of the size of the shaded amount.

**Application:** 1/2 = **4/8** and 1/2 = **3/6**. All three fractions
land on the **same point** (halfway between 0 and 1).

**Exit check**

1. **4/8**.
2. **2 = 2/1**.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice**

1. **1/3 > 1/4** (thirds are bigger pieces than fourths).
2. **5/8 > 3/8** (same-size pieces; 5 > 3).
3. **2/3 > 2/6** (thirds are bigger pieces than sixths).
4. **4/6 = 4/6**.
5. Lena is wrong: **1/2 is bigger than 1/8**. 8 is the number of pieces
   the whole was cut into, not the size of the fraction — halves are
   bigger pieces than eighths.

**Independent practice**

6. **3/4 > 3/6** — same number of pieces; fourths are bigger than
   sixths.
7. **1/6 < 5/6** — same-size pieces; 1 < 5.
8. **2/4 > 2/3** — same number of pieces; fourths are bigger than
   thirds.
9. **6/8 = 3/4** — equivalent (Lesson 05).
10. True. Sixths are bigger pieces than eighths (fewer cuts, bigger
    pieces), so 5 sixths beat 5 eighths.
11. Ben cannot tell from the fractions alone: the wholes are different
    sizes. He must check that both strips are the **same length**
    first; only then is the comparison valid.

**Application:** **1/4 < 1/2 < 3/4**. 1/2 sits in the middle because it
is bigger than 1/4 (a half piece is bigger than a fourth piece) and
smaller than 3/4 (three fourth-pieces cover more than one half).

**Exit check**

1. **2/3 > 2/4**.
2. **4/8 < 7/8**.

**Extension:** **1/8 < 1/3 < 2/3 < 5/6**. Equal pair: **2/4 = 3/6**
(both are one half).

## Practice sessions

**Session A**

1. **1/2**. 2. **1/4**. 3. **2/3**. 4. **1/6**. 5. **3/8**.
6. **4/4**, which is the same as **1**. 7. Longest to shortest:
**1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8**. 8. Sample: "1/2 is bigger than 1/8
because the whole is cut into only 2 parts for a half, so each part
is bigger, while 8 parts make small pieces."

**Session B**

1. **3/4**. 2. **2/3**. 3. **5/6**. 4. **1/8**. 5. Shaded **4/6**;
unshaded **2/6**. 6. Shaded **7/8**; unshaded **1/8**. 7. **2/4**
shaded; unshaded **2/4**. 8. Wrong: the parts are unequal, so no part
can be named 2/6. The parts must be **equal** first.

**Session C**

Round 1 (hops from 0): 2/3 = 2 hops of 1/3; 3/4 = 3 hops of 1/4;
5/6 = 5 hops of 1/6; 3/8 = 3 hops of 1/8; 5/8 = 5 hops of 1/8;
7/8 = 7 hops of 1/8.
Round 2 (hops to 1): 1/2 → 2 jumps land on 1 (2/2); 1/3 → 3 jumps
(3/3); 1/4 → 4 jumps (4/4); 1/6 → 6 jumps (6/6); 1/8 → 8 jumps
(8/8); 1/4 again: **2 jumps** make 2/4, the halfway point.

**Session D**

1. **4/6**; the numerator counts the shaded parts (4), the
   denominator counts all the equal parts in the whole (6).
2. **3/4**; it took **3 jumps** of 1/4 from 0.
3. Sample: "3/3 is three jumps of 1/3, which fills the whole interval
   from 0 to 1, and 1 is the number at the end of that interval — so
   they name the same point."
4. Shaded **5/8**; not shaded **3/8**.
5. 2/4 on a strip and on a number line show the **same amount** — the
   same size and the same point.
6. True: 1/6 is a smaller piece than 1/3 because the whole is cut into
   more pieces (6 instead of 3), so each piece is smaller.

**Session E**

1. **2/4**. 2. **3/6**. 3. **2/6**. 4. **4/6**. 5. **6/8**.
6. **2/8**. 7. **3/3, 6/6, 8/8** — all three land on **1**.
8. Wrong: "one less on top than on the bottom" is not a rule.
**2/4 = 1/2** (two fourths cover the same length as one half), not
1/3.

**Session F**

Station 1 (same denominator): 3/6 > 1/6; 5/8 > 2/8; 4/6 < 6/6;
1/4 < 3/4; 2/3 < 3/3; 5/6 > 4/6.
Station 2 (same numerator): 1/2 > 1/6; 2/3 > 2/4; 3/8 < 3/4;
1/4 > 1/3; 2/6 < 2/3; 5/8 < 5/6.
Station 3 (equal or not): 2/4 = 1/2; 3/6 = 1/2; 4/8 = 1/2;
2/6 = 1/3; 6/8 = 3/4; 4/6 = 2/3.
Station 4: 1/3 < 2/3; 3/8 > 3/6; 7/8 > 5/8; 2/4 = 1/2.
Order: **1/6 < 1/2 < 5/6** — 1/2 sits in the middle because it is
bigger than 1/6 (a half piece beats a sixth piece) and smaller than
5/6 (five sixths cover more than a half).

**Session H**

1. **8** equal parts. 2. **3** pieces; **3/8**. 3. **5** pieces; **5/8**.
4. **1/8**. 5. 3/8 + 5/8 = **8/8**, the same as **1**.
6. 3 parts: **3/8**; the other 5 parts: **5/8**.
7. Wrong: they counted the set-aside pieces (3) as the denominator.
   The denominator is the number of equal parts in the **whole tray**,

8. Sample: "The pieces can be named as eighths because the whole tray
   was cut into 8 equal parts."

## Project — Fraction Bakery

Sample complete plan: tray of **8 equal parts**; chocolate **2/8**,
vanilla **3/8**, strawberry **1/8**; total filled **6/8**; leftover
**2/8** (for a fourth flavor later, or plain slices). Number line 0 to
1 in eighths: chocolate ends at **2/8**, vanilla ends at **5/8**,
strawberry ends at **6/8**. Swap: **2/8 = 1/4** (or total 6/8 = 3/4).
Comparison: vanilla (**3/8**) takes up more than chocolate (**2/8**),
because 3/8 > 2/8 with same-size parts.
"What if" answers: 4-part tray → chocolate **1/4**; vanilla 4/8 →
filled **7/8**, leftover **1/8**; swapped amounts → strawberry **2/8**
has more than chocolate **1/8** (2 parts out of 8 beats 1 part out of
8).
