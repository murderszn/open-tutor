# Unit 05 — Fractions on Models and Number Lines

**Grade:** 3 · **Subject:** Mathematics · **Track weeks:** 19–22
**Backlog:** [issue #18](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/18), section U05
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

A fraction is a number, not two numbers stacked on top of each other.
If a tray of brownies is cut into 8 equal pieces, one piece is 1/8 of
the tray, three pieces are 3/8, and all eight pieces together are 8/8 —
the whole tray, the number 1. In this unit the learner folds paper
strips, shades rectangles, and hops along number lines to see that a
fraction names a size: how many equal parts the whole was cut into
(the denominator) and how many of those parts are counted (the
numerator). Then the same models show that 1/2 and 2/4 are the same
size, that 3 is the same as 3/1, and that 3/4 is bigger than 3/8
because fourths are bigger pieces than eighths — while 5/6 beats 2/6
because five sixths are more sixths than two. Every comparison is
about parts of the same whole; that rule is the unit's spine. No
timed speed tests: understanding here means a model and a reason, not
a memorized pair.

## Essential question

How can equal parts, models, and the number line show us what a
fraction is worth — and when two fractions are worth the same?

## Prerequisites

From the diagnostic weeks (track weeks 1–2) and Units 01–04:

- Equal sharing and equal groups (Units 02–03); the equal-parts idea
  from grade 2 (2.G.A.3) is the entry bridge — a whole split into equal
  parts, with every part the same size
- Skip counting by 2s, 3s, and 4s (used only in retrieval warm-ups and
  in counting unit fractions aloud)
- Addition within 100 and the idea that a total can be counted in parts
  (Unit 01; used only to check that shaded + unshaded parts make the
  whole, e.g. 3/8 + 5/8 = 8/8)
- Reading a number line from 0 (Units 01–04 used number lines for
  rounding and for time; this unit makes the interval from 0 to 1 the
  whole)

Each lesson's readiness check names the exact re-teach for a missing
prerequisite. This unit never uses a denominator outside 2, 3, 4, 6, 8,
and never asks the learner to add or subtract fractions with different
denominators — only to count unit fractions (3/8 + 5/8 = 8/8 as a
counting check).

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. Understand a fraction 1/*b* as one part when a whole is partitioned
   into *b* equal parts, and a fraction *a*/*b* as *a* parts of size
   1/*b*; name and write fractions from folded strips, shaded models,
   and the brownie-tray illustration. (3.NF.A.1)
2. Represent fractions on a number line: split the interval from 0 to
   1 into *b* equal parts, locate 1/*b* at the endpoint of the first
   part, and locate *a*/*b* by counting *a* jumps of 1/*b* from 0,
   including fractions that land on 1. (3.NF.A.2, 3.NF.A.2.a,
   3.NF.A.2.b)
3. Explain equivalence in special cases: recognize two fractions as
   equivalent when they are the same size or the same point on the
   number line, and generate simple equivalent fractions
   (1/2 = 2/4, 4/6 = 2/3) with a visual model. (3.NF.A.3, 3.NF.A.3.a,
   3.NF.A.3.b)
4. Express whole numbers as fractions and recognize fractions that are
   equivalent to whole numbers (3 = 3/1, 6/1 = 6; 4/4 and 1 at the same
   point). (3.NF.A.3.c)
5. Compare two fractions with the same numerator or the same
   denominator by reasoning about their size, recognizing that
   comparisons are valid only when the two fractions refer to the same
   whole; record results with >, =, or <. (3.NF.A.3.d)
6. Explain their reasoning with model, strip, or number-line language,
   and judge whether an answer is reasonable (count the parts, check
   the whole, re-mark the number line).

## Vocabulary (taught with objects first, word second)

whole · equal parts · fraction · numerator · denominator · unit
fraction · part of size 1/b · number line · interval · tick mark ·
jump · equivalent fractions · same size · same point · same whole ·
greater than (>) · less than (<) · equal to (=) · shaded · unshaded

Note on language: the adult may use *numerator*, *denominator*, and
*equivalent*, but the learner is never required to produce those words
to show understanding. "How many equal parts in the whole" and "how
many parts we are counting" are always accepted.

## Materials

Household or dollar-store items (see the track
[scope-and-sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md) for the full safe-materials
list): paper strips (all the same length — the same whole matters for
every comparison), paper rectangles and circles, scissors, crayons or
pencils, a ruler or straight edge, fraction strips in halves, thirds,
fourths, sixths, and eighths, > / = / < symbol cards, a counter for
hopping on the number line, the generated illustration
[`assets/fraction-brownie-tray.png`](assets/fraction-brownie-tray.png)
plus the reproducible SVG
[`assets/number-line-eighths.svg`](assets/number-line-eighths.svg).
No sharp tools beyond child-safe scissors; supervise small parts. Real
food is used only in the project, only with the adult, and only food
the learner can safely eat; paper and play-dough work for every
activity.

## How the 16 sessions fill the unit

Six lessons alone do not fill four weeks. Lessons introduce each idea
once; practice sessions (A, B, E, F) give the learner a second and
third pass at the same idea with new numbers; the game session builds
number-line fluency without timing; review sessions (H, D) force the
learner to explain, not just answer; the project applies all six
objectives to one real plan; and the quiz and assessment check the
objectives, not the lessons. If a lesson's exit check fails, the
following practice session becomes re-teach time — see
[teacher-guide.md](teacher-guide.md) for the ready-to-move-on criteria.
