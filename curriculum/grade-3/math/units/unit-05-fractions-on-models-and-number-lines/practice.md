# Practice Sessions — Unit 05: Fractions on Models and Number Lines

Each session is 30 minutes and opens with the 2-minute retrieval warm-up
from the unit pacing. The adult reviews every session the same day.
Answers are in [answer-key.md](answer-key.md#practice-sessions); do not
read the key before trying.

**Project prep:** Sessions 14–15 are the unit project (see the unit
README). Sessions E and F below feed directly into it: Session E builds
the equivalence-matching skill the project needs, and Session F builds
the comparison language the project asks learners to use. Session H is
the Session 3 review of equal parts using the unit's generated
illustration. Keep finished strips and number lines from Sessions A–H —
the project reuses them.

## Session A — Fraction-strip builders

*After Lesson 01. Goal: build equal-part strips and name the unit
fraction in each part.*

**Materials:** blank paper strips (all the same length), pencils,
scissors, a ruler for checking equal parts.

**Steps:** Fold or mark each strip into the equal parts named, cut or
draw the parts, shade the parts asked for, and write the fraction each
shaded part names. Check every strip by laying its parts end to end
against an uncut strip.

1. Build a strip split into 2 equal parts. Shade 1 part. What fraction
   did you shade?
2. Build a strip split into 4 equal parts. Shade 1 part. What fraction
   did you shade?
3. Build a strip split into 3 equal parts. Shade 2 parts. What fraction
   did you shade?
4. Build a strip split into 6 equal parts. Shade 1 part. What fraction
   did you shade?
5. Build a strip split into 8 equal parts. Shade 3 parts. What fraction
   did you shade?
6. Build a strip split into 4 equal parts and shade all 4 parts. What
   fraction did you shade, and what whole number is it the same as?
7. Lay your unit pieces (1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8) side by side from
   longest to shortest. Write the five unit fractions in that order.
8. Explain in one sentence why 1/2 is a bigger piece than 1/8, even
   though 2 is smaller than 8.

*Success criterion:* all strips split into truly equal parts, every
shaded fraction named correctly, and the unit pieces ordered by size
with a reason. Any uneven strip is rebuilt, not patched.

## Session B — Shade-and-name

*After Lesson 02. Goal: shade a named fraction of a shape and name a
shaded fraction.*

**Materials:** pre-drawn circles, rectangles, and strips split into 2, 3,
4, 6, or 8 equal parts (some blank, some partly shaded), pencils.

**Steps:** For shading tasks, shade exactly the fraction named and count
the shaded parts aloud. For naming tasks, count the shaded parts and the
total parts, then write the fraction.

1. Shade 3/4 of a circle split into 4 equal parts.
2. Shade 2/3 of a rectangle split into 3 equal parts.
3. Shade 5/6 of a strip split into 6 equal parts.
4. Shade 1/8 of a circle split into 8 equal parts.
5. A rectangle split into 6 equal parts has 4 parts shaded. What
   fraction is shaded? What fraction is not shaded?
6. A circle split into 8 equal parts has 7 parts shaded. What fraction
   is shaded? What fraction is not shaded?
7. Draw your own rectangle split into 4 equal parts, shade 2/4, and
   write one sentence saying what the shaded part and the unshaded part
   are each worth.
8. Error analysis: a learner shaded 2 parts of a strip split into 6
   *unequal* parts and wrote 2/6. What is wrong, and what must be true
   about the parts before a fraction can name them?

*Success criterion:* every shaded fraction matches the parts counted,
shaded and unshaded fractions named as a pair, and item 8 answered with
the equal-parts rule stated. A miscounted shading is redrawn, not
erased into correctness.

## Session H — Brownie-tray image activity and equal-parts review

*Session 3, after Lesson 01. Goal: name unit and non-unit fractions
from a real-looking picture, and re-check the equal-parts rule.*

**Materials:** the generated illustration
[`assets/fraction-brownie-tray.png`](assets/fraction-brownie-tray.png),
paper strips for re-folding.

**Steps:** Look at the picture together. Count the parts, check they are
equal, and name the fractions. Then re-fold a strip to prove the rule.

1. How many equal parts was the whole tray cut into? (8.)
2. How many pieces are set aside beside the tray? What fraction of the
   tray is that? (3 pieces; 3/8.)
3. How many pieces are still in the tray? What fraction of the tray is
   that? (5 pieces; 5/8.)
4. What fraction names one piece? (1/8 — one part of size one eighth.)
5. Check the total: 3/8 set aside + 5/8 still in the tray = 8/8. What
   whole number is 8/8 the same as? (1 — the whole tray.)
6. Re-fold a paper strip into 8 equal parts. Point to 3 parts and name
   the fraction (3/8). Point to the other 5 parts and name that fraction
   (5/8).
7. Error analysis: a learner looked at the picture and said one piece is
   1/3 "because 3 pieces are set aside." What did they count wrong?
   (They counted the set-aside pieces instead of all the equal parts in
   the whole — the denominator is 8, the number of equal parts in the
   whole tray, not 3.)
8. Write one sentence explaining why the pieces in the picture can be
   named as eighths, using the words "equal parts" and "whole."

*Success criterion:* 3/8 and 5/8 named correctly from the picture with
the denominator explained as the count of all equal parts in the whole,
and the strip re-folded into truly equal parts. A learner who names the
denominator from the set-aside count re-counts the whole tray's parts
with the adult before Session 4.

## Session C — Number-line hop game

*After Lesson 03 (game, no timer on the learner — the "game" is beating
your own last round, and accuracy beats speed).*

**Materials:** a 0-to-1 number-line board, a counter, a set of hop cards
(unit fractions 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8 and target fractions 2/3, 3/4,
5/6, 3/8, 5/8, 7/8).

**Round 1 — Hop to the target (after Lesson 03).** The adult shows a
target card. The learner says how many hops of which unit fraction it
takes from 0, then hops the counter along the line saying each fraction
aloud, and lands on the target. Play 6 rounds; every round is checked by
counting the hops before the next round starts. Sample targets: 2/3,
3/4, 5/6, 3/8, 5/8, 7/8.

**Round 2 — Hop and land on 1 (after Lesson 04).** The adult names a
unit fraction. The learner hops from 0 to 1 in that size of jump, saying
each fraction aloud, and names the fraction that lands on 1. Play 6
rounds; sample jumps: 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8, and 1/4 again with a
different starting question (how many jumps of 1/4 make 2/4, and where
is that point?).

*Success criterion:* at least 5 of 6 rounds correct in each round, every
one with the hops counted aloud from 0. A wrong round is replayed, not
skipped.

## Session D — Draw-explain review

*After Lessons 01–03. Goal: explain, not just answer.*

**Materials:** blank paper, pencils, fraction strips for checking.

1. Draw a strip split into 6 equal parts, shade 4/6, and write one
   sentence saying what the numerator counts and what the denominator
   counts.
2. Draw a number line from 0 to 1 split into 4 equal parts, mark 3/4,
   and write one sentence saying how many jumps of 1/4 it took from 0.
3. Explain in two sentences why 3/3 and 1 name the same point on the
   number line.
4. Draw a circle split into 8 equal parts, shade 5/8, and write the
   fraction that is *not* shaded.
5. Solve this two ways: show 2/4 on a strip and on a number line, and
   say in one sentence what is the same about both showings.
6. True or false, with a reason: "1/6 is a smaller piece than 1/3
   because the whole is cut into more pieces."

*Success criterion:* drawings labeled, jumps counted from 0, and every
explanation naming the numerator, the denominator, or the equal-parts
rule it rests on. Item 6 answered true with the fewer-cuts-bigger-pieces
reason.

## Session E — Equivalence matching

*After Lesson 05. Goal: match equivalent fractions and fill in missing
numerators, proving each match on a strip or a number line.*

**Materials:** fraction-strip set, number-line strips, a set of matching
cards (fractions with denominators 2, 3, 4, 6, 8).

**Steps:** For each item, find the match or the missing number, then
prove it by shading both fractions on strips of the same length or
marking both on number lines of the same length. No match counts without
the proof.

1. Match 1/2 to its equivalent fraction with denominator 4.
2. Match 1/2 to its equivalent fraction with denominator 6.
3. Match 1/3 to its equivalent fraction with denominator 6.
4. Match 2/3 to its equivalent fraction with denominator 6.
5. Match 3/4 to its equivalent fraction with denominator 8.
6. Fill in the missing numerator: 1/4 = ?/8.
7. Write 1 as a fraction with denominator 3, then with denominator 6,
   then with denominator 8. Mark all three on number lines of the same
   length and say where they land.
8. Error analysis: a learner matched 2/4 with 1/3, saying "both are one
   less on top than on the bottom." What is wrong with that rule, and
   what fraction is 2/4 really equivalent to?

*Success criterion:* every match proved on a strip or a number line of
the same length, and item 8 answered with the real equivalent fraction
and a reason the "one less" rule fails. An unproved match is redone with
the strips.

## Session F — Comparison stations

*After Lesson 06. Goal: a named reason for every comparison, checked on
a strip or a number line.*

**Materials:** fraction strips, number lines, > / = / < symbol cards.

**Station 1 — Same denominator, count the pieces.** Compare by counting
pieces of the same size: 3/6 and 1/6; 5/8 and 2/8; 4/6 and 6/6; 1/4 and
3/4; 2/3 and 3/3; 5/6 and 4/6.

**Station 2 — Same numerator, compare the pieces.** Compare by piece
size (fewer cuts, bigger pieces): 1/2 and 1/6; 2/3 and 2/4; 3/8 and 3/4;
1/4 and 1/3; 2/6 and 2/3; 5/8 and 5/6.

**Station 3 — Equal or not?** Decide whether each pair is equal, and
prove it on a strip or a number line: 2/4 and 1/2; 3/6 and 1/2; 4/8 and
1/2; 2/6 and 1/3; 6/8 and 3/4; 4/6 and 2/3.

**Station 4 — Mixed review and ordering.** Compare any way you like,
but name the reason and check it: 1/3 and 2/3; 3/8 and 3/6; 7/8 and 5/8;
2/4 and 1/2. Then order 1/6, 1/2, and 5/6 from smallest to largest and
say why 1/2 sits in the middle.

*Success criterion:* every station item correct with a named reason
(count the pieces, compare the pieces, or same size / same point) and a
strip or number-line check. Any missed item returns to its station for
a second pass before the quiz.
