# Lesson 05 — Equivalent Fractions and Whole Numbers

 **Session:** 10 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can tell when two
fractions are equivalent — the same size, or the same point on the number
line — and I can write a whole number as a fraction.

**Materials:** fraction strips (halves, thirds, fourths, sixths, eighths,
all the same length), two 0-to-1 number lines of the same length, shaded
fraction cards.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min, Lesson 04 bridge):** "Locate 3/4 on the number
line — how many jumps of 1/4 from 0?" (3 jumps.) "Where does 4/4 land?"
(On 1.) "Where does 6/6 land?" (On 1 as well — any fraction with the
numerator equal to the denominator lands on 1.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "Shade 1/2 of a strip. Now shade 2/4 of a strip the same
length. Which shaded part looks longer?" (Neither — they look the same
length.) "Count 2 jumps of 1/4 on the number line. Where do you land?"
(2/4 — the same point as 1/2.) "What is 3/3 the same as?" (1.) If the
strips do not look the same length, check that they started the same
length — equivalence only works when the wholes match.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

Two fractions are **equivalent** when they name the **same size** — the
same amount of the same whole. On a number line, equivalent fractions sit
on the **same point**. On fraction strips, equivalent fractions shade the
**same length**.

Equivalence happens when you cut the same whole into smaller pieces and
take more of them. Cut a strip into 2 parts and take 1: that is 1/2. Cut
the same strip into 4 parts and take 2: that is 2/4. The pieces are
smaller, but you took twice as many, so the shaded length is exactly the
same. So **1/2 = 2/4**.

The same idea works with thirds and sixths: 2/3 of a strip is the same
length as 4/6 of the same strip, because cutting each third in half gives
sixths, and 2 thirds become 4 sixths. So **2/3 = 4/6**.

Whole numbers are fractions too. **1 = 2/2 = 3/3 = 4/4 = 6/6 = 8/8** —
any fraction with the numerator equal to the denominator is 1 whole.
And any whole number can be written as a fraction with denominator 1:
**3 = 3/1** (three wholes, each split into 1 part), and **6/1 = 6**
(six wholes). The denominator 1 means "the whole was not cut at all."

To test whether two fractions are equivalent, use the same whole both
times: shade both on strips of the same length, or mark both on number
lines of the same length, and see whether they land in the same place.

## Worked example 1 — Show 1/2 = 2/4 with strips

1. Take two strips of the same length. Split the first into 2 equal
   parts and shade 1 part. That is 1/2.
2. Split the second into 4 equal parts and shade 2 parts. That is 2/4.
3. Lay the strips side by side, lined up at the left edge. The shaded
   parts end at exactly the same place. Same length — same size. ✓
4. Check on the number line: 1 jump of 1/2 lands halfway; 2 jumps of 1/4
   land halfway too. Same point. ✓
5. Equation: **1/2 = 2/4.**
6. Common slip: saying 2/4 must be bigger than 1/2 "because 2 and 4 are
   bigger numbers than 1 and 2." The numbers in a fraction are not the
   size — the *size of the shaded part* is. More pieces does not mean
   more amount when the pieces are smaller.

## Worked example 2 — Show 4/4 and 1 at the same point

1. On a number line from 0 to 1, split the distance into 4 equal parts.
2. Count 4 jumps of 1/4 from 0: 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, **4/4**. You land on 1.
3. The point labeled 4/4 and the point labeled 1 are the same point.
   Four quarters make the whole, and the whole is 1.
4. The same is true for any denominator: 2 jumps of 1/2 land on 1, 3
   jumps of 1/3 land on 1, 6 jumps of 1/6 land on 1, 8 jumps of 1/8 land
   on 1. So **2/2 = 3/3 = 4/4 = 6/6 = 8/8 = 1**.
5. Turn it around: write 1 as a fraction with denominator 6. The answer
   is 6/6 — six sixths make one whole.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner does
the shading, marking, and writing.

1. Use strips to show that **2/6 = 1/3**. Shade both and compare the
   lengths.
   *(Hint: split one strip into 3 parts and shade 1; split a strip the
   same length into 6 parts and shade 2. Line them up — do the shaded
   parts end in the same place?)*
2. Use strips to show that **3/4 = 6/8**. Shade both and compare.
   *(Hint: each fourth cut in half becomes two eighths, so 3 fourths
   become 6 eighths.)*
3. Mark **2/2** on a number line. What whole number is at that point?
   *(Hint: count 2 jumps of 1/2 from 0 — where do you land?)*
4. Write **3 as a fraction**. Then write **6/1 as a whole number**.
   *(Hint: a whole number n is n/1 — the whole was not cut. Read 6/1 as
   "six wholes.")*
5. Error analysis: Mia said **2/3 is bigger than 4/6** because 4 and 6
   are bigger numbers than 2 and 3. What did Mia mix up? Show with
   strips or a number line that 2/3 = 4/6.
   *(Hint: cut each third in half. How many sixths does 2 thirds
   become? Lay the shaded strips side by side.)*

## Independent practice

6. Fill in the missing numerator so the fractions are equivalent:
   **1/2 = ?/6**.
7. Fill in the missing numerator so the fractions are equivalent:
   **2/3 = ?/6**.
8. Write **5 as a fraction** with denominator 1. Then write **4/1 as a
   whole number**.
9. Write **1 as a fraction** with denominator 8. Then write **3/3 as a
   whole number**.
10. True or false: **2/8 = 1/4**. Explain with strips or a number line.
11. Error analysis: Tom said **3/6 = 1/2 is wrong** because 3 and 6 are
    bigger than 1 and 2, so 3/6 must be a bigger fraction. Is Tom right?
    Show with strips or a number line whether 3/6 and 1/2 are equivalent,
    and explain Tom's mistake.

## Application

Use fraction strips to find a fraction equal to **1/2** with denominator
8, and a fraction equal to **1/2** with denominator 6. Write both
equations (1/2 = 4/8 and 1/2 = 3/6). Then mark all three fractions on
number lines of the same length and write one sentence saying what you
notice about where they land.

## Exit check

1. Fill in the missing numerator: **1/2 = ?/8**.
2. Write **2 as a fraction** with denominator 1.

*Success criterion:* both exit items correct with the equivalence shown
on a strip or a number line — not just a memorized pair. If the learner
says the fraction with bigger numbers is bigger, re-lay the shaded
strips side by side before Session 12.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** let the learner say the equivalence aloud ("one
  half is the same size as two fourths") while the adult writes the
  equation; the learner points to the matching shaded lengths.
- **Alternate response mode:** shade and compare with strips only and
  answer orally; the adult records the written equations.
- **Extension:** find three different fractions equivalent to 1/2 using
  denominators from 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 (2/4, 3/6, and 4/8 — check each on a
  strip). Then explain why 2/3 has exactly one equivalent fraction in
  that denominator set (4/6) and why 1/3 does too (2/6).

## Teacher note

The defining misconception in this lesson is **"bigger numbers mean a
bigger fraction"** — judging 2/4 > 1/2, or 4/6 > 2/3, from the numerals
alone. It comes from treating a fraction as two separate whole numbers
instead of one amount. The fix is always the same whole: shade both
fractions on strips of the same length, or mark both on number lines of
the same length, and let the matching length or point do the convincing.
A second slip is writing a whole number as a fraction with the wrong
denominator (3 = 1/3); rehearse "3 wholes, not cut, is 3/1" until the
denominator-1 pattern is secure. Answer key:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-05).
