# Lesson 06 — Comparing Fractions

 **Session:** 12 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can compare two
fractions with the same numerator or the same denominator by reasoning
about the size of the pieces, and record the comparison with >, =, or <.

**Materials:** fraction strips (halves, thirds, fourths, sixths, eighths,
all the same length), two 0-to-1 number lines of the same length, > / =
/ < symbol cards.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min, Lesson 05 bridge):** "What fraction is
equivalent to 1/2 with denominator 4?" (2/4.) "With denominator 8?"
(4/8.) "What whole number is 6/6 the same as?" (1 — six sixths make one
whole.)

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "Shade 1/2 and 1/4 of two strips the same length. Which shaded
part is longer?" (1/2 — the half piece is bigger than the fourth piece.)
"Shade 1/6 and 5/6 of two strips the same length. Which is longer?"
(5/6 — five pieces beat one piece when the pieces are the same size.)
"Why did the first pair and the second pair need different reasons?"
(In the first pair the pieces were different sizes; in the second pair
the pieces were the same size and only the count differed.) If the
learner cannot say which piece is bigger, re-lay the unit pieces (1/2,
1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8) side by side in size order first.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

Comparing fractions means deciding which amount is bigger, or whether
they are equal. There are two safe ways to reason — and both only work
when the fractions are parts of the **same whole**.

**Same denominator — count the pieces.** When the denominators match,
the pieces are the same size, so the fraction with more pieces is
bigger. 5/6 has five sixths and 2/6 has two sixths, so **5/6 > 2/6**.
Count, and the bigger numerator wins.

**Same numerator — compare the pieces.** When the numerators match, you
have the same number of pieces, so the fraction with the *bigger pieces*
is bigger. Pieces get bigger when the whole is cut into *fewer* parts.
Fourths are bigger than eighths, because a whole cut into 4 parts gives
bigger pieces than the same whole cut into 8. So three fourths beat
three eighths: **3/4 > 3/8**. The smaller denominator makes the bigger
pieces — that feels backward, and it is the heart of this lesson.

**Same whole, always.** A comparison is only valid when both fractions
come from the same-size whole. 1/2 of a small strip can be shorter than
1/4 of a long strip — the fractions alone cannot tell you, because the
wholes differ. In this unit every comparison uses strips and number lines
of the same length, so the wholes always match.

Record comparisons with **>** (greater than), **=** (equal to), and **<**
(less than). The open mouth of the symbol always faces the bigger
amount: 3/4 > 3/8 reads "three fourths is greater than three eighths."

## Worked example 1 — Compare 3/8 and 3/4

1. Read: both numerators are 3 — same number of pieces. So compare the
   piece sizes: eighths against fourths.
2. Lay a unit fourth (1/4) beside a unit eighth (1/8). The fourth is
   bigger: cutting a whole into 4 parts gives bigger pieces than cutting
   the same whole into 8 parts.
3. Three bigger pieces beat three smaller pieces: 3 × 1/4 is longer than
   3 × 1/8.
4. Check on the number line: 3 jumps of 1/4 land farther from 0 than 3
   jumps of 1/8. ✓
5. Record: **3/4 > 3/8.** Say it: "Three fourths is greater than three
   eighths."
6. Common slip: saying 3/8 > 3/4 "because 8 is bigger than 4." The
   denominator tells you how many pieces the whole was cut into, not how
   big the fraction is — a bigger denominator means *smaller* pieces.

## Worked example 2 — Compare 2/6 and 5/6

1. Read: both denominators are 6 — same-size pieces (sixths). So count
   the pieces: 2 against 5.
2. Five sixths is more sixths than two sixths, so 5/6 is the bigger
   amount.
3. Check with strips: shade 2/6 of one strip and 5/6 of a strip the same
   length. The 5/6 shading is longer. ✓ Check on the number line: 5
   jumps of 1/6 land farther from 0 than 2 jumps. ✓
4. Record: **2/6 < 5/6.** Say it: "Two sixths is less than five sixths."
5. Notice: this is the easy case — same denominator, count the pieces.
   The trap is using this counting rule when the denominators differ: 2/6
   and 2/4 have the same numerator, so you must compare piece sizes
   instead, and 2/4 is bigger because fourths are bigger than sixths.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner does
the shading, marking, and writing.

1. Compare **1/3 and 1/4**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <.
   *(Hint: same numerator, one piece each — which piece is bigger, a
   third or a fourth? Fewer cuts mean bigger pieces.)*
2. Compare **5/8 and 3/8**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <.
   *(Hint: same denominator — the pieces match, so count them. Which
   numerator is bigger?)*
3. Compare **2/3 and 2/6**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <.
   *(Hint: same numerator — two pieces each. Are thirds or sixths the
   bigger pieces?)*
4. Compare **4/6 and 4/6**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <.
   *(Hint: the same fraction twice — what symbol says two amounts are
   the same?)*
5. Error analysis: Lena said **1/8 > 1/2** because 8 is bigger than 2.
   What did Lena mix up? Which fraction is really bigger, and how can
   strips show it?
   *(Hint: 8 is the number of pieces the whole was cut into, not the
   size of the fraction. Lay 1/8 beside 1/2 — which shaded part is
   longer?)*

## Independent practice

6. Compare **3/4 and 3/6**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <, and
   write one sentence explaining your reasoning.
7. Compare **1/6 and 5/6**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <, and
   write one sentence explaining your reasoning.
8. Compare **2/4 and 2/3**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <, and
   write one sentence explaining your reasoning.
9. Compare **6/8 and 3/4**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <.
   *(Think back to Lesson 05 before you decide.)*
10. True or false: **5/6 > 5/8 because sixths are bigger pieces than
    eighths.** Explain your answer.
11. Error analysis: Ben compared 3/4 of a short strip with 1/2 of a long
    strip and said 3/4 is bigger. Why can he not tell from the fractions
    alone? What would he need to check first?

## Application

Order **1/4, 1/2, and 3/4** from smallest to largest. Shade each on a
strip of the same length (or mark each on a number line), write the order
with < symbols, and write one sentence explaining why 1/2 sits in the
middle.

## Exit check

1. Compare **2/3 and 2/4**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <.
2. Compare **4/8 and 7/8**. Write the comparison with >, =, or <.

*Success criterion:* both exit items correct with the reasoning named
(same numerator → compare piece sizes; same denominator → count
pieces) — not just a memorized symbol. If the learner compares
denominators alone, re-lay the unit pieces in size order before
Session 13.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** let the learner say the comparison aloud ("three
  fourths is greater than three eighths") while the adult writes the
  symbols; the learner points to the bigger shaded part.
- **Alternate response mode:** shade both fractions on strips and point
  to the longer shading instead of writing symbols; the adult records
  the comparison.
- **Extension:** order 1/8, 1/3, 2/3, and 5/6 from smallest to largest,
  explaining each step. Then find a pair of fractions that are equal
  even though they look different (for example, 2/4 and 3/6), and
  explain why the = symbol is correct there.

## Teacher note

The defining misconception in this lesson is **comparing denominators
alone** — reading a bigger denominator as a bigger fraction (1/8 > 1/2)
or a bigger numerator as a bigger fraction without checking piece size.
It comes from treating fraction numerals as whole numbers. The fix is
always the same whole: lay the unit pieces side by side in size order
(1/2 > 1/3 > 1/4 > 1/6 > 1/8) until the "fewer cuts, bigger pieces"
rule is visible, then count pieces only when the denominators match. A
second slip is comparing fractions from different-size wholes; insist
that both strips or both number lines start the same length before any
comparison is recorded. Answer key:
