# Lesson 01 — Equal Parts and Unit Fractions

**Session:** 1 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can split a whole into
equal parts, name one part as a unit fraction, and use the words numerator
and denominator.

**Materials:** paper strips (2 per learner), scissors, crayons or pencils,
fraction strips (halves, thirds, fourths, sixths, eighths) if available.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min, Unit 04 bridge):** "12 ÷ 3 = ?" (4 — twelve
shared into 3 equal shares gives 4 in each share.) "16 ÷ 4 = ?" (4.) "When
we share equally, what must be true about every share?" (Every share gets
the same number — the shares are equal.) Today we split wholes the same
way: into parts that are all the same size.

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "Fold a paper strip in half. Are the two parts the same size?"
(Yes — fold so the ends meet exactly.) "Fold it in half again. How many
parts do you have now?" (4 parts.) "Are all four parts the same size?"
(Yes, if both folds were exact.) "What does *equal* mean when we split
something?" (Every part is the same size — no part may be bigger or
smaller.) If folding is shaky, the adult folds the first strip while the
learner watches the ends meet, then the learner folds the second strip.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

A **whole** is one thing: one strip, one pizza, one rectangle. When we
split a whole into parts that are all the **same size**, the parts are
called **equal parts**.

One part of a whole split into equal parts is a **unit fraction**. It is
written with a **numerator** on top and a **denominator** on the bottom:

- The **denominator** (bottom number) tells how many equal parts are in
  the whole.
- The **numerator** (top number) tells how many parts we are counting.
  For a unit fraction, the numerator is always 1 — we are counting one
  part.

So if a strip is split into 4 equal parts, one part is **1/4**, read as
"one fourth." The 4 says the whole has 4 equal parts; the 1 says we are
counting 1 of them.

In this unit we use denominators **2, 3, 4, 6, and 8** only:

- 2 equal parts → one part is **1/2** (one half)
- 3 equal parts → one part is **1/3** (one third)
- 4 equal parts → one part is **1/4** (one fourth)
- 6 equal parts → one part is **1/6** (one sixth)
- 8 equal parts → one part is **1/8** (one eighth)

The parts must be equal for the name to work. If a whole is split into 4
parts but one part is bigger than the others, none of the parts is a
fourth — they are just 4 pieces. Equal size is what makes a fraction a
fraction.

## Worked example 1 — 1/4 of a paper strip

1. Start with one whole paper strip. Say: "This strip is the whole."
2. Fold it in half so the ends meet exactly. Crease it. Now there are 2
   equal parts.
3. Fold it in half again, ends meeting exactly. Crease it. Now there are
   4 parts.
4. Open the strip and check: hold the parts up against each other. All 4
   parts are the same size — the parts are equal. ✓
5. Point to one part. That is one part out of 4 equal parts. Write
   **1/4**. Say it: "One fourth."
6. Check the numbers: the denominator is 4 because the whole has 4 equal
   parts; the numerator is 1 because we are counting 1 part.
7. Common slip: folding sloppily so the parts are different sizes and
   still calling one part "one fourth." If the parts are not equal, the
   name does not work — refold so the ends meet exactly.

## Worked example 2 — 1/6 of a pizza

1. Read: one pizza (the whole) is cut into 6 slices. Write the question:
   "What fraction is one slice?"
2. Check first: are all 6 slices the same size? Imagine the pizza cut
   like a clock face into 6 matching slices. Yes — the slices are equal.
   (This is a pretend pizza for practice; no real pizza is needed.)
3. Count the equal parts in the whole: 6. That is the denominator.
   Count the parts we are naming: 1 slice. That is the numerator.
4. Write **1/6**. Say it: "One sixth."
5. Check: 6 equal parts in the whole, 1 part counted. ✓
6. Common slip: cutting the pizza into 6 slices of different sizes — one
   big slice and five small ones — and calling the big slice "one sixth."
   Unequal parts are not sixths. The slices must all match before any
   slice can be named 1/6.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner does
the folding, shading, and writing.

1. Fold a paper strip into **2 equal parts**. Name one part and write the
   fraction.
   *(Hint: fold once, ends meeting exactly. How many equal parts is the
   whole split into? That number goes on the bottom.)*
2. A rectangle is split into **3 equal parts**. Name one part and write
   the fraction.
   *(Hint: the whole has 3 equal parts, so the denominator is 3. How many
   parts are we counting? Just one.)*
3. A strip is split into **8 equal parts**. What fraction is one part?
   *(Hint: the denominator counts all the equal parts in the whole. The
   numerator counts the parts we name — here, one.)*
4. A circle is cut into **4 parts, but one part is bigger than the
   others**. Is one part 1/4? Why or why not?
   *(Hint: check the rule — are the parts equal? A fraction name needs
   equal parts.)*
5. Error analysis: Leo split a bar into **6 parts — 5 small ones and 1
   big one** — and said one part is 1/6. What did Leo get wrong? What
   must be true before a part can be called one sixth?
   *(Hint: look at the sizes. Are all 6 parts the same size? What does
   Leo need to fix first?)*

## Independent practice

6. A square is split into 4 equal parts. Shade one part. Write the
   fraction for the shaded part.
7. Fold a paper strip into 6 equal parts. Name one part and write the
   fraction.
8. A circle is split into 2 equal parts. Write the fraction for one part.
9. A rectangle is split into 8 equal parts. What fraction is one part?
10. True or false: **"A bar cut into 3 unequal parts has parts that are
    thirds."** Explain your answer.
11. Error analysis: Ana folded a strip into 4 parts, but her folds were
    uneven, so the parts are different sizes. She wrote 1/4 for one
    part. What is wrong? What must she do first before she can write
    1/4?

## Application

Fold a paper strip into **4 equal parts**. Label each part **1/4**.
Then write one sentence explaining what 1/4 means — use the words
"equal parts," "whole," and "one part" in your sentence. Check your fold
by holding the parts against each other: if any part is a different size,
refold and label again.

## Exit check

1. A rectangle is split into **6 equal parts**. Write the fraction for
   one part.
2. A strip is split into **3 equal parts**. Name one part and write the
   fraction.

*Success criterion:* both exit items correct with the parts checked equal
(folded, drawn, or described) — not just a memorized answer. If the
learner names a fraction from unequal parts, refold or redraw together so
the parts match before Session 2.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** let the learner say the fraction ("one part out
  of four equal parts") while the adult writes 1/4; the learner points to
  the denominator and says what it counts.
- **Alternate response mode:** fold and point only, and answer orally;
  the adult records the written fractions.
- **Extension:** fold a strip into 8 equal parts and write the unit
  fraction for one part. Then compare: is 1/2 of a strip bigger or
  smaller than 1/8 of the same strip? Explain using the number of equal
  parts — fewer parts means each part is bigger (1/2 is bigger than 1/8).

## Teacher note

The defining misconception in this lesson is **naming any piece of a
split whole a fraction, even when the pieces are unequal** — "it is cut
into 4 pieces, so each piece is a fourth." It comes from counting pieces
instead of checking size. The fix is physical: no fraction is written
until the parts are checked equal by folding, overlaying, or holding them
side by side. A second slip is swapping the numerator and denominator
(writing 4/1 for one part of four); anchor the denominator as "how many
equal parts in the whole" every time. Answer key:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
