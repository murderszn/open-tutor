# Lesson 05 — Division Word Problems

**Session:** 11 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can read a division
word problem, decide whether it asks for shares or for groups, write an
equation with a symbol for the unknown, and solve it with a stated meaning
for the quotient.

**Materials:** 60+ counters, paper and pencil.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "42 ÷ 6 = ?" (7.) "When does 7 mean '7 in
each share' and when does it mean '7 groups'?" (When 42 is shared into 6
shares, 7 is per share; when 42 is split into groups of 6, 7 is the group
count.) "Today the story decides."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "What is the unknown-factor question for 54 ÷ 6?" (? × 6 = 54;
9 × 6 = 54, so 9.) "Write 63 ÷ 7 = ? as a missing-factor question."
(? × 7 = 63; 9.) If the learner cannot yet turn a division into its
multiplication question, spend this session's teaching time on that turn
before tackling stories — stories with no strategy become guessing.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

Every division word problem is solved the same way, in four steps:

1. **Find the total.** What is being shared or grouped? (54 pencils,
   72 minutes, 48 chairs.)
2. **Ask the story question.** Do we know the *number of shares* (fair
   sharing) or the *size of each group* (group counting)? The story's
   wording tells you:
   - "shared equally among 6 friends" → number of shares known (6);
     find how many each gets.
   - "6 pencils per box — how many boxes?" → group size known (6);
     find how many groups.
   - Arrays: "48 chairs in 8 rows" → rows known; find chairs per row
     (fair sharing across rows). "48 chairs, 8 per row" → group size
     known; find rows.
   - Measurement: "72 minutes of songs, 9 minutes per song — how many
     songs?" → group size known; find groups.
3. **Write the equation with a symbol for the unknown.** Use ? or a
   letter for the number you are finding, and put it where it belongs:
   54 ÷ 6 = ? (unknown quotient), or 6 × ? = 54 (unknown factor) — both
   describe the same problem.
4. **Solve, then say what the answer counts.** "9 pencils per friend"
   or "9 boxes" — and multiply back to check.

Worked problems below model all three story types: equal groups (sharing),
arrays, and measurement quantities.

## Worked example 1 — Equal groups: sharing pencils

*Ms. Ana shares 54 pencils equally among 6 students. How many pencils does
each student get?*

1. Total: 54 pencils. Story question: number of shares known (6 students)
   → fair sharing; find pencils per student.
2. Equation: **54 ÷ 6 = ?**
3. Unknown-factor question: ? × 6 = 54 — 9 × 6 = 54, so ? = 9.
4. Answer: **9 pencils per student.** Multiply back: 6 × 9 = 54. ✓
5. Meaning check: 9 counts pencils *per student*, because the story fixed
   the number of students.

## Worked example 2 — Array: chairs in rows

*A hall has 48 chairs arranged in 8 equal rows. How many chairs are in
each row?*

1. Total: 48 chairs. Story question: rows known (8) → sharing across
   rows; find chairs per row.
2. Equation: **48 ÷ 8 = ?**
3. Unknown-factor question: ? × 8 = 48 — 6 × 8 = 48, so ? = 6.
4. Answer: **6 chairs per row.** Multiply back: 8 × 6 = 48. ✓
5. Array picture (text alternative): 8 rows, 6 chairs in each row — a
   rectangle 8 by 6, totaling 48. If the story had said "8 chairs per row,"
   the same equation 48 ÷ 8 = 6 would instead answer "6 rows" — same
   numbers, different question, and the sentence after the number must
   say which.

## Worked example 3 — Measurement: songs on a playlist

*A playlist lasts 72 minutes. Each song is 9 minutes long. How many songs
are on the playlist?*

1. Total: 72 minutes. Story question: group size known (9 minutes per
   song) → group counting; find the number of songs.
2. Equation: **72 ÷ 9 = ?**
3. Unknown-factor question: ? × 9 = 72 — 8 × 9 = 72, so ? = 8.
4. Answer: **8 songs.** Multiply back: 8 × 9 = 72. ✓
5. Meaning check: 8 counts *songs*, not minutes per song — the 9 minutes
   per song was given in the story.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner names
the story type, writes the equation, and states the meaning.

1. **63 apples shared equally into 7 baskets.** How many apples per
   basket? Name the story type first.
   *(Hint: "shared equally into 7" fixes the number of shares.)*
2. **40 students sit in rows of 5. How many rows?** Name the story type
   first.
   *(Hint: "rows of 5" fixes the group size — count the groups.)*
3. **A ribbon 56 cm long is cut into pieces 8 cm long. How many pieces?**
   Name the story type first.
   *(Hint: measurement quantities — the piece size is fixed, so count
   pieces: 56 ÷ 8.)*
4. **35 stickers shared equally among 5 friends.** How many stickers per
   friend? Write the equation with a symbol for the unknown.
   *(Hint: 35 ÷ 5 = ? — ask ? × 5 = 35.)*
5. Error analysis: Ben read "24 cookies shared equally among 6 friends"
   and wrote 24 ÷ 6 = 4 groups. What did Ben get right, and what did he
   get wrong?
   *(Hint: the number 4 is right, but 4 counts cookies per friend, not
   groups — the story fixed 6 friends, so the quotient is a share size.)*

## Independent practice

6. **72 juice boxes shared equally among 8 tables.** How many boxes per
   table? Write the equation.
7. **81 stickers placed 9 stickers per page.** How many pages? Write the
   equation.
8. **42 chairs in 6 equal rows.** How many chairs per row? Write the
   equation.
9. **A rope 64 m long is cut into 8 m pieces.** How many pieces? Write
   the equation.
10. **56 marbles shared equally among 7 friends.** How many marbles per
    friend? Write the equation and the multiply-back check.
11. Two readings: write a fair-share story AND a group-count story for
    **30 ÷ 5 = 6**, and say what the 6 counts in each story.
12. Error analysis: Zoe read "48 ÷ 8 = 6" for "48 apples, 8 apples per
    bag" and answered "6 apples per bag." What is wrong? What should the
    answer be, in a full sentence?

## Application

Invent two division word problems of your own — one fair-share story and
one group-count story — using different totals within 100. For each: name
the story type, write the equation with a symbol for the unknown, solve
it, state the answer in a full sentence, and multiply back.

## Exit check

1. **42 cookies packed 7 cookies per bag. How many bags?** Write the
   equation and answer in a full sentence.
2. **35 pencils shared equally among 5 students. How many pencils per
   student?** Write the equation and answer in a full sentence.

*Success criterion:* both exit items correct with the equation and a full
sentence stating what the quotient counts. A bare number, even if right,
is sent back for its sentence before Session 12.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** let the learner say the story type ("shares known"
  or "group size known") while the adult writes the equation; the learner
  acts the story out with counters before writing.
- **Alternate response mode:** act out every story with counters and
  answer orally; the adult records the written equations.
- **Extension:** take the array story from Worked example 2 and reword it
  so the same equation answers the other question (48 chairs, 8 per row
  → 6 rows), then explain why the equation did not change but the
  sentence after the answer did.

## Teacher note

The defining misconception in this lesson is **computing before reading**:
grabbing two numbers, dividing, and reporting a bare quotient. The fix is
the four-step routine, enforced in order: total, story question, equation,
sentence. Learners who name the story type first almost never swap the
quotient's meaning. Answer key: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-05).
