# Lesson 02 — How Many Groups?

**Session:** 5 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can find how many equal
groups of a known size fit into a total, and write the matching division
equation — and I can tell this question apart from fair sharing.

**Materials:** 60+ counters, paper and pencil.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "24 ÷ 6 = ?" (4 — 24 shared into 6 shares
gives 4 in each.) "6 × 4 = ?" (24.) "Today the same equation answers a
different question."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "Count by 7s to 42." (7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42 — six counts.) "Count
by 8s to 56." (8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56 — seven counts.) "What is 5 groups
of 6?" (30.) If skip counting by 7s or 8s is shaky, build the groups with
counters today and count the groups, not the skip-count chant, first.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

Lesson 01 asked: *I know the number of shares — how many in each share?*
Today's question is the mirror image: *I know the size of each group — how
many groups are there?*

Example: 56 muffins, packed **8 muffins to a box**. How many boxes?
We do not deal into 8 plates — there is no "8 plates" here. Instead we
**make groups of 8, again and again, until 56 is used up**, then count the
groups: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56 — that is 7 groups. So **56 ÷ 8 = 7**,
and this time the 7 means *7 boxes*, not *7 muffins per box*.

Same equation, two meanings — compare:

- **56 ÷ 8 = 7 as fair shares (Lesson 01):** 56 shared into **8 shares**
  gives **7 in each share**. The 8 is the number of shares; the 7 is the
  size of one share.
- **56 ÷ 8 = 7 as group count (today):** 56 split into **groups of 8**
  gives **7 groups**. The 8 is the size of one group; the 7 is the number
  of groups.

The story tells you which question you are answering. "Shared equally
among 8 friends" is a fair-share story. "8 in each box — how many boxes?"
is a group-count story. The division equation is the same; the *meaning of
the quotient* changes. A complete answer always says what the number counts:
"7 boxes" or "7 muffins per share" — never a bare 7.

Method for group counting: **skip count by the group size and count your
counts.** For 56 ÷ 8: say 8 (1), 16 (2), 24 (3), 32 (4), 40 (5), 48 (6),
56 (7) — seven counts, so 7 groups. Or build it: lay out groups of 8
counters until you reach 56, then count the groups.

## Worked example 1 — 42 apples, 7 apples per basket

1. Read: total 42, group size 7. Question: how many baskets? Write
   **42 ÷ 7 = ?**
2. Skip count by 7s, counting counts: 7 (1), 14 (2), 21 (3), 28 (4),
   35 (5), 42 (6). Six counts.
3. Equation: **42 ÷ 7 = 6.** There are 6 baskets.
4. Multiply back: 6 baskets × 7 apples = 42. ✓
5. Contrast with fair sharing: 42 ÷ 7 could also mean 42 shared into
   7 shares (6 apples per share). Same equation, same quotient — but here
   the 6 counts *baskets*, because the story fixed the group size at 7.

## Worked example 2 — 30 chairs, 5 chairs per row

1. Read: total 30, group size 5. Question: how many rows? Write
   **30 ÷ 5 = ?**
2. Build groups of 5 counters: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 — six groups.
3. Equation: **30 ÷ 5 = 6.** There are 6 rows.
4. Multiply back: 6 × 5 = 30. ✓
5. Check the meaning: the quotient 6 counts rows, not chairs per row.
   Each row still has 5 chairs — that number was given in the story.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner does the
grouping and writing.

1. Make groups of 6 from **36 counters**. How many groups? Write the
   equation.
   *(Hint: skip count by 6s and count your counts: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36.)*
2. Make groups of 8 from **48 counters**. How many groups? Write the
   equation.
   *(Hint: 8 × ? = 48 from Unit 02 — the group count is the missing factor.)*
3. Make groups of 9 from **27 counters**. How many groups? Write the
   equation. Then say what the quotient counts.
   *(Hint: the story would be "9 in each group — how many groups?")*
4. **54 counters, groups of 9.** How many groups? Write the equation.
   *(Hint: count by 9s: 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54.)*
5. Error analysis: Tom had **40 counters in groups of 8** and said there
   are 8 groups. What did Tom mix up? How many groups are there really?
   *(Hint: 8 groups of 8 would be 64 counters, not 40. Skip count by 8s
   to 40 and count the counts.)*

## Independent practice

6. Groups of 7 from 63 counters. How many groups? Write the equation.
7. Groups of 8 from 72 counters. How many groups? Write the equation.
8. Groups of 5 from 20 counters. How many groups? Write the equation.
9. Groups of 9 from 81 counters. How many groups? Write the equation.
10. Groups of 8 from 24 counters. How many groups? Write the equation.
11. A baker packs **45 muffins, 9 muffins per box**. How many boxes?
    Write the equation and say what the quotient counts.
12. True or false: **56 ÷ 8 = 7** always means "7 in each share." Explain
    with a group-count story that proves it false.

## Application

Invent a group-count story for **48 ÷ 6** — choose your own objects and
group size (not chairs or baskets). Build the groups with counters, count
the groups, write the equation, and write one sentence saying what the
quotient counts and why it is not "6 in each share."

## Exit check

1. Groups of 9 from 45 counters. How many groups? Write the equation and
   say what the quotient counts.
2. Groups of 7 from 56 counters. How many groups? Write the equation and
   say what the quotient counts.

*Success criterion:* both exit items correct, each with the quotient's
meaning stated ("5 groups" / "8 groups"). A bare number with no meaning
is not yet complete — ask "5 what?" and have the learner finish the
sentence before Session 6.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** let the learner say the story ("45 muffins, 9 per
  box — how many boxes?") while the adult writes the equation; the learner
  points to each finished group as they count groups.
- **Alternate response mode:** build every group with counters and answer
  orally; the adult records the written equations.
- **Extension:** find two stories for **36 ÷ 4** — one fair-share story
  (36 shared among 4 friends) and one group-count story (36 items, 4 per
  bag) — and explain why both give quotient 4 but the 4 means different
  things.

## Teacher note

The defining misconception in this lesson is **answering the wrong
question**: dealing into shares when the story fixed a group size, or
reporting a bare quotient with no unit of meaning. The fix is a story
question asked before any computing: "Do we know the number of shares, or
the size of each group?" If the learner cannot answer that, they are not
ready to compute. Answer key: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
