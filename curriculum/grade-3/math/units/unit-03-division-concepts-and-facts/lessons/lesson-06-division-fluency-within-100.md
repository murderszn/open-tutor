# Lesson 06 — Division Fluency Within 100

**Session:** 12 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can divide within 100
using a strategy for every fact — think multiplication, skip-count the
groups, or break the total apart — and I can explain which strategy I used.

**Materials:** 60+ counters, a 10 × 10 multiplication chart, index cards.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "63 ÷ 7 = ?" (9 — ? × 7 = 63.) "48 ÷ 6 = ?"
(8.) "Which facts did you know at once, and which did you have to think
through? Both are fine — today we name the thinking."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "What times 8 makes 56?" (7.) "Count by 9s to 54, counting your
counts." (9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54 — six counts.) "Break 72 into two numbers
you can each divide by 8." (40 and 32: 40 ÷ 8 = 5, 32 ÷ 8 = 4.) If breaking
apart is new, model it on the board today before any independent work —
it is the lesson's third strategy, not a review.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

"Fluent" does not mean instant, and it never means timed. A fluent
divider has a **reliable strategy for every fact within 100** and knows
the easy ones from memory. Three strategies cover everything:

1. **Think multiplication (fastest when the fact is known).** For 56 ÷ 7,
   ask ? × 7 = 56. If 8 × 7 = 56 is known, the answer is 8 at once.
   This is the default strategy — try it first.
2. **Skip-count the groups (reliable when the fact is not yet known).**
   For 45 ÷ 9, count by 9s and count your counts: 9 (1), 18 (2), 27 (3),
   36 (4), 45 (5) — five counts, so 45 ÷ 9 = 5. Slower, always correct.
3. **Break the total apart (for big totals).** For 72 ÷ 8, split 72 into
   friendly parts that are each divisible by 8: 40 + 32. Then
   40 ÷ 8 = 5 and 32 ÷ 8 = 4, so 72 ÷ 8 = 5 + 4 = 9. Check: 9 × 8 = 72. ✓
   The split must use parts you *can* divide — 40 and 32 both work with 8;
   50 and 22 would not, because 22 ÷ 8 is not a whole number in this
   unit's work.

Patterns that save work:

- **Dividing by 1:** the quotient is the total itself (45 ÷ 1 = 45 —
  one share gets everything).
- **Dividing a number by itself:** the quotient is 1 (45 ÷ 45 = 1 — but
  in this unit's within-100 facts, that looks like 9 ÷ 9 = 1, 8 ÷ 8 = 1).
- **Turnaround partners:** if 72 ÷ 8 = 9 is known, then 72 ÷ 9 = 8 comes
  free — same family.
- **Tens:** 90 ÷ 9 = 10 and 100 ÷ 10 = 10 use the same "how many 9s (or
  10s) fit?" thinking: 10 × 9 = 90, 10 × 10 = 100.

Choose the strategy, say it aloud, then check by multiplying back. Saying
the strategy is part of the answer in this lesson: "56 ÷ 7 = 8, by thinking
8 × 7 = 56."

## Worked example 1 — 56 ÷ 7, three ways

1. Think multiplication: ? × 7 = 56 — 8 × 7 = 56, so 56 ÷ 7 = 8.
2. Skip-count: 7 (1), 14 (2), 21 (3), 28 (4), 35 (5), 42 (6), 49 (7),
   56 (8) — eight counts, so 8. ✓
3. Break apart: 56 = 28 + 28; 28 ÷ 7 = 4 and 28 ÷ 7 = 4; 4 + 4 = 8. ✓
4. All three roads reach 8. The learner picks the road they trust most
   for this fact — and a different road for the next fact is fine.

## Worked example 2 — 81 ÷ 9 and 63 ÷ 7

1. **81 ÷ 9:** think multiplication — ? × 9 = 81. 9 × 9 = 81 (a square
   number), so 81 ÷ 9 = 9. Multiply back: 9 × 9 = 81. ✓
2. **63 ÷ 7:** break apart — 63 = 35 + 28. 35 ÷ 7 = 5, 28 ÷ 7 = 4,
   5 + 4 = 9. So 63 ÷ 7 = 9. Multiply back: 9 × 7 = 63. ✓
   (Or think multiplication: ? × 7 = 63 — 9 × 7 = 63. Same answer.)
3. Notice: both quotients are 9, but the facts are different — 81 ÷ 9
   and 63 ÷ 7 are not partners. Partners share a total: 63 ÷ 7 = 9 pairs
   with 63 ÷ 9 = 7.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner names
the strategy aloud, then solves and checks.

1. Solve **48 ÷ 6** by thinking multiplication. Name the strategy and
   the fact.
   *(Hint: ? × 6 = 48 — 8 × 6 = 48.)*
2. Solve **54 ÷ 9** by skip-counting. Count your counts aloud.
   *(Hint: 9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54 — how many counts?)*
3. Solve **36 ÷ 4** by thinking multiplication. Name the strategy.
   *(Hint: ? × 4 = 36 — 9 × 4 = 36.)*
4. Solve **72 ÷ 8** by breaking apart. Choose friendly parts and show
   both divisions.
   *(Hint: 72 = 40 + 32; 40 ÷ 8 = 5, 32 ÷ 8 = 4.)*
5. Error analysis: Sam solved **27 ÷ 3** by skip-counting "3, 6, 9" and
   stopped, answering 3. What did Sam do wrong? What is the quotient
   really?
   *(Hint: Sam stopped at 9, not at the total 27. Keep counting: 3, 6, 9,
   12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27 — nine counts, so 27 ÷ 3 = 9.)*

## Independent practice

6. Solve 64 ÷ 8. Name your strategy.
7. Solve 45 ÷ 9. Name your strategy.
8. Solve 56 ÷ 8. Name your strategy.
9. Solve 42 ÷ 7. Name your strategy.
10. Solve 63 ÷ 7 by breaking apart (35 + 28). Show both divisions.
11. Solve 90 ÷ 9. Explain the tens pattern you used.
12. Solve 100 ÷ 10. Explain it with a multiplication fact.
13. Solve 30 ÷ 6 and 18 ÷ 2. For each, name your strategy.
14. Solve 24 ÷ 3. Then write its turnaround partner division and solve
    that too.
15. Error analysis: Ana solved **54 ÷ 6** as 8, "because 6 × 8 = 48 is
    close." What is wrong? What is the quotient really, and how does
    multiplying back catch the error?

## Application

Make a personal strategy card for the three division facts you find
hardest within 100. For each fact: write the division, your chosen
strategy, the full working, and the multiply-back check. Then explain in
one sentence per fact why you chose that strategy for that fact.

## Exit check

1. Solve **49 ÷ 7**. Name your strategy and multiply back.
2. Solve **80 ÷ 8**. Name your strategy and multiply back.

*Success criterion:* both exit items correct with a named strategy and a
multiply-back check. Speed is not scored; a correct answer with no
strategy named is sent back for the strategy before the unit assessment.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** let the learner say the strategy ("I thought
  7 × 7 = 49") while the adult writes the equation; the learner points
  to the chart row for the divisor.
- **Alternate response mode:** solve orally with counters or the chart;
  the adult records the written working.
- **Extension:** solve 96 ÷ 8 by breaking apart two ways (80 + 16:
  10 + 2 = 12; 40 + 56: 5 + 7 = 12) and explain why both splits work —
  then check: 12 × 8 = 96. ✓ (Note: 96 is within 100 and its quotient 12
  goes past the one-digit facts; this extension previews Unit 04's
  two-digit quotients without requiring them.)

## Teacher note

The defining misconception in this lesson is **"close is correct"**
(54 ÷ 6 = 8 because 48 is near 54) and **stopping a skip-count early**.
Both come from treating division as estimation. The fix is the
multiply-back check, required on every item: 8 × 6 = 48 ≠ 54, so 8 is
wrong; the fact that lands exactly on 54 is 9 × 6. Answer key:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-06).
