# Lesson 04 — Finding Unknowns in Equations

**Session:** 9 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Goal:** I can find the unknown
whole number in a multiplication or division equation, wherever the unknown
sits, and prove it by multiplying back or substituting.

**Materials:** 40+ counters, index cards, a 10 × 10 multiplication chart.

**Retrieval warm-up (2 min):** "32 ÷ 8 = ?" (4 — ? × 8 = 32, and 4 × 8 = 32.)
"Write the family for 4, 8, 32." (4 × 8 = 32, 8 × 4 = 32, 32 ÷ 8 = 4,
32 ÷ 4 = 8.) "Today the unknown can hide in any spot."

## Readiness check (3 min)

Ask aloud: "What does ? mean in 8 × ? = 48?" (A number we do not know yet
— the number that makes the equation true.) "What times 6 makes 42?" (7.)
"72 shared into 8 shares gives what?" (9.) If the learner treats ? as
"the answer goes here" without checking, today's rule fixes it: the unknown
is the number that *makes the equation true*, and we prove it by putting
it back in.

## Explicit teaching (8 min)

An **unknown** is a number hiding behind a symbol — ?, _, or a letter like
*n*. Our job is to find the number that makes the equation true, then
**prove it by putting it back in**.

There are four hiding spots, and each has a move:

1. **Unknown factor:** 8 × ? = 48. Ask: "What times 8 makes 48?" 6 × 8 = 48,
   so ? = 6. Prove: 8 × 6 = 48. ✓
2. **Unknown quotient:** 48 ÷ 8 = ?. Ask the unknown-factor question:
   "? × 8 = 48" — same question, same answer: 6. Prove: 6 × 8 = 48. ✓
   (Unknown quotients are just Lesson 03 in equation form.)
3. **Unknown dividend:** _ ÷ 3 = 5. The total is hiding. Ask: "What number
   shared into 3 shares gives 5 each?" Undo it: 3 shares × 5 each = 15, so
   _ = 15. Prove: 15 ÷ 3 = 5. ✓ Rule: **to find a missing dividend,
   multiply the divisor by the quotient.**
4. **Unknown divisor:** 72 ÷ ? = 8. The group size (or share count) is
   hiding. Ask: "72 split into groups of what size gives 8 groups?" Undo
   it: 8 groups × 8 per group = 72, so ? = 9 — because 72 ÷ 9 = 8. Prove:
   72 ÷ 9 = 8. ✓ Rule: **to find a missing divisor, divide the dividend
   by the quotient** — or ask "? × 8 = 72" and find 9 that way. Both roads
   reach 9.

One habit covers all four: **find the fact family first**. The three
numbers in the equation belong to one family; the unknown is the family
member that is missing. For 8 × ? = 48, the family is (6, 8, 48): the
missing member is 6. For _ ÷ 3 = 5, the family is (3, 5, 15): the missing
member is 15.

Watch out for the two classic traps: **adding to find an unknown**
(? ÷ 7 = 6 is not 13 — 6 + 7 is not how division works; 6 × 7 = 42 is) and
**echoing a visible number** (8 × ? = 56 is not 8 — 8 × 8 = 64, not 56;
the answer is 7).

## Worked example 1 — Four hiding spots, one family

Family (7, 9, 63). Solve each and prove it:

1. **9 × ? = 63.** Ask: what times 9 makes 63? 7 × 9 = 63, so ? = 7.
   Prove: 9 × 7 = 63. ✓
2. **63 ÷ 9 = ?.** Ask: ? × 9 = 63 — same question. ? = 7. Prove:
   7 × 9 = 63. ✓
3. **_ ÷ 9 = 7.** Missing dividend: multiply divisor × quotient:
   9 × 7 = 63, so _ = 63. Prove: 63 ÷ 9 = 7. ✓
4. **63 ÷ ? = 9.** Missing divisor: ask ? × 9 = 63 — ? = 7. Prove:
   63 ÷ 7 = 9. ✓
5. Notice: all four equations are the same family wearing different
   costumes. Finding the family finds every unknown in it.

## Worked example 2 — _ ÷ 4 = 9 and 48 ÷ ? = 8

1. **_ ÷ 4 = 9.** The total is missing. Multiply back: 4 × 9 = 36, so
   _ = 36. Prove: 36 ÷ 4 = 9. ✓ Family: (4, 9, 36).
2. **48 ÷ ? = 8.** The divisor is missing. Ask: ? × 8 = 48 — 6 × 8 = 48,
   so ? = 6. Prove: 48 ÷ 6 = 8. ✓ Family: (6, 8, 48).
3. Common slip on #2: answering 8 ("8 is in the problem") — but
   48 ÷ 8 = 6, not 8, so 8 cannot be the divisor that gives quotient 8.
   Always put the candidate back into the original equation and check.

## Guided practice (with hints)

Work through these together; the adult gives the hint, the learner finds
the unknown and proves it.

1. Solve **7 × ? = 56**. Name the family and prove it.
   *(Hint: what times 7 makes 56? 8 × 7 = 56.)*
2. Solve **? × 8 = 64**. Name the family and prove it.
   *(Hint: 8 × 8 = 64 — a square-number family.)*
3. Solve **54 ÷ ? = 6**. Is the unknown a divisor or a dividend? Prove it.
   *(Hint: ask ? × 6 = 54. 9 × 6 = 54.)*
4. Solve **_ ÷ 5 = 7**. Prove it by putting the answer back in.
   *(Hint: missing total — multiply 5 × 7.)*
5. Error analysis: Ana solved **? ÷ 7 = 6** and wrote 13, "because
   6 + 7 = 13." What did Ana mix up? What is the unknown really?
   *(Hint: division undoes multiplication, not addition. 6 shares of 7
   is 6 × 7 = 42. Prove: 42 ÷ 7 = 6.)*

## Independent practice

7. Solve ? × 4 = 36.

9. Solve _ ÷ 9 = 6.
10. Solve 6 × ? = 54.
11. Solve ? × 8 = 72 — then write the division equation that shares that

## Application

Write four equations of your own — one for each hiding spot — all from a
single fact family you choose (within 100). Solve each, prove each by
putting the answer back in, and write one sentence explaining why one
family answers four different-looking questions.

## Exit check

1. Solve **6 × ? = 54**. Prove it.
2. Solve **_ ÷ 8 = 7**. Prove it.

*Success criterion:* both exit items correct with a proof shown
(multiply-back or substitution). A correct number with no proof is half
credit — ask "how do you know?" and have the learner prove it before
Session 10.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** let the learner say the question ("What times 6
  makes 54?") while the adult writes the equation; the learner points to
  the chart row for the divisor.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer orally with counters or the chart;
  the adult records the written equations and proofs.
- **Extension:** solve ? ÷ 6 = 8 and 48 ÷ ? = 6 and explain why both
  answers are 48 and 8 respectively — the same family (6, 8, 48) with
  different members hiding — then invent a hiding-spot puzzle for the
  adult to solve.

## Teacher note

The defining misconceptions in this lesson are **adding to undo division**
(? ÷ 7 = 6 → 13) and **echoing a visible number** (8 × ? = 56 → 8). Both
come from treating the symbol as decoration. The fix is the proof habit:
no unknown is finished until it is put back into the original equation
and the equation is checked true. Answer key:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
