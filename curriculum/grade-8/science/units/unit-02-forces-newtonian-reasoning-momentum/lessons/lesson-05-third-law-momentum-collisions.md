# Lesson 05 — Newton's Third Law and Momentum in Collisions

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can name action–reaction force pairs in a
collision, explain why they don't cancel, and use momentum ($p = mv$)
to compare what happens when things crash into each other.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, two balls of different
mass (e.g., tennis ball, heavier rubber ball), index cards ·
**Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult pushes a wall lightly. "Did the wall push back?" Learners
laugh — then the adult asks them to push the wall *hard* and notice
their hands. The wall pushed back exactly as hard as they pushed. That
is the whole law, felt first.

## Explanation

Newton's **third law**: for every action there is an equal and opposite
**reaction** — when object A pushes on object B, B pushes back on A
with the same size force in the opposite direction. These are
**action–reaction pairs**, and the catch is: the two forces act on
*different objects*, so they never cancel each other out. Cancellation
only happens when two forces act on the *same* object (Lesson 1).

- A swimmer pushes water backward; the water pushes the swimmer
  forward. Same size, opposite directions, different objects.
- A rocket throws exhaust down; the exhaust throws the rocket up.
- Your foot pushes the floor back-and-down while walking; the floor
  pushes your foot forward-and-up. Walking is a controlled series of
  third-law shoves.

In a collision, the third law runs the whole show: the moving car
pushes the wall, and the wall pushes the car with an equal force — the
car stops *because* of the wall's push on it. To compare collisions we
use **momentum**: $p = m \times v$ (kilogram-meters per second,
kg·m/s). Momentum combines *how much stuff* with *how fast* — a slow
truck can carry more momentum than a fast bicycle.

Collision rules of thumb (qualitative, grade-8 level):

1. More incoming momentum → harder to stop → bigger forces during the
   stop and more "damage" to whatever absorbs it.
2. The *same* momentum change can be gentle or violent depending on
   **time**: a crumple zone or airbag stretches the stop over more
   time, so the force at any instant is smaller. (The full impulse
   story waits for later physics; the design project uses this idea
   qualitatively.)
3. Momentum transfers: a rolling cart that hits a light block gives the
   block some of its motion — heavier or faster carts shove the block
   farther. This is the Sessions 8–9 lab's collision extension and the
   design project's core evidence.

**Vocabulary:** *Newton's third law* (equal, opposite forces on two
objects), *action–reaction pair*, *momentum* ($p = mv$), *collision*,
*crumple zone* (structure that lengthens stopping time).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A 0.5 kg ball moving at 2 m/s: $p = 0.5 \times 2 =
\textbf{1 kg·m/s}$. A 0.25 kg ball moving at 4 m/s: $p = 0.25 \times 4
= \textbf{1 kg·m/s}$. **Equal momentum** — the light fast ball and the
heavy slow ball carry the same "oomph." *Neither mass nor speed alone
decides a collision.*

**Example 2.** Skater A: 40 kg at 3 m/s → $p = 120$ kg·m/s. Skater B:
60 kg at 2 m/s → $p = 120$ kg·m/s. They push off each other (third
law!). Each feels the same-size push, but Skater A (less mass) gains
speed faster — $a = F/m$ again, the second law hiding inside the third.

**Example 3.** Two toy cars hit a wall at the same speed. Car X has a
rigid bumper; Car Y has a foam crumple bumper. During the crash the
wall pushes each car with the force needed to stop its momentum — but
Y's bumper crushes over a longer time, so the *peak* force on Y's
passenger is smaller. **Prediction for the design project: the crumple
bumper protects better, because it stretches the stop.** (Sessions
14–15 will test this with real data.)

## Guided practice

1. Name the action–reaction pair when a hammer hits a nail.
   *(Hint: nail on hammer — same size, opposite, different objects.)*
2. A 2 kg cart moves at 3 m/s. Momentum? If its speed doubles, what
   happens to its momentum?
3. **Spot the misconception:** "The car pushes the wall, but the wall
   doesn't push back — walls can't push." What's wrong?

## Independent practice

1. Compute $p$ (show work, include units): (a) 0.3 kg ball at 5 m/s;
   (b) 50 kg cyclist at 4 m/s; (c) 1500 kg car at 20 m/s.
2. A 1 kg cart at 2 m/s hits a stationary block and shoves it 30 cm.
   The same cart loaded to 2 kg at the same 2 m/s shoves it 65 cm.
   (a) Compare the two momenta. (b) Explain the block-distance
   difference with the third law + momentum.
3. A runner (70 kg, 5 m/s) and a child on a scooter (35 kg, 10 m/s)
   have equal momentum (350 kg·m/s each). Which is easier to stop
   quickly with your hands, and why? *(Trick: equal momentum means the
   stopping question needs more information — say what.)*
4. **Error analysis:** "Momentum is just another word for speed."
   Correct the error with a counterexample using numbers.

## Application

**Ball-drop collisions (15 min, adult-supervised):** Drop a tennis ball
and a heavier rubber ball from the same height onto a hard floor.
Observe: bounce height, sound, how the floor "feels" (hand near, not
touching, the impact zone). Write the action–reaction pair for the
bounce and rank the two balls' momenta just before impact, with the
$p = mv$ reasoning. One rule: drops only, no throwing.

## Exit check

A 0.8 kg cart rolling at 1.5 m/s hits a wall. (a) Its momentum before
impact? (b) Name the action–reaction pair in the crash. (c) In one
sentence, why does a crumple bumper reduce the peak force on the cart?

## Supports

- **Reading/language:** pair every formula use with the sentence frame
  "The ___ has momentum ___ because ___ kg times ___ m/s equals ___."
- **Alternate response mode:** demonstrate action–reaction with
  balloon rockets (balloon taped to a straw on a string) — the air
  goes one way, the balloon the other; narrate the pair aloud.
- **Extension:** research how real crumple zones and airbags use the
  longer-time idea (resource pack), and write the one-paragraph
  "engineer's explanation" the class will use in the design challenge.

## Teacher note

The #1 error: "action and reaction cancel, so nothing happens." Drill
the two-objects rule with FBDs of *each* object side by side — on
object A's diagram only B's push on A appears. The #2 error: momentum
as speed — always demand the mass in the sentence. Preview Lesson 6:
gravity is the third-law partner story at planetary scale. See the
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05).
