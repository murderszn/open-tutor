# Lesson 02 — Free-Body Diagrams and Newton's First Law

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can draw a free-body diagram of an object
and use Newton's first law to predict what an object keeps doing when
forces are balanced.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, ruler, index cards ·
**Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult rolls a ball gently across the table. It slows and stops.
"Why did it stop?" Most learners say "it ran out of force" or "of
push." Hold that idea up — this lesson tests it.

## Explanation

A **free-body diagram (FBD)** is a force picture: draw the object as a
single dot (or a small box), then draw an arrow for every force acting
on it. Each arrow points in the force's direction, is labeled with the
force's name and source ("gravity from Earth — 10 N down"), and is drawn
longer when the force is bigger. Only forces *on* the object — never
forces the object pushes on something else.

Rules that keep diagrams honest:

1. One dot, one object. Pick the object and stay with it.
2. Arrows start at the dot and point outward in the force's direction.
3. Longer arrow = bigger force. Equal arrows = balanced pair.
4. Label every arrow: name + source + size when known.

Newton's **first law** (the law of **inertia**) says: an object keeps
its motion — at rest, or moving at a steady speed in a straight line —
*unless an unbalanced force changes it*. Inertia is the object's
tendency to resist that change, and it grows with mass: a loaded wagon
is harder to start and harder to stop than an empty one.

So the ball "ran out of push" is wrong: after your hand left the ball,
no forward force acted on it at all. **Friction** — an unbalanced force
against the motion — is what changed the motion and stopped it. In
space, where there is almost no friction, a pushed object would glide
on forever. That is why a car at a steady speed has *balanced* forces:
the engine's forward push exactly cancels air resistance and rolling
friction — net force zero, speed steady.

Everyday inertia: when a bus stops suddenly, standing passengers lean
*forward* — their bodies keep the old motion. A tablecloth can be
yanked from under dishes because the dishes' inertia resists the sudden
change. Seatbelts exist because of inertia, not because cars are
dangerous standing still.

**Vocabulary:** *free-body diagram* (arrows for all forces on one
object), *Newton's first law* (motion stays unless unbalanced force
acts), *inertia* (resistance to motion change; grows with mass).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A 0.5 kg book rests on a table. FBD: dot; arrow down
labeled "gravity from Earth — 5 N"; arrow up labeled "table pushes up —
5 N," drawn the same length. Equal, opposite → net force 0 →
**balanced → stays at rest.** *Rest is a prediction, not an
assumption.*

**Example 2.** A box slides at a steady 2 m/s across the floor. FBD:
arrow down "gravity — 40 N," arrow up "floor pushes up — 40 N" (equal,
vertical balanced); arrow left "friction — 15 N," arrow right "push —
15 N" (equal, horizontal balanced). Net force zero in both directions
→ **steady speed continues**. *A continuing motion needs no continuing
push — it needs balanced forces.*

**Example 3.** A purse sits on a bus seat. The bus brakes hard; the
purse slides forward off the seat. First-law reading: while braking,
the *seat* felt an unbalanced backward force, but no horizontal force
acted on the purse (ignoring small friction). By inertia the purse
kept its forward motion while the seat slowed — so relative to the
seat, it moved forward. **Prediction: it lands ahead of its starting
spot.**

## Guided practice

1. Draw the FBD of a basketball held motionless in someone's hands.
   *(Hint: gravity down; hands push up.)*
2. A hockey puck glides on smooth ice at constant speed. Draw its
   horizontal FBD. What is the net horizontal force?
3. **Explain your reasoning:** a satellite orbits Earth with its engines
   off and keeps moving. Does that break the first law? *(Hint: what
   unbalanced force acts on it?)*

## Independent practice

1. Draw FBDs (labeled arrows, honest relative lengths) for: (a) a
   picture frame hanging on one nail; (b) a sled being pulled at a
   steady speed; (c) a rock sinking slowly through water at a steady
   speed.
2. For each FBD in (1): state the net force and predict what the motion
   does next.
3. A 2 kg block and a 20 kg block sit on identical carts. The same
   quick sideways shove is given to each cart. Which block is more
   likely to stay in place (not tip or slide), and why — in inertia
   language?
4. **Error analysis:** "A rocket coasting in deep space needs its
   engine on to keep moving." Identify the error and give the
   first-law correction.

## Application

**Inertia theater (10 min):** With the adult, stage three mini-demos —
a coin on an index card over a cup (flick the card), a stack of books
on a rolling chair (quick stop), a marble in a shallow pan (tilt, then
hold steady). For each: sketch the FBD before the change, state the
first-law prediction, then run it and record whether the prediction
held. One sentence per demo: *claim → evidence → verdict.*

## Exit check

A delivery van moves at a steady 15 m/s. Boxes on its floor are not
tied down. The van brakes suddenly. Use the first law to predict what
the boxes do — and sketch the horizontal FBD of one box *during the
braking*.

## Supports

- **Reading/language:** draw arrows before writing any words; the
  diagram is the sentence. A word bank (gravity, friction, push, pull,
  balanced) supports labels.
- **Alternate response mode:** build FBDs with cut-paper arrows on a
  magnet board — length by hand measurement, direction by placement.
- **Extension:** add a tilted-surface case: a book on a ramp held still
  by friction — draw the three arrows (gravity down, ramp pushing
  perpendicular to the surface, friction up the slope) and explain the
  balance in words.

## Teacher note

Two stubborn ideas: (1) "motion needs a force" — counter with the
steady-speed box: motion continues, *change* needs force; (2) drawing
forces the object exerts (the book "pushing down on the table") on the
book's FBD — drill the rule: arrows are forces *on the dot*. Preview
Lesson 3's $F = ma$ by asking what happens when the arrows are *not*
equal. See the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
