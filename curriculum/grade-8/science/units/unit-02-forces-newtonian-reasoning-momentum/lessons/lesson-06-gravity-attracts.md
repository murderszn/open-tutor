# Lesson 06 — Gravity Attracts: An Attractive, Mass-Dependent Force

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can argue with evidence that gravity pulls
masses together (attractive, never pushing apart) and that bigger
masses pull harder.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, index cards, a
pendulum (string + washer) · **Teacher key:**
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult drops a crumpled paper ball and a flat sheet of paper from
the same height. The ball wins. "So heavier falls faster?" — the flat
sheet is *lighter* but the point is air, not weight. Keep this demo in
mind: it is the misconception this lesson kills.

## Explanation

**Gravity** is the attraction between any two masses: it always pulls
them *toward* each other, never pushes them apart. Two patterns, both
arguable from evidence:

1. **Attractive.** Dropped objects fall *toward* Earth. The Moon stays
   near Earth instead of wandering off — something pulls it inward as
   it moves. Tides rise on the side of Earth facing the Moon *and* on
   the opposite side (the full two-bulge story is high-school physics;
   the takeaway here: the Moon's pull reaches across 384,000 km of
   empty space and moves oceans).
2. **Mass-dependent.** Earth (huge mass) holds the Moon in orbit and
   pulls a person down with about 600–800 N; the Moon (much smaller
   mass) pulls the same person with only about 100–130 N — which is why
   astronauts bounce there. A bigger mass pulls harder; at the same
   distance, doubling a mass doubles the pull.

The flat-sheet demo: in air, shape matters because air resistance
fights the fall. In a vacuum — famously shown by the Apollo 15 hammer
and feather drop on the Moon — a hammer and a feather hit the ground
*together*. Gravity accelerates all masses equally at one place; air is
the confound, exactly the kind Lesson 4 taught us to control. (The
reason "equal acceleration, different forces" works is the second law:
twice the mass feels twice the gravitational force, and $a = F/m$
stays the same.)

**Mass vs. weight, settled:** mass (kg) is how much matter — the same
on Earth and the Moon. Weight (N) is gravity's pull *on* that mass —
smaller where gravity is weaker. A 60 kg astronaut *weighs* ~590 N on
Earth and ~98 N on the Moon, but is 60 kg in both places.

**Vocabulary:** *gravity* (attractive force between masses), *weight*
(gravity's pull on a mass, in newtons), *mass* (amount of matter, in
kilograms — not the same as weight), *evidence* (observations that
support or challenge a claim).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — attractive.** Claim: "Gravity pulls things together."
Evidence: (a) a released ball accelerates downward, toward Earth's
center — never upward or sideways on its own; (b) the Moon orbits
Earth in a closed path, which requires a continuous inward pull;
(c) ocean tides follow the Moon's position. *Three independent lines
of evidence, one pattern: attraction.*

**Example 2 — mass-dependent.** Claim: "Bigger masses pull harder."
Evidence: (a) Earth's surface gravity (~9.8 m/s² of acceleration)
holds our atmosphere; the Moon's (~1.6 m/s²) cannot hold one —
same physics, smaller mass, weaker pull; (b) Jupiter's moons orbit
*Jupiter*, not the Sun directly, because nearby Jupiter's pull wins
locally despite the Sun's enormous mass — distance matters too, but
the mass pattern is visible; (c) a scale reads a larger weight for a
larger mass on the same planet. *Weight scales with the pulled mass
when the pulling mass is fixed.*

**Example 3 — the vacuum argument.** Observation: hammer and feather
fall together on the airless Moon. Reasoning: with air removed (the
confound controlled), shape and mass stop mattering to the *motion*.
Conclusion: at one location, gravity changes every mass's motion at
the same rate — so differences we see on Earth come from air, not from
gravity favoring heavy things.

## Guided practice

1. Give one observation that shows gravity is attractive, and one that
   shows it depends on mass. *(Hint: tides; Moon vs. Earth.)*
2. An astronaut's mass is 70 kg. On Earth they weigh about 690 N; on
   the Moon about 115 N. What changed and what stayed the same?
3. **Spot the misconception:** "Heavy things fall faster because
   gravity pulls them more." Which half is true, and what actually
   decides the fall time on Earth?

## Independent practice

1. A 2 kg rock and a 0.02 kg pebble are dropped from the same height
   in a vacuum chamber. (a) Which hits first? (b) Which feels the
   larger gravitational force? (c) Reconcile (a) and (b) with $F = ma$.
2. **Argue from evidence:** "Gravitational interactions are attractive
   and depend on the masses of the interacting objects." Write a
   4–6 sentence argument using at least two pieces of evidence from
   this lesson, in claim–evidence–reasoning form.
3. Ocean tides are higher during a full or new moon (Sun, Moon, and
   Earth line up) than during quarter moons. Explain this pattern with
   the mass-dependence idea — what are the Sun and Moon doing to
   Earth's oceans?
4. **Error analysis:** "Astronauts float on the space station because
   there is no gravity up there." Correct the error: is there gravity
   at the station's altitude, and why do astronauts float?

## Application

**Pendulum gravity check (15 min):** Hang a washer on a 30 cm string.
Pull it aside and release; count 10 swings with a stopwatch, twice.
Now double the washer mass (add a second washer) and repeat. Question:
does the swing time change? Record, then write the verdict in one
sentence — *evidence first, then what it suggests about mass and
gravity's pull on motion.* (Result: the period barely changes — a
preview of the "gravity accelerates all masses equally" idea.)

## Exit check

In your own words: (a) what does "gravitational interactions are
attractive" mean — give one observation; (b) what does "depend on the
masses" mean — give one observation.

## Supports

- **Reading/language:** an evidence bank on the board (tides, Moon
  orbit, falling ball, hammer–feather, scale readings) — arguments are
  built by *selecting* from it, not by recalling from nothing.
- **Alternate response mode:** deliver the argument orally as a
  60-second "gravity lawyer" speech; the adult scores the same
  claim–evidence–reasoning rubric.
- **Extension:** look up the actual surface gravities of Earth, the
  Moon, and Mars (resource pack) and compute the weight of a 60 kg
  person on each — then explain why "mass vs. weight" matters for
  planning a Mars mission.

## Teacher note

"Mass vs. weight" confusion is the unit's last big vocabulary battle —
keep the units visible (kg vs. N) and it mostly resolves itself. The
space-station error is gold: there *is* strong gravity up there (~90%
of Earth's); astronauts float because they and the station are *falling
together* around Earth — save the full orbital-motion story for the
waves/energy years, but the correction ("gravity is very much present")
is fair game now. See the [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06).
