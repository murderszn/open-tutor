# Lesson 03 — Newton's Second Law: F = ma

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can use $F = ma$ to find force, mass, or
acceleration, and I can predict how doubling the force or the mass
changes the acceleration — with a reason, not a guess.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, calculator (optional),
index cards · **Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult asks: "You push an empty shopping cart and a full one with
the *same* push. Which speeds up faster?" Then: "You push the empty one
twice as hard. Now what?" The answers are the second law in plain
words.

## Explanation

Newton's **second law** connects force to *changing* motion. The
**acceleration** ($a$) is how fast the motion changes — speeding up,
slowing down, or turning. The law, in symbols:

$$F_{net} = m \times a$$

- $F_{net}$ — the **net** force in newtons (N), after balancing.
- $m$ — the **mass** in kilograms (kg).
- $a$ — the acceleration in meters per second squared (m/s²). Read
  "3 m/s²" as "gains 3 m/s of speed every second."

Two readings matter more than the arithmetic:

1. **Proportional reasoning.** Double the net force on the same mass →
   double the acceleration. Double the mass with the same net force →
   *half* the acceleration (bigger inertia resists more). Triple the
   force *and* triple the mass → acceleration unchanged.
2. **Net, not any single force.** Only the unbalanced remainder
   accelerates the object. A 50 N push fighting 30 N of friction gives
   $F_{net} = 20$ N in $F = ma$ — the friction does not disappear from
   the story, but only the remainder changes the motion.

Rearrange for the unknown you need:

$$a = \frac{F_{net}}{m} \qquad m = \frac{F_{net}}{a} \qquad
F_{net} = m \times a$$

Check units as you go: N = kg × m/s². If the mass is in grams, convert
to kilograms first (500 g = 0.5 kg).

**Vocabulary:** *Newton's second law* ($F_{net} = ma$), *acceleration*
(rate of motion change, m/s²), *proportional* (doubling one quantity
doubles/halves another in a fixed ratio).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A 2 kg cart feels a net force of 6 N.
$a = F/m = 6 / 2 = \textbf{3 m/s²}.$ Every second, the cart gains 3 m/s
of speed. Unit check: N/kg = m/s² ✓.

**Example 2.** Same cart, 4 kg now (loaded with washers), same 6 N net
force. $a = 6 / 4 = \textbf{1.5 m/s²}$ — exactly half. *Doubling the
mass halved the acceleration at the same force.* (Reasoning path: more
inertia → less response to the same push.)

**Example 3.** A 5 kg sled must accelerate at 2 m/s². What net force?
$F = ma = 5 \times 2 = \textbf{10 N}.$ Now double the force to 20 N on
the same sled: $a = 20/5 = 4$ m/s² — **doubled**. *Doubling the net
force doubled the acceleration at the same mass.*

**Example 4 (net force first).** A 3 kg box: you push 25 N right,
friction pushes 10 N left. Step 1 — net force: 25 − 10 = 15 N right.
Step 2 — $a = 15 / 3 = \textbf{5 m/s²}$ to the right. *Never plug a
single push into $F = ma$ when an opposing force exists.*

## Guided practice

1. A 4 kg wagon feels a net force of 12 N. Find $a$.
   *(Hint: $a = F/m$.)*
2. The same wagon carries a load: mass is now 8 kg, net force still
   12 N. Find the new $a$ and state the proportional rule you used.
3. A 1.5 kg book must accelerate at 4 m/s². What net force is needed?
4. **Spot the error:** "A 10 N force on a 2 kg cart gives $a = 20$
   m/s² because you multiply." Fix the arithmetic and name the rule.

## Independent practice

1. Complete the table (show one line of work each):

   | $F_{net}$ (N) | $m$ (kg) | $a$ (m/s²) |
   |---|---|---|
   | 8 | 2 | ? |
   | 8 | 4 | ? |
   | 16 | 2 | ? |
   | ? | 3 | 5 |
   | 9 | ? | 3 |

2. A 60 kg cyclist and bike feel 120 N of net forward force.
   Acceleration? If the rider stands on the pedals and the net force
   becomes 180 N, what's the new acceleration — and what does the ratio
   180/120 tell you?
3. **Explain your reasoning:** Two carts roll down identical ramps.
   Cart X has mass 1 kg, cart Y has mass 3 kg. The ramp gives each a net
   downhill force proportional to its mass (gravity pulls harder on
   more mass). Which cart reaches the bottom first — or do they tie?
   Justify with the second law. *(This predicts the lab's outcome.)*

## Application

**F = ma in the kitchen (15 min):** With the adult, slide three
objects of clearly different mass (spoon, mug, full water bottle)
across a smooth table with "the same gentle push" (adult judges
consistency). Rank the accelerations by observation, then write the
proportional argument: same $F$, bigger $m$ → smaller $a$. One
paragraph: *observation → second-law explanation → prediction for a
fourth object.*

## Exit check

A 2.5 kg toy car experiences a net force of 10 N.
(a) Acceleration? (b) If the mass doubles to 5 kg with the same 10 N,
what is the new acceleration? (c) State the proportional rule in one
sentence.

## Supports

- **Reading/language:** translate each problem into a three-line
  template — *Know: / Find: / Rule:* — before touching numbers.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer proportional questions with
  arrow sketches (↑ force → ↑ acceleration; ↑ mass → ↓ acceleration)
  plus a one-sentence rule, instead of full calculations.
- **Extension:** a 0.4 kg ball falls with gravity pulling 4 N down and
  air pushing 1 N up. Compute the net force, then the acceleration —
  and explain why it is less than the free-fall value the class will
  meet in Lesson 6.

## Teacher note

Watch for three errors: (1) plugging a *gross* push into $F = ma$
without subtracting friction — always ask "what's the net?"; (2) unit
mixing (grams into a kilogram formula); (3) treating acceleration as
speed — stress "how fast the *speed changes*." The independent-practice
ramp question previews the investigation: heavier carts do *not* win,
because the downhill force scales with mass. See the
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
