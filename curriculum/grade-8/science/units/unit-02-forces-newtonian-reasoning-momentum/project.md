# Project — Force Lab + Crash-Design Challenge

**Type:** adult-supervised household-scale investigation (Sessions 8–9)
plus an engineering design challenge with test-data analysis (Sessions
14–15, 18) · **Rubrics:** below · **Teacher scoring notes:**
[teacher guide](teacher-guide.md#project-scoring)

## Driving questions

1. **Lab:** How does the cart's mass (or the ramp height) change how
   quickly it covers a fixed 1-meter run — and what does the data say
   about the second law?
2. **Design challenge:** Can you build a bumper that keeps a passenger
   (egg) unbroken when a cart crashes into a wall — and can your test
   data prove which design features work?

## Safety box (adult reads aloud before Session 8 and again before 14)

- The ramp and the crash track sit on a clear floor or sturdy table —
  **never aimed at people, pets, stairs, pools, or breakables.**
- Carts roll; they are **never thrown or launched by hand toward the
  wall** — release from rest at the tape line only.
- The adult places the ramp, sets the wall target, and handles the raw
  egg (salmonella: wash hands after handling; cracked eggs go straight
  in the trash). The clay-ball passenger alternative skips this risk
  entirely.
- **Observation alternative:** a learner who cannot run the physical
  setups takes the timer/reader/observer role, or runs both the lab and
  the design tests as a simulation study with the PhET "Forces and
  Motion: Basics" sim (resource pack) — same tables, same claims, same
  rubric.

## Materials

**Lab:** smooth board 1–1.5 m, toy car or low-friction cart, books or
blocks for ramp height, washers or coins (weighed on the kitchen scale),
meter stick or tape measure, masking tape, stopwatch, notebook, graph
paper. **Design challenge:** the same cart plus a "crash kit" the adult
assembles — raw egg *or* a 50 g clay ball as the passenger; tape,
straws, cotton balls, foam scraps, paper, rubber bands in a fixed
budget (adult's call, e.g., 30 cm of tape + 6 straws + 4 cotton balls);
a wall target (a book stack or box braced against a real wall);
cushioning behind the target is *not* allowed — the bumper must do the
work. Printed or on-screen copy of
[`assets/crash-test-bumper-designs.png`](assets/crash-test-bumper-designs.png).

## Lab sessions 1–2 (Sessions 8–9)

*Adapted from the track's verified legacy assignment
[`newtons-laws-investigation.md`](../../../stem/assignments/newtons-laws-investigation.md);
re-verified for grade-8 use in this unit (see scope-and-sequence).*

**Session 8 — build and baseline (50 min):**

1. Build the ramp: board raised on books, tape a start line and a
   finish line exactly 1.00 m apart (measure twice).
2. Choose the investigation arm with the adult: **(A) mass arm** — ramp
   fixed, conditions empty cart / +100 g / +200 g; or **(B) ramp arm**
   — cart fixed, ramp low / medium / high (measure each height in cm).
3. Weigh the cart and each added mass; record in grams and kilograms.
4. Baseline: 3 release trials of the *first* condition. Release from
   rest at the start line (no pushing); the timer starts at release
   and stops as the cart's front crosses the finish line. Record all 3
   times; re-run any trial with a timing slip.

**Session 9 — conditions, repeats, analysis (50 min):**

1. Run the remaining two conditions, 3 trials each, same releaser and
   timer throughout.
2. Compute each condition's average time and average speed
   ($v = 1.00\ \text{m} / t_{avg}$). Record in a summary table.
3. Optional collision extension (ties to Lesson 5): place the target
   block just past the finish line, run the heaviest and lightest
   conditions once each, and measure how far each shoves the block.
4. Draft the lab conclusion (half page): the question, the pattern in
   the data (numbers cited), and the second-law interpretation —
   "heavier carts arrived at nearly the same time, consistent with
   $a = F/m$ when the downhill force grows with mass" (mass arm) or
   "higher ramps gave shorter times, consistent with a larger net
   downhill force" (ramp arm). Name one uncontrolled variable and one
   improvement.

## Design sessions (Sessions 14–15)

**The generated illustration** (`assets/crash-test-bumper-designs.png`)
opens Session 14 as a prediction prompt — it is a story starter, not
evidence:

> ![Two AI-generated panels: a red toy car with a hard flat bumper and
> a blue toy car with a soft foam-block bumper, each about to hit a
> brick wall](assets/crash-test-bumper-designs.png)
>
> *Caption: An AI-generated picture of two crash-test bumpers. Which
> design do you predict protects a passenger better — and which force
> idea from this unit explains your prediction?*
>
> *Text-only alternative: Left panel — a red toy car with a flat hard
> bumper about to hit a gray brick wall, motion lines behind it. Right
> panel — a blue toy car with a thick bumper made of light foam blocks
> about to hit the same kind of wall, motion lines behind it.*

**Session 14 — criteria, plan, build (50 min):**

1. State the **criteria** (a good design: passenger unbroken after 3
   crashes; bumper stays attached) and **constraints** (crash-kit
   budget; bumper mass ≤ 100 g; cart must still roll 1 m down the ramp
   unassisted).
2. Sketch **two** bumper ideas, labeling which force idea each uses
   (crumple/longer stopping time; spreading the force; protecting the
   passenger's position).
3. Build design 1. Weigh it; check the 100 g constraint.

**Session 15 — test, record, analyze, improve (50 min):**

1. Test protocol (identical every run): cart + passenger released from
   rest at the ramp's start line; wall target at the bottom; 3 trials.
   Record per trial: passenger broken/intact, bumper damage notes,
   block displacement if the target moves.
2. Analyze: which features appeared in the surviving runs? Compare
   with a partner's design — identify the **best characteristics of
   each** (MS-ETS1-3).
3. Improve once: rebuild combining the best features into design 2;
   re-test 2 trials; record whether the improvement worked.
4. Write the design argument (half page): *our data → the features
   that survived → the force/momentum explanation → the one change we
   would make next.*

## Capstone presentations (Session 18)

Each learner (or pair) presents 5 minutes: the lab's second-law
conclusion with its data table, then the crash-design story — criteria,
both designs, test results, the data-backed argument for which features
work and why (third law + momentum + stopping time). The audience asks
one evidence question each. The adult scores with the rubric below;
the written lab conclusion and design argument are collected.

## Rubrics

**Lab report (Sessions 8–9)** — 12 points

| Points | Evidence and reasoning | Data and method |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Claim names the pattern *and* ties it to the second law correctly | 3 conditions × 3 trials, labeled tables with units, averages shown |
| 3 | Claim names the pattern; law link attempted | Minor gap (e.g., one missing trial rerun, units on most columns) |
| 2 | Pattern described vaguely; no law link | Table present but unlabeled or single trials only |
| 0–1 | No supported claim | No usable data record |

Plus: **safety and honesty** — pass/fail gate: any fabricated or
"fixed" data caps the report at 6/12, no exceptions.

**Design challenge (Sessions 14–15, 18)** — 16 points

| Points | Design process | Data analysis | Argument |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | Two sketched ideas tied to force ideas; built within constraints | 3-trial test log with damage notes; best features identified across designs | Claim about which features work, backed by test results *and* a correct force/momentum explanation |
| 3 | One solid idea; minor constraint miss | Test log complete; comparison thin | Claim backed by data *or* by physics, not both |
| 2 | Built without a plan | Incomplete log | Claim without evidence |
| 0–1 | No testable build | No record | No argument |

**Presentation (Session 18)** — 4 points: states both conclusions
(1), shows the data (1), answers one evidence question accurately
(1), stays within time and credits the partner/observer role (1).
