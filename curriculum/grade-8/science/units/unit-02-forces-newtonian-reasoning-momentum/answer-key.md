# Answer Key — Unit 02: Forces, Newtonian Reasoning, and Momentum

**For the guiding adult only.** Every solution below was worked
independently; where the learner's valid alternative differs, score the
reasoning. Numbers were re-checked 2026-10-20.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice.**
1. Net = 60 − 45 = **15 N forward**; unbalanced → the dog (or the
   owner, if the owner holds firm) accelerates forward. Direction
   follows the bigger force.
2. Gravity downward (toward Earth); air drag opposite the ball's motion
   (upward/backward while rising, upward while falling — always
   opposing motion).
3. The claim is wrong: gravity *does* act on the book (pulling down).
   The table pushes up with an equal force, so the *net* force is
   zero. Balanced forces ≠ no forces.

**Independent practice.**
1. (a) 12 + 7 = **19 N east**, unbalanced. (b) 20 − 20 = **0**,
   balanced. (c) 50 − 30 = **20 N right**, unbalanced.
2. (a) Vertical: 400 up − 400 down = **0**. (b) Horizontal: 90 − 60 =
   **30 N forward**. (c) The sled speeds up forward; the horizontal
   net force decides (vertical forces are balanced).
3. Net force is **zero** — steady speed means balanced forces. The
   engine's forward force exactly equals air resistance + rolling
   friction combined. (Common error: "the engine force is bigger
   because it's moving." No — bigger would mean accelerating.)

**Exit check.** 10 − 6 = **4 N north**; unbalanced → the kite's motion
changes toward the north (accelerates northward / drifts north).

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice.**
1. FBD: dot; arrow down labeled "gravity from Earth"; arrow up,
   same length, labeled "hands push up." Net zero.
2. Horizontal FBD: essentially no arrows (friction negligible on ice;
   no push after release). Net horizontal force = **0** → keeps
   gliding at constant speed (first law).
3. No break: the first law says straight-line motion continues
   *unless* an unbalanced force acts — **gravity is that unbalanced
   force**, continuously bending the path into an orbit. The law is
   exactly what predicts curved motion needs a force.

**Independent practice.**
1. (a) Frame: gravity down, nail-hook up (equal length). (b) Sled:
   gravity down = snow up; pull forward = friction backward (equal
   lengths within each pair). (c) Rock: gravity down; water's upward
   push (buoyancy + drag) equal length.
2. All three: net force **zero** → first-law prediction: frame stays
   hung; sled keeps its steady speed; rock keeps sinking at its steady
   speed.
3. The **20 kg block** stays put more reliably — greater mass means
   greater inertia, more resistance to the shove (the 2 kg block is
   easier to set sliding/tipping).
4. Error: nothing "keeps it moving." First-law correction: in deep
   space there is ~no friction, so with zero net force the rocket
   keeps its velocity; the engine is only needed to *change* motion.

**Application (sample).** Coin/card/cup: card flicked away, coin's
inertia keeps it over the cup → drops in. Claim–evidence–verdict in
one sentence each; accept any correctly reasoned trio.

**Exit check.** The boxes keep moving forward at 15 m/s while the van
slows (inertia) — they slide toward the front of the van. FBD during
braking: gravity down = floor up; horizontal arrow backward labeled
"friction from floor" — too small to slow the box with the van, so the
box slides forward relative to the van.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice.**
1. $a = F/m = 12/4 =$ **3 m/s²**.
2. $a = 12/8 =$ **1.5 m/s²** — halved. Rule: doubling the mass halves
   the acceleration at the same net force.
3. $F = ma = 1.5 \times 4 =$ **6 N**.
4. Error: multiplied instead of dividing. Correct: $a = F/m = 10/2 =$
   **5 m/s²**.

**Independent practice.**
1. Row by row: $a = 8/2 =$ **4 m/s²**; $a = 8/4 =$ **2 m/s²**;
   $a = 16/2 =$ **8 m/s²**; $F = 3 \times 5 =$ **15 N**;
   $m = 9/3 =$ **3 kg**. (Pattern check: doubling $F$ doubled $a$;
   doubling $m$ halved $a$.)
2. $a = 120/60 =$ **2 m/s²**. New: $a = 180/60 =$ **3 m/s²**. The
   force ratio 180/120 = 1.5 and the acceleration ratio 3/2 = 1.5
   match — acceleration scales with net force.
3. **Tie** (ignoring friction differences). Gravity's downhill pull
   scales with mass ($F \propto m$), so $a = F/m$ is the same for both
   — the extra mass brings its own extra force. This is the lab
   hypothesis for the mass arm.

**Application (sample).** Observation: spoon fastest, bottle slowest.
Explanation: same push, larger mass → smaller $a = F/m$. Prediction:
a fourth object between mug and bottle in mass accelerates between
them. Accept any correctly proportional ranking + reasoning.

**Exit check.** (a) $a = 10/2.5 =$ **4 m/s²**. (b) $a = 10/5 =$
**2 m/s²**. (c) At constant net force, doubling the mass halves the
acceleration.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice.**
1. Sample: "How does the ramp surface (smooth board vs. towel-covered
   board) change the cart's travel time over 1 m?" Changed: surface;
   measured: travel time. (Any question naming both variables
   counts.)
2. Controlled: ramp height, release point, cart, surface, timer,
   releaser. If the releaser changes, release technique varies —
   time differences could come from the person, not the mass, and the
   question becomes unanswerable (confounded).
3. Table: rows = the 3 conditions; columns = Trial 1, Trial 2,
   Trial 3, Average — every header carrying the unit (s), plus a mass
   or height column with its unit (g/kg or cm).

**Independent practice.**
1. Ramp-height arm plan: question ("How does raising the ramp from 10
   cm to 20 cm to 30 cm change the cart's 1-m travel time?");
   changed = ramp height; measured = travel time (→ speed);
   controlled = cart, release point, surface, timer, releaser;
   3 trials per height; blank table with units. (Any complete,
   consistent plan scores.)
2. Trial 3 (3.1 s) is an **outlier** — more than double the others;
   re-run it, or drop it with a written note. Average of the kept
   trials: $(1.5 + 1.6)/2 =$ **1.55 s**.
3. Changing two things at once **confounds** the result: if times
   change, you cannot tell whether the cart or the mass caused it.
   One changed variable per investigation.

**Exit check (sample).** "How does added mass change the cart's 1-m
travel time? Changing: added washers (0/100/200 g). Measuring: travel
time in seconds. Keeping the same: ramp height, release point, cart,
timer, releaser." Any equivalent 2–3 sentence plan scores.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice.**
1. Hammer pushes nail (down/forward); nail pushes hammer (up/backward)
   — equal size, opposite directions, different objects.
2. $p = 2 \times 3 =$ **6 kg·m/s**; doubling speed doubles it to
   **12 kg·m/s**.
3. The claim is wrong: the wall *does* push back — Newton's third law
   guarantees it, and you feel it in your hands/shoulders. The two
   forces act on different objects (wall on you; you on wall), so
   nothing cancels.

**Independent practice.**
1. (a) $0.3 \times 5 =$ **1.5 kg·m/s**. (b) $50 \times 4 =$
   **200 kg·m/s**. (c) $1500 \times 20 =$ **30,000 kg·m/s**.
2. (a) Light cart: $1 \times 2 =$ **2 kg·m/s**; loaded: $2 \times 2 =$
   **4 kg·m/s** — doubled. (b) The loaded cart shoves the block
   farther (65 cm vs 30 cm): more momentum → larger third-law push on
   the block during the hit → more motion transferred. The data
   pattern matches the momentum comparison.
3. **Trick honored:** the momenta are equal (350 kg·m/s each), so
   momentum alone doesn't decide — with the same stopping distance,
   both need the same average stopping force. The real difference is
   practical (grip, reach, the scooter's handlebars). Full credit for:
   "it's a tie on momentum; I'd need the stopping distance/time to go
   further."
4. Correction with counterexample: a 0.25 kg ball at 4 m/s and a
   0.5 kg ball at 2 m/s have *different* speeds but *equal* momentum
   ($1$ kg·m/s each) — so momentum is $mv$, not $v$.

**Application (sample).** Action–reaction: ball pushes floor down;
floor pushes ball up. Momenta before impact: heavier ball's $mv$ is
larger (same drop height → same speed, more mass). Accept any
correctly computed ranking with the pair named.

**Exit check.** (a) $p = 0.8 \times 1.5 =$ **1.2 kg·m/s**.
(b) Cart pushes wall; wall pushes cart — equal, opposite.
(c) The crumple bumper stretches the stopping time, so the peak force
on the cart (and passenger) is smaller.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice.**
1. Attractive: a dropped ball falls *toward* Earth (never away); the
   Moon orbits Earth in a closed path. Mass-dependent: the Moon's
   surface gravity is ~1/6 of Earth's (smaller mass, weaker pull); a
   scale reads a bigger weight for a bigger mass on the same planet.
   (Any one correct observation per pattern scores.)
2. **Mass stays 70 kg** (amount of matter doesn't change); **weight
   changes** 690 N → 115 N because the Moon pulls less strongly.
3. The true half: gravity *does* pull harder on heavier things (force
   ∝ mass). What decides fall time is *acceleration* $a = F/m$ — the
   same for all masses at one place. On Earth, the flat sheet falls
   slowly because of **air resistance**, not weaker gravity.

**Independent practice.**
1. (a) **Tie** — both land together. (b) The rock feels **100×** the
   gravitational force ($2 / 0.02 = 100$). (c) No contradiction:
   $a = F/m$ — 100× the force on 100× the mass gives the *same*
   acceleration, so the motion matches.
2. **Sample argument:** "Claim: gravitational interactions are
   attractive and depend on mass. Evidence: (1) the Moon stays in orbit
   around Earth — a continuous inward pull; (2) the Moon's surface
   gravity is only ~1/6 of Earth's because the Moon is much less
   massive. Reasoning: a force that holds the Moon in a closed path
   must pull inward, not push away; and the weaker pull of the smaller
   mass shows the strength follows the masses." Any 4–6 sentence
   claim–evidence–reasoning chain with two valid evidence pieces
   scores.
3. When Sun, Moon, and Earth line up, the Sun's gravitational pull on
   Earth's oceans adds to the Moon's pull (both attractive,
   mass-dependent forces reaching across space) → higher high tides.
   At quarter moons the pulls work at right angles and partially
   offset → lower (neap) tides.
4. Correction: there **is** strong gravity at the station's altitude
   (~90% of Earth's). Astronauts float because the station and crew
   are **falling together** around Earth — continuous free-fall, not
   zero gravity.

**Application (sample).** "10 swings took 11.2 s and 11.4 s with one
washer; 11.3 s and 11.2 s with two washers — doubling the mass did not
change the swing time, suggesting gravity changes the motion the same
way regardless of mass." Any evidence-first verdict with numbers
scores.

**Exit check (sample).** (a) "Attractive" means masses pull *toward*
each other — e.g., dropped things fall toward Earth's center. (b)
"Depends on the masses" means bigger masses pull harder — e.g., the
Moon's weaker surface gravity than Earth's.

   unbalanced. (c) 100 + 60 − 30 = **130 N south**, unbalanced.

   (flat street, parked) — balanced. Both nets zero → stays parked.
   (If a tiny push existed, static friction would balance it.)

**Session B.**
1. (a) Ball: gravity down, grass up (equal); friction arrow opposite
   the rolling direction (the unbalanced one slowing it). (b) Carried
   book: gravity down, hands up (equal). (c) Skydiver at terminal
   velocity: gravity down, air resistance up (equal — that's why the
   speed is steady).

   motion for the next second (ball keeps slowing only because of
   friction — name it; book keeps the walking pace; skydiver keeps

   tending to stay where it was while the bus accelerates forward
   beneath it.

   diagram shows only forces **on the chosen object**. That arrow
   belongs on the *table's* diagram. Remove it.
5. Least → most inertia: **feather < bowling ball < bicycle < loaded
   school bus** (mass order: ~0.01 kg < ~7 kg < ~15 kg < thousands of
   kg). Inertia grows with mass.

   **9 m/s²**. (c) $a = 30/2 =$ **15 m/s²** — only the *net* force

**Session D.**

4. The **45 kg skater** moves faster. Third law: the pushes are equal

**Session E.**
1. (a) **Tie** — land together. (b) The bowling ball feels
