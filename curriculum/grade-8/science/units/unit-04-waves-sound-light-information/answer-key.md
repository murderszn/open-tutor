# Answer Key — Unit 04: Waves, Sound, Light, and Information

Teacher material. Every item below was solved independently from
the student pages; where a student item admits a defensible
variation, the acceptable range is stated. Do not hand this file
to learners before they have attempted the work.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice.** 1. The wave carries energy (and can carry a
pattern), not matter: the cork's patch of water stays put. 2. (a)
transverse — rope moves up/down, wave travels sideways; (b)
longitudinal — coils move along the slinky, same line as travel;
(c) longitudinal — air bunches along the travel line; (d)
transverse — people move up/down while the wave travels around.
3. Right: waves travel toward the beach. Wrong: the water itself
does not travel from mid-ocean to the beach; each bit of water
oscillates in place while the pattern and energy travel.

**Independent practice.** 1. Longitudinal: the air's motion
(bunching and spreading) is along the travel line — the direction
test. 2. No. Faster shaking raises the frequency; the rope still
oscillates in place and the pattern travels. 3. No: the current
carried the leaf, not the wave. A wave bobs a floater up and down
in place; a current transports matter. 4. Accept any clear sketch
or description showing: transverse — travel arrow horizontal,
medium arrows vertical; longitudinal — travel arrow horizontal,
medium arrows horizontal (bunch/spread labeled).

**Applied task.** (a) wave, (b) matter itself travels, (c) wave,
(d) matter itself travels, (e) wave. Justifications must use the
test: does the medium return to place (wave) or does the object
end up somewhere new (matter)?

**Exit check.** The wave already on the rope keeps traveling
until its energy spreads out (damping); the flag keeps bobbing
in place, smaller and smaller, then stops. It does not travel to
the far end.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice.** 1. $v = 4 \times 0.25 = 1$ m/s. 2. $\lambda
= 12 / 3 = 4$ m. 3. $f = 8 / 2 = 4$ Hz; $T = 1/4 = 0.25$ s.
4. Wave B: larger amplitude means more energy, no calculation
needed.

**Independent practice.** 1. $v = 5 \times 0.4 = 2$ m/s. 2. $f =
20 / 5 = 4$ Hz. 3. Wavelength halves: at 2 Hz, $\lambda = 10/2 =
5$ m; at 4 Hz, $\lambda = 10/4 = 2.5$ m. 4. Learner's method:
$3 + 2 = 5$ (units: Hz + m, meaningless). Correct: $v = 3 \times 2
= 6$ m/s. Units prove it: (waves/second) × meters = meters/second;
adding a per-second rate to a length cannot give a speed.

**Applied task.** $\lambda = v/f = 6/3 = 2$ m; $T = 1/3 \approx
0.33$ s. Make it by shaking 3 times per second with a big shake
(big amplitude = more energy).

**Exit check.** $v = 10 \times 0.2 = 2$ m/s; $T = 1/10 = 0.1$ s.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice.** 1. $343 \times 0.4 / 2 = 68.6$ m. 2. $343
\times 3 = 1{,}029$ m ≈ 1.0 km. 3. Slowest to fastest: air, water,
steel. Consequence (any one): a train is heard through the rails
first; whale sounds cross oceans fast and far; sonar works over
long water distances. 4. Higher pitch: B (440 Hz). Loudness: equal
— "same volume" means equal amplitude; frequency decides pitch,
amplitude decides loudness.

**Independent practice.** 1. $343 \times 1.2 / 2 = 205.8$ m.
2. Steel: $596 / 5{,}960 = 0.1$ s. Air: $596 / 343 = 1.7376\ldots
\approx 1.74$ s. Steel's particles are tightly linked, so the push
is handed on far faster than in air. 3. Frequency: tightening
raises the vibration rate, hence the pitch; amplitude (loudness)
is unchanged by the peg alone. 4. Space between Sun and Earth is
essentially a vacuum — no material medium for sound to travel in.
Sunlight reaches us because light is an electromagnetic wave and
needs no medium.

**Applied task.** Accept any safe plan with: a known reflecting
surface, one sharp clap, timing of the echo (adult helps with the
stopwatch), distance $= 343 \times t / 2$, and an error check such
as: repeat from a different spot, or check for a second echo from
another surface and re-measure facing only one wall.

**Exit check.** Sound travels far faster in steel (about 5,960
m/s) than in air (343 m/s), so the rail-borne sound arrives first.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice.** 1. Black shirt: it absorbs more light (and
reflects less), and absorbed light becomes thermal energy. 2. The
mirror reflects light in one direction (specular reflection),
preserving the image; paper reflects in all directions (diffuse
reflection), scrambling the image. 3. It leaves at 30° from the
normal, on the other side of the normal. 4. Translucent: light
passes but is scattered, so no readable image comes through.

**Independent practice.** 1. (a) Windowpane: mostly transmitted,
some reflected, little absorbed. (b) Black curtain: mostly
absorbed, some reflected, almost none transmitted. (c) Foil,
shiny side out: mostly reflected, little absorbed or transmitted.
2. Under white light it reflects blue and absorbs the other
colors, so it looks blue. Under red-only light it looks dark:
it absorbs red and there is no blue light arriving to reflect.
3. Refraction: light bends when it passes from air into water
(or water into air), so the straw's image shifts at the surface.
4. Correction: "Translucent means some light gets through, but
scattered, so you cannot see clearly through it." Example: wax
paper or frosted glass.

**Applied task.** (a) Greenhouse window: transparent — transmit
the most light to the plants. (b) Blackout curtain: opaque —
transmit practically nothing. (c) Lampshade: translucent — transmit
soft, scattered light without glare.

**Exit check.** Translucent; no, you cannot read a book through
wax paper.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice.** 1. Radio, microwave, infrared, visible,
ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma. 2. Radio has the longer wavelength;
gamma carries more energy (higher frequency = more energy).
3. Infrared is below visible light in frequency and is not
visible to human eyes. 4. Radios use electromagnetic waves, which
need no medium; shouting needs air, and the Moon has practically
none.

**Independent practice.** 1. X-ray: higher frequency, shorter
wavelength, greater energy — all three agree. 2. The flaw: being
invisible does not group waves. Sunscreen targets ultraviolet
specifically because of its place in the spectrum (high enough
frequency to damage skin); radio waves sit at the opposite,
low-energy end and are not blocked by sunscreen for any related
reason. 3. Violet. 4. Two valid differences: (a) sound needs a
material medium, light does not; (b) sound is a mechanical
vibration of matter, light is an electromagnetic wave (and it
travels vastly faster than sound).

**Applied task.** Accept any two exhibits with an observation and
a conclusion, for example: (infrared) a warmth detector that
registers a hand without touch — proves invisible waves carry
energy we feel as heat; (ultraviolet) a fluorescent poster that
glows under a UV lamp — proves invisible waves beyond violet
exist and interact with materials. Eye-safety rules for any UV
lamp use must be stated.

**Exit check.** Ultraviolet carries more energy and has the
higher frequency.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice.** 1. The analog receiver must reproduce the
exact shape, so noise is copied as if it were signal. The pulse
receiver only decides "short or long" (or "pulse or not"), so
small noise does not change the decision. 2. Each digital copy is
cleaned up and re-sent exactly, pulse for pulse, so errors do not
accumulate. 3. Digital transmission preserves the recording
faithfully — including its flaws. Transmission reliability is not
performance quality. 4. Any two: fiber-optic light pulses; Wi-Fi
or radio-wave pulses; a flashlight pulse code (from the lesson).

**Independent practice.** 1. The pulse decision is coarse: a
small wobble does not turn a short flash into a long one, while
the same wobble changes a smooth shape permanently. 2. A smooth
beam's brightness variations *are* the message, so noise in the
brightness becomes part of the message forever; pulses are
re-decided and cleaned at each step. 3. Acceptable rewrite (no
binary counting): "Digital signals are more reliable because the
message is sent as distinct pulses; the receiver only decides
pulse-or-not, so small noise is ignored, and copies are cleaned
and re-sent exactly instead of accumulating noise." 4. The analog
receiver must reproduce the exact wave shape, so city noise
becomes audible hiss. The digital receiver decides pulses, so the
same noise leaves the message clean unless it is big enough to
fake or erase a whole pulse.

**Applied task.** Accept any five-message chart with distinct
short/long patterns and pauses, plus a breaking noise (for
example: a bright flash that looks like a long flash, or two
flashes overlapping) and a fix (for example: repeat each message
twice and compare, or add a longer pause between messages).

**Exit check.** Claim: digitized signals are a more reliable way
to encode and transmit information than analog signals. Evidence:
in the noisy-channel game, the copied shape drifted while the
pulse message arrived intact (accept the learner's own counts).

## Practice

**Set A.** 1. Wavelength 6 cm, amplitude 2 cm. 2. (a) transverse,
(b) longitudinal — direction tests as in Lesson 01. 3. The flag
stays near the middle, bobbing up and down; the pattern and
energy travel, the rope does not. 4. Wave B (4 cm amplitude).
5. $f = 12/4 = 3$ Hz; $T = 1/3 \approx 0.33$ s. 6. Push:
longitudinal (coils move along the travel line); shake:
transverse (coils move up/down, wave travels sideways). 7. No:
the cork oscillates in place; the wave passes under it. 8.
Correction: "A wave carries energy (and information) through a
medium; the medium itself only oscillates in place." Proof: the
bobbing cork (or the flag) stays put.

**Set B.** 1. 6 m/s. 2. 3 m. 3. $f = 8$ Hz, $T = 0.125$ s.
4. 6 m, 4 m, 3 m, 2 m; at 12 Hz, 1 m. Relationship: inverse — at
fixed speed, doubling frequency halves wavelength. 5. 50 cm =
0.5 m; $v = 6 \times 0.5 = 3$ m/s. (Without converting, 300 is
wrong by a factor of 100 and has impossible units.) 6. Speed
unchanged — in this simple model the medium sets the speed, not
the amplitude; energy carried increases. 7. Both 6 m/s ($2 \times
3$; $6 \times 1$). Speed in a given medium is fixed; frequency
and wavelength trade off against each other. 8. $\lambda = 9/3 =
3$ m; $T = 1/3 \approx 0.33$ s.

**Set C.** 1. $343 \times 0.5 / 2 = 85.75$ m. 2. $343 \times 9 =
3{,}087$ m ≈ 3.1 km. 3. Air (343) < water (1,480) < steel (5,960);
consequence as in Lesson 03. 4. Higher pitch: 880 Hz tone.
Louder: 220 Hz tone (played loudly — larger amplitude). Pitch is
set by frequency, loudness by amplitude. 5. No: the Moon has
practically no air — no medium for the sound wave. 6. Steel:
$1{,}192 / 5{,}960 = 0.2$ s. Air: $1{,}192 / 343 = 3.474\ldots
\approx 3.47$ s. 7. A taut string passes vibrations from cup to
cup efficiently; a slack string absorbs and damps them, so little
vibration reaches the other cup. 8. $1{,}480 \times 0.2 / 2 =
148$ m.

**Set D.** 1. (a) transparent, (b) translucent, (c) opaque,
(d) mostly reflecting. 2. Black shirt: absorbs more, reflects
less; absorbed light becomes thermal energy. 3. 40° from the
normal, other side. 4. Under white light it looks blue (reflects
blue, absorbs the rest). Under red-only light it looks dark
(absorbs red; no blue arrives to reflect). 5. Transparent: it must
transmit light to the plants. 6. Opaque blocks transmission, so
no light reaches the surface behind it — sharp shadow.
Translucent transmits scattered light, so some light reaches
behind it — fuzzy, partial shadow. 7. Refraction: light bends
passing between air and water. 8. Correction as in Lesson 04;
example: wax paper (light passes, image does not).

**Set E.** 1. Radio, microwave, visible, gamma. 2. Ultraviolet:
higher frequency, and higher frequency means more energy.
3. Radio uses electromagnetic waves (no medium needed); shouting
needs air, which the Moon practically lacks. 4. Each copy adds
hiss, and the hiss is copied as signal; after three copies the
quiet parts are buried, and the hiss cannot be removed because
it is indistinguishable from the original shape. 5. The receiver
decides short vs. long; soft claps are neither, and small wobbles
do not change a flash's length category. 6. Pulses are re-decided
and cleaned at each step, so noise does not accumulate the way it
does in a smooth shape. 7. Each email copy is regenerated exactly
from the pulse pattern, not traced from a degrading shape.
8. Acceptable rewrite as in Lesson 06, item 3 — no binary counting.

**Set F.** 1. $120 / 20 = 6$ m/s. 2. Slope $= 40/10 = 4$ m/s: the
speed. 3. $a = F/m = 10/2 = 5$ m/s². 4. $KE = \tfrac12 \times 2
\times 3^2 = 9$ J. 5. Pushing: $20 \times 5 = 100$ J. Holding
still: 0 J (no displacement). 6. Amplitude is the height from the
rest line to a crest; wavelength is the length of one repeat
(crest to crest). They measure different directions. 7. $\lambda
= 6/2 = 3$ m. 8. Correction: light travels through empty space;
sunlight crosses the vacuum between Sun and Earth, while sound
cannot.

## Unit assessment

**Part A.** 1. (a) 5 cm. (b) 3 cm. (c) The first wave: larger
amplitude means more energy. 2. (a) $8 \times 0.75 = 6$ m/s.
(b) $T = 1/8 = 0.125$ s. 3. (a) No air on the Moon — no medium for
sound. (b) Air < water < steel. (c) Steel carries sound far
faster (about 5,960 vs. 343 m/s), so the rail signal arrives
first. 4. $343 \times 1.0 / 2 = 171.5$ m. The divide-by-2 is needed
because the measured time is the round trip (to the wall and
back); the wall is half the round-trip distance away. 5. (a)
Mostly transmitted. (b) Mostly transmitted but scattered
(translucent). (c) Mostly absorbed. 6. (a) It reflects blue
light and absorbs the other colors. (b) Dark: it absorbs red,
and no blue light arrives to reflect. 7. (a) Radio, infrared,
visible, gamma. (b) Gamma (most energy). (c) Radio (longest
wavelength). 8. Claim: digitized signals encode and transmit
information more reliably than analog signals. Evidence: analog
copies accumulate noise that cannot be removed; a pulse receiver
only decides short/long (or pulse/not), so small noise is ignored
and copies can be cleaned and re-sent exactly.

**Part B.** 9. Any pair with $f \times \lambda = 4$, for example
$f = 2$ Hz, $\lambda = 2$ m (shown as $2 \times 2 = 4$ m/s).
Shake description must match the chosen frequency (2 shakes per
second for the example) and state that a bigger shake carries
more energy. 10. (a) $1{,}480 / 1{,}480 = 1$ s. (b) $1{,}480 / 343
= 4.3148\ldots \approx 4.31$ s. (c) Sound travels faster in water
than in air because water's particles pass the push along more
efficiently. 11. (a) Mirror: mostly reflected, leaving at 25° from
the normal on the other side. (b) Black cloth: mostly absorbed
(warms slightly). (c) Clear glass: mostly transmitted. Straight
past: mirror — no, reflected away; cloth — no, absorbed; glass —
yes, transmitted onward. 12. Full marks require: the claim stated;
evidence from the shape-copy chain (drifted) vs. the pulse chain
(arrived intact, with the learner's counts if given); reasoning
that the pulse receiver's coarse decision (short/long) ignores
small noise and allows exact re-sending. Deduct for any binary
counting used as the explanation; do not penalize a passing
mention of computers if the reasoning itself is pulse-based.

## Project

Score with the rubric in [project.md](project.md). A complete
submission has: a five-message list; an analog chart (five
distinct shapes) and a pulse chart (five distinct short/long
patterns); clean and noisy scores for both channels; and a
recommendation that cites the table's counts. The expected
pattern — pulse noisy score equal to or better than analog noisy
score — is the usual outcome, but score honestly: if a group's
analog score beat its pulse score, the recommendation must follow
their data, and full marks go to a recommendation that argues
from that data and names a genuine breaking noise and fix.
Acceptable breaking noises: a flash bright enough to mimic a long
flash, overlapping flashes, a missed pause. Acceptable fixes:
repeat each message twice and compare; lengthen pauses; add a
"start" signal.
