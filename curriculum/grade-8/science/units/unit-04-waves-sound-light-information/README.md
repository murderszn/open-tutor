# Unit 04 — Waves, Sound, Light, and Information

**Grade:** 8 · **Subject:** Science (Physical Science pathway) · **Track weeks:** 15–18
**Backlog:** [issue #39](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/39), section U04
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

A wave is the cheapest way to move energy and information without
moving the stuff itself. Shake one end of a rope and the shake
travels to the other end, but the rope stays where it is. Shout
across a field and the sound arrives, but the air does not travel
with it. Flash a light and the signal arrives faster than anything
else we know, through air, glass, and even empty space. This unit
teaches learners to describe those travelers with numbers —
wavelength, frequency, amplitude, speed — to predict what happens
when a wave meets a new material, and to explain why a message sent
as a pattern of pulses survives a noisy trip better than a message
sent as a smooth, wobbling wave. Week 18 doubles as the midyear
review: the unit quiz and a retrieval block revisit Units 01–03
before the unit assessment.

## Essential question

How can something travel across a room, a field, or a fiber-optic
cable without the material itself traveling — and how do we send a
message so it still arrives readable after a noisy trip?

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–03 and the diagnostic weeks:

- SI units for length and time; reading labeled tables and graphs;
  recording data with units and repeating trials
- Speed as distance ÷ time; slope of a distance–time graph
- Forces as pushes and pulls; energy as something that transfers
  and transforms but is conserved in a closed system; kinetic
  energy grows with mass and with the square of speed
- Arithmetic with decimals and fractions; solving a one-step
  formula for an unknown (for example, finding λ from v and f)
- Writing a one-sentence claim with one reason
  (claim–evidence–reasoning)
- Safe habits for adult-supervised household-scale investigations

New content here: wave, medium, transverse and longitudinal waves,
crest, trough, amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period, wave speed
($v = f\lambda$), sound as a longitudinal wave that needs a medium,
pitch, loudness, echo, reflection, absorption, transmission,
transparent / translucent / opaque, the electromagnetic spectrum
(descriptive only), analog and digital signals, and the noisy-channel
argument for digitized signals. Nothing here assumes Unit 05's
electric and magnetic interactions or Unit 06's atomic models.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. Distinguish a wave from the motion of matter: explain, with a
   rope, slinky, or water example, that a wave carries energy (and
   can carry information) through a medium while the medium itself
   only oscillates in place, and classify a wave as transverse or
   longitudinal from the direction of that oscillation.
2. Use mathematical representations of a simple repeating wave:
   measure or state wavelength, frequency, period, and amplitude;
   calculate wave speed with $v = f\lambda$ (and solve for any one
   variable); and explain qualitatively that a larger amplitude
   means a wave carrying more energy.
3. Explain sound as a longitudinal wave that needs a material
   medium: compare sound speeds in air (about 343 m/s at 20 °C),
   water (about 1,480 m/s), and steel (about 5,960 m/s); relate
   pitch to frequency and loudness to amplitude; and use echo
   timing to estimate a distance.
4. Develop and use a model, for both sound and light, of what
   happens at a material: reflection, absorption, or transmission —
   including transparent, translucent, and opaque materials and
   why an object's color depends on which colors it reflects.
5. Describe the electromagnetic spectrum in order (radio,
   microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma) and
   explain qualitatively that higher-frequency waves in the
   spectrum carry more energy; state that light, unlike sound,
   can travel through empty space.
6. Support, with qualitative evidence from a noisy-channel
   comparison, the claim that digitized signals (sent as wave
   pulses) encode and transmit information more reliably than
   analog signals, without using binary counting or any specific
   device's mechanism.

## Safety, materials, and accessibility

- Adult supervision for every investigation. Rope/slinky work
  stays at waist height or below; nobody aims a rope, slinky, or
  flashlight at anyone's face or eyes. Flashlight signals are
  pointed at a wall or screen, never at eyes.
- Sound work uses ordinary speaking and clapping volumes only —
  no shouting contests, no headphones at high volume, no sharp
  sounds near anyone's ears. The echo activity is done outdoors
  or in a large room with the adult present.
- The light-materials lab uses household items only (glass,
  wax paper, cardboard, a mirror, cloth). No lasers, no open
  flames, no looking directly at any bright light source.
- Accessibility: every image-based question has a text-only
  alternative carrying the same information; the wave-speed and
  echo formulas are given on a formula card for the assessment;
  learners may answer orally, by drawing, or in writing; the
  project can be completed with a partner reading the code chart
  aloud. The PhET simulation alternative is named in the
  [project](project.md) and [resource pack](resource-pack.md) for
  learners who cannot do the rope work.
