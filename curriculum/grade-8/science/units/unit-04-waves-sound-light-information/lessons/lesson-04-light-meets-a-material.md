# Lesson 04 — Light Meets a Material

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can predict whether light will be
reflected, absorbed, or transmitted by a material, and explain why
an object has the color it does.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** flashlight (adult-operated,
never aimed at eyes), clear glass or plastic, wax paper, cardboard,
a small mirror, white and black cloth · **Teacher key:**
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult shines a flashlight at a mirror, then at a black cloth,
then through clear plastic. Ask after each: "Where did the light
go?" Collect three different answers — bounced, disappeared into
the cloth, went through — and say: those are today's three verbs.

## Explanation

When light reaches a material, three things can happen, and often
more than one happens at once:

- **Reflection:** the light bounces off the surface. A mirror
  reflects most of the light that hits it, which is why you see
  an image in it. A white wall reflects light in all directions
  (diffuse reflection), which is why you can see the wall from
  anywhere in the room but cannot see your face in it.
- **Absorption:** the material takes the light's energy in, and
  the light does not come back out as light. Absorbed light
  usually becomes thermal energy — the material warms up. A
  black shirt in sunlight warms faster than a white shirt because
  it absorbs more and reflects less.
- **Transmission:** the light passes through the material. How
  cleanly it passes decides the word:
  - **Transparent** materials transmit light clearly: clear glass
    and clear plastic. You can read through them.
  - **Translucent** materials transmit some light but scatter it:
    wax paper and frosted glass. Light gets through, but you
    cannot read through them — the image is blurred because the
    rays are scattered in many directions.
  - **Opaque** materials transmit practically no light: cardboard
    and wood. They reflect some light and absorb the rest, which
    is why they cast shadows.

For a single beam hitting one material, a useful check is that
the three shares add up: what is reflected plus what is absorbed
plus what is transmitted accounts for (nearly) all the light that
arrived. A mirror is mostly reflection; a black cloth is mostly
absorption; clear glass is mostly transmission. Real materials
mix the three — frosted glass, for example, transmits and
scatters, reflects a little, and absorbs a little.

Reflection has one more rule worth knowing: for a smooth mirror,
the angle at which light arrives (measured from a line
perpendicular to the surface, the **normal**) equals the angle
at which it leaves, on the other side of the normal. Shine a beam
at 30° from the normal and it reflects at 30° on the other side.
That is why a periscope's angled mirrors can steer a view around
a corner, and why you must stand in the right spot to catch a
mirror's glare.

**Color** follows directly from reflection and absorption.
White light contains all the colors of the rainbow. An object
looks red because it reflects red light to your eye and absorbs
most of the other colors. An object looks white because it
reflects (nearly) all colors; it looks black because it absorbs
(nearly) all colors and reflects almost none. This also predicts
a strange-looking result: a red apple lit by green-only light
looks dark, almost black — there is no red light arriving for
the apple to reflect, and it absorbs the green.

Two cautions. First, light, unlike sound, **can travel through
empty space** — sunlight crosses the vacuum between the Sun and
Earth. Second, when light passes from one material into another
(for example, air into water), it **bends** at the surface. That
bending is called **refraction**, and it is why a straw in a glass
looks broken at the waterline and why a lens can focus light.
Refraction is named and described here; its angle rules are a
later course's work, not this unit's.

**Vocabulary:** *reflection* (light bouncing off), *absorption*
(light taken in, usually becoming thermal energy), *transmission*
(light passing through), *transparent* (clear transmission),
*translucent* (scattered transmission), *opaque* (no
transmission), *normal* (line perpendicular to a surface, used
to measure reflection angles), *refraction* (bending of light
passing into a new material).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Classify each material and predict what happens
to a flashlight beam: (a) clear glass — transparent: most light
transmitted, a bright spot appears on the wall behind it;
(b) wax paper — translucent: light passes but is scattered, so
the spot is dim and fuzzy and you cannot read through it;
(c) cardboard — opaque: no light passes; a shadow appears, and
the cardboard's lit side warms slightly as it absorbs light;
(d) mirror — mostly reflection: the beam bounces off at the same
angle it arrived, and little is absorbed or transmitted.

**Example 2.** A red apple sits under white light, then under
green-only light. Under white light it looks red: it reflects
the red part of the white light and absorbs the rest. Under
green-only light it looks dark: the only light arriving is green,
the apple absorbs green, and there is no red light to reflect —
so almost nothing reaches your eye from the apple. The apple did
not change color; the light changed.

## Guided practice

1. A black shirt and a white shirt hang in the sun. Which warms
   faster, and which of reflection/absorption explains it?
2. You can see your face in a mirror but not in a sheet of white
   paper, though both reflect light. What is the difference
   between the two reflections?
3. A beam hits a mirror at 30° from the normal. At what angle
   does it leave, measured from the normal, and on which side?
4. Frosted glass is in a bathroom window. Is it transparent,
   translucent, or opaque? Justify with what happens to light
   and whether you can read through it.

## Independent practice

1. Predict reflection, absorption, and transmission (most /
   some / almost none) for: (a) a windowpane, (b) a black curtain,
   (c) a sheet of aluminum foil with its shiny side out.
2. A blue ball under white light looks blue. Explain in terms of
   reflection and absorption. What would it look like under
   red-only light? Explain.
3. A straw in a glass of water looks bent at the surface. Name
   the effect and explain in one sentence why it happens.
4. **Error analysis.** A learner writes: "Translucent means no
   light gets through." Correct the sentence, and give one
   example that proves the correction.

## Applied task

Choose the right material for each job and justify it with
reflection, absorption, and transmission: (a) a greenhouse
window, (b) a blackout curtain for a bedroom, (c) a lampshade
that should glow softly without glare. One or two sentences each.

## Exit check

A beam shines on wax paper. Is the paper transparent,
translucent, or opaque — and can you read a book through it?
One sentence.

## Support, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** keep a three-column chart
  (reflect / absorb / transmit) on the board; every new material
  gets sorted into it before any explanation.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may sort physical
  samples into three labeled trays instead of writing the
  classification table.
- **Extension:** a periscope uses two mirrors at 45° to shift a
  view sideways. Learners who finish early can draw the ray path
  (in at 45°, out at 45°, twice) and explain why the image is
  not upside down.
- **Teacher note:** the persistent confusion is translucent vs.
  transparent ("both let light through, so both are the same").
  The test is always: can you read through it? If yes,
  transparent; if light passes but the image is lost,
  translucent. Key: [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
