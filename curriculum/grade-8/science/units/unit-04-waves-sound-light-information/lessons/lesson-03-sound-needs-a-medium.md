# Lesson 03 — Sound Needs a Medium

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain why sound needs a material
to travel through, compare sound speeds in air, water, and steel,
and use echo timing to estimate a distance.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, a tuning fork or
phone tone app (adult-operated), a cup-and-string telephone made
with two cups and taut string · **Teacher key:**
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult taps a desk and asks: "What is actually moving between
the desk and your ear?" Learners usually say "the sound." Push
once more: "The sound is a pattern in what?" (The air.) Today
that pattern gets its rules.

## Explanation

Sound is a **longitudinal wave**. A vibrating object — a guitar
string, a speaker cone, your vocal cords — pushes the air next to
it into bunches (**compressions**) and then pulls back, leaving
spread-out parts (**rarefactions**). That bunch-and-spread pattern
travels outward. When it reaches your ear, it pushes your eardrum
in and out at the same rhythm, and your brain hears that rhythm
as sound.

Because sound is a pattern *in* a material, **sound needs a
medium**. No medium, no sound. On the Moon, which has practically
no air, two astronauts standing face to face could shout all day
and hear nothing through the vacuum between their helmets — they
must use radios, which send information as electromagnetic waves,
not as sound. (Their helmet radios work because radio waves, like
light, do not need a medium. Lesson 05 returns to this.)

How fast sound travels depends on the medium. The particles of a
solid are tightly linked, so a push is passed along quickly; the
particles of a gas are far apart, so the push takes longer to
hand on. Approximate reference speeds, verified for this unit:

| Medium | Speed of sound (approximate) |
|---|---|
| Air (at 20 °C) | 343 m/s |
| Water | about 1,480 m/s |
| Steel | about 5,960 m/s |

So sound travels roughly 4 times faster in water than in air, and
roughly 17 times faster in steel than in air. That is why you can
hear a distant train sooner through the rails than through the
air, and why whales can communicate across whole oceans: water
carries their sounds fast and far. In air, sound speed also rises
with temperature (about 0.6 m/s faster per degree Celsius), which
is why the table states its air temperature.

Two words describe a sound, and learners constantly swap them:

- **Pitch** is how high or low a sound seems, and it follows
  **frequency**: a faster vibration (more waves per second) makes
  a higher pitch. A 440 Hz tone sounds higher than a 220 Hz tone.
- **Loudness** is how strong a sound seems, and it follows
  **amplitude**: a bigger vibration makes a louder sound. A
  whisper and a shout at the same pitch differ in amplitude, not
  in frequency.

Finally, the **echo**: sound reflects off a hard surface and
comes back. If you clap once near a wall and the echo returns
after a time $t$, the sound traveled to the wall and back, so the
distance to the wall is:

$$\text{distance} = \frac{v \times t}{2}$$

The divide-by-2 is the whole lesson: the measured time covers a
round trip, not a one-way trip. Forgetting it doubles the answer.

**Vocabulary:** *compression* (bunched-up air in a sound wave),
*rarefaction* (spread-out air), *pitch* (high/low, set by
frequency), *loudness* (strong/weak, set by amplitude), *echo*
(reflected sound returning to the listener), *medium* (revisited:
sound's medium must be matter — solid, liquid, or gas).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** You stand facing a cliff, clap once, and hear the
echo 0.8 s later. How far away is the cliff? (Use 343 m/s for
sound in air.)
Round-trip distance = $343 \times 0.8 = 274.4$ m. One-way distance
= $274.4 / 2 = 137.2$ m. The cliff is about 137 m away. Check:
sound travels 343 m every second, so in 0.8 s it covers about
274 m out and back — 137 m each way is sensible.

**Example 2.** You see lightning, then hear thunder 6 s later.
About how far away was the strike? Light travels so fast that its
travel time over a few kilometers is negligible (a few millionths
of a second), so count only the sound's time: distance ≈
$343 \times 6 = 2{,}058$ m, about 2.1 km. This is the origin of the
rule of thumb "3 seconds between flash and thunder means about
1 km" ($343 \times 3 = 1{,}029$ m ≈ 1 km). It is an estimate, not
a survey: temperature and wind shift the true value slightly.

## Guided practice

1. An echo returns 0.4 s after a clap. How far away is the
   reflecting wall? (Use 343 m/s.)
2. Flash-to-thunder time is 3 s. Estimate the strike distance
   in meters and in kilometers.
3. Rank air, water, and steel from slowest to fastest sound
   travel, and give one real-world consequence of that ranking.
4. Two tones: A at 220 Hz, B at 440 Hz, both played at the same
   volume. Which has the higher pitch? Are they equally loud, or
   is one louder? Explain which property (frequency or amplitude)
   decides each answer.

## Independent practice

1. An echo returns 1.2 s after a shout. How far away is the
   reflecting surface?
2. A sound travels 596 m. How long does it take through steel
   (5,960 m/s)? How long through air (343 m/s)? Give the air
   time to two decimal places and explain why the two times
   differ so much.
3. A learner turns a guitar tuning peg to tighten a string, and
   the note gets higher. Did the learner change the string's
   frequency or its amplitude? Explain.
4. Why can't sound travel from the Sun to Earth, even though
   sunlight does? (Two sentences: name the medium problem for
   sound and the medium freedom for light.)

## Applied task

Design a safe echo measurement for the schoolyard or a large
room: where you stand, what you clap with, how you time the echo,
and how you compute the distance. Include one way the measurement
could go wrong (for example, a second reflecting surface) and
how you would check for it.

## Exit check

A train approaches. You press your ear to the rail and hear it
before you hear it through the air. Why — in one sentence using
the speeds from this lesson?

## Support, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** keep the speed table visible all
  lesson; pitch = frequency and loudness = amplitude stay paired
  on the board as a two-column chart.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may demonstrate pitch
  vs. loudness with their own voice (high/low, soft/loud) instead
  of writing definitions.
- **Extension:** sonar uses the same echo math in water: a ship
  sends a sound pulse and times its return. Learners who finish
  early can compute the depth for a 0.2 s round trip in water
  ($1{,}480 \times 0.2 / 2 = 148$ m).
- **Teacher note:** the two classic errors are forgetting the
  divide-by-2 in echo problems and swapping pitch and loudness.
  If a learner writes "louder = higher frequency," have them hum
  one note softly, then loudly, and name what changed. Key:
  [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
