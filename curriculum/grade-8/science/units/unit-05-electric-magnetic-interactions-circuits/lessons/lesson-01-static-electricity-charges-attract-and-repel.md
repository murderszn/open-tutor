# Lesson 01 — Static Electricity: Charges Attract and Repel

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain why a rubbed balloon
sticks to a wall, using charges that move and charges that stay.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** a balloon, wool or fleece
cloth, small torn paper bits, two strips of clear tape, notebook ·
**Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult rubs a balloon on a wool cloth and holds it near the
learner's hair (or near small paper bits on the table). Ask:
"What did the rubbing do? What is pulling the hair (or the paper)
toward the balloon — is the balloon touching it?" Accept all
answers. Hold the question: the balloon is not touching the hair,
yet something pulls. That "something across a gap" is this
unit's central idea.

## Explanation

Everything around you is made of atoms, and atoms carry two kinds
of **electric charge**: **positive charge** and **negative
charge**. The positive charge sits in the middle of the atom (the
nucleus) and does not move. The negative charge is carried by tiny
particles called **electrons**, which sit on the outside of the
atom — and electrons *can* move from one material to another.

Most objects start out **neutral**: they have equal amounts of
positive and negative charge, so the two cancel out and there is
no net charge. Rubbing changes that. When you rub a balloon on a
wool sweater, electrons move from the sweater onto the balloon.
The balloon now has more negative charge than positive charge —
it has a **net charge**, and we say it is negatively charged. The
sweater lost those electrons, so it now has more positive than
negative charge. Nothing was created or destroyed: the charge
moved. That is the first rule of this unit — **charge is
conserved; it moves, it is not created**.

Charged objects push and pull on each other, and the rules are
simple:

- **Like charges repel:** two negatively charged objects push
  each other away; two positively charged objects push each
  other away.
- **Opposite charges attract:** a negatively charged object and
  a positively charged object pull toward each other.

That is why the balloon sticks to the wall. The balloon is
negatively charged. The wall is neutral — but "neutral" does not
mean "no charges." Inside the wall there are positive and
negative charges in equal numbers. When the charged balloon
comes close, it pushes the wall's negative charges slightly away
and pulls the positive charges slightly toward it. The wall's
surface, right next to the balloon, ends up slightly positive —
and the balloon is attracted to that positive surface. The
charges inside the wall only shifted a tiny distance; nothing
left the wall. This is called **induction**, and it is why a
charged object can attract a neutral one.

A charged object can also attract a *stream* of water: the
balloon pulls the water's positive charges toward it, and the
thin stream bends toward the balloon. The same rule explains the
tape strips in the investigation: two strips of tape pulled off
the same surface carry the same kind of charge, so they push
each other apart.

Two more facts before the examples. First, the force is stronger
when there is more charge, and stronger when the objects are
closer together — double the charge, about double the pull;
halve the distance, a much stronger pull. (The exact distance
rule is high-school work; here we use the proportional idea.)
Second, the word **static** means the charges are staying put.
The balloon's extra electrons sit on its surface and do not flow
away — because rubber is an insulator, which is Lesson 02's
subject. A charged balloon will slowly lose its charge to the air
over minutes, which is why the trick stops working if you wait.

**Vocabulary:** *electric charge* (a property of matter that
causes electric forces; two kinds, positive and negative),
*positive charge* (the kind in the nucleus), *negative charge*
(the kind carried by electrons), *electron* (a tiny particle
carrying negative charge, able to move between materials),
*static electricity* (charge that stays in one place),
*net charge* (the leftover charge when positive and negative do
not cancel), *attract* (pull together), *repel* (push apart).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A balloon is rubbed on wool and then held near a
wall. It sticks. Explain with the charge model. Rubbing moved
electrons from the wool onto the balloon, so the balloon has a
negative net charge and the wool has a positive net charge. Held
near the wall, the balloon's negative charge pushes the wall's
electrons slightly away and pulls the wall's positive charges
slightly toward it, so the wall's surface near the balloon is
slightly positive. Opposite charges attract, so the balloon is
pulled to the wall. Nothing touched the wall except the field —
the force acted across the gap. If the balloon had not been
rubbed, it would be neutral and nothing would happen.

**Example 2.** Two strips of clear tape are stuck to a table and
pulled off quickly, then held near each other. They push apart.
Why? Both strips were pulled off the same surface in the same
way, so both picked up the same kind of net charge. Like charges
repel, so the strips push each other away. Contrast: if one strip
were charged positively and the other negatively, they would pull
together. The test "do they attract or repel?" tells you whether
two objects carry the same kind of charge or opposite kinds —
without ever seeing a charge.

## Guided practice

1. A balloon rubbed on wool picks up extra electrons. Is the
   balloon positively or negatively charged? What about the
   wool? Explain where the charge came from.
2. A charged balloon is held near small bits of paper on a
   table. The bits jump up to the balloon. The paper started
   neutral. Explain the jump using induction — what shifted
   inside the paper?
3. Two balloons are both rubbed on wool and hung from strings.
   They swing away from each other. What does that tell you
   about their charges? What would happen if one balloon were
   rubbed on wool and the other on a material that gives it a
   positive charge?

## Independent practice

1. You rub a plastic ruler on your sleeve and it picks up paper
   bits. A learner says, "The rubbing created new charge." Is
   that right? Correct the sentence using charge conservation.
2. A negatively charged balloon is brought near a neutral metal
   doorknob (metal is a conductor — Lesson 02 explains why that
   matters). Predict: will the balloon be attracted, repelled,
   or unaffected? Give the reason, and say what is different
   from the wall case.
3. A charged balloon is held 10 cm from a wall and sticks
   weakly. The adult moves it to 5 cm from the wall. Predict
   whether the pull gets stronger or weaker, and explain using
   the distance rule.
4. Draw (or describe) a before-and-after charge diagram for the
   balloon and the sweater: show the + and − charges on each
   before rubbing (equal numbers) and after rubbing (balloon
   with extra −, sweater with extra +).

## Applied task

Sort these into "attract" or "repel" and give the charge reason
for each: (a) a negatively charged balloon and a positively
charged sweater; (b) two negatively charged balloons; (c) a
negatively charged balloon and a neutral wall; (d) a positively
charged rod and a positively charged ball; (e) a charged balloon
and a thin stream of water. For (c) and (e), name the mechanism
(induction) in your reason.

## Exit check

A balloon rubbed on wool sticks to a wall. An hour later it has
fallen off. In one or two sentences, explain what changed — and
say whether any charge was created or destroyed.

## Support, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** act out the balloon-and-wall
  demo before naming any term; the words "net charge" and
  "induction" land after the observation, not before.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the exit
  check by pointing at the balloon and wall and describing the
  charge movement in words.
- **Extension:** lightning is the same story at a huge scale —
  charge separates inside a storm cloud until the pull across
  the air gap is strong enough for charge to jump as a bolt.
  Learners who finish early can explain, in the charge model,
  why lightning is a discharge and not a creation of charge.
- **Teacher note:** the most common misconception is that
  rubbing *creates* charge. If it persists, count charges in
  the before/after diagram (independent practice 4): the total
  number of + and − signs is the same before and after; only
  their addresses changed. The second misconception is that a
  neutral object has no charges at all — stress that neutral
  means equal numbers, and induction is those charges shifting
  in place. Key: [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01).
