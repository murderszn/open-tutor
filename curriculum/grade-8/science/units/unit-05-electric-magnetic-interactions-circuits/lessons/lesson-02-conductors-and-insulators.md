# Lesson 02 — Conductors and Insulators

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can test whether a material lets
charge move, and I can explain why static sticks to some things
and drains away from others.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** a balloon and wool cloth
(from Lesson 01), a simple test circuit (one AA battery, one
small bulb, wires with alligator clips), test samples: a metal
spoon, a plastic spoon, a rubber band, a glass jar, a wooden
pencil, a piece of aluminum foil, a paper clip, a piece of string ·
**Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult holds a charged balloon near a metal spoon and near a
plastic spoon. Ask: "Which one will the balloon's charge move
onto — or will it move onto neither? Predict first, then watch."
Touch the charged balloon to the metal spoon, then to the plastic
spoon, then test each against paper bits. (The metal spoon takes
the charge away and the balloon goes dead; the plastic spoon
leaves the balloon charged.) Ask: "What is different about the
metal?" Hold that thought — it is the whole lesson.

## Explanation

Lesson 01 said electrons can move. This lesson asks: move
*through what*? The answer splits every material into two
groups.

A **conductor** is a material that lets electric charge move
through it easily. Metals are the conductors you meet every day:
copper, aluminum, steel, the metal in a spoon or a paper clip.
In a metal, some electrons are not held tightly to any one atom;
they are free to wander through the whole piece of metal. Touch
a charged balloon to a metal spoon and the balloon's extra
electrons spread out over the spoon (and, if you are holding the
spoon, over you and into the floor) — the balloon's net charge
drains away, and the balloon stops attracting paper.

An **insulator** is a material that does *not* let charge move
through it easily. Plastic, rubber, glass, wood, dry air, and
cloth are insulators. Their electrons are held tightly to their
own atoms, so extra charge stays where it was put. That is why
static electricity is a balloon-and-sweater phenomenon and not a
metal-spoon phenomenon: the balloon is rubber (an insulator), so
the electrons rubbed onto it stay on its surface, and the charge
builds up instead of draining away.

How do you test which group a material belongs to? Build a
simple circuit — battery, bulb, wires — and put the material in
the loop as a bridge between two wire ends. If the bulb lights,
charge is flowing through the material: it is a conductor. If
the bulb stays dark, charge cannot get through: it is an
insulator. (This test is a preview of Lesson 05's circuits; the
adult builds the circuit and the learner only swaps the test
samples.)

Two safety ideas follow directly from this. First, the reason
electrical cords are metal inside and plastic outside: the metal
carries the charge where it is wanted, and the plastic keeps it
from going where it is not wanted — including through you.
Second, water: pure water is a poor conductor, but tap water
contains dissolved salts and conducts weakly — enough to matter.
**Never mix water and electricity.** That is why you never use
electrical equipment with wet hands, and why this unit's builds
use batteries only.

**The battery-only rule for this unit (adult reads aloud):**
every electrical task in this unit uses AA or AAA batteries
(1.5 V each), at most two cells. Never use a wall outlet, a
mains adapter, or a phone charger. Never connect a bare wire
directly across a battery's two ends — that is a short circuit,
and the wire heats up fast enough to burn. If any wire or
battery feels warm, disconnect it at once and tell the adult.

**Vocabulary:** *conductor* (a material charge moves through
easily, e.g. metals), *insulator* (a material charge does not
move through easily, e.g. plastic, rubber, glass, wood),
*short circuit* (a wire connected directly across a battery's
two ends, with no bulb or other load — it heats up dangerously).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A learner touches a charged balloon to a metal
spoon, then holds the balloon near paper bits. Nothing happens.
The same balloon, charged again the same way, is touched to a
plastic spoon, then held near paper bits. The bits jump. Explain
both. The metal spoon is a conductor: the balloon's extra
electrons spread through the spoon and away, so the balloon
loses its net charge and cannot attract the paper. The plastic
spoon is an insulator: the electrons cannot move through it, so
the balloon keeps its charge and still attracts the paper. The
test result (balloon dead vs. balloon live) is the evidence for
the classification, not the look of the material.

**Example 2.** A lamp cord has copper wire inside and plastic
outside. A learner asks, "Why not make the whole cord out of
copper? It would conduct better." Answer with the two groups:
the copper inside is the conductor — it carries the charge to
the bulb. The plastic outside is the insulator — it stops the
charge from leaving the wire into your hand. A bare copper cord
would work electrically and be dangerous to touch; the plastic
is what makes it safe to handle. The same pair of jobs appears
in every electrical device: conductors carry, insulators contain.

## Guided practice

1. Predict, then test with the circuit: which of these will let
   the bulb light — a metal paper clip, a plastic spoon, a
   rubber band, aluminum foil? Write your prediction before
   each test and one sentence after each test.
2. A charged balloon is touched to a wooden pencil. The balloon
   stays charged. Touched to a metal paper clip, the balloon
   goes dead. Classify the pencil and the paper clip, and say
   what moved in the paper-clip case.
3. Why does static charge build up on a rubber balloon but not
   on a metal rod rubbed the same way? Use "free electrons" in
   your answer.

## Independent practice

1. A learner tests a glass rod in the circuit: the bulb stays
   dark. Then the learner rubs the glass rod with silk and it
   picks up paper bits. Are those two results consistent?
   Explain — can an insulator hold a charge?
2. You are given four unlabeled rods: copper, plastic, wood,
   steel. Describe the test you would run to sort them into
   conductors and insulators, and what result you expect for
   each rod.
3. A learner says, "Insulators have no charges in them, so they
   cannot attract anything." What is wrong with that sentence?
   (Hint: think back to the wall in Lesson 01.)
4. Explain why the handle of a cooking pot is plastic or wood
   rather than metal — and why the pot itself is metal. Use
   both "conductor" and "insulator," and note that heat, not
   charge, is the flow in this example.

## Applied task

A flashlight has: a metal spring inside, a plastic case, copper
wires, and a glass bulb cover. For each part, state whether it
is a conductor or an insulator, and explain what job that
property does in the flashlight. Then state the one safety rule
from this lesson that applies when you change the flashlight's
batteries.

## Exit check

A charged balloon touches a metal doorknob and goes dead; the
same balloon touches a wooden door and stays charged. In one or
two sentences, explain both results with the words conductor
and insulator.

## Support, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** sort the test samples into two
  piles by hand before naming the groups; the words conductor
  and insulator label the piles, not the other way round.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the exit
  check by pointing at the two test results and describing what
  moved, in words.
- **Extension:** grounding — a conductor connected to the Earth
  drains charge away completely, which is why a lightning rod
  (a metal rod wired into the ground) protects a building: it
  gives the charge an easy conducting path instead of a path
  through the building. Learners who finish early can explain
  why a lightning rod is a conductor and what would go wrong if
  it were an insulator.
- **Teacher note:** the most common misconception is that
  insulators "have no charge." Keep returning to the wall:
  neutral insulators have equal + and − charges, and induction
  shifts them. The second misconception is that "shiny means
  conductor" — aluminum foil is shiny and conducts, but a shiny
  plastic wrapper is an insulator; the test, not the look,
  decides. Key: [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
