# Lesson 06 — Troubleshooting Circuits

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can find out why a dead circuit is
dead, and I can explain where the energy goes in a working one.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** a working series circuit
and a working parallel circuit (from Lesson 05), one bulb known
to be burned out, one tired battery, a loose wire, notebook ·
**Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult shows a circuit that does not light and asks: "It was
working yesterday. What could have changed?" Collect guesses —
dead battery, burned-out bulb, loose wire, open switch — and
write them on the board. Ask: "Which one would you check first,
and how would you check it without guessing?" Hold that thought:
troubleshooting is checking in order, not guessing in a circle.

## Explanation

A dead circuit is a puzzle with a short list of suspects. A
circuit needs five things to light a bulb: a battery with push
left, a closed loop, wires that actually connect, a switch that
is closed, and a bulb whose filament is intact. If any one of
them fails, the whole loop is open and nothing lights. The
troubleshooting routine checks them in order, easiest first:

1. **Is the switch closed?** Look at it. Flip it off and on
   again. This is the most common "fault" and the easiest fix.
2. **Is the battery fresh?** Swap in a battery you know is good.
   If the bulb lights, the old battery was dead. (A battery
   that has been left connected to an electromagnet coil for a
   long time is often the dead one.)
3. **Are the wires connected?** Wiggle each alligator clip
   gently and watch the bulb. A clip that fell off the wire, or
   a wire whose end is still insulated, breaks the loop.
4. **Is the bulb good?** Swap in a bulb you know is good. If it
   lights, the first bulb's filament burned out. (Hold a burned
   bulb up to the light: the filament inside is broken, and the
   glass may be darkened.)
5. **Is the loop complete?** Trace the whole loop with a finger:
   battery → wire → switch → wire → bulb → wire → battery. Any
   gap is the fault.

The swap test is the key move: replace one suspect part with a
known-good one and see if the circuit comes back to life. If it
does, you found the fault. If it does not, put the old part back
and test the next suspect. Never change two parts at once — that
is the same fair-test rule from the electromagnet investigation,
and breaking it means you will never know which part was dead.

Once a circuit works, ask the energy question: where does the
energy go? The battery stores **chemical energy**. When the
circuit is closed, that energy becomes the energy of the moving
charge — **electrical energy** — which the bulb turns into
**light energy** and **thermal energy** (the bulb gets warm).
Nothing is created or destroyed; the energy is transferred and
transformed, exactly as Unit 03 taught. A battery that has run a
bulb for a long time is not "empty of charge" — it is empty of
usable chemical energy. And the wires do not get hot in a normal
circuit because the bulb limits the current; only a short circuit
(a loop with no bulb) lets the current run wild, which is why
the safety rule forbids it.

**Vocabulary:** *troubleshoot* (find the fault in a circuit by
checking in order), *filament* (the thin wire in a bulb — when
it breaks, the bulb is dead), *dead battery* (a battery with no
usable chemical energy left).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A series circuit with two bulbs suddenly goes
dark. Both bulbs were lit a minute ago. The adult checks the
switch (closed), swaps in a fresh battery (still dark), checks
the wires (all connected), and swaps bulb A for a known-good
bulb (both bulbs light). What was the fault, and why did both
bulbs go dark? Bulb A's filament had burned out. In a series
circuit there is one loop, so one broken filament opens the
whole loop and both bulbs go dark. The swap test proved it: a
good bulb in A's place restored the loop. Notice the order of
checks — switch, battery, wires, bulb — and that only one part
was changed at a time.

**Example 2.** A parallel circuit with two bulbs: bulb A is
dark, bulb B is lit. The learner says, "The battery must be
dead." Is that right? No. If the battery were dead, neither
branch would have push and both bulbs would be dark. Bulb B is
lit, so the battery, the switch, and the main wires are fine.
The fault is in bulb A's branch only — either bulb A burned out
or its branch wire is loose. The swap test for this case: swap
bulb A for a known-good bulb. If it lights, bulb A was the
fault; if not, the branch wire is the fault.

## Guided practice

1. A circuit is dead. The switch is closed and the battery is
   fresh. You swap in a known-good bulb and it lights. What was
   the fault? What did the swap prove?
2. A parallel circuit has both bulbs dark. The switch is closed.
   Name the two most likely faults, and say how you would tell
   them apart with one swap.
3. Trace the energy in a working circuit: start with the battery
   and end with light and heat. Name each energy form in order.

## Independent practice

1. A learner's electromagnet stopped lifting clips. The learner
   blames the nail. Use the troubleshooting routine: list the
   checks in order, and say what each check would prove.
2. A series circuit lights, but dimly. The battery is fresh and
   both bulbs are good. Give two possible causes for the
   dimness, and a test for each. (Hint: think about what else
   could be in the loop.)
3. A learner leaves a battery connected to an electromagnet coil
   overnight. In the morning the battery is warm and dead. Use
   the energy story to explain what happened to the battery's
   chemical energy.
4. **Error analysis.** A learner writes: "The bulb uses up the
   electricity, so the wire after the bulb has no electricity
   left." Correct the sentence using the energy story: what
   does the bulb take from the moving charge, and what keeps
   flowing?

## Applied task

Write a troubleshooting card for the unit's circuit kit — five
checks, in order, each with one sentence saying what to do and
one saying what the result proves. The card must work for both
a series and a parallel circuit, and it must say where the two
circuits need different thinking. (The teacher guide keeps a
model card for comparison.)

## Exit check

A parallel circuit's bulb A is dark and bulb B is lit. In one
or two sentences, say where the fault must be — and where it
cannot be.

## Support, extension, teacher note

- **Reading/language access:** act out the routine on a real
  dead circuit before writing the card; the five checks are
  things the learner already did with their hands.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the exit
  check by tracing the two branches on the Lesson 05 diagram
  and describing which loop is broken, in words.
- **Extension:** a fuse is a deliberate weak link: a thin wire
  that melts and opens the circuit when the current gets too
  big, protecting everything else. A circuit breaker does the
  same job and can be reset. Learners who finish early can
  explain why a house has fuses or breakers, using the short
  circuit story.
- **Teacher note:** the most common misconception is changing
  two things at once ("I swapped the battery AND the bulb and
  it works now, so the battery was dead"). Insist on one swap
  at a time — it is the same fair-test discipline as the
  electromagnet investigation, and it is the skill the project
  rubric scores. The second misconception is "the bulb used up
  the electricity": keep the energy language exact — the bulb
  takes energy, the charge keeps flowing. Key:
  [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06).
