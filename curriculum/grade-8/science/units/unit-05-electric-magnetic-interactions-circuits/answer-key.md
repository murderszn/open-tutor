# Answer Key — Unit 05: Electric and Magnetic Interactions and Circuits

Teacher material. Every item below was solved independently from
the student pages; where a student item admits a defensible
variation, the acceptable range is stated. Do not hand this file
to learners before they have attempted the work.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice.** 1. The balloon is negatively charged (it
gained electrons); the wool is positively charged (it lost
electrons). The charge came from the wool: rubbing moved
electrons from wool to balloon; nothing was created. 2. The paper
is neutral, so its charges are equal in number. The balloon's
negative charge pushes the paper's electrons slightly away and
pulls its positive charges slightly toward the balloon, so the
paper's surface near the balloon is slightly positive and the
balloon attracts it (induction). 3. Both balloons carry the same
kind of charge (negative, from the wool), and like charges repel,
so they swing apart. If one were positive and the other negative,
they would swing toward each other (opposite charges attract).

**Independent practice.** 1. No — rubbing does not create charge.
Rubbing moves electrons from one material to the other; the total
charge is unchanged (charge is conserved). Corrected: "The
rubbing moved electrons onto the ruler, giving it a net negative
charge." 2. Attracted. The balloon's negative charge pushes the
doorknob's free electrons away (metal is a conductor, so its
electrons can move through the whole knob and away into the door
or the person touching it), leaving the knob's surface near the
balloon slightly positive; opposite charges attract. Different
from the wall case only in that the metal's electrons can move
farther (through the conductor) instead of shifting a tiny
distance in place. 3. Stronger. The pull grows as the distance
shrinks; at half the distance the pull is much stronger. 4. Accept
any clear before/after diagram: before — balloon and sweater each
with equal numbers of + and − (for example, 6 + and 6 − each);
after — balloon with extra − (for example, 6 + and 9 −, net −3),
sweater with extra + (9 + and 6 −, net +3). The total number of +
signs and the total number of − signs are the same before and
after.

**Applied task.** (a) Attract — opposite charges. (b) Repel —
like charges. (c) Attract — induction: the balloon's charge
shifts the wall's charges so the near surface is slightly
positive. (d) Repel — like charges. (e) Attract — induction: the
balloon pulls the water's positive charges toward it, bending the
stream. Each reason must name the charge rule or the mechanism.

**Exit check.** The balloon slowly lost its charge to the air
(the extra electrons leaked away over the hour), so its net charge
fell toward zero and the attraction faded. No charge was created
or destroyed — the charge moved off the balloon into the air.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice.** 1. Accept the learner's predictions, then
the results: paper clip and aluminum foil light the bulb
(conductors); plastic spoon and rubber band do not (insulators).
Each result is the evidence for the classification. 2. The metal
spoon is a conductor: the balloon's extra electrons spread
through the spoon (and away), so the balloon loses its net charge.
The plastic spoon is an insulator: the electrons cannot move
through it, so the balloon keeps its charge. 3. On a metal rod
the charge drains away because the rod's free electrons move
easily; on a rubber balloon the electrons are held in place, so
the charge stays and builds up.

**Independent practice.** 1. Yes, consistent. The dark bulb in
the circuit test shows the glass rod is an insulator (charge
cannot flow through it); the rubbed rod picking up paper shows
the same insulator can *hold* a charge on its surface — the two
results are about different things (flow vs. storage). 2. Test:
put each rod in the circuit loop as a bridge. Expect the bulb to
light for copper and steel (conductors) and stay dark for plastic
and wood (insulators). 3. The sentence is wrong: insulators do
have charges — equal numbers of + and −, so they are neutral.
A charged balloon attracts a neutral wall precisely because the
wall's charges shift in place (induction). 4. The pot is metal
(a conductor) so heat flows easily from the burner into the food;
the handle is plastic or wood (an insulator) so heat does not
flow easily into your hand. (Here the flow is heat, not charge —
the same conductor/insulator idea applied to a different flow.)

**Applied task.** Metal spring: conductor — it carries charge
from the battery to the bulb. Plastic case: insulator — it keeps
the charge (and the user) safe by containing it. Copper wires:
conductors — they carry the charge between parts. Glass bulb
cover: insulator — it contains the charge and the heat while
letting light through. Safety rule when changing batteries: use
only AA/AAA cells, never a wall outlet or charger, and never
connect a bare wire across the battery.

**Exit check.** The doorknob is metal (a conductor): the
balloon's extra electrons spread through the knob and away, so
the balloon loses its net charge and goes dead. The wooden door
is an insulator: the electrons cannot move through it, so the
balloon keeps its charge.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice.** 1. North-to-south: attract (opposite poles).
South-to-south: repel (like poles). 2. Away from the magnet (to
the left). Field lines leave the north pole outward in all
directions before curving around to the south pole, so at a point
just left of the north pole the field points away from the magnet,
and the needle's north-seeking end points along the field — away.
(Do not confuse this with a compass *above* the magnet, where the
field runs from the north pole over the top toward the south pole
and the needle points to the right.)
3. The field is stronger close to the magnet and weaker farther
away: at 2 cm it is strong enough to lift the clip; at 8 cm it
is too weak. 4. (Covered in independent practice 1; accept:)
the needle turned with nothing touching the magnet, so the force
exists in the space around the magnet.

**Independent practice.** 1. Two observations: (a) a paper clip
jumps to the magnet across a gap of air, with nothing touching
it; (b) a compass needle turns when a magnet is slid under the
table below it, again with no contact. Both show the force acts
in the space around the magnet, not only where the magnet is.
2. Above the north pole: the needle's north-seeking end points
up and away from the pole (the field leaves N upward); above the
south pole: it points down and toward the pole (the field enters
S from above); below the north pole: it points down and away;
below the south pole: it points up and toward the pole. Accept
any description consistent with "field lines leave N and enter
S." 3. The needle turns toward the magnet's south pole: the
needle's north-seeking end is attracted to the nearby south pole,
and the nearby field is stronger than Earth's field, so the
magnet wins. 4. Correct the drawing: field lines are curves
that leave the north pole, arc around the magnet, and enter the
south pole — they do not shoot straight out in all directions.
The straight-line version is disproved by the compass: above the
magnet the needle points sideways (along the curve), not straight
away from the pole.

**Applied task.** Accept any clear 3-step procedure, for example:
(1) Place the compass 2 cm from the north pole, mark the
direction the needle points and how strongly it swings (or
whether a paper clip jumps there). (2) Repeat 2 cm from the
middle of the magnet. (3) Compare: the spot where the needle
swings harder (or the clip jumps) has the stronger field.
Expected result: the pole is stronger than the middle. The
evidence is the comparison of the two observations.

**Exit check.** The swinging needle proves the magnet's field
exists in the space around the magnet — under the table, through
the wood, with nothing touching the compass. The force acts
across the gap.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice.** 1. About 12 clips (double the turns, about
double the lift: 6 × 2 = 12). The assumption is the proportional
rule — that the lift grows in proportion to the turns over the
tested range, with the same battery and core. 2. No. Two things
changed at once (turns and batteries), so the learner cannot tell
how much of the rise came from the turns and how much from the
batteries. The test is not fair; change one thing at a time.
3. The nail-core coil lifts more. Iron concentrates the magnetic
field; wood does not, so the pencil-core coil is barely a magnet.

**Independent practice.** 1. The pattern is proportional: each
extra 10 turns adds about 3 clips (3, 6, 9). Predicted lift for
40 turns: about 12 clips. Distrust the prediction if the battery
is getting tired, the winding is loose, or the counts vary a lot
between trials — the rule holds in the tested range, not
forever. 2. The data show batteries do matter: the same coil
lifted 6 clips on one battery and 12 on two — doubling the
batteries (current) doubled the lift. Turns are not the only
factor. 3. Two reasons: (a) the battery drains and warms up when
left connected to a low-resistance coil, so later tests would use
a weaker battery; (b) fairness — every test should start with
the same battery strength, so the only difference between tests
is the variable being tested. 4. "Longer nail" is a weak
explanation for one extra clip: one clip is within the normal
wobble between trials. The check: repeat the test three times
with each nail and compare averages; if the averages are still
6 vs. 7, the difference is small and the nail length is not the
important factor — turns, batteries, and core are.

**Applied task.** Accept any fair-test design with: question
("Does the core material matter?"); variable changed = core
material (iron nail vs. no core / wooden pencil); variables kept
the same = turns, batteries, wire, lifting method; measurement =
paper clips lifted, three trials each; "yes" = the iron core
lifts clearly more clips on average than the no-core coil.

**Exit check.** Two changes (either is fine): (1) double the
turns (for example, 10 → 20) keeping the battery and core the
same — expect about 8 clips; (2) add a second battery keeping
the turns and core the same — expect about 8 clips; or (3) swap
an air core for an iron nail core keeping turns and battery the
same. Each answer must name what stays the same.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice.** 1. A closed loop: battery → wire → switch
→ wire → bulb → wire → battery. Opening the switch breaks the
loop, current stops, and the bulb goes out. 2. Series: one path,
so the bulbs share the battery's push — each gets about half,
so each is dim. Parallel: each bulb has its own path and gets
the battery's full push, so each stays bright. 3. Two possible
causes: (a) one bulb burned out (its filament broke, opening the
single loop); (b) a wire came loose (same effect). Test: swap in
a known-good bulb for each bulb in turn; if swapping one bulb
restores both, that bulb was dead; if not, check the wires.

**Independent practice.** 1. The most likely problem is a loose
or disconnected wire (or a wire end that is still insulated).
Fix: check and re-clip every connection, then trace the loop.
2. Dimmer. Three bulbs in series share the battery's push three
ways, so each gets about a third of the push one bulb alone
would get. 3. Two reasons: (a) one bulb burning out does not
darken the house — each light has its own path; (b) each light
gets the full push, so each is as bright as it should be no
matter how many other lights are on. 4. Correct the sentence:
the current is the *same* through both bulbs (one loop, one
current); what is shared is the battery's *push*, so each bulb
gets less push and is dimmer. The bulb does not "use up"
current — it takes energy from the moving charge.

**Applied task.** Build the parallel circuit: battery → switch →
two branches, one bulb per branch → back to the battery. Both
bulbs are as bright as possible (each gets the full push) and
one burning out does not kill the other (each has its own path).
If one switch must control both bulbs at once, put the switch
on the main wire before the branches split (as in the Lesson 05
diagram) — opening it breaks both branches at once.

**Exit check.** The difference is the path and the push: in
series the two bulbs share one path and share the battery's push,
so each is dim; in parallel each bulb has its own path and gets
the full push, so each stays bright.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice.** 1. The fault was the bulb: its filament
had burned out. The swap proved it — a known-good bulb in the
same place restored the circuit, so the battery, switch, and
wires were fine. 2. The battery or the main switch or the main
wires (the parts shared by both branches); test with one swap —
a known-good battery. If both bulbs light, the battery was dead;
if not, check the switch and main wires. 3. Chemical energy in
the battery → electrical energy (moving charge) → light energy
and thermal energy in the bulb.

**Independent practice.** 1. Checks in order: (1) Is the
battery connected? Swap in a fresh battery — proves whether the
battery is dead. (2) Are the wire ends connected? Wiggle the
clips — proves whether a connection is loose. (3) Is the coil
wound tightly and are the turns counted right? Rewind carefully
— proves whether the build changed. (4) Is the nail iron?
Test with a bar magnet — proves whether the core is the problem.
The nail is the *last* suspect, not the first. 2. Two possible
causes: (a) the battery is tired (test: swap in a fresh battery);
(b) a connection is loose and adding resistance (test: re-clip
every wire and watch the brightness). Either test changes one
thing at a time. 3. The coil is almost a short circuit: current
flowed through it all night, turning the battery's chemical
energy into electrical energy and then into thermal energy in
the wire and the battery — so the battery's chemical energy ran
out and the battery got warm. 4. Correct the sentence: the bulb
takes *energy* from the moving charge (turning it into light and
heat); the charge itself keeps flowing around the loop. The wire
after the bulb still carries the same current.

**Applied task.** Accept any card with the five checks in order
(switch, battery, wires, bulb, loop trace), each with what to do
and what the result proves, plus the series/parallel difference
(one fault darkens all in series; in parallel a dark bulb with a
lit bulb means the fault is in that branch only). Compare with
the model card in the [teacher guide](teacher-guide.md).

**Exit check.** The fault must be in bulb A's branch — bulb A
itself or its branch wire. It cannot be the battery, the switch,
or the main wires, because bulb B is lit and those parts are
shared.

## Practice

**Set A.** 1. Rubbing moved electrons from the wool onto the
balloon; the balloon has a negative net charge and the wool a
positive one. The balloon's charge pushes the wall's electrons
slightly away and pulls its positive charges closer, so the
wall's surface near the balloon is slightly positive and attracts
the balloon (induction). 2. The same kind — both strips were
pulled off the same surface the same way, and like charges
repel. 3. The pattern is proportional: doubling the rubs doubles
the bits (12 → 24). Prediction for 30 rubs: about 36 bits.
Assumption: the proportional pattern continues and the balloon
does not lose charge between tests. 4. No. Neutral means equal
numbers of positive and negative charges, not no charges. The
wall is neutral and still attracts the balloon, because its
charges shift in place. 5. Attracting — the balloon pulls the
water's positive charges toward it; the mechanism is induction.
6. Corrected: "Rubbing moved electrons onto the balloon; no
charge was created." Charge is conserved — it moves, it is not
created. 7. After rubbing: before rubbing the balloon is neutral
(equal + and −); after, it has more − than +, so it has a net
negative charge. "Net" means the leftover after + and − cancel.
8. Corrected: "Like charges repel and opposite charges attract."
Proof: two balloons rubbed on wool swing apart (like charges
repel); a charged balloon sticks to the positively charged
sweater (opposite charges attract).

**Set B.** 1. Paper clip: conductor — the bulb lit, so charge
flowed through it. Rubber band: insulator — the bulb stayed
dark, so charge could not get through. 2. Copper inside:
conductor — carries the charge to the device. Plastic outside:
insulator — keeps the charge from leaving the wire into your
hand. 3. The metal spoon is a conductor: the balloon's extra
electrons spread through the spoon and away, so the balloon
loses its net charge. The plastic spoon is an insulator: the
electrons cannot move through it, so the balloon keeps its
charge. What moved in the metal case: electrons, off the balloon
and through the spoon. 4. Conductors: copper wire, steel nail,
aluminum foil (bulb lights). Insulators: glass rod, wooden
ruler, rubber glove (bulb stays dark). 5. Tap water conducts
weakly, so with wet hands charge can flow through the water and
through you; never use electrical equipment with wet hands. 6. A
conductor — but a poor one: the dim bulb shows some charge
flows through the graphite, but less than through a metal, so
the current is smaller. 7. An insulator's electrons are held
tightly to their atoms, so extra charge stays where it was put.
A conductor's electrons are free to move, so the charge spreads
through the conductor (and through your hand into the floor)
and drains away. 8. Corrected: "Insulators have equal numbers
of + and − charges, so they are neutral — and a charged object
can still attract them by induction." Proof: the charged balloon
sticks to the neutral wall.

**Set C.** 1. North-to-north: repel. North-to-south: attract.
Rule: like poles repel, opposite poles attract. 2. To the right:
above the magnet the field lines run from the north pole (left)
over the top toward the south pole (right), and the needle's
north-seeking end points along the field. 3. The field is
stronger near the magnet and weaker farther away — strong enough
to lift the clip at 2 cm, too weak at 8 cm. 4. The swinging
needle proves the magnetic field exists in the space around the
magnet — under the table, through the wood — with nothing
touching the compass. 5. Earth is a giant magnet: the compass
needle's north-seeking end is pulled toward Earth's magnetic
pole near the geographic north. 6. The magnets now repel: the
two south poles push each other apart. 7. The filings form curved
lines leaving the north pole, arcing around the magnet, and
entering the south pole. The lines are closest together near the
poles, where the field is strongest. 8. Corrected: "The magnetic
field exists in the space around the whole magnet, strongest near
the poles and weaker farther away." Proof: a compass needle
turns in empty space centimeters from the magnet, with nothing
magnetic between it and the magnet.

**Set D.** 1. 8 clips on average. 2. The pattern is
proportional: doubling the turns about doubles the lift (10 → 4,

stronger magnet. 4. The iron nail core is stronger: 6 clips vs.
1 clip — about 5 more clips (six times as many). The core's job

once (turns and batteries), so the learner cannot tell how much

Two reasons: (a) the battery drains and warms up when left

the core's own limits mean the lift does not keep doubling

**Set E.** 1. In series the two bulbs share the battery's push —
each gets about half the push one bulb alone had, so each is
