# Practice — Unit 05: Electric and Magnetic Interactions and Circuits

Work in your notebook. Show every calculation with units. The
adult checks with the [answer key](answer-key.md); re-work any
missed item before moving on. Datasets in these sets are fictional
practice data unless a set says otherwise.

## Set A — Static electricity (Lesson 01)

1. A balloon is rubbed on a wool sweater, then held near a wall.
   It sticks. Explain what moved during the rubbing, what net
   charge the balloon now has, and why the wall (which started
   neutral) attracts it.
2. Two strips of clear tape are pulled off the same table in the
   same way and held near each other. They push apart. Do the
   strips carry the same kind of charge or opposite kinds? How
   do you know?
3. Fictional practice data: a balloon rubbed 10 times picks up
   12 paper bits. The same balloon rubbed 20 times picks up 24
   paper bits. Describe the pattern and predict how many bits
   30 rubs would pick up. What are you assuming?
4. A learner says, "A neutral object has no charges in it." Is
   that right? Explain what "neutral" actually means, using the
   wall as your example.
5. A charged balloon is held near a thin stream of water from a
   tap. The stream bends toward the balloon. Is the balloon
   attracting or repelling the water? Name the mechanism.
6. A learner says, "Rubbing the balloon created new charge."
   Correct the sentence using charge conservation.
7. Which has a net charge: the balloon before rubbing, or the
   balloon after rubbing? Explain what "net" means here.
8. **Error analysis.** A learner writes: "Opposite charges repel
   and like charges attract." Correct the sentence and give one
   observation that proves the correction.

## Set B — Conductors and insulators (Lesson 02)

1. In the circuit test, a metal paper clip in the loop makes the
   bulb light; a rubber band in the loop leaves the bulb dark.
   Classify the paper clip and the rubber band, and say what
   each result proves.
2. Electrical cords have copper wire inside and plastic outside.
   Explain what job each material does, using the words
   conductor and insulator.
3. A charged balloon is touched to a metal spoon and goes dead;
   touched to a plastic spoon, it stays charged. Explain both
   results. What moved in the metal-spoon case?
4. Sort these into conductors and insulators: copper wire, glass
   rod, steel nail, wooden ruler, aluminum foil, rubber glove.
   Give the test result you would expect for each one.
5. Pure water is a poor conductor, but tap water conducts weakly
   because of dissolved salts. Explain why the safety rule says
   never to use electrical equipment with wet hands.
6. A learner tests a pencil lead (graphite) in the circuit and
   the bulb lights dimly. Is graphite a conductor or an
   insulator? Explain what "dimly" tells you.
7. Why can an insulator hold a static charge for minutes, while
   a conductor held in your hand cannot? Use "free electrons"
   in your answer.
8. **Error analysis.** A learner writes: "Insulators have no
   charges inside them, so they cannot attract anything."
   Correct the sentence and name the observation from
   Lesson 01 that proves it wrong.

## Set C — Magnets and fields (Lesson 03)

1. Two bar magnets are held north pole to north pole. Do they
   attract or repel? What about north pole to south pole? State
   the rule you used.
2. A compass is placed above the middle of a bar magnet whose
   north pole is on the left. The needle's north-seeking end
   points to the right. Explain why, using the field map.
3. A paper clip jumps to a magnet from 2 cm away but does not
   move from 8 cm away. What does that tell you about how
   magnetic field strength changes with distance?
4. A compass sits on a table and a magnet is slid under the
   table, below the compass, without touching it. The needle
   swings. What does the swinging prove about where the
   magnet's force exists?
5. A compass needle points roughly north when it is far from any
   magnet. Explain why, using the fact that Earth itself is a
   magnet.
6. Two magnets attract, north to south. You flip one magnet
   around so its south pole now faces the other's south pole.
   What happens, and why?
7. Iron filings in a sealed clear bag are sprinkled over a bar
   magnet. What pattern should the filings form? Where are the
   lines closest together, and what does that mean?
8. **Error analysis.** A learner writes: "The magnetic field only
   exists at the two poles of the magnet." Correct the sentence
   and name one observation that proves the field exists in the
   space around the magnet.

## Set D — Electromagnets (Lesson 04)

Fictional practice data (averages of three trials each; same
battery, same iron nail core):

| Coil turns | Paper clips lifted (average) |
|---|---|
| 10 | 4 |
| 20 | 8 |
| 30 | 12 |

Fictional practice data (20 turns, iron nail core):

| Batteries | Paper clips lifted (average) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 5 |
| 2 | 10 |

Fictional practice data (20 turns, 1 battery):

| Core | Paper clips lifted (average) |
|---|---|
| Air (no core) | 1 |
| Iron nail | 6 |

1. From the first table, how many clips does the 20-turn coil
   lift on average?
2. Describe the pattern in the first table in one sentence.
   Predict the lift for 40 turns, and state the assumption
   behind the prediction.
3. From the second table, what does adding a second battery do
   to the lift? What does that tell you about the link between
   current and strength?
4. From the third table, which core is stronger, and by about how
   many clips? What does that tell you about the core's job?
5. A learner tests "more turns = stronger" by rewinding the coil
   with more turns *and* adding a second battery at the same
   time. The lift rises from 5 clips to 14. Can the learner say
   the extra turns caused the change? Explain the fair-test
   problem.
6. Why should you disconnect the battery between tests? Give
   two reasons — one about the battery, one about the fairness
   of the test.
7. Name two ways an electromagnet differs from a bar magnet.
8. **Error analysis.** A learner writes: "More wire always means
   a stronger electromagnet, even if you keep adding wire
   forever." Correct the sentence: what limits the pattern, and
   over what range does the proportional rule hold?

## Set E — Circuits: series and parallel (Lesson 05)

1. One bulb on one battery shines brightly. A second identical
   bulb is added in series. Both bulbs glow dimly. Explain
   using the shared-push idea.
2. The same two bulbs are wired in parallel instead. Both shine
   brightly. Explain using the full-push idea.
3. In a series circuit, one bulb burns out and both go dark. In
   a parallel circuit, one bulb burns out and the other stays
   lit. Explain both results with the path idea.
4. Describe (in words or a sketch) a series circuit with one
   battery, one switch, and two bulbs. Mark the single path the
   current follows.
5. True or false: in a series loop, the current is the same
   through both bulbs. Explain your answer.
6. Two batteries are wired in series with one bulb (adult
   supervised, battery voltages only). Predict whether the bulb
   is brighter or dimmer than with one battery, and explain.
7. A learner says, "The first bulb in a series circuit uses up
   the current, so the second bulb gets less current." Correct
   the sentence: what is the same through both bulbs, and what
   is actually shared?
8. The lights in a house are wired in parallel. Give two reasons
   this is the right choice, using the path and push rules.

## Set F — Troubleshooting and retrieval (Lesson 06 + Units 01–03)

1. A series circuit is dead. The switch is closed and the
   battery is fresh. You swap in a known-good bulb and the
   circuit lights. What was the fault, and what did the swap
   prove?
2. In a parallel circuit, bulb A is dark and bulb B is lit. Where
   must the fault be — and where can it not be? Explain.
3. The troubleshooting routine checks, in order: switch, battery,
   wires, bulb, whole loop. Why is the switch checked first?
4. Trace the energy in a working bulb circuit: name each energy
   form in order, from the battery to the light and heat.
5. Retrieval (Unit 03): a 4 kg object moves at 2 m/s. What is
   its kinetic energy? Show the calculation with units.
   ($KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$)
6. Retrieval (Unit 02): a 2 kg cart is pushed with a force of
   6 N. What is its acceleration? Show the calculation with
   units. ($F = ma$)
7. Retrieval (Unit 04): a wave has frequency 5 Hz and wavelength
   2 m. What is its speed? Show the calculation with units.
   ($v = f\lambda$)
8. **Error analysis.** A learner swaps the battery *and* the bulb
   at the same time; the circuit lights. The learner says, "The
   battery was dead." What is wrong with that conclusion, and
   what should the learner have done instead?
