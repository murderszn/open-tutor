# Lesson 03 — Potential Energy: Height, Arrangement, and Stretch

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can calculate gravitational potential
energy from mass and height, and I can compare stored energy for
different positions and arrangements.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, a book, a rubber
band, a spring if available · **Teacher key:**
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult holds a book at knee height, then at shoulder height:
"From which height would the book hit the floor harder — and what
did I have to give it to get it up there?" (Work, Lesson 01 — the
book now stores that work as potential energy.)

## Explanation

**Potential energy** is stored energy — energy an object has because
of where it is or how it is arranged, ready to become motion or
another form. The clearest kind is **gravitational potential
energy**: lifting an object against gravity stores the work you did
as potential energy, and letting go converts it back to kinetic
energy as it falls.

For an object of mass $m$ (kg) at height $h$ (m) above a chosen
reference level, with $g = 9.8$ m/s²:

$$PE = mgh$$

in joules. Two things to get right:

- **Height is measured from a reference you state.** "A book 1 m
  above the floor" and "the same book 0 m above the table it sits
  on" are both correct descriptions with different reference
  levels. The *change* in potential energy when the book moves
  between two heights does not depend on which reference you
  picked — only differences matter, which is why the adult always
  asks "height above what?"
- **Mass and height both matter, proportionally.** Double the mass
  at the same height and the stored energy doubles. Double the
  height at the same mass and it doubles too. That is why a light
  object held very high can store as much as a heavy object held
  low — the product $m \times h$ decides.

A second kind needs no formula at grade 8: **elastic potential
energy**. Stretch a rubber band or compress a spring and you store
energy in the stretched arrangement; let go and it snaps back,
converting that stored energy to motion. More stretch means more
stored energy — the relationship is qualitative here (the exact
formula waits for later grades), but the pattern — arrangement
changes stored energy — is the same idea as height, and it is the
whole point of MS-PS3-2: change the arrangement of interacting
objects and you change the energy stored in the system.

**Vocabulary:** *potential energy* (stored energy from position or
arrangement), *gravitational potential energy* ($PE = mgh$),
*reference level* (the height counted as zero, stated before any
calculation), *elastic potential energy* (energy stored by
stretching or compressing an elastic object, described
qualitatively here).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A 2 kg ball held 5 m above the floor. $PE = 2 \times
9.8 \times 5 = 98$ J (relative to the floor). Lift it and you did
98 J of work; the ball stores 98 J.

**Example 2.** Double the height: the same 2 kg ball at 10 m.
$PE = 2 \times 9.8 \times 10 = 196$ J — double the height, double
the stored energy.

**Example 3.** A different combination with the same product: a
5 kg object at 2 m. $PE = 5 \times 9.8 \times 2 = 98$ J — exactly
Example 1's value. Same stored energy from a heavier object held
lower. When two setups give the same $mgh$, they store the same
energy, however different they look.

## Guided practice

1. A 2 kg ball is held 5 m above the floor. Gravitational potential
   energy (floor as reference)?
2. The same ball is raised to 10 m. New potential energy? What
   changed, and by what factor?
3. A 5 kg object sits 2 m above the floor. Potential energy? How
   does it compare with question 1, and why?

## Independent practice

1. A 4 kg object is held 3 m above the floor. Potential energy?
2. Which stores more gravitational potential energy above the
   floor: a 1 kg object at 10 m, or a 3 kg object at 2 m? Show both
   calculations.
3. A book sits on a shelf 1.5 m above the floor. (a) Its potential
   energy relative to the floor is positive. (b) What is its
   potential energy relative to the shelf itself? (c) If the book
   falls from the shelf to the floor, does the *change* in its
   potential energy depend on which reference you chose? Explain.
4. A rubber band is stretched a little, then stretched a lot. Which
   stretched state stores more elastic potential energy, and what
   happens to that energy when it is released?
5. **Error analysis:** A learner calculated the potential energy of
   a 2 kg ball at 5 m as $2 \times 9.8 \times 500 = 9{,}800$ J.
   What unit mistake did they make, and what is the correct value?

## Exit check

Name two different ways to increase an object's gravitational
potential energy, and one way to increase a rubber band's elastic
potential energy.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language support:** the setup card "mass × 9.8 × height
  above ___" with the reference blank filled in before any numbers.
- **Alternate response mode:** order three pictured setups (light
  object high, heavy object low, heavy object high) by stored energy
  without calculating, then check one with the formula.
- **Extension:** A roller coaster's first hill is 30 m high. A
  500 kg car at the top stores $500 \times 9.8 \times 30 = 147{,}000$
  J relative to the bottom. If it rolls down with no friction, how
  much kinetic energy does it have at the bottom? (147,000 J —
  Lesson 04 makes this conversion the whole story.)

## Teacher note

The reference-level idea confuses learners who expect one "true"
potential energy. Stress the sentence: "Potential energy is always
relative to a stated zero." The falling-book question (independent
3) is the check: learners who answer "the change is the same
either way" have it; learners who insist one reference is "right"
need the shelf/floor comparison acted out with the real book.
