# Lesson 01 — Work: Force Times Distance

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can calculate the work a force does when
it moves something, and I can spot the cases where physics says the
work is zero even though the job felt hard.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, a book, a rubber band
· **Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-01)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult holds a book out at arm's length, perfectly still: "Am I
doing work?" Accept all answers, then ask: "Is the book moving?"
(No.) Hold that thought — physics work needs motion, not just effort.

## Explanation

In everyday speech, "work" means any hard job. In physics it means
something narrower: **work is energy transferred by a force acting
through a distance**. When a force pushes or pulls an object and the
object moves in the direction of that force, the force does work on
the object, and the object's energy changes by that amount.

The rule, for a steady force in the direction of motion:

$$W = F \times d$$

- $W$ = work, measured in **joules (J)**
- $F$ = force, in newtons (N)
- $d$ = distance moved in the direction of the force, in meters (m)
- One joule is one newton-meter: $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N·m}$.

Two conditions matter, and both cause the classic mistakes:

1. **Only the distance in the force's direction counts.** Push a box
   5 m across the floor with a 40 N horizontal push, and the work is
   $40 \times 5 = 200$ J. Push straight down on a box while it slides
   sideways and that downward push does no work on the sideways
   motion — its direction does not match the motion.
2. **No motion, no work — in physics.** Hold a heavy box still for a
   minute and your arms ache, but the box gained no energy: $d = 0$,
   so $W = F \times 0 = 0$ J. Your muscles burned energy internally
   (that is biology, not the box's energy account), but the force on
   the box transferred nothing to it.

Carrying a box across a level room is the famous in-between case:
you lift it (force up, motion up — work done while lifting), then
you walk with it held steady (force up, motion sideways — the
carrying force does zero work on the box's motion). The box's energy
does not change while you carry it levelly, which is exactly why
physics says the carrying itself does no work.

**Vocabulary:** *work* (energy transferred by a force through a
distance, $W = Fd$), *joule* (unit of work and energy, $1\ \text{J} =
1\ \text{N·m}$), *direction of motion* (the direction the object
actually moves, which decides whether a force counts).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** You push a crate with a steady 50 N force and it slides
4 m in the direction of the push. Work: $W = 50 \times 4 = 200$ J.
The push transferred 200 J to the crate (which mostly becomes
thermal energy through friction — Lesson 04 follows that trail).

**Example 2.** A learner lifts a 20 N backpack straight up 1.5 m onto
a shelf. Work against gravity: $W = 20 \times 1.5 = 30$ J. The
backpack now stores 30 J more gravitational potential energy
(Lesson 03) — work in, stored energy out. Same formula, same joule.

**Example 3.** A strong learner pushes a wall with 300 N. The wall
does not move. $W = 300 \times 0 = 0$ J. Big force, zero distance,
zero work. Feeling tired is not the test; motion in the force's
direction is.

## Guided practice

1. You push a toy cart with 60 N and it rolls 5 m. How much work?
   *(Hint: multiply force by distance in the push direction.)*
2. You hold a 100 N suitcase still for 10 seconds. How much physics
   work do you do on the suitcase, and why?
3. **Spot the misconception:** "I carried my backpack across the
   school, so I did a lot of work on it the whole way." What is
   wrong with this claim, in physics language? *(Hint: compare the
   force direction with the motion direction while walking.)*

## Independent practice

1. A gardener pushes a wheelbarrow with 25 N for 8 m. Work done?
2. A crane lifts a 150 N beam 0.4 m. Work done?
3. A learner pushes a stuck door with 200 N. The door does not budge.
   Work done? Explain in one sentence.
4. Worker A pushes with 40 N over 10 m; Worker B pushes with 80 N over
   4 m. Who does more work? Show both calculations.
5. **Application:** A mover slides a box 3 m across a floor with a
   steady 90 N push. (a) How much work does the mover do?
   (b) The box does not speed up — it slides at a steady speed.
   Where do you think that 270 J of energy ends up? (One sentence;
   Lesson 04 gives the full answer.)

## Exit check

In your own words, in two sentences: what does physics count as
work, and name one everyday situation where the physics work is
zero.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language support:** sentence frame — "The force is ___
  N, the distance is ___ m, so the work is ___ J."
- **Alternate response mode:** act out each case with a toy cart and
  narrate "force direction / motion direction / work or zero" instead
  of writing, if writing is the barrier.
- **Extension:** A force of 30 N pushes at an angle so that only
  20 N points along the motion over 5 m. What work is done?
  (Answer: $20 \times 5 = 100$ J — only the along-motion part counts.
  This previews high-school vector work; it is not required.)

## Teacher note

The holding/carrying cases are where learners argue hardest, because
their bodies genuinely tire. Do not dismiss that feeling: name it
(muscles use chemical energy internally to stay contracted) and then
return to the test that decides the box's account — did the force
move the object in its own direction? The answer key separates
"work on the object" from "effort felt" throughout this unit.
