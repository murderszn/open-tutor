# Lesson 06 — Conservation: The Total Never Changes

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can build an energy account for a
system and argue, with evidence, that its total stays the same even
when the moving part shrinks.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, the energy accounts
from Lessons 04–05 · **Teacher key:**
[answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

## Readiness check (5 min)

"Yesterday's falling ball: 98 J at the top, 98 J at the bottom,
49 + 49 in the middle. Coincidence?" The learner should smell a
rule. This lesson names it and tests it against the hardest case:
friction.

## Explanation

**Conservation of energy** says: in a **closed system** — one with
no energy entering or leaving across its boundary — the total
energy never changes. Energy transforms (potential to kinetic),
transfers (car to spring), and spreads (mechanical to thermal), but
the total at the end equals the total at the start. The slogan
version is "energy cannot be created or destroyed"; the working
version is the account: **add up every form before, add up every
form after, and the two totals must match**.

That working version also states MS-PS3-5 precisely: **when the
kinetic energy of an object changes, energy is transferred to or
from the object**. A car that slows down did not lose kinetic
energy into nothing — it transferred that energy to the brakes,
tires, road, and air as thermal energy (and a little sound). A car
that speeds up gained kinetic energy because something transferred
energy to it — the engine burning fuel (chemical to thermal to
mechanical) or gravity pulling it downhill (potential to kinetic).
Every kinetic-energy change has a sender and a receiver. If your
account cannot name both, it is incomplete, not mysterious.

Three applications that test whether the learner really believes it:

1. **The bouncing ball.** Each bounce peaks lower. The mechanical
   account shrinks — but the full account does not: the missing
   mechanical energy is thermal energy in the ball and floor plus
   sound in the air. Open the system boundary wide enough (ball +
   floor + air) and the total holds.
2. **The roller coaster.** Without a motor, a coaster can never
   climb higher than its first hill. Its starting potential energy
   is its whole budget; friction only spends part of it as thermal
   energy, leaving less for later heights, never more.
3. **The "free-energy" machine.** Any device claimed to output more
   energy than it takes in fails the account check. Either a hidden
   input was missed (a battery, a raised weight, a wound spring)
   or a measurement is wrong. Conservation is the audit tool, not
   just a fact to recite.

Note what conservation does *not* say: it does not say useful
energy stays useful. Thermal energy spread thinly through brakes
and air cannot easily be gathered back to push the car. Total energy
is conserved; *useful* energy is not. That distinction is why cars
need fuel even though no energy is ever destroyed.

**Vocabulary:** *conservation of energy* (total energy in a closed
system stays constant), *closed system* (no energy crosses the
boundary), *useful energy* (energy in a form that can readily do
the job at hand, as opposed to spread-out thermal energy).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A closed-system account, ideal (no friction): a
skater starts at rest at the top of a ramp with PE 200 J, KE 0 J
(total 200 J). Midway: PE 120 J, KE 80 J (total 200 J). At the
bottom: PE 0 J, KE 200 J (total 200 J). Three rows, one total.

**Example 2.** The same skater, with friction: at the bottom, KE
170 J and thermal energy 30 J (total 200 J). The mechanical part
shrank; the total did not. A learner who reports only "KE 170 J"
and calls 30 J "lost" has drawn the boundary too small — include
the track and the thermal column, and the account balances.

**Example 3.** A car brakes from speed to rest. Its kinetic energy
was 500 J (model number, not a measurement). After braking: KE
0 J, thermal energy in brakes/tires/road/air 500 J, total 500 J.
The braking did not destroy 500 J; it moved 500 J into a form
that cannot push the car anymore. That is why the brakes get hot
and why the car cannot brake itself back up to speed for free.

## Guided practice

1. A skater's account: start PE 200 J, KE 0 J. Midway: PE 120 J.
   What is KE midway (ideal, no friction)? What is the total at
   both moments?
2. With friction, the same skater reaches the bottom with KE 170 J.
   How much thermal energy was produced, and what is the total?
3. **Spot the misconception:** "My phone battery's energy is used
   up, so energy is destroyed." What is wrong? Name where the
   battery's energy went. *(Hint: the chemicals changed; the
   energy became light, sound, heat, and signals.)*

## Independent practice

1. A skate park with no friction: a skater's total bar stays the
   same height through the whole run while the PE and KE bars swap
   sizes. Explain in two sentences what that shows.
2. The same park with friction: the skater's mechanical energy
   drops each lap. Does conservation fail? Explain, naming the
   growing column.
3. A roller coaster starts at the top of a 30 m first hill with no
   motor. Can it later climb a 35 m hill on its own? Why not, in
   energy-account language?
4. **Account problem:** Before: KE 40 J, PE 60 J, thermal 10 J.
   After: KE 90 J, thermal 20 J. What must PE be after, if the
   system is closed? Show the addition.
5. **Error analysis:** A learner's account reads: before total
   150 J, after total 170 J. Something is wrong. Name two possible
   causes (one missed input, one arithmetic) and what to re-check
   first.

## Exit check

State conservation of energy in your own words (one sentence) and
give one everyday example where energy seems to disappear but the
account shows it did not.

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language support:** the account template — "Before:
  ___ + ___ = ___. After: ___ + ___ = ___. Totals match? ___" —
  used for every account problem.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the account with paper strips
  (total strip cut and re-divided among forms) instead of numbers.
- **Extension:** A hydroelectric dam: water behind the dam stores
  gravitational potential energy; falling water gains kinetic
  energy; turbines transform it to electrical energy. Write the
  three-step account and name the thermal losses at each step
  (friction in water pipes, turbine bearings, wires).

## Teacher note

This is the unit's thesis lesson; do not rush the "useful vs.
total" distinction. Learners who can say "total conserved, useful
shrinks" have the idea that makes every later energy topic
(thermal energy in Unit 08, chemical energy in Unit 07) intelligible.
Learners who only recite "energy cannot be created or destroyed"
will still write "lost" on the next friction problem — send them
back to Lesson 04's "lost to where?" question.
