# Lesson 02 — Kinetic Energy: Mass and the Square of Speed

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can calculate kinetic energy from mass
and speed, and I can explain why doubling speed matters far more
than doubling mass.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, graph paper,
[`ke-graphs.svg`](../assets/ke-graphs.svg) (printed or on screen)
· **Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

## Readiness check (5 min)

"Two identical balls roll toward you — one slowly, one fast. Which
would you rather catch, and why?" Then: "Now a bowling ball rolls
slowly and a tennis ball rolls fast. Does your answer change?" The
point: both mass and speed matter, but not in the same way.

## Explanation

**Kinetic energy** is the energy of motion. Anything moving has it;
anything still has none (kinetic energy is zero at rest, whatever
its mass). For an object of mass $m$ (kg) moving at speed $v$ (m/s):

$$KE = \tfrac12 m v^2$$

measured in joules. Two patterns fall out of that formula, and the
unit keeps them separate because they are easy to confuse:

- **Mass pattern (speed held fixed):** kinetic energy grows in
  direct proportion to mass. Double the mass at the same speed and
  the kinetic energy doubles. Triple it and the kinetic energy
  triples. On a graph of kinetic energy against mass, this is a
  straight line through the origin.
- **Speed pattern (mass held fixed):** kinetic energy grows with the
  **square** of speed. Double the speed and the kinetic energy
  becomes **four** times as large ($2^2 = 4$). Triple the speed and
  it becomes **nine** times as large ($3^2 = 9$). On a graph of
  kinetic energy against speed, this is a curve that gets steeper
  as speed grows.

That is why a car at highway speed carries far more energy to
dissipate in braking than the same car at half that speed — not
twice as much, four times as much. Brakes, crumple zones, and speed
limits are all, underneath, about this square.

The deterministic diagram [`ke-graphs.svg`](../assets/ke-graphs.svg)
shows both patterns from the same practice dataset used below:
kinetic energy against mass at 4 m/s (a straight line: 8, 16, 24,
32 J for 1–4 kg) and kinetic energy against speed for a 2 kg object
(a rising curve: 1, 4, 9, 16 J at 1–4 m/s). These are computed
practice values, not measurements — the adult says so aloud.

**Vocabulary:** *kinetic energy* (energy of motion, $KE = \tfrac12
mv^2$), *proportional* (doubling one quantity doubles the other),
*square* (a number multiplied by itself; doubling speed quadruples
the square).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A 4 kg cart moves at 3 m/s. $KE = \tfrac12 \times 4
\times 3^2 = 2 \times 9 = 18$ J. Note the order: square the speed
first, then multiply.

**Example 2.** Double the mass, same speed: an 8 kg cart at 3 m/s.
$KE = \tfrac12 \times 8 \times 9 = 36$ J — exactly double Example 1.
Mass doubled, kinetic energy doubled. Straight-line pattern.

**Example 3.** Double the speed, same mass: the 4 kg cart at 6 m/s.
$KE = \tfrac12 \times 4 \times 36 = 72$ J — four times Example 1's
18 J. Speed doubled, kinetic energy quadrupled. Curve pattern.
Forgetting to square is the most common arithmetic slip in this
lesson; the key checks every setup for it.

## Guided practice

1. A 2 kg ball moves at 5 m/s. What is its kinetic energy?
   *(Hint: square 5 first.)*
2. A 6 kg ball moves at 2 m/s. What is its kinetic energy? Compare
   it with question 1 — which is larger, and why does that surprise
   some learners?
3. **Spot the misconception:** "Twice the speed means twice the
   kinetic energy." Show with numbers why this is wrong.
   *(Hint: use one mass at 2 m/s and at 4 m/s.)*

## Independent practice

1. A 3 kg cart moves at 4 m/s. Kinetic energy?
2. A 5 kg cart moves at 2 m/s. Kinetic energy?
   before and after? By what factor did it change?
4. **Graph reading (fictional practice dataset, not measurements):**
   At a fixed speed of 4 m/s, masses of 1, 2, 3, and 4 kg have kinetic
   energies of 8, 16, 24, and 32 J. (a) What is the kinetic energy
   for a 3 kg object at 4 m/s? (b) Is the mass graph a straight line
   or a curve? (c) For a 2 kg object at speeds of 1, 2, 3, and 4 m/s,
   the kinetic energies are 1, 4, 9, and 16 J — straight line or
   curve, and what does the shape tell you?
5. **Error analysis:** A learner calculated the kinetic energy of a
   2 kg object at 4 m/s as 32 J. What did they forget, and what is
   the correct value?

## Exit check

One sentence each: what happens to kinetic energy when mass doubles
(speed fixed)? When speed doubles (mass fixed)?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language support:** a two-column setup card — "Step 1:
  square the speed. Step 2: halve the mass. Step 3: multiply." —
  kept beside every calculation until the learner drops it.
- **Alternate response mode:** describe each graph in words ("as
  mass goes up by equal steps, kinetic energy goes up by equal
  steps") instead of plotting, with the data table as the
  alternative to the graph.
- **Extension:** A 1,000 kg car at 20 m/s has $KE = \tfrac12 \times
  1000 \times 400 = 200{,}000$ J. At 40 m/s it has 800,000 J. How
  many times more braking energy must the brakes handle? (Four
  times — and the brakes convert it to thermal energy, Lesson 04.)

## Teacher note

Learners who compute $KE = mv^2$ (no half) get exactly double the
right answer every time — a clean diagnostic. If a learner's answers
are consistently double, do not re-teach the whole formula; point at
the missing $\tfrac12$ and have them redo two problems. The
mass-vs-speed asymmetry is the conceptual prize of this lesson;
protect time for the graph discussion over extra drill.
