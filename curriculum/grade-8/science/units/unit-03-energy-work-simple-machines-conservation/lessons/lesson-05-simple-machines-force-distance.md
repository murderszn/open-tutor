# Lesson 05 — Simple Machines Trade Force for Distance

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can explain how a lever, a pulley, and
a ramp make a job easier, and I can prove with numbers that they
never create energy.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, a ruler, string, a
board · **Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

## Readiness check (5 min)

"Would you rather lift a heavy box straight up 1 m, or push it up a
long gentle ramp to the same height? Which needs the smaller force?
Which takes the longer path?" Both intuitions are right — and the
trade between them is the whole lesson.

## Explanation

A **simple machine** is a device that changes the size or direction
of a force. The three this unit requires are:

- **Lever:** a rigid bar that turns on a **fulcrum**. Push down on
  the long end and the short end lifts a heavy **load**. The effort
  arm (fulcrum to effort) and load arm (fulcrum to load) decide the
  trade: a longer effort arm means a smaller effort force.
- **Pulley:** a grooved wheel with a rope. One fixed pulley only
  changes direction (pull down to lift up). A movable pulley, or
  several rope segments sharing the load, reduces the force: with
  $n$ rope segments supporting the load, the ideal effort is about
  load ÷ $n$ — but you must pull $n$ times as much rope.
- **Inclined plane:** a ramp. Raising a load up a long ramp to a
  height takes a smaller force than lifting it straight up, spread
  over the ramp's longer distance.

Two numbers describe any machine:

- **Mechanical advantage (MA)** = output force ÷ input (effort)
  force. MA above 1 means the machine multiplies your force.
- **Efficiency** = (useful work out ÷ work in) × 100%. In an ideal,
  frictionless machine, work out equals work in and efficiency is
  100%. In every real machine, friction steals some input work as
  thermal energy, so work out is smaller and efficiency is below
  100%.

The deep rule, and the reason machines do not violate conservation:
**a machine trades force for distance, never force for nothing.**
Halve the force and you must double the distance (or the rope
pulled, or the ramp length). Work — force times distance — stays
the same in the ideal case and grows slightly in the real case
because friction adds its own work to the bill. A machine that
multiplied force without increasing distance would create energy
from nothing, and no such machine exists.

The other three classical machines appear only as extension here:
**wheel-and-axle** (a big wheel turning a small axle multiplies
force like a lever wrapped in a circle), **wedge** (two inclined
planes that split things apart), and **screw** (an inclined plane
wrapped around a shaft). Learners name them; they do not calculate
with them.

**Vocabulary:** *simple machine* (device changing a force's size or
direction), *lever*, *fulcrum*, *effort*, *load*, *pulley*,
*inclined plane*, *mechanical advantage* (output force ÷ input
force), *efficiency* (useful work out ÷ work in, as a percentage).

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — Lever.** A load of 300 N sits 0.5 m from the fulcrum;
you push 1.5 m from the fulcrum. In the ideal case, effort × effort
arm = load × load arm, so effort = $300 \times 0.5 \div 1.5 = 100$
N. MA = $300 \div 100 = 3$. If the load rises 0.2 m, the effort
end moves $0.2 \times 3 = 0.6$ m. Work in = $100 \times 0.6 = 60$ J;
work out = $300 \times 0.2 = 60$ J. Smaller force, longer distance,
same work.

**Example 2 — Pulley.** A 200 N load hangs from a pulley system with
2 rope segments supporting it. Ideal effort = $200 \div 2 = 100$ N.
To lift the load 1 m you pull 2 m of rope. Work in = $100 \times 2 =
200$ J; useful work out = $200 \times 1 = 200$ J. Same trade, in
rope.

**Example 3 — Inclined plane, real (with friction).** A 240 N load
must rise 1.2 m. Useful work = $240 \times 1.2 = 288$ J. The ramp is
6 m long. Ideal effort (no friction) = $288 \div 6 = 48$ N. With
friction the measured effort is 60 N, so work in = $60 \times 6 =
360$ J and efficiency = $288 \div 360 = 0.80 = 80\%$. Actual MA =
$240 \div 60 = 4$; ideal MA = $6 \div 1.2 = 5$. Friction explains
the whole gap between 5 and 4, and between 360 J and 288 J — 72 J
went to thermal energy.

## Guided practice

1. A lever lifts a 300 N load. The load arm is 0.5 m and the effort
   arm is 1.5 m. Ideal effort force? *(Hint: effort × 1.5 = 300 ×
   0.5.)*
2. A pulley system with 2 supporting ropes lifts a 200 N load.
   Ideal effort? How much rope must you pull to lift the load 1 m?
3. A ramp 6 m long rises 1.2 m, carrying a 240 N load. (a) Ideal
   effort with no friction? (b) If the real effort is 60 N, what
   is the efficiency?

## Independent practice

1. A lever's effort is 50 N and its load is 200 N. Mechanical
   advantage?
2. A pulley system has 4 rope segments supporting a 400 N load.
   Ideal effort?
3. A ramp job: work in is 360 J and useful work out is 288 J.
   Efficiency? How many joules went to friction (as thermal
   energy)?
4. **Application:** A piano must be loaded onto a truck bed 1.2 m
   high. Compare lifting it straight up with pushing it up a 6 m
   ramp: which needs less force, which needs more distance, and is
   the useful work the same? (Three short answers.)
5. **Error analysis:** A learner says, "This lever multiplies force
   by 3, so it creates energy — 3 times the energy comes out."
   What is wrong? Use the distance trade to explain.

## Exit check

In one sentence: what does a mechanical advantage above 1 buy you,
and what does it cost you?

## Supports and extension

- **Reading/language support:** a three-row machine card — lever:
  "long effort arm, small effort"; pulley: "count the ropes";
  ramp: "longer ramp, smaller push" — used for every setup.
- **Alternate response mode:** demonstrate each machine with the
  kit and narrate force vs. distance instead of calculating, if
  arithmetic is the barrier; the adult records the learner's
  numbers.
- **Extension:** Identify the wheel-and-axle, wedge, and screw in
  three household objects (doorknob, knife, jar lid) and state, for
  each, what force it changes and what distance trade it makes.

## Teacher note

The persistent misconception is "machines make work disappear."
Keep returning to the work calculation: every time a learner says a
machine "saves work," ask them to compute work in and work out. The
numbers settle it — work out is equal (ideal) or smaller (real),
never larger. Do not let MA alone carry the lesson; MA without the
distance trade is exactly how the free-energy myth starts.
