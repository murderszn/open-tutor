# Answer Key — Unit 03: Energy, Work, Simple Machines, and Conservation

**Teacher-only file.** Do not show it to the learner. Every answer
below was solved independently from the question text; where a
question allows variation, the acceptable range is stated. Use
$g = 9.8$ m/s² throughout.

## Lesson 01

**Guided practice**

1. $W = 60 \times 5 = 300$ J.
2. 0 J. The suitcase does not move, so $d = 0$ and $W = F \times 0
   = 0$ J, however tired the holder feels.
3. The claim confuses everyday effort with physics work. While
   walking levelly, the carrying force points up and the motion is
   sideways, so the carrying force does zero work on the box (the
   lifting work happened when the backpack was first raised).

**Independent practice**

1. $25 \times 8 = 200$ J.
2. $150 \times 0.4 = 60$ J.
3. 0 J. No displacement means no work, whatever the force.
4. A: $40 \times 10 = 400$ J. B: $80 \times 4 = 320$ J. Worker A
   does more work — distance matters as much as force.
5. (a) $90 \times 3 = 270$ J. (b) At steady speed the box's kinetic
   energy does not grow, so the 270 J ends up mostly as thermal
   energy through friction (plus a little sound).

**Exit check (acceptable):** Physics work is energy transferred by
a force moving an object in the force's direction ($W = Fd$).
Zero-work example: holding a box still, pushing a wall that does
not move, or carrying a box levelly.

## Lesson 02

**Guided practice**

1. $KE = \tfrac12 \times 2 \times 25 = 25$ J.
2. $KE = \tfrac12 \times 6 \times 4 = 12$ J. The lighter, faster
   object has more kinetic energy here — speed is squared, so it
   outweighs the mass difference in this pair.
3. Wrong. Same mass at 2 m/s: $KE = \tfrac12 m \times 4 = 2m$;
   at 4 m/s: $KE = \tfrac12 m \times 16 = 8m$ — four times as much,
   not twice.

**Independent practice**

1. $KE = \tfrac12 \times 3 \times 16 = 24$ J.
2. $KE = \tfrac12 \times 5 \times 4 = 10$ J.
3. Before: $\tfrac12 \times 3 \times 4 = 6$ J. After: $\tfrac12
   \times 3 \times 16 = 24$ J. Factor: 4×.
4. (a) 24 J. (b) Straight line — equal mass steps give equal KE
   steps. (c) Curve — equal speed steps give growing KE steps
   (1, 4, 9, 16), showing KE grows with the square of speed.
5. They forgot the $\tfrac12$. Correct: $\tfrac12 \times 2 \times
   16 = 16$ J, not 32 J.

**Exit check (acceptable):** Mass doubled (speed fixed) → KE
doubles. Speed doubled (mass fixed) → KE quadruples.

## Lesson 03

**Guided practice**

1. $PE = 2 \times 9.8 \times 5 = 98$ J above the floor.
2. $PE = 2 \times 9.8 \times 10 = 196$ J — double the height,
   double the stored energy.
3. $PE = 5 \times 9.8 \times 2 = 98$ J — the same as question 1,
   because the product $m \times h$ is the same.

**Independent practice**

1. $PE = 4 \times 9.8 \times 3 = 117.6$ J.
2. 1 kg at 10 m: 98 J. 3 kg at 2 m: $3 \times 9.8 \times 2 = 58.8$
   J. The 1 kg object at 10 m stores more.
3. (a) Positive relative to the floor. (b) 0 J relative to the
   shelf itself. (c) No — the change is the same whatever
   reference is chosen; only the absolute numbers shift.
4. The far-stretched band stores more elastic potential energy.
   On release it becomes kinetic energy (motion) first.
5. Unit mistake: height was used in centimeters (500 cm) instead
   of meters (5 m). Correct: $2 \times 9.8 \times 5 = 98$ J.

**Exit check (acceptable):** Increase mass or height for
gravitational PE; stretch a rubber band farther for elastic PE.

## Lesson 04

**Guided practice**

1. Halfway (2.5 m up): PE = $2 \times 9.8 \times 2.5 = 49$ J,
   KE = 49 J, total 98 J.
2. At the highest point PE is maximum and KE is zero (momentarily
   still). Each swing peaks lower because air resistance and pivot
   friction transfer a little mechanical energy to thermal energy
   on every swing.
3. Wrong. The energy did not disappear; it transferred to thermal
   energy in the wheels, track, brakes, and air (and a little
   sound). The car stopped because its kinetic energy moved into
   those forms.

**Independent practice**

1. Acceptable: transfer — kinetic energy from the skateboarder to
   the ramp/air as it slows (or from the ramp's height to the
   skater at the start); transformation — gravitational potential
   energy transforms into kinetic energy on the way down.
2. Chemical energy (battery) → electrical energy (wires) → light
   energy + thermal energy (bulb). Thermal is the commonly
   forgotten form.
3. (a) 20 units. (b) Thermal energy and sound (in the ball, floor,
   and air). (c) Less mechanical energy remains after each bounce
   to convert back to height.
4. Release: PE maximum. Rolling down: PE → KE. On the flat: KE →
   thermal energy (friction in wheels/track/air) + sound, until
   the car stops with KE 0.
5. Top: PE maximum, KE zero. Bottom: PE minimum, KE maximum. Next
   top: PE slightly smaller than the first top; the difference
   became thermal energy (air/pivot friction) and sound.

**Exit check (acceptable):** Transfer example: a moving car gives
kinetic energy to a spring bumper. Transformation example: a
falling ball's PE becomes KE. A stopping car's energy becomes
thermal energy in brakes/tires/air.

## Lesson 05

**Guided practice**

1. Effort = $300 \times 0.5 \div 1.5 = 100$ N.
2. Ideal effort = $200 \div 2 = 100$ N; pull 2 m of rope to lift
   1 m.
3. (a) Ideal effort = $240 \times 1.2 \div 6 = 48$ N. (b) Work in
   = $60 \times 6 = 360$ J; useful work = $240 \times 1.2 = 288$
   J; efficiency = $288 \div 360 = 80\%$.

**Independent practice**

1. MA = $200 \div 50 = 4$.
2. Ideal effort = $400 \div 4 = 100$ N.
3. Efficiency = $288 \div 360 = 80\%$; $360 - 288 = 72$ J went to
   friction (thermal energy).
4. Ramp: less force, longer distance; useful work the same
   (240 N × height in both cases, ideally) — the ramp only
   changes how the work is delivered, and friction makes the
   real work in slightly larger.
5. Wrong. Force multiplied by 3 does not mean energy multiplied
   by 3: the effort moves 3 times as far as the load rises, so
   work in equals work out (ideally). The machine trades force
   for distance; it creates nothing.

**Exit check (acceptable):** MA above 1 buys a smaller force at
the cost of a longer effort distance.

## Lesson 06

**Guided practice**

1. Midway KE = 80 J (total 200 J − PE 120 J); total 200 J at both
   moments.
2. Thermal energy = $200 - 170 = 30$ J; total still 200 J.
3. Wrong. The battery's chemical energy transformed into light,
   sound, thermal energy, and signals; the battery's chemicals
   changed. Nothing was destroyed — the account moved forms.

**Independent practice**

1. Acceptable: the PE and KE bars swap sizes while the total bar
   stays constant, showing energy transforming without changing
   the total.
2. No. The mechanical energy drops because some of it becomes
   thermal energy each lap; the thermal column grows and the full
   total still holds.
3. No. The first hill's potential energy is the whole budget, and
   friction spends part of it as thermal energy, so less remains
   for any later height — never more than the start.
4. Total before = $40 + 60 + 10 = 110$ J. PE after = $110 - 90 -
   20 = 0$ J.
5. Acceptable causes: (i) a missed energy input (for example, a
   push, a raised weight, or a wound spring not counted before);
   (ii) an arithmetic error in one column. Re-check first: re-add
   both columns and re-list every input to the system.

**Exit check (acceptable):** Total energy in a closed system stays
constant; it only changes form or moves. Example: a bouncing ball
seems to lose energy, but the missing part is thermal energy and
sound in the ball, floor, and air.

## Practice sessions

**Session A — Work**

1. 300 J. 2. 150 J. 3. 0 J — no displacement. 4. (a) 0 J while
   walking levelly. (b) Muscles burn chemical energy internally
   to stay contracted; that energy does not go into the box.
5. 0 J. 6. A: $50 \times 6 = 300$ J. B: $100 \times 2 = 200$ J.
   Worker A does more.

**Session B — Kinetic-energy graphs**

1. 40 J (8 J per kilogram at 4 m/s; 5 × 8 = 40 J).
2. $\tfrac12 \times 2 \times 36 = 36$ J — compute from the formula,
   not by extending the table by addition.
3. 5 kg at 4 m/s: 40 J. 2 kg at 6 m/s: 36 J. The 5 kg object has
   more.
4. Mass graph: straight line (KE proportional to mass). Speed
   graph: curve (KE grows with speed squared).
5. No. The values 1, 4, 9, 16 are squares ($1^2, 2^2, 3^2, 4^2$);
   the growing differences show KE accelerating with speed, not
   adding evenly.
6. Forgot the $\tfrac12$. Correct: $\tfrac12 \times 4 \times 9 =
   18$ J.

**Session C — Potential energy and energy bars**

1. $3 \times 9.8 \times 4 = 117.6$ J.
2. $6 \times 9.8 \times 2 = 117.6$ J — equal to question 1.
3. Top: PE 98 J, KE 0 J, total 98 J. Halfway (2.5 m): PE 49 J,
   KE 49 J, total 98 J. Just before impact: PE 0 J, KE 98 J, total
   98 J.
4. No. 0 J relative to the table does not mean no PE at all: above
   the floor the book has $PE = m \times 9.8 \times 0.8$ J. PE is
   always relative to a stated reference.
5. The far-stretched band stores more; on release it becomes
   kinetic energy (motion) first.
6. (a) The 10 kg object. (b) 5× ($294$ J vs. $58.8$ J). (c) The
   10 kg object — its larger PE converts to larger KE.

**Session D — Transfer chains and accounts**

1. Acceptable chain: at release, PE maximum; falling, PE →
   KE; rising on the other side, KE → PE; each peak slightly
   lower as some energy becomes thermal (air/pivot friction).
2. Chemical → electrical → light + thermal. Thermal is the
   commonly forgotten form.
3. Thermal energy in brakes, tires, road, and air, plus a little
   sound. All 400 J is accounted for in those forms.
4. (a) 80% remains after each bounce ($80 \div 100$; $64 \div 80$).
   (b) Thermal energy and sound. (c) $64 \times 0.8 = 51.2$ units.
5. Peak heights (PE) shrink each run; the thermal column grows by
   the same amount each run; the total holds.
6. Acceptable: the pendulum's energy did not vanish — each swing
   transfers a little mechanical energy to thermal energy in the
   air and pivot and to sound. Widen the system boundary to
   pendulum + air + pivot and the total is conserved; only the
   useful, swinging part shrinks.

**Session E — Machines**

1. MA = $400 \div 100 = 4$. Effort distance (ideal) = $0.3 \times
   4 = 1.2$ m.
2. Ideal effort = $300 \div 3 = 100$ N. Rope pulled = $2 \times 3
   = 6$ m.
3. (a) Work in = $130 \times 5 = 650$ J. (b) Efficiency = $500
   \div 650 \approx 77\%$. (c) $650 - 500 = 150$ J to friction.
4. Useful work = $0.75 \times 200 = 150$ J.
5. (a) Useful work = $600 \times 1.5 = 900$ J in both cases.
   (b) Ramp work in = $120 \times 9 = 1{,}080$ J. (c) Efficiency
   = $900 \div 1{,}080 \approx 83\%$. (d) The ramp — lifting
   needs 600 N, far above the 150 N limit, while the ramp needs
   only 120 N (at the cost of a 9 m push).
6. Load rises 0.2 m, so with MA 4 the effort moves 0.8 m. Work in
   = $50 \times 0.8 = 40$ J; work out = $200 \times 0.2 = 40$ J.
   Equal (ideal), not 4× — MA multiplies force, not work.

## Project

**Ramp lab (Sessions 8–9):** No fixed numerical answers — the
learner's data are the answers. Acceptable analysis: (1) mean
travel distance rises with release height; (2) higher release →
more PE → more KE at the bottom → more work against friction
before stopping; (3) travel distance is indirect because friction,
release pushes, and wheel differences also affect it; (4) by the
stop, the energy is thermal (wheels/track/air) and sound;
(5) a rougher surface shortens travel because friction converts
KE to thermal energy faster. Mark down any report that invents
trials or reports a single trial as a mean.

**Machine lifter (Sessions 14–15, 18):** Score with the project
rubric. A strong report shows: work in ≥ useful work out (never
the reverse), MA computed from measured forces, efficiency below
100% explained by friction, and an improvement round with both
rounds' numbers. Any report claiming work out exceeded work in
must be returned for re-measurement, not accepted.
