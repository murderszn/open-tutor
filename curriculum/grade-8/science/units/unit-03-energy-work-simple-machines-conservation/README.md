# Unit 03 — Energy, Work, Simple Machines, and Conservation

**Grade:** 8 · **Subject:** Science (Physical Science pathway) · **Track weeks:** 11–14
**Backlog:** [issue #39](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/39), section U03
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Work is the bridge between forces and energy. In this unit the learner
stops asking only "what pushed it?" and starts keeping an energy ledger:
how much work a force does, how much kinetic energy a moving object
carries, how much potential energy a raised object or a stretched spring
stores, and where every joule goes when motion slows down. The unit
keeps the three questions separate on purpose — *how much work?*,
*how much energy, of which kind?*, and *where did it go?* — because
mixing them up is the most common grade-8 energy error. Simple machines
then become the payoff: a lever, a pulley, or a ramp does not create
energy, it trades a smaller force for a longer distance, and the ledger
proves it. The unit closes with the conservation argument itself: in a
closed system the total never changes, even when the useful, moving
part shrinks as thermal energy grows.

## Essential question

If energy is never created or destroyed, why does everything eventually
slow down and stop — and how can a machine make a hard job easier
without breaking that rule?

## Prerequisites

From Units 01–02 and the diagnostic weeks:

- SI units for length, mass, time, and force (newtons); reading labeled
  tables; recording data with units and repeating trials
- Speed as distance ÷ time; reading distance–time graphs (slope = speed)
- Forces as pushes and pulls; balanced vs. unbalanced forces;
  $F = ma$ and proportional reasoning ("twice the force, twice the
  acceleration"); friction as a force opposing sliding
- Arithmetic with decimals; solving a one-step formula for an unknown;
  computing a percentage
- Writing a one-sentence claim with one reason (claim–evidence–reasoning)
- Safe habits for adult-supervised household-scale investigations

New content here: work ($W = Fd$), the joule, kinetic energy
($KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$), gravitational potential energy ($PE = mgh$),
elastic potential energy (qualitative), energy transfer vs.
transformation, thermal energy from friction, simple machines
(lever, pulley, inclined plane; wheel-and-axle, wedge, and screw as
extension), mechanical advantage, efficiency, and conservation of
energy in a closed system. Nothing here assumes Unit 04's wave ideas
or Unit 06's atomic models.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. Calculate the work done by a force acting in the direction of motion
   ($W = Fd$, joules) and identify zero-work cases (no displacement,
   or force perpendicular to motion), explaining the physics meaning
   separately from the everyday meaning of "hard work."
2. Calculate kinetic energy from mass and speed ($KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$);
   construct and interpret graphical displays showing that kinetic
   energy grows in proportion to mass but with the square of speed.
3. Calculate gravitational potential energy ($PE = mgh$, $g = 9.8$
   m/s²) relative to a stated reference height, and compare stored
   energy for objects in different positions or arrangements,
   including stretched elastic objects (qualitatively).
4. Track energy through a system as transfers and transformations,
   naming where energy goes when motion slows — including thermal
   energy from friction and sound — and present an energy account
   whose parts add up to the starting total.
5. Explain how a lever, a pulley system, and an inclined plane trade
   force for distance: compute mechanical advantage and efficiency,
   and show with numbers that useful work out never exceeds work in.
6. Construct, use, and present an argument, with evidence, that when
   the kinetic energy of an object changes, energy is transferred to
   or from the object, and that total energy in a closed system is
   conserved.

## Materials

Notebook, pencils, index cards for vocabulary, graph paper or printed
grids, a stopwatch, a kitchen or postal scale (grams), masking tape,
a meter stick or tape measure, a toy car or low-friction cart, books
or blocks to raise a ramp, a smooth board or cookie sheet (1–1.5 m),
a spring or a set of rubber bands, string, a ruler, washers or coins
for added mass, and the simple-machine kit: a sturdy ruler or dowel
for a lever, a small pulley wheel or a smooth spool with string, and
a board for a ramp — the adult assembles the kit and sets the exact
material budget. Printed or on-screen copy of
[`assets/ramp-cart-spring-bumper.png`](assets/ramp-cart-spring-bumper.png)
and [`assets/ke-graphs.svg`](assets/ke-graphs.svg). Adult-supervised
data collection happens in a safe open area — no racing toward walls
at speed by people, no throwing objects; see the project's safety
box. Digital option: a spreadsheet app for data tables; the free
PhET "Energy Skate Park: Basics" simulation (resource pack) as an
observation alternative for the investigation.

## Accessibility and supports

All lessons include reading/language supports, an alternate response
mode, and extensions (see each lesson and the
[teacher guide](teacher-guide.md)). Energy-bar diagrams can be built
with stacked paper strips or blocks instead of drawn; graph tasks
have a data-table alternative; the ramp lab has a timer/reader role
and a full observation-only alternative via the PhET simulation.
The machine-lifter project offers a verbal-presentation alternative
to the written report, and all calculations may be done with a
calculator once the setup is written out by hand.
