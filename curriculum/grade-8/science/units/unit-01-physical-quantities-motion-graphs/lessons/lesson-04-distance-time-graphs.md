# Lesson 04 — From Data Tables to Distance–Time Graphs

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can turn position–time data into a labeled
distance–time graph and read coordinates back off a graph.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** graph paper, ruler, pencil, the
fictional practice dataset below · **Teacher key:** [answer
key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04)

## Readiness check (5 min)

The adult shows a table: time (s): 0, 5, 10, 15; distance (m): 0, 10, 20,
30. "If you walked at this pace, where would you be at 20 seconds?" (35 m
is wrong — the table only proves the pattern to 15 s; predicting beyond
is a guess. Plant the seed: graphs make patterns visible, not magic.)

## Explanation

A **distance–time graph** draws motion as a picture:

- **Horizontal axis (x):** time in seconds — the independent variable.
- **Vertical axis (y):** distance from the start in meters.
- Each (time, distance) pair is a point; connecting the points shows the
  story.

**How to build one:**

1. Label both axes with quantity **and unit** ("Time (s)",
   "Distance (m)").
2. Choose scales that use most of the paper: if times run 0–10 s and
   distances 0–20 m, let 1 grid square = 1 s and 2 m.
3. Plot each point carefully; mark it with a small dot or cross.
4. Connect points **in time order** with straight segments (or a smooth
   curve for real data — straight segments between measured points is the
   honest choice here).
5. Title it: what moved, and what is shown.

**Reading a graph reverses the steps:** find the time on the x-axis, go
up to the line, then across to the distance. Example: the point (6 s,
12 m) means "after 6 seconds, the mover was 12 meters from the start."

**Worked example — the full build.** Fictional practice data (a toy car
on a track; label all numbers as practice data, not your measurements):

| Time (s) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Distance (m) | 0 | 4 | 8 | 8 | 8 | 14 |

Plot (0,0), (2,4), (4,8), (6,8), (8,8), (10,14). Time axis 0–10 s,
distance axis 0–16 m. The line climbs, goes flat from 4 s to 8 s, then
climbs again. Reading: at 5 s the car is at 8 m (on the flat part); at 9 s
it's about 11 m (halfway up the last segment).

**Example 2 — reading only.** On a printed graph, a point sits at
t = 30 s, d = 60 m. The mover's position at 30 s was 60 m from the start.
If the adult asks "where at 45 s?" and the line ends at 40 s, the honest
answer is "the graph doesn't show that" — no guessing past the data.

**Vocabulary:** *distance–time graph*, *axis*, *scale*, *coordinate*
(a (time, distance) pair), *segment*.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Label blank axes for the worked-example data: quantities, units, and
   a sensible scale.
2. Plot the six points from the worked example on graph paper.
   *(Hint: count grid squares before marking each point.)*
3. From your graph, read the car's distance at t = 3 s and t = 7 s.
4. What does the flat segment from 4 s to 8 s mean in words?
5. **Error analysis:** Jo plots time on the vertical axis and distance on
   the horizontal axis. What breaks when someone tries to read "distance
   at 6 s" from Jo's graph?

## Independent practice

1. New practice dataset (a walker's position): time (s): 0, 10, 20, 30,
   40; distance (m): 0, 15, 30, 30, 50. Build the full graph with labels,
   scale, points, and title.
2. From your graph in (1): distance at 25 s? at 35 s? What happened
   between 20 s and 30 s?
3. Two graphs show the same trip, but one uses 1 square = 5 m and the
   other 1 square = 10 m. Do they tell different stories? Explain why
   scale changes looks but not facts.
4. A graph's line starts at (0, 5 m) instead of (0, 0). What does the 5 m
   tell you about how the measurement began?

## Application

Re-plot your three walking trials from Lesson 3 as *one* graph idea on
paper: you only measured total time for 10 m (one point per trial plus
the start). Sketch what each trial's line would look like and label which
trial was fastest. This is a sketch, not precise data — write "sketch"
on it.

## Exit check

Sketch and label a blank distance–time axes pair (quantities + units),
and in one sentence say what the point (10 s, 25 m) means.

## Supports

- **Reading/language:** a printed axis-labeling checklist; vocabulary
  cards for *axis*, *scale*, *coordinate*.
- **Alternate response mode:** points may be plotted on large-grid or
  raised-line paper; coordinates may be dictated ("at 4 seconds, 8
  meters") instead of drawn.
- **Extension:** collect real position–time data for a rolling ball at
  1-second intervals (adult calls time, learner marks positions with
  tape), then graph it — a rehearsal of Sessions 8–9.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** the line is the *path* the mover took ("it went
up a hill"). Stress: the line is distance *over time* — "up" on the
paper means "farther away," not "higher in the air." A flat line means
*stopped*, not "moving flat." Also: learners often start the line at the
origin even when the first measurement wasn't at time zero — check that
the first plotted point matches the first data row. Key: [answer
key](../answer-key.md#lesson-04).
