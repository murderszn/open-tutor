# Lesson 06 — Velocity–Time Sketches

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can sketch and read velocity–time graphs:
constant speed, faster vs. slower, stopped — and explain that the area
under the line equals the distance traveled.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** graph paper, notebook, the PhET
"Moving Man" simulation (resource pack) as an optional observation ·
**Teacher key:** [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06)

## Readiness check (5 min)

"Yesterday's graphs showed *distance* climbing over time. What if the
vertical axis showed *speed* instead — what would a steady walk look
like?" (Accept any sketch; the lesson builds the convention.)

## Explanation

A **velocity–time graph** (v–t graph) puts velocity on the vertical axis
and time on the horizontal axis. For straight-line motion in this unit,
"velocity" just means speed with the direction understood (away from the
start).

- **Steady speed** → a **horizontal line**. The height of the line is the
  speed: higher line = faster, lower line = slower, line on the axis
  (height zero) = **stopped**.
- This is the *opposite* visual habit from distance–time graphs: on a
  d–t graph, faster meant steeper; on a v–t graph, faster means
  **higher**.

**The area idea.** Take a steady 2 m/s walk for 10 s. The v–t graph is a
rectangle 2 units tall and 10 units wide. Its area:

$$\text{area} = \text{height} \times \text{width} = 2\ \tfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} \times 10\ \text{s} = 20\ \text{m}$$

The units multiply out: (m/s) × s = m. The area **is** the distance
traveled — 20 m, exactly what $d = v \times t$ gives. This is not a
coincidence; it is the same formula drawn as a picture.

**Worked example 1.** Sketch the v–t graph for: walk 3 m/s for 6 s,
then stop for 4 s. The line sits at height 3 from t = 0 to t = 6, then
drops to height 0 from t = 6 to t = 10. Distance = area of the first
rectangle: $3 \times 6 = 18\ \text{m}$. (The stop adds no area — no
motion, no distance.)

**Worked example 2.** Which is faster — a v–t line at height 5 m/s or one
at height 2 m/s? The 5 m/s line (higher = faster). Over the same 10 s,
whose area is bigger? The faster mover's: $5 \times 10 = 50\ \text{m}$
vs. $2 \times 10 = 20\ \text{m}$ — faster for the same time means
farther, and the graph shows it as a bigger rectangle.

**Vocabulary:** *velocity* (speed with understood direction here),
*v–t graph*, *area* ("space under the line" = distance).

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Sketch a v–t graph for 4 m/s steady for 5 s. What is the area, and
   what does it equal in words?
2. A v–t line sits at height 0 from 3 s to 7 s. What is the mover doing?
   *(Hint: height zero means…)*
3. Two v–t lines: Ana at 6 m/s, Ben at 3 m/s, both for 8 s. Whose
   rectangle is bigger, and by how many meters of area?
4. **Error analysis:** "On the v–t graph, the steeper line is the faster
   mover." What's wrong with applying the d–t habit here?

## Independent practice

1. Sketch v–t graphs for: (a) 2 m/s for 10 s; (b) 5 m/s for 4 s; (c)
   stopped for 6 s. Compute each area and state the distance.
2. A cyclist rides 4 m/s for 30 s, rests 10 s, rides 4 m/s for 20 s.
   Sketch the v–t graph (three segments) and compute total distance from
   the areas. Check with $d = v \times t$ per segment.
3. Explain in 2–3 sentences why the area under a v–t line has units of
   meters. (Hint: multiply the axis units.)
4. **Challenge:** a v–t graph shows a line that *rises* from 2 m/s to
   6 m/s over 5 s (the mover is speeding up — Unit 02's topic). Without
   computing exactly, is the distance more or less than $6 \times 5 =
   30\ \text{m}$? Explain using the area picture.

## Application

Convert the jogger's story from Lesson 5 into a v–t sketch: 2 m/s for
30 s, 0 m/s for 20 s, 2 m/s for 20 s. Compute the total area and confirm
it equals 100 m — the same total the d–t graph ended at. Write one
sentence about what stayed the same and what looked different between the
two graph types.

## Exit check

Sketch a v–t graph for a 3 m/s walk lasting 8 s, shade the area, and
write the distance the area represents.

## Supports

- **Reading/language:** the "height = how fast, area = how far" chant
  card; vocabulary cards for *velocity* and *area*.
- **Alternate response mode:** sketches may be described orally
  ("horizontal line at height 4 from 0 to 5 seconds") or built with the
  PhET "Moving Man" simulation as an observation alternative.
- **Extension:** in the PhET simulation, set a steady velocity, watch
  both the d–t and v–t graphs draw together, and write one paragraph on
  how the two pictures describe the same motion.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** carrying the d–t habit over ("steeper = faster"
on a v–t graph). Drill the contrast explicitly: d–t asks "how steep?";
v–t asks "how high?" Also: learners sometimes compute area as
height + width — catch it early with the unit check (m/s + s is
meaningless; m/s × s = m). The rising-line challenge previews
acceleration honestly labeled as Unit 02 content. Key: [answer
key](../answer-key.md#lesson-06).
