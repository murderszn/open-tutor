# Lesson 02 — Measuring Well: Instruments, Precision, Repeats

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can choose the right instrument for a
measurement, repeat trials to improve reliability, and record results in a
labeled table.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** ruler, meter stick, tape measure,
kitchen scale, stopwatch, notebook · **Teacher key:** [answer
key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

## Readiness check (5 min)

"Which tool would you use to measure: the width of your thumb? the mass of
an apple? how long you can hold your breath?" Name a tool for each — no
wrong answers yet.

## Explanation

Choosing the instrument is the first measurement decision:

| Measuring | Instrument | SI unit | Example precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Length | ruler / meter stick / tape measure | m (cm, mm) | a ruler reads to the nearest mm |
| Mass | balance / kitchen scale | kg (g) | a kitchen scale reads to the nearest gram |
| Time | stopwatch / digital timer | s | a phone stopwatch reads to 0.01 s |

**Precision** is the smallest difference an instrument can show. A meter
stick marked in centimeters cannot tell 12.1 cm from 12.2 cm — record
what the instrument shows, and read to the *marked* precision (estimate
between marks only when the adult agrees that's the lesson's goal).

Good measurers follow four habits:

1. **Right tool, right precision** — don't time a 100-meter dash with a
   wall clock.
2. **Zero check** — a ruler starts at 0; a scale should read 0 with
   nothing on it.
3. **Repeat trials** — measure the same thing 3 times. Similar results
   (12.1, 12.3, 12.2 cm) mean the measurement is **repeatable**; a wild
   one out (12.2, 12.1, 18.4 cm) signals a mistake in that trial, not a
   new fact.
4. **Record with units, every time** — "12.2" is not a measurement;
   "12.2 cm" is. A labeled table keeps trials organized:

| Trial | Length (cm) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 12.1 |
| 2 | 12.3 |
| 3 | 12.2 |

**Controlled variable:** when you measure *motion* later, everything that
could change the result but isn't being studied stays the same — same
course, same start signal, same person timing. Write the controlled
variables down before you start.

**Vocabulary:** *instrument*, *precision* (smallest readable difference),
*trial*, *repeatability* (similar results across trials), *controlled
variable* (kept the same on purpose).

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** Measure a pencil's length with a ruler marked in
centimeters. Trials: 17.5, 17.5, 17.6 cm. The results are repeatable —
they agree within the ruler's precision. Report: "about 17.5 cm."

**Example 2.** Time how long it takes to walk across the room. Trials:
6.2 s, 6.4 s, 11.8 s. The third trial disagrees — probably a slow start
or a late stop press. The right move is to re-run the third trial, not to
average the mistake in silently. (When a re-run isn't possible, the
teacher guide explains how to flag and exclude it — never silently.)

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. Which instrument and unit for: (a) mass of a backpack, (b) time for a
   toy car to roll 2 m, (c) thickness of a coin?
2. A tape measure is marked in centimeters. Which readings are honest:
   24 cm, 24.3 cm, 24.35 cm? Explain.
3. Trials of a book's width: 21.0, 21.2, 21.1 cm. Are they repeatable?
   What would you report?
4. Name two controlled variables for "time how long it takes to walk
   10 m."
5. Your scale reads 50 g with nothing on it. What do you do, and why?

## Independent practice

1. Pick an object at home. Measure it 3 times with a ruler, record a
   labeled table, and report your best value with a one-sentence
   justification.
2. A group times a runner: 12.4, 12.6, 12.5 s. Another group gets 12.5,
   18.9, 12.4 s. Which group's data is more repeatable, and what should
   the second group do about 18.9 s?
3. **Error analysis:** Lee records "the car took 4" in the data table.
   Name two things missing and explain why each matters.
4. Write a one-paragraph measurement plan for Session 8's motion lab:
   what will be measured, with which instrument, how many trials, and
   which variables stay controlled.

## Application

With the adult, set up a 10-meter course with tape markers. Walk it once
at a steady pace while the adult times you. Record the time in a labeled
table. This single trial is your rehearsal for the Sessions 8–9 lab —
what will you do differently when it counts?

## Exit check

List the four good-measurer habits in your own words, and explain in one
sentence why a measurement without a unit is incomplete.

## Supports

- **Reading/language:** a printed "measurement checklist" card the
  learner ticks off each session.
- **Alternate response mode:** tables may be dictated to the adult while
  the learner operates the instruments.
- **Extension:** measure the same length with a ruler (mm) and a tape
  (cm); explain which instrument gives the more precise result and
  whether more precise always means more correct.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** "the average of three trials is automatically
the truth." Teach *first* to look at spread: tight spread → trustworthy;
one outlier → re-run or flag. Averages come in Lesson 3. Also watch for
learners recording extra digits their instrument can't support (false
precision). Key: [answer key](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
