# Lesson 03 — Speed: Distance ÷ Time

**Goal (learner-facing):** I can calculate average speed from distance and
time, convert the inputs when needed, and tell constant speed from changing
speed in a data table.

**Time:** 50 minutes · **Materials:** stopwatch, tape measure, notebook,
calculator (allowed for division) · **Teacher key:** [answer
key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

## Readiness check (5 min)

Two friends walk the same 400-meter block. Ana takes 5 minutes; Ben takes
8 minutes. Who is faster — and how do you know? (Accept "Ana, less time
for the same distance." The formula comes next.)

## Explanation

**Average speed** is a *rate*: how much distance passes per unit of time.

$$v = \frac{d}{t}$$

where $v$ = average speed, $d$ = distance, $t$ = time. The unit tells the
story: **m/s** means "meters per second" — how many meters each second
carries you.

Worked step-by-step:

1. Write the numbers **with units**: $d = 100\ \text{m}$,
   $t = 25\ \text{s}$.
2. Divide: $100 \div 25 = 4$.
3. Attach the combined unit: **4 m/s**.

Rearrange the same relationship for the other two questions:

- **Distance:** $d = v \times t$ — a 3 m/s walk for 40 s covers
  $3 \times 40 = 120\ \text{m}$.
- **Time:** $t = d \div v$ — 800 m at 2 m/s takes
  $800 \div 2 = 400\ \text{s}$.

**Convert before you divide.** A cyclist rides 12 km in 30 minutes. Two
clean routes:

- Metric: $12\ \text{km} = 12{,}000\ \text{m}$; $30\ \text{min} =
  1800\ \text{s}$; $12{,}000 \div 1800 \approx 6.7\ \text{m/s}$.
- Mixed: $30\ \text{min} = 0.5\ \text{h}$; $12 \div 0.5 = 24\ \text{km/h}$.

Both are correct — 24 km/h and 6.7 m/s describe the *same* ride. Mixing
units inside one calculation (km ÷ seconds) is the classic error; convert
first.

**Constant vs. changing speed.** Average speed hides the details. A car
that covers 60 m in 10 s (6 m/s) might have cruised steadily — or crawled
then sprinted. A data table reveals it: equal distances in equal times →
**constant speed**; unequal → **changing speed**.

**Vocabulary:** *average speed*, *rate* (amount per unit time),
*constant speed*, *changing speed*.

## Worked examples

**Example 1.** A jogger runs 100 m in 25 s. Speed?
$v = d/t = 100\ \text{m} \div 25\ \text{s} = 4\ \text{m/s}$.

**Example 2.** A walker covers 600 m in 10 minutes. Speed in m/s?
Convert: $10\ \text{min} = 600\ \text{s}$. $v = 600 \div 600 = 1\ \text{m/s}$.

**Example 3.** Constant or changing? Times for each 20-m segment:
10 s, 10 s, 10 s, 10 s → equal times for equal distances → **constant
speed** (2 m/s each segment). Times: 8 s, 10 s, 12 s, 14 s → the mover
is slowing down → **changing speed**, even though the average over 80 m
is still about 1.8 m/s.

## Guided practice (with hints)

1. A swimmer covers 50 m in 40 s. Speed? *(Hint: write d and t with
   units first.)*
2. A bus travels 90 km in 1.5 h. Speed in km/h?
3. How far does a 5 m/s runner cover in 12 s? *(Hint: use $d = v \times t$.)*
4. How long does 240 m take at 3 m/s? *(Hint: use $t = d \div v$.)*
5. Constant or changing? 10-m split times: 2.1, 2.0, 2.1, 2.0 s.
   Explain in one sentence.

## Independent practice

1. (a) 200 m in 50 s → speed? (b) 1.5 km in 5 min → speed in m/s?
   (c) 72 km/h express as m/s. *(Hint: 1 km = 1000 m, 1 h = 3600 s.)*
2. A cyclist's 25-m split times: 5, 5, 9, 5 s. Average speed over 100 m?
   Constant or changing — what might explain the 9 s split?
3. **Error analysis:** Kim computes a 300 m walk in 5 minutes as
   $300 \div 5 = 60$ and reports "60 m/s." Find both errors (units and
   conversion) and fix them.
4. Two runners: Priya averages 5 m/s, Sam averages 4.5 m/s, over the same
   400 m. Who finishes first, and by how many seconds?

## Application

Time yourself walking the 10-meter rehearsal course from Lesson 2 three
times. Compute each trial's speed and the average. Is your speed closer
to constant or changing across trials? One paragraph, numbers included.

## Exit check

State the speed formula in words, compute the speed of a 150-m walk
completed in 60 s, and give the answer with the correct unit.

## Supports

- **Reading/language:** a formula triangle card ($d$ on top; $v$, $t$
  below — cover the unknown to see the operation).
- **Alternate response mode:** computations may be done on a calculator
  with the setup written by hand.
- **Extension:** derive that 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h and explain which unit is
  handier for (a) a sprinter, (b) a highway sign.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** "faster" confused with "farther" — a car that
drove farther isn't necessarily faster; only distance *per time* decides.
Also watch unit-mixing (km ÷ min reported as m/s). Insist on the
convert-first habit; it pays off in every later unit. Key: [answer
key](../answer-key.md#lesson-03).
