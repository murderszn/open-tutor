# Lesson 4 — The War of 1812 and the Monroe Doctrine: Claims vs. Power

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can rank the causes of the War of
1812 by relative influence, state what the Treaty of Ghent settled and did
not settle, and separate what the Monroe Doctrine claimed from what the
United States could enforce in 1823.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, the U02
timeline (ends 1783) extended through 1823.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) What does "impressment" mean, from the
vocabulary list? (2) If a country declares a policy it cannot enforce, is
the declaration meaningless? Keep your answers; 1823 will test the second one.

## Explicit teaching

The War of 1812 is sometimes called the "Second War for Independence," but
that nickname hides a messier story. The United States declared war on
Britain on June 18, 1812, for several causes at once. First, **impressment**:
the Royal Navy, desperate for sailors in its war against Napoleon's France,
seized sailors from American merchant ships — including American citizens —
and forced them into British service. Second, **trade restrictions**: Britain's
Orders in Council limited American trade with Europe, strangling the commerce
of a trading nation. Third, **western grievances**: American settlers blamed
Britain for arming and encouraging Native resistance in the Northwest, above
all the confederacy built by the Shawnee leader Tecumseh and his brother
Tenskwatawa, who sought to unite Native nations against American expansion.
Fourth, **ambition**: some congressmen, the "War Hawks," wanted Canada and
Florida and believed war would deliver them. Historians still argue about the
ranking. Impressment and trade were the causes Congress named; western
grievances mattered most to the frontier states whose representatives pushed
hardest for war; Canada was a war aim more than a cause. Ranking causes is
not guessing — it is weighing evidence about who pushed, what they said, and
what changed after the war.

The war itself settled almost nothing it was fought over. Britain burned
Washington in August 1814. American forces won at Baltimore (the battle
behind "The Star-Spangled Banner") and at Plattsburgh. The Treaty of Ghent,
signed December 24, 1814, restored the pre-war boundaries — *status quo ante
bellum* — and said nothing about impressment or trade. The most famous
American victory, New Orleans on January 8, 1815, was fought after the
treaty was signed, because news crossed the Atlantic slowly; the Senate
exchanged ratifications in February 1815. Impressment faded anyway, not
because the treaty banned it but because Napoleon's defeat ended Britain's
wartime manpower emergency. The war's real consequences lay elsewhere: Native
resistance lost its British counterweight — Tecumseh was killed in 1813 and
his confederacy dissolved — and American nationalism surged. The Hartford
Convention (December 1814–January 1815), where New England Federalists
protested the war, discredited the Federalist Party when the war ended in
what looked like a draw Americans celebrated as victory.

Nine years later, President James Monroe's annual message of December 2,
1823, announced what became the Monroe Doctrine: the American continents
were no longer open to new European colonization, Europe must not intervene
in the independent nations of Latin America, and in return the United States
would not interfere in Europe's wars. The claim was bold; the power behind it
was thin. The United States in 1823 had a tiny navy and no ability to expel a
European army from the hemisphere. What made the doctrine workable was
Britain: the Royal Navy, the world's strongest, also wanted to keep rival
Europeans — especially France and Spain — out of Latin American trade, and it
enforced the open seas whether or not it endorsed Monroe's words. A doctrine
is a statement of policy; enforcement is a separate question. Confusing the
two is one of the commonest errors in studying foreign policy.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *status quo ante bellum* is Latin for "the
state before the war" — Ghent's outcome in one phrase. The *Monroe Doctrine*
was not a law and not a treaty; no foreign power signed it. It was a
unilateral declaration whose bite depended on British sea power and, later,
on American power that did not yet exist.

## Worked example 1 — Ranking causes

Rank these three causes of the War of 1812: (a) impressment, (b) trade
restrictions, (c) British support for Native resistance.

- Evidence for (a): Congress's war message named impressment; sailors'
  seizures were a public outrage and an insult to sovereignty.
- Evidence for (b): trade restrictions hit New England merchants hardest —
  yet New England largely *opposed* the war. That weakens (b) as the driving
  cause, even though it was a genuine grievance.
- Evidence for (c): western representatives pushed hardest for war, and
  Tecumseh's confederacy was the frontier's immediate fear — but Britain's
  aid to Native nations was limited and partly a pretext for taking Canada.
- A defensible ranking: (a) first, (c) second, (b) third — with the note
  that another historian could swap (a) and (c) with good reasons. The
  ranking matters less than the reasons: a ranking without reasons is an
  opinion; a ranking with evidence is an argument.

## Worked example 2 — Claim vs. enforcement in 1823

Monroe declares: "No new European colonization in the Americas."

- Step 1 (claim): What does the sentence promise? That the United States
  opposes any European power planting a new colony in the hemisphere.
- Step 2 (power check): Could the United States in 1823 stop, say, France
  from landing troops in Mexico? No — the U.S. Navy was small and the army
  smaller.
- Step 3 (who actually enforced it?): Britain's navy, which patrolled the
  Atlantic for its own commercial reasons and did not want rivals in Latin
  America.
- Conclusion: the doctrine stated American policy truthfully and American
  power inaccurately. Both halves matter: the statement shaped later policy
  when American power caught up, but in 1823 it leaned on Britain.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Name three causes of the War of 1812 in your own words. (Impressment,
   trade, western/Native front.)
2. Why does New England's opposition weaken the case for trade restrictions
   as the main cause? (The merchants most hurt opposed the war.)
3. What did the Treaty of Ghent settle, and what did it leave unsettled?
   (Boundaries restored; impressment and trade unmentioned.)
4. Why is the Battle of New Orleans a strange "victory"? (Fought after the
   treaty; news traveled slowly.)

## Independent practice

1. Write your own cause ranking (all four causes, including War Hawk
   ambition) with one reason per rank. Then write one sentence a historian
   who disagrees with your top choice would say.
2. Claim-vs-enforcement table for the Monroe Doctrine: three rows (no new
   colonization; no intervention in independent American nations; no U.S.
   interference in Europe). For each, write what was claimed and what could
   actually be enforced in 1823.
3. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "The War of 1812 ended impressment
   because the Treaty of Ghent banned it." Correct the sentence with two
   facts from this lesson.

## Application

Suppose you are an adviser to Monroe in 1823. Write a five-sentence memo:
two sentences stating the doctrine's benefit, two sentences stating its
enforcement problem honestly, and one sentence recommending whether to issue
it anyway, with a reason. Date the memo December 1823.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) the war's declaration date and the treaty's
date, (2) one sentence on what Ghent did not settle, (3) one sentence on
who enforced the Monroe Doctrine in practice in 1823.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the ranking example aloud; the
  learner restates the ranking and one reason in their own words.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may rank causes by ordering cards
  and defending the order orally.
- **Extension:** research the Hartford Convention and write three sentences
  on why it damaged the Federalists, with a citation.

## Teacher note

Misconception alert: learners often assume New Orleans "won" the war and
that Ghent settled impressment. Correct both explicitly, and stress that
Tecumseh's confederacy pursued Native interests, not British ones — alliance
is not obedience. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
