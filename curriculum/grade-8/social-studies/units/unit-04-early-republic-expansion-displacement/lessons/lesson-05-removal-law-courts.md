# Lesson 5 — Removal: Law, Courts, and Defiance

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can sequence the removal chain from
the 1830 Act through the court cases to the forced Cherokee removal of 1838,
and explain Cherokee resistance at each step.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, the U03
vocabulary for sovereignty and treaty.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) What is a treaty, and who are its parties?
(2) If the Supreme Court rules in your favor, does that guarantee the ruling
will be enforced? Keep your answers; the Cherokee tested both questions.

## Explicit teaching

By the 1820s the Cherokee Nation in Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North
Carolina had adopted a written constitution (1827), a newspaper (the
*Cherokee Phoenix*, from 1828), farms, schools, and a capital at New Echota.
Georgia, eager for Cherokee land — especially after gold was found near
Dahlonega in 1828 — extended state law over Cherokee territory in 1828–1830
and held land lotteries for Cherokee land. President Andrew Jackson, elected
in 1828, supported removal: Native nations east of the Mississippi should move
west of it, he argued, to make room for American settlement and, in his
framing, to preserve Native peoples from conflict with settlers.

Congress passed the Indian Removal Act, signed May 28, 1830. Read it
carefully: the Act did not itself order any tribe to move. It authorized the
president to negotiate removal treaties — exchanging eastern land for western
land and money — with tribes that agreed. In practice, "negotiation" backed by
state pressure, withheld annuities, and the threat of force was not a free
choice, and this unit teaches that distinction explicitly. The Choctaw signed
the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek in 1830 and were removed 1831–1833; the
Creek, Chickasaw, and Seminole followed under their own treaties and
pressures, the Seminole resisting in a long war in Florida.

The Cherokee chose the courts. In *Cherokee Nation v. Georgia* (1831) the
Supreme Court declined to hear the case, calling tribes "domestic dependent
nations" — neither foreign states nor nothing at all. In *Worcester v.
Georgia* (1832), the case of a missionary jailed by Georgia for living in
Cherokee territory without a state license, Chief Justice John Marshall's
Court held that Georgia's laws had no force inside Cherokee territory: the
Cherokee were a distinct political community, and only the federal government
could deal with them. It was a legal victory of the first order — and it was
not enforced. Georgia ignored the ruling, and President Jackson, who favored
removal, did not use federal power to enforce it against Georgia. (The famous
line "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it" is
apocryphal — it first appeared decades later — and you should never quote it
as Jackson's words.)

In 1835 a minority Cherokee faction, without the authority of the elected
Cherokee government under Principal Chief John Ross, signed the Treaty of
New Echota, ceding all Cherokee eastern land for $5 million and western
territory. Ross and the overwhelming majority of Cherokee protested that the
signers did not represent them, and petitioned Congress — the petitions
carried thousands of Cherokee signatures. Congress ratified the treaty anyway
by a single vote in the Senate. When most Cherokee refused to leave
voluntarily, federal troops in May 1838 began rounding up families and holding
them in stockades through a hot summer; disease spread. In the fall and winter
of 1838–1839, about 16,000 Cherokee were forced west to Indian Territory
(present-day Oklahoma) on routes remembered as the Trail of Tears; about
4,000 died from disease, exposure, and starvation. The Cherokee reconstituted
their government in the west under Ross in 1839. Removal did not end Cherokee
history; it is a chapter of survival as well as catastrophe, and this unit
insists on both truths.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *sovereignty* here means the Cherokee Nation's
authority to govern its own territory — the very thing *Worcester* affirmed
and removal violated. A *stockade* was not a waiting room; it was a fenced
military enclosure where families were held for months. The phrase "Trail of
Tears" is the Cherokee people's own name for the forced journey, not a
textbook invention.

## Worked example 1 — Following the chain

Trace the removal chain and mark where Cherokee resistance appears:

- Step 1 (1830): Indian Removal Act authorizes negotiated treaties.
  Cherokee response: the elected government refuses to sign; memorials to
  Congress argue Cherokee sovereignty.
- Step 2 (1831–1832): Court cases. Cherokee response: litigation itself —
  using American courts to defend Cherokee law.
- Step 3 (1835): Treaty of New Echota signed by an unauthorized minority.
  Cherokee response: mass petitions protesting that the signers did not
  represent the Nation.
- Step 4 (1838): Forced removal. Cherokee response: endurance, mutual aid on
  the march, and reconstitution of government in 1839.
- Conclusion: at every step there was a Cherokee answer, and at every step
  American power overrode it. A chain with only American actors is an
  incomplete chain; add the resisting actor at each link.

## Worked example 2 — What the Act did and did not do

Suppose a classmate says, "The Indian Removal Act of 1830 ordered all eastern
tribes to move west immediately."

- Check the text's structure: the Act authorized the president to *negotiate
  treaties* for exchange of lands. It did not itself order removal.
- Check the practice: state laws, land lotteries, withheld payments, and
  military force turned "negotiation" into compulsion — but the mechanism
  matters, because it shows how a law can authorize pressure while claiming
  consent.
- Conclusion: the accurate sentence is, "The 1830 Act authorized the
  president to negotiate removal treaties; in practice, backed by state and
  military pressure, those negotiations produced forced removals." Precision
  about mechanism is not pedantry — it is how you tell a voluntary treaty
  from a coerced one.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Sequence these five events with dates: Indian Removal Act, *Worcester*,
   Treaty of New Echota, forced Cherokee removal, *Cherokee Nation v.
   Georgia*. (1830, 1831, 1832, 1835, 1838.)
2. What did *Worcester* decide, in one sentence? (Georgia's laws had no force
   in Cherokee territory.)
3. Why did the Cherokee protest the Treaty of New Echota? (The signers did
   not represent the elected government.)
4. Name two ways the Cherokee resisted removal before 1838. (Courts;
   petitions/memorials; constitution and newspaper as evidence of nationhood.)

## Independent practice

1. Write the removal chain as five dated links, each with one Cherokee action
   and one American action. Do not write any link with only one side.
2. Paraphrase task: in three sentences, explain the difference between the
   1830 Act's letter (negotiated treaties) and its practice (compulsion).
   Label your paragraph as paraphrase, not quotation.
3. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Jackson said, 'John Marshall has made
   his decision; now let him enforce it,' proving he defied the Court."
   Correct the sentence: what is wrong with the quotation, and what is the
   accurate evidence that the ruling was not enforced?

## Application

You are advising a museum label writer. Write a four-sentence label for an
exhibit panel titled "The Trail of Tears" that (a) names the Cherokee removal
of 1838–1839, (b) gives the estimated numbers (about 16,000 removed, about
4,000 died) and labels them as estimates, (c) names Cherokee resistance and
reconstitution, and (d) avoids presenting the apocryphal Jackson quotation
as fact. The adult checks the label against this lesson before it is used.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) the five chain links with dates, (2) one
sentence on what *Worcester* decided, (3) one sentence on why the New Echota
treaty was contested.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult previews this lesson's content before the
  learner reads it (see teacher guide): forced removal, stockades, and death
  tolls are discussed plainly, without graphic detail, and the learner may
  pause or ask questions at any point.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may sequence the chain with dated
  cards and state each link orally.
- **Extension:** research the Cherokee *Phoenix* newspaper or the 1838
  petitions and write three sentences on how Cherokee writers argued their
  case in their own words, with a citation to the source you used.

## Teacher note

Misconception alert: learners often believe the Act directly ordered removal
or that the Cherokee "agreed" via New Echota. Correct both with the
letter-vs.-practice distinction. Sensitive-content note: keep the human cost
factual and unsensational — numbers, causes (disease, exposure, starvation),
and Cherokee agency — and never assign learners to role-play a forced march
or a stockade. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
