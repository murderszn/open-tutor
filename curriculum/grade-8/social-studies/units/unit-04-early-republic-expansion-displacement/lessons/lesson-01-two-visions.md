# Lesson 1 — Two Visions: Hamilton vs. Jefferson

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can state Hamilton's and Jefferson's
positions on the national bank and explain strict vs. loose construction in
your own words.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, the U03
necessary-and-proper clause note (Article I, §8).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) What does the Constitution say Congress may
do about money? (2) If the Constitution does not mention a bank, does that
mean Congress may not create one? Keep your answers; the cabinet of 1791
disagreed about exactly this question.

## Explicit teaching

George Washington's first cabinet contained the two men who would split
American politics. **Alexander Hamilton**, secretary of the treasury, believed
the new nation needed a strong national government with good credit: pay the
war debts in full, have the federal government assume the states' debts, tax
imports and whiskey, and charter a national bank to hold government money,
issue a stable currency, and lend to businesses. **Thomas Jefferson**,
secretary of state, believed the nation's strength lay in independent farmers
and in governments close to the people: keep the federal government small,
read its powers narrowly, favor farming over manufacturing, and sympathize
with revolutionary France rather than monarchical Britain.

The bank fight of 1791 forced the disagreement into the open. A bill to
charter the Bank of the United States passed both houses of Congress by
February 1791. Washington, unsure whether the bill was constitutional, asked
his cabinet for written opinions. Jefferson (with Attorney General Edmund
Randolph) advised a veto: the Constitution lists Congress's powers, and a
bank is not among them; the necessary-and-proper clause, Jefferson argued,
allows only means that are truly *necessary*, not merely convenient. Hamilton
answered on February 23, 1791, with a long opinion arguing the opposite:
every power of government includes the right to use the means fairly suited to
carrying it out, so long as the Constitution does not forbid those means. A
bank was a proper means of carrying out listed powers such as borrowing
money, coining money, and collecting taxes. Washington signed the bill. The
Bank of the United States received a 20-year charter.

Two labels capture the split. **Strict construction** reads the Constitution
as granting only what it specifically lists. **Loose construction** (or
implied powers) reads it as also granting the reasonable means to carry out
the listed powers. Notice what the labels do not mean: Jefferson was not
against all government action, and Hamilton was not for unlimited government.
Both men claimed to be defending the Constitution — they disagreed about how
to read it. That disagreement, repeated over debt, taxes, foreign policy, and
the French Revolution, became the first party system: Hamilton's Federalists
against Jefferson's and Madison's Democratic-Republicans.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *Assumption* is the federal government taking
over state debts — Hamilton's plan paid state war debts at full value, which
pleased creditors (often northern merchants and speculators) and angered
southerners who had already paid much of theirs. *Implied powers* are powers
not listed but understood to follow from listed ones — the heart of loose
construction.

## Worked example 1 — Is a bank "necessary"?

Suppose Congress has the listed power to collect taxes and borrow money.

- Step 1 (Jefferson's test): Is a bank *indispensable* to collecting taxes?
  No — taxes can be collected without a bank, awkwardly. So, Jefferson argued,
  a bank is not "necessary" and therefore not allowed.
- Step 2 (Hamilton's test): Is a bank a *fair and useful means* of carrying
  out the taxing and borrowing powers? Yes — it holds the money safely, moves
  it across the country, and lends on the government's credit. So, Hamilton
  argued, it is "necessary and proper."
- Conclusion: the two men used the same word, "necessary," to mean different
  things — indispensable vs. reasonably useful. When you argue about the
  Constitution, define your terms first, or you will argue past each other.

## Worked example 2 — Whose interest is behind the argument?

Hamilton's debt plan paid Revolutionary War bonds at full face value. Suppose
a soldier was paid in a bond worth $100 but sold it for $20 to feed his family,
and a merchant later bought it for $20.

- Under Hamilton's plan, the merchant who holds the bond is paid $100 by the
  government. The soldier gets nothing more.
- Supporters said: paying in full restores the government's credit, so it can
  borrow cheaply in the future. That benefits everyone.
- Critics said: the plan rewards speculators and punishes the soldiers who
  actually fought.
- Conclusion: a constitutional argument is also an economic argument. Always
  ask: if this policy wins, who gets paid? Jefferson's supporters asked that
  question constantly, and it is a good habit for this whole unit.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. In one sentence, state Jefferson's bank argument. In one sentence, state
   Hamilton's. (Use "listed powers" for one and "means" for the other.)
2. A new invention appears — a national road system. Jefferson's strict
   construction would ask what question first? (Does the Constitution list
   road-building?)
3. Hamilton's side says the road is a proper means of carrying out the
   commerce and defense powers. Which construction is that? (Loose.)
4. Why did paying debts at full value anger some southerners? (They had
   already paid; speculators profited.)

## Independent practice

1. Fill in the table with one phrase per cell:

   | Policy | Hamilton's position | Jefferson's position |
   |---|---|---|
   | National bank | ? | ? |
   | Federal assumption of state debts | ? | ? |
   | Future economy: manufacturing or farming? | ? | ? |
   | Closer ties: Britain or France? | ? | ? |

2. Write two sentences: why would a merchant in Philadelphia tend to favor
   Hamilton, and why would a Virginia tobacco farmer tend to favor Jefferson?
   Tie each answer to that person's economic interest, not just to personality.
3. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Jefferson opposed the bank because he
   hated all banks and all business." Explain two things wrong with that
   sentence, using this lesson.

## Application

Imagine Washington asks you, as a cabinet adviser, whether Congress may charter
a company to build lighthouses. Write a four-sentence opinion: one sentence
giving the listed power involved, one sentence arguing strict construction,
one sentence arguing loose construction, and one sentence stating your
recommendation and why.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) the year of the bank debate, (2) one sentence
defining strict construction, (3) one sentence defining loose construction.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the Hamilton/Jefferson paraphrases aloud
  and the learner underlines "necessary" each time it appears, then states
  which meaning each writer uses.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the table orally or by
  sorting pre-written position cards into Hamilton/Jefferson columns.
- **Extension:** research the Second Bank (1816) and *McCulloch v. Maryland*
  (1819): did the Supreme Court side with Hamilton's reading? Write three
  sentences with a citation.

Misconception alert: learners often treat "strict" as "careful and good" and
"loose" as "sloppy." Correct it explicitly: both are disciplined readings with
