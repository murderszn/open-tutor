# Lesson 6 — Expansion's Price: Whose Story?

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can argue about expansion using at
least two perspectives, a genuine counterclaim, and one stated limit of the
evidence.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, your notes
from Lessons 1–5, the project argument outline (Task H).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) Who benefited most from the Louisiana
Purchase? (2) Who paid the highest price for it? If your two answers name the
same group, you have missed half the unit — revise before continuing.

## Explicit teaching

Every expansion event in this unit has at least two true stories. The
Louisiana Purchase was a bargain for the treasury (a few cents an acre), a
highway for western farmers, a land rush for speculators, a forced migration
for enslaved people carried to new cotton fields, and a gathering threat for
Native nations whose land the United States now claimed the right to sell.
The War of 1812 was a defense of sailors' rights for Congress, a disaster for
Tecumseh's confederacy, and a political embarrassment for New England
Federalists. Removal was, in Jackson's framing, a humane alternative to
conflict; in the Cherokee petitions' framing, a violation of treaties, court
decisions, and the Cherokee Nation's own sovereignty. None of these
descriptions cancels the others. The historian's job is to hold them together
and judge — not to pick the most flattering story and discard the rest.

Three tools keep a multi-perspective argument honest. First, **weigh causes
and effects by group**: ask "for whom?" after every benefit and every cost.
Second, **separate intention from outcome**: Jefferson intended an empire of
liberty for farmers; the outcome included slavery's expansion and Native
dispossession. Intentions explain decisions; outcomes judge them. Third,
**state the limit**: our evidence for Cherokee perspectives comes largely
through English-language petitions, court records, and newspapers — powerful
sources, but filtered through American institutions and translation. A claim
that names its limit is stronger, not weaker, than one that pretends to
completeness.

The essential question — expansion made the United States bigger, richer,
and more powerful; for whom, and at whose expense? — has no one-word answer.
A strong answer has a claim ("expansion was progress for American farmers
and merchants, and catastrophe for Native nations and enslaved people"),
a genuine counterclaim ("defenders argue it spread republican institutions
and raised living standards for millions of settlers"), evidence for both,
and a judgment that weighs them. A weak answer picks one side's evidence and
ignores the other. The unit assessment asks for the strong kind.

**Key vocabulary in context:** a *counterclaim* is not a paragraph you
defeat with a slogan; it is the best version of the other side's case, stated
fairly. *Perspective* is not the same as *opinion*: a perspective is a
position in the events (a Cherokee farmer, a Kentucky flatboatman, a
Philadelphia merchant), with interests and evidence of its own.

## Worked example 1 — One event, three perspectives

Event: the Louisiana Purchase (1803).

- Perspective A (Ohio farmer): the river highway is open; land is cheap;
  grain can reach New Orleans and the world. Evidence: the purchase price
  and the river's role in Lesson 3.
- Perspective B (enslaved person in Virginia): the purchase opens new cotton
  land, and enslavers may carry enslaved workers southwest — family
  separation as a direct consequence of "opportunity." Evidence: the
  Missouri Compromise fight shows slavery's expansion was the purchase's
  immediate political consequence.
- Perspective C (a Native leader in the territory): a distant government
  has sold a claim over your homeland without your consent. Evidence: the
  territory's peoples never signed the 1803 treaty; France sold a claim, not
  a consent.
- Conclusion: all three are true at once. An argument that mentions only A
  is advertising, not history.

## Worked example 2 — Building a counterclaim fairly

Claim: "American expansion in 1803–1838 was, on balance, a catastrophe
because its benefits depended on dispossession and slavery's spread."

- Weak counterclaim: "Some people disagree." (No content; nothing to weigh.)
- Strong counterclaim: "Defenders argue expansion spread republican
  government, cheap land, and rising living standards to millions of
  settlers, and that the federal government pursued treaties and laws
  rather than open conquest in most cases."
- Weighing: the counterclaim's "treaties and laws" point is real — and
  Lesson 5 shows how treaties could be coerced and laws unenforced. The
  judgment can therefore concede the settlers' gains while holding that
  gains built on coerced treaties do not cancel the catastrophe for those
  dispossessed.
- Conclusion: steel-man the other side, then answer it with evidence, not
  with adjectives.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Give two perspectives on the War of 1812 that differ, with one piece of
   evidence each. (E.g., Baltimore defender vs. Tecumseh's ally.)
2. What is the difference between a perspective and an opinion? (Position
   in events with interests vs. a personal liking.)
3. Why does stating an evidence limit strengthen an argument? (It shows
   what the claim does not cover.)
4. Rewrite this weak counterclaim strongly: "Expansion was good, actually."
   (Name the benefit, the group, and the mechanism.)

## Independent practice

1. Three-perspective table for the Monroe Doctrine: a Latin American
   independence leader, a British merchant, and a U.S. settler. One interest
   and one reaction each.
2. Intention-vs.-outcome paragraph: Jefferson's intention for the West
   (republic of farmers) vs. two outcomes (slavery's spread; Native
   dispossession). Four to six sentences.
3. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "History has one true story, and the
   other perspectives are just opinions." Explain why that sentence fails,
   using one event from this unit.

## Application

Draft the thesis paragraph of your unit argument (project §4): claim,
counterclaim in one fair sentence, and one stated limit. Exchange drafts
with a partner; each partner checks only whether the counterclaim is fair
and the limit is real. Revise before Week 18 S4.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) two perspectives on one expansion event,
(2) one sentence defining a strong counterclaim, (3) one limit of this
unit's evidence about Cherokee perspectives.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult models Worked Example 2 aloud, pausing at
  the weak/strong contrast, before learners draft.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may present their thesis paragraph
  orally and receive the fairness check as spoken feedback.
- **Extension:** read one Cherokee memorial paraphrase (resource pack) and
  write three sentences on what changes when the Cherokee case is stated in
  the Nation's own terms rather than in Jackson's.

## Teacher note

Misconception alert: learners often treat "multiple perspectives" as
"everyone is equally right about facts." Correct it: perspectives differ in
position and interest, but facts (dates, votes, death tolls) are still
checkable — and this unit's argument rubric scores evidence accuracy
separately from perspective coverage. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 6 items.
