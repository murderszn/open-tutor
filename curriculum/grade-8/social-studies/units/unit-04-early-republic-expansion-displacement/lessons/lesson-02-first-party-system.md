# Lesson 2 — Parties, Precedents, and the Election of 1800

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can name three Washington precedents,
explain how the first two parties differed, and explain why the election of
1800 mattered.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, the timeline
from U03 (ends 1791).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) What is a precedent? (2) Why might the first
president's choices matter more than a later president's? Keep your answers;
Washington's choices were watched as closely as his policies.

## Explicit teaching

Washington knew every move he made would become a model. The **precedents**
he set include: governing through a cabinet of department heads who advised
him (and disagreed with each other in writing, as in the bank debate);
issuing a Neutrality Proclamation in 1793 keeping the United States out of the
war between Britain and revolutionary France, even though many Americans
sympathized with France; and stepping down after two terms in 1796, refusing
a third, which fixed the expectation of limited presidential tenure for 140
years until Franklin Roosevelt. Washington also signed the Jay Treaty (1794)
with Britain, settling debts and trade disputes but angering France's
supporters — a reminder that neutrality itself is a policy with winners and
losers.

By the mid-1790s the cabinet quarrel had become organized politics. The
**Federalists** (Hamilton, Adams) favored a strong national government,
British trade, and a commercial economy. The **Democratic-Republicans**
(Jefferson, Madison) favored states' rights, farming, and France. These are
not the modern parties: the Democratic-Republicans later split, and the
Federalists faded after the War of 1812. The point for this unit is the
invention itself: for the first time, Americans organized permanent,
competing teams for elections — and many founders, Washington included,
thought parties ("factions") were dangerous. Madison had argued in
*Federalist No. 10* that a large republic could control factions; now the
republic would test that claim on itself.

The test sharpened under John Adams (president 1797–1801). The Federalist
Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts (1798), which lengthened the
residence requirement for citizenship and made it a crime to publish "false,
scandalous, and malicious" writing against the government. Democratic-Republican
newspapermen were prosecuted. Jefferson and Madison answered secretly with
the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798–1799), arguing that states could
declare federal laws unconstitutional — a states'-rights doctrine that would
echo dangerously in later decades. Then came the election of 1800. Jefferson
tied with his own running mate, Aaron Burr, in the Electoral College, and the
House of Representatives took 36 ballots to choose Jefferson. The Federalists
handed over power without an army in the streets. Jefferson called it "the
revolution of 1800" — not because blood was shed, but because it wasn't: a
ruling party lost an election and left office. That had almost never happened
in world history, and it is why 1800 is remembered as a triumph of the
constitutional system rather than merely a change of personnel.

**Key vocabulary in context:** a *political party* is not just a club; it
nominates candidates, organizes voters, and gives ordinary citizens a way to
act together between elections. A *precedent* binds by example, not by law —
which is why Washington's two terms mattered even though no amendment
required them (the 22nd Amendment came in 1951).

## Worked example 1 — Is neutrality neutral?

In 1793 Britain and France are at war. American merchants want to trade with
both; American farmers owe debts to British merchants; many Americans admire
the French Revolution.

- Washington's Neutrality Proclamation says: no American aid to either side's
  war effort.
- Effect on Britain: good — its navy controls the seas and American trade
  flows its way anyway.
- Effect on France: bad — France had expected American help under the 1778
  alliance, and gets none.
- Conclusion: "neutral" describes the rule, not the result. A policy can be
  even-handed in wording and uneven in effect. When you evaluate a policy,
  trace effects, not just intentions.

## Worked example 2 — The tie that tested the system

In 1800 Jefferson and Burr each receive 73 electoral votes. Under the rules
then in force, the House decides, voting by state delegation.

- Step 1: The House ballots again and again; Federalists, who lost the
  election, hold the deciding votes in the House.
- Step 2: On the 36th ballot, enough Federalists abstain to let Jefferson win.
- Step 3: Adams leaves office; Jefferson is inaugurated March 4, 1801; no
  troops move, no rival government is declared.
- Conclusion: the system worked precisely because the losers accepted losing.
  The 12th Amendment (1804) later fixed the tie problem by having electors
  vote separately for president and vice president — a rule change produced
  by a near-miss, like the Constitution itself after Shays' Rebellion.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Name two Washington precedents and, for each, one way it shaped later
   presidencies. (Cabinet; two terms; neutrality.)
2. A Federalist and a Democratic-Republican argue about Britain. What does
   each side most likely say? (Trade partner vs. old enemy; France's friend
   vs. monarchy.)
3. Why did the Sedition Act alarm Jefferson's supporters? (It criminalized
   criticism of the government.)
4. Explain in your own words why Jefferson called 1800 a "revolution" even
   though no one was overthrown by force. (Power changed hands by election.)

## Independent practice

1. Sequence these events and give a one-line reason each mattered: Jay Treaty
   (1794), Alien and Sedition Acts (1798), Washington's Farewell Address
   (1796), election of 1800, 12th Amendment (1804).
2. Two-column chart: Federalist positions vs. Democratic-Republican positions
   on (a) national bank, (b) Britain vs. France, (c) strength of the national
   government. Add one economic interest behind each party's column.
3. Error analysis: a classmate writes, "Washington set the two-term precedent
   because the Constitution limited him to two terms." Correct the sentence
   and explain the difference between a precedent and an amendment.

## Application

Write a five-sentence newspaper paragraph dated March 5, 1801, from the point
of view of a Democratic-Republican printer, announcing Jefferson's inauguration
and explaining why the peaceful transfer matters. Then write one sentence a
Federalist printer might add as a caution. Label which sentences are your
invention (a modern reconstruction) and which facts come from this lesson.

## Exit check

Without looking back, list: (1) two Washington precedents, (2) the two first
parties and one difference, (3) why the election of 1800 is called a peaceful
transfer of power.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the 1800 tie example aloud as a story
  with numbered steps; the learner retells it in three sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may sequence events with cards on a
  table instead of writing a list.
- **Extension:** compare the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions' states'-rights
  argument with the nullification crisis of 1832 in three sentences: what
  stayed the same, what changed?

## Teacher note

Misconception alert: learners often map the first parties directly onto modern
parties. Correct it every time: names were reused later by different coalitions.
The Democratic-Republicans are not today's Democrats. Answer-key reference:
