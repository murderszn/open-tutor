# Lesson 5 — Images as Arguments: Revere's Engraving and the Limits of the Record

**Unit 02 · Week 10 S1 · ~45 minutes · Session type: source investigation**

## Goal

I can explain how a maker's purpose shapes what an image shows, and name what images — old or new — cannot prove about the past.

## Materials

- The unit's generated reconstruction (below) and its text-only alternative
- Description of Revere's engraving (below; the adult may show the public-domain print via the resource pack link)
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 min)

From Lesson 1: on March 5, 1770, British soldiers fired into a Boston crowd; five colonists died. Three weeks later, Paul Revere was selling an engraving of the event. If you were a patriot leader, what would you want that picture to show? (British cruelty, colonial innocence — the version that recruits supporters.)

## Explicit teaching (18 min)

**The engraving.** Paul Revere's "The Bloody Massacre perpetrated in King-Street, Boston, on March 5th 1770" was engraved, printed, and sold by Revere in March 1770 — based on a drawing by the young artist Henry Pelham, whom Revere did not credit. It shows a neat line of British soldiers firing in unison, on an officer's order, into a defenseless crowd — with the Custom House labeled "Butcher's Hall." Written accounts describe something messier: a chaotic nighttime scrum, snowballs and clubs, a soldier firing without orders, then ragged shots. Revere's version is **propaganda**: the most effective patriot image of the war, and not an accurate depiction (Smithsonian National Museum of American History; Gilder Lehrman Institute).

**The reconstruction.** Below is a modern AI-generated illustration imagining a related scene — a print shop at dusk, townspeople crowding to read a freshly posted broadside. It is **not** a primary source and proves nothing about 1770.

![AI-generated reconstruction of a colonial Boston print shop at dusk in March 1770: townspeople in 18th-century dress gather around the shop window reading a freshly posted broadside by lantern light while an apprentice carries a stack of papers; snow on the cobblestones, brick buildings, a church steeple in the distance.](../assets/print-shop-broadside-reconstruction.png)

*Caption: AI-generated reconstruction of a Boston print shop at dusk, March 1770 — a modern illustration imagining how printed news circulated. Not a primary source; not evidence of any specific event.*

**Text-only alternative** (everything needed for the activity): (1) What is visible — a brick print shop at dusk; a broadside sheet pasted inside the window; six to eight townspeople in 18th-century coats, hats, and shawls reading it by lantern light; an apprentice carrying stacked papers; snow on cobblestones; lit windows and a church steeple down the street. (2) What is not shown — what the broadside *says*; whether its claims are true; what actually happened on King Street on March 5; who printed it or why. Learners can complete the activity from this description alone.

**The method (D2.His.6, D2.His.10, D3.2).** Ask of any image: who made it, for whom, and what did they need it to do? Revere needed to enrage colonists against British troops — so his soldiers are orderly and merciless, his victims innocent. The reconstruction's maker (this unit's author) needed to show *print culture* — how news traveled — so it shows readers, not the event. Both images are shaped by purpose. Neither can prove what happened on King Street; for that we need written accounts from multiple sides, compared with attention to *their* purposes too.

### Worked example 1 — Reading Revere with purpose in mind

*Question: What does the "Butcher's Hall" label on the Custom House tell us?*

*Reasoning:* Step 1 — the building was the Custom House, a tax office. Step 2 — Revere renamed it "Butcher's Hall" in the image. Step 3 — a label is a choice: it frames the building (tax collection) as a slaughterhouse before the viewer even looks at the soldiers. Conclusion: the label does persuasive work — it tells the viewer how to feel. Method: treat every label, caption, and title as part of the argument, not as neutral description.

### Worked example 2 — What the reconstruction can and cannot do

*Question: A classmate says, "This picture proves Bostonians learned about the Massacre from broadsides." Evaluate.*

*Reasoning:* The picture proves **nothing** — it is a modern illustration, not evidence. What it *can* do: help us imagine a true general fact (print shops sold engravings; Revere advertised his print in Boston newspapers in March 1770; crowds did gather to read posted news) — but each of those facts needs a real source. The reconstruction is useful as a **thinking prompt** and useless as **proof**. Method: sort every claim about the image into "shown" (I can see it) vs. "proven" (a source establishes it) — only the first column belongs to the image.

## Guided practice (8 min)

1. Together, list three things Revere's engraving emphasizes and, for each, state the purpose that emphasis serves.
2. "Read" the reconstruction: list two things it shows about how news traveled, and two things it cannot prove.
3. Discuss: Pelham (whose drawing Revere used) was a loyalist sympathizer, yet his drawing became patriot propaganda in Revere's hands. What does that tell us about who controls a source's meaning? (The publisher and the audience, not just the artist.)

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Write 3–4 sentences: how did Revere's perspective as a Son of Liberty shape what his engraving shows and hides? (D2.His.6)
2. **Error analysis:** A classmate claims, "Revere's engraving is a primary source, so it shows what really happened." Identify two errors: (a) primary sources are evidence *of their maker's perspective*, not cameras — this one is propaganda by design; (b) "primary" describes when it was made, not whether it is accurate — accuracy must be checked against other sources.
3. Name one voice missing from both the engraving and the written patriot accounts of the Massacre (e.g., the British soldiers' perspective; Crispus Attucks's own story — we know his name from a casualty list, almost nothing else), and state what that voice might have recorded. (D2.His.10)

## Application

Find one modern persuasive image (political ad, protest poster, viral photo — the adult helps verify context). Write 3–4 sentences applying the lesson's method: who made it, what they need it to do, and one thing it cannot prove. (D2.Civ.14 — comparing historical and contemporary means of shaping opinion.)

## Exit check

Explain in two sentences how the engraver's purpose shaped Revere's image, and name one limit of the historical record about the Boston Massacre.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** The text-only alternative above carries the full activity; no learner must interpret the image visually to participate.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may answer the independent-practice paragraph as a labeled two-column chart (shows / hides).
- **Extension:** Compare Revere's engraving with Pelham's original (descriptions in the resource pack): the compositions are nearly identical, but the texts differ — Pelham's quotes scripture against violence, Revere's poem demands retribution. Write a half-page on how the *same picture* argues differently with different words.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"Old pictures are more trustworthy than new ones."* Age does not equal accuracy; the 1770 engraving is less reliable about the event than a careful modern historian's account — though it is excellent evidence of patriot persuasion.
- *"The AI picture is fake, so it's useless."* It is not evidence, but it is useful — as a labeled reconstruction it prompts questions about print culture that real sources then answer.
