# Lesson 4 — Whose Liberty? Enslaved People and the Revolution's Promise

**Unit 02 · Week 9 S1 · ~45 minutes · Session type: concept launch**

## Goal

I can explain how enslaved people used the Revolution's language and wartime upheaval to pursue their own freedom, using two primary sources as evidence.

## Materials

- Printed excerpts below (public domain)
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 min)

From Lesson 2: the Declaration says "all men are created equal" — and Congress deleted the passage condemning the slave trade. If you were enslaved in 1776 and heard those words, what would you do with them? (Hold the nation to them; use them as an argument — which is exactly what happened.)

## Explicit teaching (18 min)

The Revolution's ideals were written by enslavers and read by the enslaved — who took them seriously.

**Source 1 — Phillis Wheatley to the Reverend Samson Occom (published March 11, 1774).** Wheatley, an enslaved-born Black poet in Boston (freed in 1773; her *Poems* had been published in London in 1773), wrote to Occom, a Mohegan minister:

> "for in every human Breast, God has implanted a Principle, which we call Love of Freedom; it is impatient of Oppression, and pants for Deliverance; and by the Leave of our modern Egyptians I will assert, that the same Principle lives in us. ... How well the Cry for Liberty, and the reverse Disposition for the exercise of oppressive Power over others agree, — I humbly think it does not require the Penetration of a Philosopher to determine."

Maker: a formerly enslaved Black woman writer. Audience: a Native minister and the newspaper's readers. Purpose: to expose the contradiction — colonists crying for liberty while holding people in bondage. "Modern Egyptians" casts slaveholders as Pharaoh.

**Source 2 — Lord Dunmore's Proclamation (November 7, 1775).** Virginia's royal governor, desperate for soldiers, declared "all indentured Servants, Negroes, or others, (appertaining to Rebels,) free, that are able and willing to bear Arms, they joining his Majesty's Troops." Maker: a British official losing a war. Audience: Virginia's enslaved and indentured population (and the rebels he hoped to frighten). Purpose: military strategy — gain soldiers and terrify slaveholding patriots. Hundreds of enslaved people fled to British lines; Virginia's leaders answered by threatening execution for those who left.

Read the two together: Wheatley claims liberty as a **universal principle**; Dunmore offers freedom as a **military bargain**. Enslaved people acted on both — thousands served in the Continental Army and in British forces, weighing which side offered the better chance at freedom. After the war, Black petitioners in Massachusetts (1777) quoted the new state's own liberty language to demand abolition. Perspective shaped the sources (D2.His.6): Wheatley's letter argues from principle; Dunmore's proclamation argues from strategy — and each reveals its maker's position.

### Worked example 1 — Maker, audience, purpose (D2.His.11)

*Question: Why does Dunmore's purpose matter for how we read his offer of freedom?*

*Reasoning:* Step 1 — maker: the royal governor of Virginia, a representative of the king. Step 2 — situation: November 1775, his forces are weak, patriots control the colony. Step 3 — purpose: he needs soldiers *and* wants to frighten slaveholding rebels into submission. Step 4 — consequence for reading: the offer is **real** (people who reached British lines were freed and served) but **instrumental** — freedom is a weapon of war, not a principle. Compare Wheatley: she offers no army, only an argument — but her argument outlived the war. Method: always ask *what the maker needed* before deciding what the source proves.

### Worked example 2 — The same words, different claims

*Question: Both Wheatley and the Massachusetts petitioners of 1777 use "liberty" language. Are they making the same argument as the patriots?*

*Reasoning:* They use the patriots' **vocabulary** but press a **different claim**. Patriots: liberty means freedom from British rule (while keeping slavery). Wheatley and the petitioners: liberty is a universal principle — if it is true, it covers the enslaved too. The evidence: Wheatley's "Cry for Liberty" sentence directly measures patriot conduct against patriot words. This is how ideals escape their authors: once "all men are created equal" is public, anyone can quote it. The limit: quoting did not free anyone by itself — it took war, law, and decades of organizing.

## Guided practice (8 min)

1. Together, fill a two-column chart for Wheatley vs. Dunmore: maker / audience / purpose / what "freedom" means in each source.
2. Underline the sentence in each excerpt that best shows the maker's purpose. Compare choices.
3. Discuss: which source would a historian trust more to prove *what enslaved people believed*? (Wheatley — she speaks from that experience; Dunmore speaks *about* them, to use them.)

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Write a 3–4 sentence paragraph: why did enslaved people join *both* sides of the war? Cite one detail from each source.
2. **Error analysis:** A classmate claims, "Dunmore freed the slaves because he believed in equality." Identify two errors: (a) his offer covered only those who would bear arms for Britain — a military bargain, not a principle; (b) nothing in the proclamation criticizes slavery itself. What did he believe in? (Winning the war.)
3. Draft one supporting question (D1.4) that Source 1 helps answer and one that Source 2 helps answer, and state which source fits which question and why.

## Application

Find one modern example of an excluded group quoting a nation's founding ideals back at it (the adult helps verify). Write 3–4 sentences: who quoted what, what they demanded, and whether the strategy worked. How is this like or unlike Wheatley's move?

## Exit check

Name the two sources, state each maker's purpose in one sentence, and give one reason the same ideal ("liberty") produced different claims.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Pre-underline the two key sentences in each excerpt; read the excerpts aloud together before silent analysis.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may present the two-column chart as a spoken comparison instead of writing the paragraph.
- **Extension:** Research the 1777 Massachusetts freedom petitions (e.g., Prince Hall's petition): what language did the petitioners borrow, and what was the legislature's answer? Half-page with one quoted phrase.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"The British were the anti-slavery side."* Britain offered freedom as a wartime tactic in 1775 — and returned many Black loyalists to slavery after the war; the British Empire abolished slavery in 1833, decades later. Neither side fought *for* abolition.
- *"Enslaved people were passive victims of the war."* They were strategic actors — fleeing, enlisting, petitioning, and forcing both sides to bid for their service.
