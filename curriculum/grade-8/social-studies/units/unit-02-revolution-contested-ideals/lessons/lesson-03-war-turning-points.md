# Lesson 3 — Why the Colonies Could Win: Turning Points and Alliances

**Unit 02 · Week 8 S1 · ~45 minutes · Session type: concept launch**

## Goal

I can explain why the weaker side won the Revolutionary War, and analyze how Saratoga, the French alliance, and Yorktown connect as causes and effects.

## Materials

- Outline map of eastern North America and the Atlantic
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 min)

From Lesson 1: the war began at Lexington and Concord (April 19, 1775) — fourteen months *before* independence was declared. If Britain was the world's strongest empire, why wasn't the rebellion crushed in a year? List two guesses before the lesson.

## Explicit teaching (18 min)

On paper, Britain should have won quickly: the world's strongest navy, a professional army, and far more money. The colonies won because several advantages compounded:

- **Distance and geography:** Britain fought 3,000 miles from home across an ocean; every soldier, musket, and barrel of flour had to be shipped. Colonial forces fought on familiar ground with short supply lines.
- **A war of wills, not just battles:** Britain had to *conquer and hold* a huge territory; the colonies only had to *avoid losing* until Britain tired. As long as George Washington's army survived, the rebellion survived.
- **French support:** After the American victory at **Saratoga** (British General Burgoyne's surrender, October 17, 1777) proved the rebellion was serious, France signed the **Treaty of Alliance** (February 6, 1778) — money, weapons, troops, and, decisively, a navy. French warships at **Yorktown** trapped the British army between Washington's land forces and the French fleet; General Cornwallis surrendered on October 19, 1781.
- **The Treaty of Paris** (signed September 3, 1783) recognized American independence and set generous boundaries.

Note the causal chain — this is D2.His.1, connecting events in broader contexts: colonial survival → Saratoga victory → French alliance → French navy → Yorktown → independence recognized. Remove any link and the outcome is uncertain. Also note who is missing from this standard account: the thousands of Black soldiers who served on both sides, the Native nations who chose sides strategically, and the women who ran farms, businesses, and spy networks — Lesson 4 and the project recover them.

### Worked example 1 — Saratoga as cause and effect

*Question: Was Saratoga more important as an effect (of earlier events) or as a cause (of later ones)?*

*Reasoning:* As an **effect**: Saratoga resulted from Burgoyne's overextended supply lines, American knowledge of the terrain, and the survival of the Continental Army through 1776's defeats. As a **cause**: it convinced France the Americans could win — no Saratoga, likely no Treaty of Alliance; no French navy, likely no Yorktown trap. Verdict: its importance is mostly as a **cause** — a turning point is defined by what follows it. Method: test a "turning point" claim by asking the counterfactual — *if this hadn't happened, would the next link still hold?*

### Worked example 2 — Evaluating "the most important" claim

*Question: A textbook calls Yorktown "the decisive battle of the war." Evaluate.*

*Reasoning:* Yorktown ended major fighting — but it was **decisive as a consequence**, not as a standalone battle. Its causes (French fleet, Washington's march, Cornwallis's position) were built over six years. Calling Yorktown "decisive" is accurate if it means *the battle that ended the war*; misleading if it implies the war was won at Yorktown alone. Better phrasing: Yorktown was the *culmination* of the alliance strategy that Saratoga made possible. The method: separate *when fighting stopped* from *why one side could force it to stop*.

## Guided practice (8 min)

1. Together, build the causal chain on the board: 1775 survival → 1777 Saratoga → 1778 French alliance → 1781 Yorktown → 1783 Treaty of Paris. For each arrow, state the link in one sentence.
2. Sort British vs. colonial advantages into two columns: which advantages were about *resources*, and which about *geography and will*?
3. Discuss: could the colonies have won without France? What evidence from the chain supports your answer?

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. On the outline map, mark Saratoga, Yorktown, and the Atlantic crossing route of French aid. Add one cause-effect note per location.
2. **Error analysis:** A classmate claims, "America won because its soldiers were braver." Identify the reasoning error: bravery was roughly equal on both sides; the explanation must be structural (distance, alliance, war aims), not a character trait. Rewrite the claim with one structural cause.
3. Write 3–4 sentences: why did *France* join the war? (Hint: consider France's own rivalry with Britain — allies act from self-interest, not charity.)

## Application

Research one present-day alliance (e.g., NATO — the adult helps verify current facts). Write 3–4 sentences comparing it with the 1778 Franco-American alliance: what does each side gain? How is an alliance different from a friendship? (D2.Civ.14 — comparing means of pursuing shared goals.)

## Exit check

Name the three turning points in order and state, in one sentence each, what each one caused.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Keep the causal chain diagram posted; use arrows and dates as the visual anchor all week.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may explain the chain as a labeled diagram (boxes and arrows) instead of a paragraph.
- **Extension:** Investigate the winter at Valley Forge (1777–78): what did the army's survival there prove to the French? Write a half-page connecting endurance to diplomacy.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"The minutemen beat the British army by themselves."* The war was won with French money, arms, troops, and sea power — the alliance was decisive.
- *"France helped out of love for liberty."* France's monarchy opposed republican ideas at home; it joined to weaken Britain, its rival.
