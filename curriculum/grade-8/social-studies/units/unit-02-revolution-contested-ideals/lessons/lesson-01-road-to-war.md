# Lesson 1 — The Road to War: Taxes, Representation, and Protest

**Unit 02 · Week 7 S1 · ~45 minutes · Session type: concept launch**

## Goal

I can explain how new British taxes after 1763 led to colonial protest, and what colonists meant by "no taxation without representation."

## Materials

- Timeline strip (1763–1776) and event cards
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 min)

From U01: the Navigation Acts told colonists *where* they could trade. Were colonists represented in the Parliament that passed those laws? (No — and most colonists accepted this while the laws were lightly enforced.) What changed after the French and Indian War ended in 1763? (Britain had a huge war debt and decided the colonies should help pay it.)

## Explicit teaching (18 min)

Britain's victory in the French and Indian War (1754–1763) doubled the national debt and left thousands of British soldiers stationed in North America. Parliament decided the colonies should pay a share — and, for the first time, taxed them **directly** to raise revenue, not just to regulate trade.

The sequence ran like this:

- **Stamp Act (March 1765):** a tax on printed materials — newspapers, legal documents, playing cards. Colonists called it taxation without representation: they elected no members of Parliament, so Parliament had no right to tax them. Protests, boycotts of British goods, and the Stamp Act Congress (October 1765) followed. Parliament **repealed** the tax in March 1766 — but on the same day passed the **Declaratory Act**, claiming Parliament could legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever."
- **Townshend Acts (1767):** taxes on imported glass, paint, paper, and tea. Colonists answered with organized **boycotts** — refusing to buy British goods. The boycott was their economic weapon: colonial women spun homespun cloth instead of buying British fabric.
- **Boston Massacre (March 5, 1770):** after months of tension between Bostonians and British soldiers, a street confrontation ended with soldiers firing into a crowd. Five colonists died, including **Crispus Attucks**, a man of African descent. Patriot leaders turned the event into a rallying story across the colonies.
- **Tea Act (1773) and the Boston Tea Party (December 16, 1773):** Parliament gave the East India Company a tea monopoly with the tax still attached. Colonists read it as taxation disguised as a bargain. In Boston, men disguised as Mohawk warriors boarded ships and dumped 342 chests of tea into the harbor.
- **Coercive Acts ("Intolerable Acts," 1774):** Parliament punished Massachusetts — closing Boston's port, restricting town meetings, allowing royal officials accused of crimes to be tried in Britain. Instead of isolating Massachusetts, the punishment united the colonies: the **First Continental Congress** met in Philadelphia (September 1774).
- **Lexington and Concord (April 19, 1775):** British troops marched to seize colonial weapons stores; minutemen met them. Shots were exchanged — "the shot heard round the world." The war had begun before anyone declared independence.

The pattern: each British action aimed at control produced a colonial response that widened the conflict. This is **change** — but note the **continuity**: even in 1775, most colonists still called themselves British and hoped for reconciliation. Independence was not the plan at the start.

### Worked example 1 — What "no taxation without representation" meant

*Question: A colonist in 1765 pays taxes to her town meeting and her colonial assembly, but refuses the Stamp Act. Is she against all taxes?*

*Reasoning:* No — she pays taxes voted by bodies she helps elect. Her objection is to a tax voted by a Parliament in which she has no representative. The principle: legitimate taxation requires **consent through representation**. The British counter-argument was "virtual representation" — that Parliament represented all British subjects everywhere. The colonist's reply: a representative I never chose and cannot remove is not my representative. The method: separate the *principle* (consent) from the *amount* (the Stamp Act was not ruinously expensive — the principle was the point).

### Worked example 2 — Change vs. continuity on the timeline

*Question: Is the repeal of the Stamp Act (1766) an example of change or continuity?*

*Reasoning:* Both — and the coding depends on what you compare. As **change**: a colonial boycott forced Parliament to back down, something that had never happened before; colonial collective action worked. As **continuity**: the Declaratory Act, passed the same day, kept Parliament's claim of total authority — the constitutional conflict was unchanged. Strong answers code the event twice, once for each comparison. This is D2.His.2: the same event can be change *and* continuity depending on the question you ask.

## Guided practice (8 min)

1. Together, place these on the timeline strip: Stamp Act (1765), repeal (1766), Townshend Acts (1767), Boston Massacre (1770), Boston Tea Party (1773), Coercive Acts (1774), Lexington/Concord (1775).
2. For each event, name the British action and the colonial response in one sentence each.
3. Discuss: which event was the point of no return? Defend with one reason — there is no single right answer.

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. In your notebook, explain "no taxation without representation" in your own words (3–4 sentences), using the Stamp Act as your example.
2. **Error analysis:** A classmate claims, "The colonists rebelled because British taxes were the highest in the world." Identify the error: British subjects in Britain actually paid *higher* taxes than colonists. What was the real objection? (The principle of consent, not the amount.)
3. Code two events as change or continuity with a one-sentence reason each — one must be a "both" answer like Worked example 2.

## Application

Find one modern example of people using an economic boycott as political pressure (any country, any era — the adult helps verify). Write 3–4 sentences: who boycotted what, what they wanted, and whether it worked. What made the colonial boycotts of 1767–1770 effective or limited?

## Exit check

Name three events between 1765 and 1775 and, for each, state whether it moved the colonies toward or away from reconciliation with Britain.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Keep the timeline strip visible all week; color-code British actions (red) and colonial responses (blue).
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may narrate the timeline as a 60-second "news report" instead of writing the paragraph.
- **Extension:** Research the Stamp Act Congress (October 1765): who attended, what did it resolve, and why does it matter that nine colonies acted together for the first time?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"The colonists wanted independence from the start."* Through 1775 most sought reconciliation with rights protected — independence became the majority position only in 1776 (Common Sense, January 1776, helped).
- *"Taxes were crushing."* The amounts were modest; the fight was over the constitutional principle of consent.
