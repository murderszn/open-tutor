# Lesson 6 — Was the Revolution a Turning Point? Significance and Memory

**Unit 02 · Week 10 S1/S4 bridge · ~45 minutes · Session type: synthesis launch (argument drafted in S4)**

## Goal

I can argue whether the American Revolution was a turning point, using the significance test and evidence from at least three unit sources.

## Materials

- Unit sources gathered so far (timeline, Declaration excerpts, Wheatley, Dunmore, engraving notes)
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 min)

From Lesson 3: Saratoga was called a turning point because of what followed it. Apply the same test to the whole Revolution: after 1783, what was *different* — and for whom was it not different at all? (Independent nation, republican government — but slavery continued, women gained no political rights, Native nations faced a new expansionist neighbor.)

## Explicit teaching (18 min)

Historians judge **significance** (D2.His.3) with questions, not feelings: Did it change many lives? Did it change what was possible afterward? Do people still use it to explain their world? By those tests, the Revolution scores unevenly — which is exactly why it is worth arguing about.

**The "turning point" case:** thirteen colonies became an independent republic — the first large-scale republic of the modern era — founded on written principles of equality and consent. Those principles became tools: abolitionists, women's-rights advocates, and anti-colonial movements worldwide quoted the Declaration. The war also redistributed power: Britain lost an empire's richest colonies; France's victory spending helped bankrupt its monarchy toward its own 1789 revolution.

**The "not a turning point (for most)" case:** for enslaved people, the war changed masters' flags more than bondage — slavery *expanded* in the new nation after 1783 (the cotton boom was coming). For women, Abigail Adams's March 31, 1776 plea — "remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors" — was answered with silence; no political rights followed. For Native nations, the Revolution was catastrophic: leaders like the Mohawk **Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea)** allied with Britain precisely because the British limited colonial expansion — American victory removed that restraint.

**Two secondary accounts to compare** (summarized; full references in the resource pack): Account A (a standard political narrative) treats the Revolution as the birth of modern democracy — its evidence is constitutions, elections, and the Declaration's global influence. Account B (a social history) treats it as a war that preserved existing hierarchies — its evidence is the postwar expansion of slavery, the exclusion of women, and Native dispossession. Same events, different questions, different verdicts. Neither account is "biased" in the careless sense; each selects the evidence its question requires — which is why the *question* matters as much as the sources (D1.4).

### Worked example 1 — The significance test on one person

*Question: Was Phillis Wheatley historically significant?*

*Reasoning:* Apply the three questions. (1) Many lives? Directly, few — but her published *Poems* (London, 1773) made her the most famous Black writer of the age, reaching thousands. (2) Changed what was possible? She modeled Black authorship and used liberty language against slavery, a move abolitionists repeated for a century. (3) Still used to explain the world? Yes — her Occom letter is still quoted in debates about American ideals vs. practice. Verdict: significant, not for power held but for arguments supplied. Method: significance is not fame or virtue — it is *consequence*, traced with evidence.

### Worked example 2 — Building the counterclaim honestly

*Question: Draft a genuine counterclaim to "the Revolution was a turning point toward liberty."*

*Reasoning:* A strawman counterclaim: "Some say nothing changed." A genuine one: "The Revolution was a turning point toward *self-government for white male property holders* — it transferred power from a distant Parliament to local elites while preserving enslavement, excluding women, and accelerating Native dispossession; the liberty it secured was real but rationed." Check it is genuine: it concedes something true (self-government was real and new) while denying the universal claim — and it can be evidenced (postwar slavery expansion, Adams's unanswered letter, Brant's alliance logic). This is D3.3/D4.1 practice: the counterclaim must be strong enough to deserve an answer.

## Guided practice (8 min)

1. Together, run the significance test on the Revolution as a whole: one piece of evidence per question (many lives / new possibilities / still used today).
2. Sort unit evidence into two piles: "turning point" and "continuity" — each slip needs a one-sentence reason.
3. Discuss: does it matter *who* is asking whether it was a turning point? (A patriot veteran, an enslaved Virginian, and a Mohawk leader would answer differently — and all three answers can be evidenced.)

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Write a thesis sentence answering "Was the American Revolution a turning point?" that includes a qualifier (for whom / in what respect).
2. **Error analysis:** A classmate claims, "The Revolution was a turning point because America became the freest country on earth." Identify two errors: (a) "freest on earth" is unmeasurable praise, not a significance argument — replace with a testable claim; (b) it ignores for whom freedom contracted — enslaved people and Native nations. Rewrite as a qualified thesis.
3. List the three sources you will cite in the S4 argument and, for each, one sentence on what it proves and one on its limit.

## Application

Choose one modern debate about a historical figure's significance (a statue, a holiday, a renamed school — the adult helps verify facts). Write 3–4 sentences applying the three significance questions. Does the debate turn on different evidence or different questions? (D2.Civ.14.)

## Exit check

State your qualified thesis in one sentence and name the strongest evidence against it.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Post the three significance questions where the S4 writing happens; let learners answer them as a pre-writing organizer.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may deliver the S4 argument as a structured 3-minute speech (claim, counterclaim, limit) instead of an essay.
- **Extension:** Research Joseph Brant's diplomacy after the war (1783–1807): what did he try to secure for the Haudenosaunee, and what did the new United States do instead? Half-page with one cited source.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"Significance means importance, so it's just opinion."* Significance judgments are arguable, but they must be evidenced — "it mattered because" needs a because-clause with sources.
- *"A turning point must be good."* Turning points turn in bad directions too — for Native nations, 1783 was a turning point toward dispossession.
