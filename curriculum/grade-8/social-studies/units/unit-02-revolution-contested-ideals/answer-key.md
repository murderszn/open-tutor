# Answer Key — Unit 02: Revolution, Independence, Contested Ideals

**For the adult/educator only. Never place beside student questions.**
Scoring: award partial credit for partially correct reasoning; the notes show what "complete" looks like.

## Lesson 1 — Independent practice

1. "No taxation without representation" (accept equivalents): colonists accepted taxes voted by assemblies they elected, but rejected taxes imposed by a Parliament in which they had no elected representatives. Example: the Stamp Act (1765) taxed legal documents and newspapers; colonists boycotted and protested not because the tax was large but because they had no vote on it.
2. Error: the amounts were modest — British subjects in Britain paid higher taxes. The real objection was the constitutional principle: taxation requires the consent of the governed through their representatives.
3. Accept any two well-coded events, e.g.: Boston Tea Party (1773) — change (colonists destroyed property, escalating from boycott to direct action) / continuity (still protesting the same principle of consent); Declaratory Act (1766) — continuity (Parliament kept its total-authority claim) even as the repeal was change. One "both" answer required.

## Lesson 2 — Independent practice

1. Paraphrase must contain both moves: (a) governments are legitimate only with the consent of the governed and to protect unalienable rights (life, liberty, pursuit of happiness); (b) when a government systematically destroys those rights, the people may alter or abolish it.
2. Errors: (a) the Declaration declared independence from Britain — it freed no enslaved person and changed no slave law; (b) it protected slavery by silence, and slavery continued in all 13 states. What it gave: language — "all men are created equal" became an argumentative resource that enslaved petitioners, abolitionists, and later movements quoted back at the nation.
3. Congress deleted Jefferson's anti-slave-trade passage to preserve colonial unity: per Jefferson's later account, South Carolina and Georgia objected because they wanted the trade to continue, and northern delegates went along.

## Lesson 3 — Independent practice

1. Map marks: Saratoga (northern New York, 1777 — Burgoyne's surrender); Yorktown (Virginia coast, 1781 — Cornwallis trapped between Washington and the French fleet); Atlantic route (French money, arms, troops, warships crossing to America). One cause-effect note each, e.g., Saratoga: cause — Burgoyne overextended; effect — France allied.
2. Reasoning error: bravery was not one-sided; wars are decided structurally. Rewrite example: "America won because Britain fought 3,000 miles from home while France's navy and money let the colonies outlast British will."
3. France joined from self-interest: weakening Britain, its great rival, and regaining influence lost in the Seven Years' War — plus, after Saratoga, backing a rebellion that looked winnable. (4-sentence answers should name rivalry and the Saratoga signal.)

## Lesson 4 — Independent practice

1. Example paragraph: Enslaved people joined both sides because each side offered a path to freedom and neither offered a principle of it. Dunmore's Proclamation (1775) promised freedom to those who would bear arms for Britain — hundreds fled to British lines. Wheatley's letter shows the other route: using the patriots' own liberty language as an argument, later echoed in Black freedom petitions. The choice was strategic: which army, which petition, which master was most likely to deliver freedom.
2. Errors: (a) the offer was conditional — only "able and willing to bear Arms" for the king, a military bargain; (b) the proclamation never criticizes slavery as an institution. Dunmore believed in winning the war; freedom was a weapon, not a principle.
3. Accept any well-matched pair, e.g.: Source 1 → "How did enslaved writers use revolutionary ideals?" (Wheatley argues from principle as a formerly enslaved author); Source 2 → "How did wartime strategy create freedom openings?" (Dunmore's offer is evidence of military calculation, not of Black belief).

## Lesson 5 — Independent practice

1. Example: As a Son of Liberty, Revere needed an image that would enrage colonists and recruit support. So his soldiers stand in a neat line firing on command (order, not chaos), his victims are unarmed and innocent, and the Custom House is labeled "Butcher's Hall." What he hides: the snowball-throwing crowd, the disorder, and any British perspective — all of which written accounts record.
2. Errors: (a) "primary" means made at the time by a participant/observer — it describes proximity, not accuracy; this primary source is propaganda by design; (b) accuracy must be checked by comparing multiple sources with attention to each maker's purpose.
3. Accept any well-reasoned missing voice, e.g.: the British soldiers — a soldier's account might record the fear of a surrounding crowd and the order (or lack of order) to fire, which patriot sources omit; reason the source may not exist: soldiers' letters home were private, many lost, and the patriot press had no interest in printing them. Or: Crispus Attucks — we know his name from a casualty list but nothing of his own words; the illiterate and the dead leave no memoirs.

## Lesson 6 — Independent practice

1. Qualified thesis examples: "The Revolution was a turning point toward republican self-government for white men, but a continuity of unfreedom for enslaved people." / "Politically it was a turning point — a new nation on new principles; socially it was mostly continuity until later generations used those principles to force change."
2. Errors: (a) "freest country on earth" is unmeasurable praise — replace with a testable significance claim (e.g., "created the first large modern republic"); (b) it erases those for whom freedom contracted — enslaved people, Native nations. Rewrite must include a qualifier.
3. Accept any three unit sources with a "proves" and a "limit" sentence each, e.g.: the Declaration preamble — proves the stated ideals; limit — the deleted passage shows the ideals were negotiated, not unanimous. Dunmore's Proclamation — proves wartime freedom openings; limit — proves British strategy, not British principle. Revere's engraving — proves patriot persuasion methods; limit — proves nothing about the event itself.

## Practice tasks

**Task A:** Check structure — one dated grievance (e.g., Stamp Act 1765, Townshend 1767, Tea Act 1773); one colonial action (boycott, Stamp Act Congress, Tea Party, Continental Congress); one British response (repeal + Declaratory Act, troops, Coercive Acts); closing want-sentence (repeal with rights secured, or by 1775: independence). Deduct if the paragraph narrates without a grievance.

**Task B:** Each battle needs one earlier cause and one later consequence, e.g.: Lexington/Concord — cause: Coercive Acts and the arms seizure attempt; consequence: Second Continental Congress and the war's start. Saratoga — cause: survival through 1776 + Burgoyne's overreach; consequence: French alliance. Yorktown — cause: French fleet + Washington's march; consequence: end of major fighting, Treaty of Paris. "Turning point" verdict: accept Saratoga (alliance followed) or Yorktown (war ended) with a defended reason; reject Lexington/Concord as the turning point with the note that it *started* the war rather than deciding it.

**Task C:** Two causes and two effects, each tied to a fact. Saratoga example — causes: Burgoyne's supply lines stretched through hostile territory; American forces knew the ground and had survived 1776. Effects: France signed the Treaty of Alliance (Feb 1778); British strategy shifted south. Strongest-link defense must pick one arrow and argue it (e.g., "the alliance link is strongest because without French sea power Yorktown was impossible").

**Task D:** Chart — Wheatley: maker = Phillis Wheatley, formerly enslaved Black poet (Boston, freed 1773); audience = Rev. Samson Occom (Mohegan minister) and newspaper readers; purpose = expose the contradiction between patriot liberty-talk and slaveholding; "freedom" = universal principle God implanted in every breast. Dunmore: maker = Lord Dunmore, royal governor of Virginia; audience = Virginia's enslaved/indentured population (and rebels he hoped to frighten); purpose = gain soldiers and terrorize slaveholding patriots; "freedom" = a conditional military reward. Key phrases: Wheatley's "Cry for Liberty... reverse Disposition for the exercise of oppressive Power"; Dunmore's "free, that are able and willing to bear Arms." Final judgment: Wheatley better shows what enslaved people wanted — she writes from that experience and argues for universal liberty, while Dunmore writes *about* enslaved people as instruments.

**Task E:** Score the prep, not the debate performance: two evidence cards per side, each with a source and a one-sentence use; judgment paragraph with a qualifier (for whom/in what respect); one sentence naming the strongest evidence against the learner's own verdict. Full credit requires the against-verdict sentence to be genuine (not "some might disagree").

**Task F:** Three missing voices, each with: a plausible source kind, two sentences on what it might record, and one reason it may not exist. Example: loyalist farmers — letters or petitions to British officials; might record fear of patriot mobs and economic ruin; many such papers were destroyed or never kept. Award for specificity — "women" alone is not enough; "the wives who ran farms while husbands fought — account books, letters" is.

**Task G:** 1. Account A claim: the Revolution birthed modern democracy (evidence: constitutions, elections, Declaration's influence). Account B claim: the Revolution preserved hierarchies under new management (evidence: slavery's expansion, women's exclusion, Native dispossession). 2. A uses/emphasizes founding documents and institutional change; downplays who was excluded. B uses/emphasizes demographic and economic outcomes; downplays the novelty of republican government. 3. Full credit: the accounts mostly disagree about *which question matters* (political founding vs. social outcomes) while sharing most facts — with one example (e.g., both agree slavery continued; they differ on whether that fact defines the Revolution).

**Task H:** Score with the project rubric's Argument row: qualified thesis; genuine counterclaim with evidence; 3+ unit sources (at least one primary excerpt); explicit limit passage naming what the evidence cannot establish (not "more research is needed" — a specific unprovable claim, e.g., "my sources show what leaders argued, not what most farmers believed"). No new uncited factual claims in the conclusion — deduct if the conclusion introduces new facts.

## Formative quiz

**Part A (16):** 1. Order: Stamp Act (1765), Boston Massacre (1770), Boston Tea Party (1773), Lexington and Concord (1775) — 1 pt each. 2. "No taxation without representation": taxes require consent through elected representatives; one specific tax named (4; partial 2 for the slogan without the mechanism). 3. Coding: full credit for "both" with defense — change: colonial pressure forced repeal (boycotts worked); continuity: the Declaratory Act kept Parliament's total-authority claim (4; 2 for one-sided coding). 4. Two action→response pairs, e.g., Townshend Acts → boycotts; Tea Act → Tea Party → Coercive Acts (2 pts per pair).

**Part B (13):** 1. British: professional army/navy, greater wealth, (global empire). Colonial: distance/geography, fighting on home ground, only needing to avoid losing, French aid. 1 pt each (4). 2. Chain links: Saratoga → *convinced France the Americans could win* → French alliance → *provided money, arms, troops, and a navy* → Yorktown. 2 pts per linking sentence (4). 3. Correction must note: France's money/arms/troops/navy were decisive (Yorktown was a Franco-American trap), and "single-handedly" erases the alliance (5; partial 3 for one fact).

**Part C (14):** 1. Quotation or close paraphrase + what it argues, e.g., the "Love of Freedom" passage argues liberty is a universal God-implanted principle (3; 1 for the quote alone). 2. Purpose: military strategy — gain soldiers and frighten slaveholding rebels (2); why it matters: the offer is evidence of wartime calculation, not of principle, so it cannot prove British anti-slavery sentiment (2). 3. Wheatley better: she writes from enslaved experience and argues for universal liberty (vs. Dunmore writing *about* enslaved people as instruments); two distinct reasons required (4; 2 for one). 4. One missing group + one plausible recorded detail, e.g., loyalists — petitions recording fear of patriot committees (3; 1 for the group alone).

## Unit assessment (52)

**§1 (24):** 1. Six events in order with years ±1 accepted (1 pt each). 2. Two codings with reasons (3 pts each: 1 for the code, 2 for the reason). 3. Two structural causes with explanation — e.g., distance/supply lines; French alliance; war-of-attrition logic (3 pts per cause). 4. Paraphrase containing consent-of-the-governed + right to alter/abolish (6; partial 3 for one move).

**§2 (14):** 5. Per source: maker (1), audience (1), purpose (1). Wheatley: formerly enslaved Black poet / Occom + newspaper readers / expose the liberty/slavery contradiction. Dunmore: royal governor of Virginia / enslaved & indentured Virginians (+ rebels) / gain soldiers, frighten patriots. 6. "Can establish": the pair proves that liberty language was *used* by both a Black writer (as universal principle) and a British official (as military bargain), and that wartime conditions created real freedom openings people acted on (4). Limit: two sources cannot establish what most enslaved people believed or experienced — Wheatley is one extraordinary voice, Dunmore writes about them instrumentally; corroboration across more sources is required (4).

**§3 essay (14):** Per the project rubric's Argument row. A strong "turning point" essay cites the republic's novelty + the Declaration's afterlife, with a genuine counterclaim on slavery/women/Native continuity. A strong "not a turning point" essay cites the rationed liberty with evidence, with a genuine counterclaim conceding real self-government. The limit passage must name something specific the evidence cannot prove. Deduct 2 for a strawman counterclaim, 2 for fewer than three cited sources, 2 for a vague limit.
