# Unit 02 — Revolution, Independence, Contested Ideals

**Grade 8 Social Studies · Weeks 7–10 · 20 sessions (~45 minutes each)**
**Track:** [Grade 8 Social Studies](../../README.md) · [Scope and sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md) · Issue #41

## Essential question

Whose liberty did the American Revolution secure — and how do we know?

## Overview

Between 1763 and 1783, thirteen British colonies argued, boycotted, fought, and finally broke away to form a new nation. The Revolution was not one event but a sequence: new taxes and the colonial slogan "no taxation without representation," street confrontations like the Boston Massacre (March 5, 1770), the destruction of taxed tea (December 16, 1773), war beginning at Lexington and Concord (April 19, 1775), the Declaration of Independence (July 4, 1776), a French alliance after Saratoga (1777), and British surrender at Yorktown (October 19, 1781).

The ideals were sweeping — "all men are created equal" — and the practice was contested from the start. Jefferson's draft condemned the slave trade; Congress deleted the passage. Enslaved people seized wartime promises of freedom (Dunmore's Proclamation, November 1775) and petitioned in the language of liberty. Abigail Adams urged her husband to "remember the Ladies." Native nations weighed which empire to back, and many lost either way. Paul Revere's famous engraving of the Massacre was persuasive propaganda, not a photograph — perspective shaped what the record shows.

Learners in this unit:

- sequence 1763–1783 events on a timeline and classify them as change or continuity;
- close-read adult-selected public-domain excerpts (the Declaration; Wheatley's 1774 letter; Dunmore's Proclamation) and explain how the same ideals served different claims;
- compare patriot, loyalist, enslaved, women's, and Native perspectives — and analyze how perspective shaped the sources each left behind;
- evaluate source credibility and name what the record cannot prove;
- argue, with claim, counterclaim, evidence, and stated limits, whether the Revolution was a turning point.

This unit assumes the U01 habits (claim + evidence + limit) and adds source criticism: who made this, for whom, and what does their position hide?

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, learners will be able to:

1. **Sequence the road to independence** — place at least eight 1763–1783 events on a timeline and classify each as an example of change or continuity, with reasons (D2.His.1.6-8; D2.His.2.6-8).
2. **Explain the Declaration's ideals and their contested reach** — paraphrase the preamble's argument, cite two grievances, and explain how the deleted slavery passage and the "all men are created equal" line were used by different groups (D2.Civ.8.6-8; D1.4.6-8).
3. **Analyze multiple perspectives and their sources** — compare how patriots, loyalists, enslaved people, women, and Native nations experienced the Revolution, and explain how each group's perspective shaped what their sources show and hide (D2.His.4.6-8; D2.His.6.6-8).
4. **Evaluate sources and detect record limits** — judge a source's credibility for a stated purpose and name at least two limits in the historical record (whose voices are missing; what an image cannot prove) (D3.2.6-8; D2.His.10.6-8; D2.His.11.6-8).
5. **Argue significance with acknowledged limits** — write an argument on whether the Revolution was a turning point that states a claim and a genuine counterclaim, cites at least three sources, and names what the evidence cannot establish (D2.His.3.6-8; D3.3.6-8; D4.1.6-8 — first supported practice of the full argument the crosswalk sequences in U03).

## Prerequisites

From U01: placing events on a timeline; reading a scaled map; claim + evidence + one stated limit (D3.3–3.4.6-8 habits); identifying a primary source's maker, audience, and purpose (D2.His.11.6-8). From grade 7: comparing two accounts of one event. Learners should be comfortable with the U01 vocabulary of primary sources and evidence limits.

## Vocabulary

**Representation** — having a voice in the body that makes the laws you must obey. **Boycott** — refusing to buy goods as political pressure. **Repeal** — the official cancellation of a law. **Grievance** — a stated complaint of wrongdoing. **Unalienable rights** — rights that cannot rightfully be taken away. **Patriot** — a colonist who supported independence. **Loyalist** — a colonist who remained loyal to Britain. **Militia** — citizen-soldiers called up for local defense. **Alliance** — a formal agreement between nations to act together. **Turning point** — an event after which the direction of developments changes decisively. **Propaganda** — persuasive communication designed to shape opinion, often by selective or distorted presentation. **Proclamation** — an official public announcement. **Petition** — a formal written request, often signed by many. **Historical significance** — the judgment that a person or development mattered, and why. **Continuity** — what stayed the same across a period; **change** — what was different afterward.
