# Answer Key — Unit 05: Industry, Reform, Abolition, and Social Change

**Adult only.** Keep this file separate from student pages. Every item below
was solved independently of the student wording; where a student answer may
legitimately vary, the key states the acceptable range and the reasoning that
must be present.

## Lesson 1 items

- **Guided 1:** Four changes with figures: Erie Canal — 363 miles, cost
  $7,143,000, completed October 26, 1825. Steamboat — Fulton's *Clermont*,
  first commercial run 1807. Railroad — spread in the 1830s–1840s, mostly
  east–west. Telegraph — first public message May 24, 1844, "What hath God
  wrought," Washington to Baltimore.
- **Guided 2:** A supply change: cheaper transport means more wheat can
  reach New York buyers, so the supply available to them rises.
- **Guided 3:** The price of raw cotton fell (cleaning got cheaper) while
  the demand for enslaved labor rose — cheaper cloth meant more buyers,
  more cotton needed, more enslaved workers forced to grow it.
- **Guided 4:** The telegraph carried prices and news instantly, so a
  merchant could learn a price in another city and buy or sell the same
  day, without waiting for a letter or a ship.
- **Independent 1 (table):** Canal — goods and people moved cheaper,
  363 miles / 1825 / $7,143,000 / freight down about 90%. Steamboat —
  upstream river travel, *Clermont* 1807. Railroad — overland freight and
  passengers, 1830s–1840s east–west lines. Telegraph — information,
  May 24, 1844, about 38–40 miles of wire.
- **Independent 2:** (a) A western farmer could ship wheat east cheaply
  and sell to distant buyers. (b) An enslaved person faced greater demand
  for cotton labor: more land planted, more people forced to work it.
  (c) The gin made cleaning cheap, so cloth got cheaper, more people
  bought it, and the demand for the raw cotton — and the enslaved labor
  that grew it — rose.
- **Independent 3 (error analysis):** Wrong (a) because the canal cost
  $7,143,000, paid by New York taxpayers — someone paid. Wrong (b)
  because the gains were uneven: mill workers paid with noise, dust, and
  73-hour weeks, and enslaved people paid as the gin expanded slavery.
  "Cheaper for everyone" ignores who paid the external costs.
- **Application (Albany letter):** Accept any five-sentence letter with
  the five required sentences (what changed; one verified canal figure;
  what the writer can now buy or sell; who loses; what to ask the state),
  labeled a modern reconstruction.

## Lesson 2 items

- **Guided 1:** Shows: power looms, tall windows, belts and pulleys,
  baskets of bobbins, workers' plain dress and aprons. Hides: noise,
  cotton dust, the 12–14-hour day, boarding-house curfew and church
  rules, wage cuts, the boarding-house keeper's authority.
- **Guided 2:** $3.00 − $1.50 = $1.50 left after board. Half sent home =
  $0.75. She keeps $0.75.
- **Guided 3:** Corporations preferred young farm women because they
  were cheaper than men, were seen as easier to control, and left after
  a few years (most averaged about three years) — a steady supply of new,
  low-paid workers.
- **Guided 4:** *Commonwealth v. Hunt* made organizing lawful in
  principle — a union was not automatically a criminal conspiracy. It did
  not make unions strong, end employer opposition, or make judges
  friendly; strikes stayed risky.
- **Independent 1 (table):** Hours — about 73 a week (12–14 a day plus a
  half day Saturday). Wages — $1.85–$3.00 a week. Board — $1.25–$1.50 a
  week deducted. 1834 turnout — about 800 women walked out over a wage
  cut. 1836 turnout — about 1,500 walked out over a rise in board price.
- **Independent 2:** (a) A farm family sent a daughter because she earned
  cash wages — the highest paid to women in America — and sent much of it
  home to pay debts or school brothers. (b) A mill girl joined the 1836
  turnout because the board increase cut her take-home pay without any
  wage rise. (c) Corporations owned both the mill and the boarding house,
  set wages, board, and rules, and controlled hiring and honorable
  discharge — so a fired worker could not easily work elsewhere.
- **Independent 3 (error analysis):** Wrong (a) because "best wages for
  women" were still small after board was deducted (as little as 35 cents
  a week left at the low end). Wrong (b) because the workers' own actions
  — the 1834 and 1836 turnouts, the ten-hour campaign, the *Lowell
  Offering* — show they judged conditions harsh, not happy.
- **Application (mill girl letter):** Accept any six-sentence letter with
  the six required sentences (wage and board; mill room; why she walked
  out; what she risked; what the turnout won or did not win; stay or go
  home), labeled a modern reconstruction, signed "your sister" (no real
  person's name).

## Lesson 3 items

- **Guided 1:** Temperance — persuasion, then law. Common schools —
  institution-building. Asylums — investigation and petition. Women's
  rights — organization and argument (convention).
- **Guided 2:** Dix traveled first to gather documented evidence: a
  petition without named facts is easy for a legislature to ignore; with
  300 jails and 500 almshouses documented, the claim could not be
  dismissed as one bad case.
- **Guided 3:** Persuasion could not build the school system because
  families who saw no use for school would not pay voluntarily, and a
  system needs steady public money, buildings, and trained teachers —
  only taxation and the state could provide those at scale.
- **Guided 4:** The Maine Law of 1851 banned *manufacture* as well as
  sale: banning sale alone left sellers supplied; banning manufacture cut
  the supply itself.
- **Independent 1 (table):** Temperance — American Temperance Society,
  persuasion then law, Maine Law 1851 / 13 states by 1855. Common schools
  — Horace Mann, institution-building, first normal schools and *Common
  School Journal*, copied by most northern states. Asylums — Dorothea
  Dix, investigation and petition, 6 hospitals helped by 1845. Women's
  rights — Stanton and Mott, convention and argument, Declaration of
  Sentiments 1848.
- **Independent 2:** (a) Print carried arguments to people reformers
  never met: the *Liberator*, the *Common School Journal*, Dix's
  Memorial, the Declaration of Sentiments. (b) Reformers disagreed: Mott
  vs. Stanton on suffrage; Garrison vs. Douglass on politics; temperance
  and abolitionist priorities clashed. (c) Institution-building is slower
  because it needs laws, taxes, buildings, and trained staff — years of
  work — but it lasts because it does not depend on one person's
  persuasion continuing.
- **Independent 3 (error analysis):** Wrong (a) because reformers
  disagreed sharply — Mott was barred in London in 1840, Garrison split
  with Douglass over politics, and temperance leaders quarreled with
  abolitionists. Wrong (b) because "kind" is not the test: they were
  strategic organizers using different methods, and some of their actions
  (Garrison burning the Constitution, Dix's blunt memorial) were
  confrontational, not merely kind.
- **Application (reform plan):** Accept any five-sentence plan with the
  five required sentences (problem; method; who must act; what the method
  cannot reach; what to do at that limit), labeled a modern exercise.

## Lesson 4 items

- **Guided 1:** Accept any paraphrase that keeps the idea (some truths
  need no proof; women and men are equally human; that equality gives
  both the same basic rights, which no government may take away) and
  changes the words. Reject near-copies that only swap one or two words.
- **Guided 2:** Any two of: no vote; married women could not own property
  or sign contracts; unequal access to education; no place in the church;
  unequal pay for equal work; no legal standing apart from a husband.
- **Guided 3:** Mott thought demanding the vote was too radical: it would
  make the convention look ridiculous and sink the other demands.
- **Guided 4:** Douglass argued the vote is the foundation of all other
  rights. It mattered that he said it because he was the convention's
  only African American attendee and a leading abolitionist — his support
  linked the women's cause to the antislavery cause.
- **Independent 1 (table):** Dates — July 19–20, 1848. Place — Wesleyan
  Chapel, Seneca Falls, New York. Attendance — about 300, including about
  40 men. Principal author — Elizabeth Cady Stanton (with Mary Ann
  M'Clintock). Signers — 100 (68 women, 32 men). Most contested
  resolution — women's suffrage. Who argued for it — Frederick Douglass.
- **Independent 2:** (a) Borrowing strategy: Stanton used the nation's
  own language and principles, so the reader's own promise had to answer
  for leaving women out. (b) Suffrage touched power itself — the power to
  make the laws — so even supporters feared it. (c) 100 signatures meant
  a public commitment by a third of those present; they did not prove
  majority support and they changed no law.
- **Independent 3 (error analysis):** Wrong (a) because a convention can
  demand but not grant: only a legislature or constitutional amendment
  could give the vote. Wrong (b) because American women did not win the
  vote nationally until the 19th Amendment in 1920 — 72 years later.
- **Application (delegate speech):** Accept any six-sentence speech with
  the six required sentences (doubt; opening paraphrase or quotation;
  Mott's worry fairly; Douglass's argument fairly; vote; reason), labeled
  a modern reconstruction.

## Lesson 5 items

- **Guided 1:** Gradualism: end slavery slowly, over time, often with
  colonization. Immediatism: end it at once, with full civil rights.
- **Guided 2:** David Walker — the 1829 *Appeal*. Nat Turner — the 1831
  rebellion. Frederick Douglass — the 1845 *Narrative* and the 1847
  *North Star*.
- **Guided 3:** Garrison's method was moral suasion through the press,
  outside politics. Douglass later argued for using the Constitution and
  politics against slavery — the position Garrison rejected.
- **Guided 4:** The gag rule (1836–1844) automatically tabled
  antislavery petitions without reading them. It removed petitioners'
  voices — including thousands of women, who could not vote but could
  petition.
- **Independent 1 (table):** Walker's *Appeal* — 1829; called for
  resistance, frightened Southern legislatures, was banned. Turner's
  rebellion — August 1831; killed about 55 white people, was crushed, and
  brought harsher laws. *The Liberator* — January 1, 1831; Garrison's
  weekly, the movement's bulletin board. AASS — December 1833,
  Philadelphia; national organization. Gag rule — 1836–1844; House
  auto-tabled petitions. *North Star* — December 3, 1847; Douglass and
  Delany's Black-led paper. *Uncle Tom's Cabin* — 1852; Stowe's novel
  carried the argument into millions of homes.
- **Independent 2:** (a) Southern states banned abolitionist mail because
  it was reaching readers — including enslaved people — and spreading
  resistance. (b) The gag rule hurt beyond the enslaved: it struck at the
  petition right itself, silencing women and other citizens who could not
  vote. (c) Garrison's circulation of about 3,000 mattered because his
  readers were the movement's leaders; influence is not measured in
  copies alone.
- **Independent 3 (error analysis):** Wrong (a) because Black people led
  abolition — Walker, Douglass, Tubman, Truth, Stewart — and white
  allies joined a fight already under way. Wrong (b) because "freed the
  slaves" is false: enslaved people freed themselves by escaping and
  resisting, and slavery ended through war and constitutional amendment,
  not by white abolitionists alone.
- **Application (1836 editorial):** Accept any five-sentence editorial
  with the five required sentences (what the gag rule did; why petitioning
  matters; Garrison's line quoted or paraphrased; one risk of printing;
  decision and reason), labeled a modern reconstruction.

## Lesson 6 items

- **Guided 1:** Conductor — a guide who led escapers along the route.
  Station — a safe house where escapers hid and rested. Passenger — a
  person escaping slavery.
- **Guided 2:** The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 let owners seize suspected
  fugitives in free states with few legal protections, so Tubman began
  guiding people all the way to Canada.
- **Guided 3:** Without escapers there were no passengers and no urgency;
  without organizers there were no safe routes, stations, or newspapers.
  Each half made the other possible.
- **Guided 4:** Two versions of Truth's Akron speech survive; the famous
  "Ain't I a Woman?" wording comes from an 1875 retelling, not a
  transcript, so the unit does not quote it as her exact words.
- **Independent 1 (table):** Escaped — 1849. Trips back — about 13.
  People led — about 70. People helped with instructions — 50–60 more.
  Her own words — "I never ran my train off the track and I never lost
  a passenger." What the 1850 Act changed — routes had to continue to
  Canada.
- **Independent 2:** (a) Enslaved resistance made organizing possible:
  escapes proved slavery could not hold people who refused it and gave
  the Railroad passengers. (b) Organizing made resistance possible:
  stations, routes, and newspapers gave escapers somewhere safe to go.
  (c) The Fugitive Slave Act is evidence the Railroad was working:
  Congress acted because escapes were succeeding at a scale that alarmed
  slaveholders.
- **Independent 3 (error analysis):** Wrong (a) because the network
  existed since the late 1700s, decades before Tubman. Wrong (b) because
  what Tubman did was conduct at unmatched scale and courage — about 13
  trips, about 70 people led — not invent the network.
- **Application (station keeper plan):** Accept any six-sentence plan with
  the six required sentences (first action; hiding; the 1850 Act's risk;
  who else must help; what to tell passengers about Canada; why take the
  risk), labeled a modern reconstruction. No escape role-play is required
  or accepted as a substitute.

## Practice items

- **Task A1:** 40 tons × $100 = $4,000 before the canal. 40 tons × $10 =
  $400 after. Saving: $4,000 − $400 = $3,600, a cut of 90%.
- **Task A2:** $7,143,000 ÷ 363 = $19,677.69…, about **$19,678 per
  mile**. The canal was expensive to build, and worth it because tolls
  repaid the cost within about nine years and it remade trade: freight
  fell about 90% and New York became the nation's busiest port.
- **Task A3:** (a) Week 1 → Week 2: supply rose (100 → 160 yards), price
  fell ($4.00 → $3.00). (b) Week 3 → Week 4: supply fell (160 → 90
  yards), price rose ($3.00 → $4.50). The canal matches (a): cheaper
  transport increased the supply reaching eastern buyers, so flour prices
  fell.
- **Task A4:** The gin made cleaning cotton cheap, so cloth got cheaper
  and more people bought it; that raised the demand for raw cotton, so
  planters grew more cotton — with enslaved labor. One invention lowered
  one price and raised the demand for something else.
- **Task B1:** Ana: $2.00 − $1.25 = $0.75 left; half home $0.375, about
  38 cents; keeps about 38 cents. Beth: $3.00 − $1.50 = $1.50 left; half
  home $0.75; keeps $0.75. Cara: $1.85 − $1.50 = $0.35 left; half home
  $0.175, about 18 cents; keeps about 18 cents.
- **Task B2:** No. Cara has $0.35 after board and needs $0.50 — she is
  $0.15 short, so she cannot send that much and keep anything.
- **Task B3:** 73 ÷ 5.5 ≈ 13.3 hours a day. A modern 40-hour week is 8
  hours a day over 5 days — the mill girl worked about 33 more hours a
  week, nearly double.
- **Task B4:** Corporations owned the mill and the boarding house, set
  wages, board, and rules, and controlled hiring and honorable discharge.
  A turnout was risky because a striker could lose wages, be fired, and
  be unable to get hired at another Lowell mill without a discharge.
- **Task C1:** Temperance — problem: alcohol's harm to families and work;
  method: persuasion, then law; who: drinkers, sellers, legislatures;
  result: Maine Law 1851, 13 states by 1855. Common schools — problem:
  few public schools, untrained teachers; method: institution-building;
  who: state legislature and taxpayers; result: Massachusetts system,
  normal schools, copied by most northern states. Asylums — problem:
  mentally ill people chained in jails and almshouses; method:
  investigation and petition; who: state legislatures; result: 6
  hospitals helped by 1845, Massachusetts law. Women's rights — problem:
  women denied vote, property, education; method: convention,
  organization, argument; who: women themselves and allies; result:
  Declaration of Sentiments, 1848.
- **Task C2:** (a) Maker: Dorothea Dix. (b) Audience: the Massachusetts
  Legislature. (c) Purpose: persuade the legislature to build separate
  hospitals for the insane. (d) Limit: it is an advocate's document — it
  shows what Dix saw and wanted changed, and may select the worst cases;
  it is not a neutral survey of every asylum.
- **Task C3:** Persuasion achieved voluntary change — 170,000 members
  signed pledges and drinking fell — without coercion. Law achieved what
  persuasion could not: reaching the unwilling — drinkers who would not
  sign and sellers who profited — by banning sale and manufacture.
- **Task C4:** Institution-building takes longer because it needs laws,
  taxes, buildings, and trained teachers — years of work — while a pledge
  needs only a meeting. It can last longer because it does not depend on
  one person's persuasion continuing.
- **Task D1:** (a) Accept any paraphrase keeping the idea (some truths
  need no proof; women and men are equally human with the same basic
  rights). (b) Accept any paraphrase keeping the idea (men's treatment of
  women is a long list of wrongs meant to give men total power over
  women). Reject near-copies.
- **Task D2:** Charging "man" — laws and customs — instead of one king
  makes the argument harder to answer, because there is no single villain
  to remove; and harder to enforce, because the fix requires changing many
  laws and customs, not winning one war.
- **Task D3:** Mott's worry fairly stated (too radical; would discredit
  the convention and sink the other demands). Douglass's argument fairly
  stated (the vote is the foundation of all other rights). Own judgment
  with a reason — accept either side if the reason is tied to the lesson.
- **Task D4:** Signing was a public commitment to a radical demand in
  1848. Non-signers might have feared public backlash or losing work, or
  agreed with some demands but not suffrage, or thought the convention
  too radical. Any two plausible reasons with "might."
- **Task E1 (order):** Walker *Appeal* 1829 → *Liberator* January 1,
  1831 → Turner rebellion August 1831 → American Anti-Slavery Society
  December 1833 → gag rule begins 1836 → gag rule ends 1844 → *North
  Star* December 3, 1847 → *Uncle Tom's Cabin* 1852. One line of
  significance each; accept reasonable significance tied to Lesson 5.
- **Task E2:** (a) gradualism — ends slavery for a future generation,
  not for the living. (b) immediatism — ends it now, with full rights.
  (c) colonization — sends freed people away; assumes they cannot belong.
  (d) immediatism by political means — ends it now through law and the
  Constitution. (e) immediatism by moral suasion only — ends it now by
  argument, never politics. Garrison held (b) and (e) together; Douglass
  came to hold (d), which Garrison rejected.
- **Task E3:** Southern states banned abolitionist mail because it was
  reaching readers — including enslaved people — and spreading resistance.
  Banning it shows the movement was working: governments do not ban what
  nobody reads.
- **Task F:** Accept any complete five-row trace with a problem, action,
  opposition, result, and a named cost-bearer, plus two sentences naming
  the main lever and what it failed to reach. Score the completeness and
  the honesty of the cost row, not the choice of movement.
- **Task G:** Source 1 — maker: Frederick Douglass. Audience: the
  Rochester audience (white Northerners celebrating July 4th). Purpose:
  expose the hypocrisy of celebrating liberty while slavery continued.
  Limit: one man's speech — it shows Douglass's argument, not enslaved
  people's own experience of the day. Source 2 — maker: William Lloyd
  Garrison (via the *Liberator*). Audience: abolitionist readers.
  Purpose: argue for immediate emancipation and against political action.
  Limit: it is a paraphrase, not Garrison's words, and it states his
  position, not the movement's consensus. More useful for "why did
  abolitionists disagree about method": Source 2 — it states Garrison's
  anti-politics position, which is exactly what Douglass later rejected.
  The paraphrase label matters because the words are the unit's summary,
  not Garrison's; quoting them as his would fabricate a quotation.
- **Task H:** Check only fairness of the counterclaim and reality of the
  limit. The counterclaim must be one a supporter of the other side would
  accept as fair; the limit must name something the unit's evidence
  genuinely does not show.
