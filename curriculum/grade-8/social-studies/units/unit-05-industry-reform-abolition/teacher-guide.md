# Teacher Guide — Unit 05: Industry, Reform, Abolition, and Social Change

This guide is for the guiding adult. It is not student material. Keep it separate
from student pages when assigning work.

## Week-by-week notes

### Week 20 — The market revolution

- **S1 (Lesson 1):** Do the canal freight arithmetic on paper with the
  learner, step by step ($100 → $10 a ton; 40 tons = $4,000 → $400 in
  Task A1). The numbers are the lesson's anchors; learners who miss the
  90% cut will miss the whole point of the market revolution.
- **S2 (Task A):** The fictional dataset is labeled fictional — check the
  label out loud before starting. The most common error is calling the
  Week 3 → Week 4 price rise a demand change; it is a supply *fall*
  (160 → 90 yards). Ask "did supply move or did demand move?" before
  accepting any answer.
- **S3 (innovation case + schematic):** The Erie Canal schematic is a
  learner-drawn copy of the SVG, labeled "not to scale." Check the labels
  (Albany, Buffalo, Hudson, Lake Erie, 363 miles, 1825) before accepting
  it. For the telegraph case, stress the funder: Congress paid $30,000 —
  innovation here was publicly funded, not purely private enterprise.
- **S4 (Lesson 2):** Show the mill illustration with its caption and the
  "reconstruction, not evidence" label every time. If the learner asks
  what the image proves, answer: it shows what a mill room looked like in
  general; it proves nothing about any particular mill, worker, or day,
  and it hides the noise, dust, hours, and rules — that absence is the
  activity's point.
- **S5 (quiz Part A):** Q4 (supply vs. demand) is the most-missed item;
  re-teach with the Task A dataset if missed. Q7 (turnout years 1834,
  1836) is often confused with the 1840s ten-hour campaign; keep the
  decades separate.

### Week 21 — Reform and its methods

- **S1 (Lesson 3):** Teach the methods, not the biographies. The matrix
  (Task C1) is the week's core: if a learner can name four movements but
  not their methods, the lesson has not landed.
- **S2 (Task C):** Read the Dix quotation aloud. The close read's limit
  (C2d) is often missed: a memorial is an advocate's document — it shows
  what Dix saw and wanted changed, not a neutral survey of every asylum.
  That is exactly why it is powerful and why it is limited.
- **S3 (Lesson 4):** Model the paraphrase aloud (worked example 1)
  before assigning Task D1. Learners' first paraphrases are nearly always
  near-copies of the original; require at least three words changed and
  the idea kept.
- **S4 (deliberation):** Accept any ranking of methods with reasons;
  score the reasoning, not the ranking. The point is that methods have
  different limits, not that one method always wins.
- **S5 (quiz Part B):** Q8 (Seneca Falls did not give women the vote) is
  the most-missed item; re-teach the 1848 vs. 1920 gap if missed. It
  returns in the assessment Part 2 Q7's framing.

### Week 22 — Abolition and Black agency

- **S1 (Lesson 5):** Preview before reading (see sensitive-content note).
  Stress the leadership point twice: Black people led abolition — as
  writers, organizers, speakers, and escapers. If a learner retells the
  lesson as a white rescue story, send them back to the table in
  independent practice 1.
- **S2 (Task E):** The timeline has eight events; check the order before
  the sort. The most common error is placing the gag rule (1836) before
  the AASS founding (1833). The E2 sort's (d) and (e) are the point:
  Garrison held immediatism *and* moral-suasion-only politics; Douglass
  came to hold political action through the Constitution.
- **S3 (Lesson 6):** Model the two-versions reasoning aloud (worked
  example 2) before assigning Task G. Learners often want one version to
  be "the true one"; the lesson is that both versions are evidence for
  different questions.
- **S4 (Task G):** The paraphrase label matters: learners must not quote
  Source 2 as if it were Garrison's words. Check the "paraphrase, not
  quotation" label on every answer.
- **S5 (quiz Part C):** Give the Week 23 sensitive-content preview here
  (see below) so the learner is not surprised by project presentations
  that touch enslavement.

### Week 23 — Synthesis

- **S1 (project §2 due):** Check the organizer's source column first; a
  row without a source is incomplete even if the figure is right. The
  take-home calculation must show the subtraction.
- **S2 (Task F + project §3 draft):** The trace's "Cost — who paid it?"
  row is the point. If a learner writes only winners, send them back for
  the losers.
- **S3 (presentations):** Adult-review every presentation before any
  external sharing; nothing is mailed or posted without the adult. Peer
  critique must name one strength *and* one weakness; "it was good" is
  not a critique.
- **S4 (argument assembly):** Check Task H fairness before the essay is
  written. Learners' first counterclaims are nearly always straw men; the
  worked example in Lesson 6 exists to prevent that.
- **S5 (unit assessment):** Score Part 3 (Douglass source task) for
  maker/audience/purpose/limit discipline, not for eloquence. Part 4's
  argument is scored on claim, fair counterclaim, two sources, two
  perspectives, and one stated limit — the same five elements as project
  §4.

## Sensitive-content preview

- **Enslavement (Lessons 5–6, project §3, assessment Part 3):** The unit
  names killings (Nat Turner: about 55 white people killed; Turner and
  more than fifty others executed), the Fugitive Slave Act's dangers, and
  the daily risks of escape. Keep the discussion factual and brief. Do not
  ask learners to imagine being enslaved, hunted, or captured, and do not
  role-play escapes, captures, or auctions.
- **Asylum conditions (Lesson 3, Task C):** Dix's Memorial describes
  people chained, kept in cages and cellars, and beaten. Name the
  conditions once, factually, and move on. Do not ask learners to imagine
  themselves in a cell.
- **Child labor (Lesson 2):** Harriet Hanson began mill work at about
  ten. State the fact once; do not ask learners to role-play a child
  worker.
- **General rule:** if any part of the unit feels heavy, the learner may
  pause and return with the adult. The project repeats this rule in §3.

## Misconception alerts

- "The market revolution helped everyone." The cotton gin is the
  counterexample: a productivity gain that deepened slavery. Ask *who
  gained and who paid* for every change.
- "The mill girls were happy because wages were high." Wages were the
  highest for women in America *and* small after board; hours were about
  73 a week; the turnouts are the workers' own verdict.
- "Reformers all agreed." They quarreled — Mott vs. Stanton on suffrage,
  Garrison vs. Douglass on politics, temperance vs. abolitionist
  priorities. Disagreement inside a movement is normal and worth
  teaching.
- "Seneca Falls gave women the vote." The convention demanded the vote;
  the 19th Amendment came in 1920, and the fight over who could actually
  vote continued after that.
- "White abolitionists freed the slaves." Black leadership — Walker,
  Douglass, Tubman, Truth, Stewart — was central, and enslaved people
  freed themselves by escaping and resisting. White allies joined a fight
  already under way.
- "Harriet Tubman invented the Underground Railroad." The network
  predated her by decades; she made it work at unmatched scale and
  courage.
- "The famous Truth quotation is exact." Two versions survive; the famous
  wording is an 1875 retelling. Famous is not the same as accurate.

## Reteaching moves

- **Supply/demand confusion:** return to Task A3's labeled dataset and
  ask only "did supply move or did demand move?" until the learner
  answers that question first, every time.
- **Take-home pay errors:** re-do Lesson 2's worked example 1 on paper,
  then Task B1 row by row; the common slip is forgetting to deduct board
  before halving.
- **Method vs. cause confusion:** have the learner cover the "movement"
  column of Task C1 and reconstruct it from the "method" column alone.
- **Straw-man counterclaims:** require the counterclaim to be stated so
  well that a supporter of the other side would accept it as fair; if the
  learner cannot do that, model one aloud from Lesson 6.
- **Source evaluation:** if Task G's limits are vague ("the source is
  biased"), require a specific limit tied to maker, date, or audience.

## Readiness signals

The learner is ready for U06 when they can: (a) state the Erie Canal's
figures and one price effect without notes; (b) compute a mill worker's
take-home pay; (c) name two reform methods and one limit of each;
(d) paraphrase the Declaration of Sentiments' opening; (e) distinguish
gradualism from immediatism; and (f) write a claim with a fair
counterclaim and a stated limit. If (a) or (b) is missing, re-teach Week
20 before starting U06. If (f) is missing, re-teach Task H before the
U06 argument work begins.
