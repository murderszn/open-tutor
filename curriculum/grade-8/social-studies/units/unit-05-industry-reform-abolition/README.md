# Unit 05 — Industry, Reform, Abolition, and Social Change

**Grade 8 Social Studies · Weeks 20–23 · 20 sessions (~45 minutes each)**
**Track:** [Grade 8 Social Studies](../../README.md) · [Scope and sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md) · Issue #41

## Essential question

New machines, new markets, and new reformers changed American life — who
gained, who paid, and who got to decide what changed next?

## Overview

Between about 1820 and 1850, the United States became a different kind of
country. Canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph tied distant
places together and made goods, people, and news move faster and cheaper
than ever before. Factories replaced household workshops: in Lowell,
Massachusetts, young women from farm families tended power looms for cash
wages, lived under company rules, and then organized some of the first
strikes in American history. The same decades produced a wave of reformers
who looked at the new society and demanded it do better — temperance
societies against alcohol, Horace Mann for public schools, Dorothea Dix for
asylums, and, most consequentially, abolitionists against slavery and
women's-rights advocates at Seneca Falls. And enslaved people themselves
resisted every day: through the Underground Railroad, through petitions and
newspapers, and through the speeches of Frederick Douglass and Sojourner
Truth.

This unit teaches the market revolution and the reform movements as one
story with two questions running through it: *who benefits?* and *how does
change happen?* Learners compare the levers reformers pulled — persuasion,
petition and law, institution-building, organization, and direct action —
and judge which lever worked, when, and for whom. They learn that the same
change could be progress for one group and loss for another: the cotton gin
made cotton cheap and slavery bigger; the telegraph made news instant and
abolitionist newspapers national; the factory gave young women wages and
then took their hours, health, and freedom to choose.

Learners in this unit:

- explain how canals, steam, railroads, and the telegraph changed prices,
  distances, and who could sell what to whom — with the Erie Canal's
  verified freight figures and a labeled practice dataset for supply and
  demand;
- analyze factory labor at Lowell from the workers' side: wages, hours,
  boarding-house rules, strikes, and the 1842 court decision that said
  unions were not automatically a crime;
- compare four reform movements by method, not just by cause, and trace one
  reform from problem to action to result;
- read the Declaration of Sentiments as an argument built on the
  Declaration of Independence, and explain why its suffrage resolution was
  the hardest fight in the room;
- explain abolitionism as a movement with Black leadership at its center —
  Garrison's press, Walker's appeal, Douglass's newspapers and speeches,
  Tubman's Railroad, Truth's testimony — and the backlash it met, from the
  gag rule to the banning of abolitionist mail;
- argue, with claim, counterclaim, evidence, and stated limits, about which
  lever changes a society — and present one reform case to an audience
  outside the classroom.

This unit assumes the U03 habits (claim + evidence + one stated limit;
maker/audience/purpose source criticism) and the U04 skills (cause-ranking
and perspective-taking across a frontier where the same event was
opportunity for some and catastrophe for others). It adds economic
reasoning — supply, demand, wages, and external costs — and the habit of
asking, for every reform, *who had to act, and what did it cost them?*

**A note on U03:** this unit refers to the Constitution's compromises over
slavery (the Three-Fifths Clause) and to the Bill of Rights, which were

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, learners will be able to:

1. **Explain the market revolution** — state how the Erie Canal, the
   steamboat, the railroad, and the telegraph changed transport cost, travel
   time, and communication, using verified figures (canal length, cost,
   freight change, telegraph date), and explain supply-and-demand shifts
   with period examples (D2.Eco.6.6-8; D2.Eco.7.6-8; D2.His.14.6-8).
2. **Analyze factory labor at Lowell** — describe who worked in the mills,
   their hours, wages, board, and rules; compute a mill worker's take-home
   pay from verified wage and board figures; and explain the 1834 and 1836
   turnouts and the *Commonwealth v. Hunt* (1842) decision as worker
   responses (D2.Eco.1.6-8; D2.Eco.9.6-8; D2.His.4.6-8).
3. **Compare reform movements and their methods** — describe temperance,
   common schools, and asylum/prison reform, compare persuasion, law,
   institution-building, and organization as methods, and trace one reform
   from problem to action to result (D2.Civ.6.6-8; D2.Civ.14.6-8;
   D2.His.5.6-8).
4. **Analyze the Declaration of Sentiments** — explain how it borrows the
   Declaration of Independence's structure and language, state what its
   suffrage resolution demanded and why it was the most contested, and name
   the roles of Stanton, Mott, and Douglass at Seneca Falls (D2.Civ.8.6-8;
   D2.His.3.6-8; D2.His.6.6-8).
5. **Explain abolitionism and Black agency** — distinguish gradualism from
   immediatism, explain the roles of Garrison's press, Walker's appeal,
   Douglass's newspapers and speeches, Tubman's Underground Railroad, and
   Truth's testimony, and describe the backlash (gag rule, banned mail)
   (D2.Civ.2.6-8; D2.His.5.6-8; D2.His.12.6-8).
6. **Argue about how societies change** — write an argument on which lever
   (law, persuasion, organization, or direct action) changes a society that
   states a claim and a genuine counterclaim, cites at least three sources,
   weighs at least two perspectives, and states one limit of the evidence
   (D3.3.6-8; D3.4.6-8; D4.1.6-8; D4.4.6-8).

## Prerequisites

From U03: the Constitution's structure, the Three-Fifths Clause as a
compromise that counted enslaved people for representation without freeing
anyone, and the habit of asking who benefits from a rule. From U02: the
Declaration of Independence's language ("all men are created equal") and
claim + evidence + one stated limit. From U04: cause-ranking,
perspective-taking, and the Missouri Compromise as a postponed conflict
over slavery's spread. Learners should be comfortable reading a map key
and scale and working with a small dataset before Lesson 1.

## Vocabulary

**Market revolution** — the shift, roughly 1820–1850, from household
production for local use to factory production and farming for distant
markets, driven by canals, steamboats, railroads, and the telegraph.
**Canal** — a human-made waterway; the Erie Canal (1825) joined Lake Erie
to the Hudson River. **Interchangeable parts** — identical machine parts
that fit any copy of a product, making repair and mass production possible.
**Factory system** — production in a central building where workers tend
machines for wages, rather than making goods at home. **Wage labor** —
work paid in cash wages, rather than work paid in food, lodging, or a
share of the crop. **Corporation** — a business chartered as a legal body
that can own property, borrow, and be sued; Lowell mills were run by
corporations. **Labor union** — an organization of workers who bargain
together over wages, hours, and conditions. **Strike (turnout)** — workers
refusing to work together to force a change; Lowell's 1834 and 1836
walkouts were called turnouts. **Boycott** — refusing to buy or use
something as a protest. **Temperance** — the movement against alcohol use;
its members signed pledges and pushed for laws. **Common school** — a free,
tax-supported public school open to all local children; Horace Mann's
cause. **Normal school** — a school that trained teachers. **Asylum** — in
this period, a state institution for people with mental illness; Dorothea
Dix campaigned to move them out of jails and almshouses. **Suffrage** —
the right to vote. **Declaration of Sentiments** — the women's-rights
declaration adopted at Seneca Falls in 1848, modeled on the Declaration
of Independence. **Abolitionism** — the movement to end slavery.
**Immediatism** — the demand for immediate, uncompensated emancipation.
**Gradualism** — the older position that slavery should end slowly, or
that freed people should be sent elsewhere (colonization).
**Underground Railroad** — the secret network of safe houses and guides
that helped enslaved people escape to free states and Canada.
**Conductor** — a Railroad guide; **station** — a safe house; **passenger**
— a person escaping. **Petition** — a written request to a government
body. **Gag rule** — the U.S. House rule (1836–1844) that automatically
tabled antislavery petitions without reading them. **Moral suasion** —
changing minds by argument and example rather than by law or force.
**External costs (externalities)** — costs of an activity paid by people
who did not choose it (for example, mill pollution or a worker's ruined
health). **Supply and demand** — the relationship between how much of a
good is available and how much people want, which moves prices.

**Factual claims verified 2026-11-09 for this unit:** Erie Canal — 363
miles, Albany to Buffalo, 40 feet wide and 4 feet deep, 83 locks; built
1817–1825 at a cost of $7,143,000; completed October 26, 1825; freight
from Buffalo to New York fell by roughly 90% (about $100 a ton to about
$10), and travel time from Albany to Buffalo fell from about two weeks to
about five days (New York State Archives summaries; NPS Erie Canalway
background). Samuel Slater's water-powered cotton-spinning mill at
Pawtucket, Rhode Island (1790); Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) and
interchangeable parts for muskets; Robert Fulton's *Clermont* (1807);
Samuel Morse's telegraph, first public message "What hath God wrought"
(Numbers 23:23, chosen by Annie Ellsworth) sent May 24, 1844, from
Washington to Baltimore over about 38–40 miles of wire, after Congress
appropriated $30,000 in 1843 (U.S. Senate Historical Office; Smithsonian).
Lowell — first factory opened 1823; mill girls generally 15–30 years old,
recruited from New England farms; 12–14 hour days plus a half day
Saturday (about 73 hours a week); wages $1.85–$3.00 a week, board
$1.25–$1.50 a week deducted; company boarding houses of 20–40 women with
curfews and required church attendance; turnouts in 1834 (about 800
women) and 1836 (about 1,500); *Lowell Offering* 1840–1845; Sarah Bagley
and the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association campaigned for a ten-hour
day; the corporations eventually cut the workday to 11 hours; by 1850
Lowell had 32 mills and about 33,000 people, up from about 200 in 1820
(National Park Service, Lowell National Historical Park; Massachusetts
Historical Society). *Commonwealth v. Hunt* (1842) — Massachusetts
Supreme Judicial Court, Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw: a labor society was
not automatically a criminal conspiracy; workers could agree on wages and
refuse to work with non-members so long as their means stayed lawful.
Temperance — American Temperance Society founded 1826; by 1831 it had
2,220 local chapters and 170,000 members; Maine passed a statewide
sale ban in 1845 and the Maine Law of 1851 (Portland mayor Neal Dow),
the first state law banning both sale and manufacture; by 1855, 13 states
had prohibition laws. Common schools — Horace Mann became the first
secretary of the Massachusetts Board of Education in 1837, founded the
*Common School Journal* in 1838, and opened the first state normal
schools; he served until 1848 (Britannica; EBSCO). Dorothea Dix — visited
the East Cambridge jail in 1841, presented her *Memorial to the
Legislature of Massachusetts* in January 1843; by 1845 she had traveled
about 10,000 miles, visited 18 state penitentiaries, 300 county jails,
and more than 500 almshouses, and helped establish 6 hospitals; Congress
passed her federal land-grant bill in 1854 and President Pierce vetoed it
(Disability History Museum, full text of the 1843 Memorial; University of
New England). Seneca Falls — convention July 19–20, 1848, at the Wesleyan
Chapel; about 300 attendees including about 40 men; organized by
Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Lucretia Mott, Martha Wright, Mary Ann
M'Clintock, and Jane Hunt; the Declaration of Sentiments, drafted mainly
by Stanton (with M'Clintock) and modeled on the Declaration of
Independence, opens "all men and women are created equal"; 100 people
signed it (68 women, 32 men); the suffrage resolution was the most
contested and passed with Frederick Douglass's support — Douglass was
the convention's only African American attendee (National Park Service,
Women's Rights National Historical Park). Abolition — David Walker's
*Appeal to the Coloured Citizens of the World* (1829); William Lloyd
Garrison's *The Liberator*, first issue January 1, 1831, Boston, with
Isaac Knapp, running until December 29, 1865 (circulation about 3,000);
New England Anti-Slavery Society 1832; American Anti-Slavery Society
founded December 1833 in Philadelphia; Nat Turner's rebellion, August
1831; the House "gag rule" 1836–1844 automatically tabled antislavery
petitions; Frederick Douglass escaped from Maryland in 1838, published
his *Narrative* in 1845, and began the *North Star* with Martin Delany
on December 3, 1847; his Rochester address "What to the Slave Is the
Fourth of July?" was delivered July 5, 1852; Harriet Tubman escaped in
1849 and made about 13 trips leading about 70 people to freedom (plus
instructions to 50–60 more), "never lost a passenger"; the Fugitive Slave
Act of 1850 pushed escape routes on to Canada; Sojourner Truth spoke at
the Akron women's-rights convention in 1851 — two versions of her speech
survive (an 1851 newspaper report and an 1875 retelling), and the famous
"Ain't I a Woman?" wording comes from the later version, so this unit
quotes neither version as exact words and teaches the difference;
Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin* appeared in 1852 (National
Park Service; Britannica; Grolier Club). All quoted primary-source
wording in this unit is public domain (pre-1929 documents); lesson
paraphrases are labeled as paraphrases, not quotations. The Lowell mill
illustration is a labeled AI reconstruction, not evidence. The
supply-and-demand numbers in Lesson 1 Task A are a labeled fictional
practice dataset, not historical observations; the Erie Canal freight
figures beside them are the verified real ones.
