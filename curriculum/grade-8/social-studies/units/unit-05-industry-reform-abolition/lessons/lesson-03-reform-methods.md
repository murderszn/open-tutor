# Lesson 3 — Reformers and Their Methods

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can describe four reform
movements of the 1820s–1840s, match each to its main method, and explain
why reformers chose different methods for different problems.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) If you wanted your town to stop selling
alcohol, would you rather persuade every drinker one by one, or pass a
law? (2) If you wanted every child in your state to go to school, could
persuasion alone do it — or would you need money and buildings? Keep
your answers; the reformers split over exactly this question.

## Explicit teaching

The market revolution changed how Americans lived — and a generation of
reformers looked at the new society and found it wanting. They shared a
belief that people could improve their world on purpose, and they shared
the Second Great Awakening's revival energy, but they used very different
methods. Learn the four movements below by their **method**, not just
their cause.

**Temperance — persuasion, then law.** Alcohol was everywhere in early
America: workers drank at midday, and hard liquor was cheap. The
American Temperance Society, founded by Boston ministers in 1826, began
with persuasion — pledge-signing, sermons, pamphlets, and the argument
that drink ruined families and work. It worked fast: by 1831 the society
had 2,220 local chapters and 170,000 members, and American drinking fell.
But persuasion has a limit — it only reaches the willing. So temperance
leaders turned to law. Maine banned the sale of alcohol statewide in
1845, and the Maine Law of 1851 — pushed by Portland mayor Neal Dow —
became the first state law to ban both the *sale and the manufacture* of
liquor. By 1855, 13 states had prohibition laws. Temperance shows the
method ladder: persuade first, then legislate what persuasion cannot
finish.

**Common schools — institution-building.** In 1837 Massachusetts created
a state Board of Education, and Horace Mann became its first secretary.
Mann's method was to build an institution: tax-supported, free,
nonsectarian public schools — "common schools" — open to all children,
staffed by professionally trained teachers. He founded the *Common School
Journal* in 1838, wrote twelve annual reports, and opened the first state
normal schools to train teachers. He argued that a republic needed
educated citizens, and that only the state could provide schooling at the
scale the problem required. Most northern states copied some version of
his system. Note the difference from temperance: Mann did not ask each
family to choose school; he built schools and taxed everyone to pay for
them. Institution-building is slow and expensive — and it is the method
you use when the problem is too big for persuasion alone.

**Asylums and prisons — investigation, then petition.** Dorothea Dix was
a former schoolteacher who, in 1841, visited the East Cambridge jail to
teach a Sunday school class and found mentally ill women locked in with
criminals, in filth, chained and beaten. Her method was investigation:
she spent two years traveling Massachusetts, documenting conditions in
jails, almshouses, and asylums. Then she wrote it up as a *Memorial to
the Legislature of Massachusetts* (January 1843) — a formal petition that
named names, quoted what she had seen, and asked the state to build
separate hospitals. Read this sentence from her Memorial (public domain,
1843):

> "I come to present the strong claims of suffering humanity. I come to
> place before the Legislature of Massachusetts the condition of the
> miserable, the desolate, the outcast."

Her memorial worked in Massachusetts, and she repeated the method state
by state: by 1845 she had traveled about 10,000 miles, visited 18 state
penitentiaries, 300 county jails, and more than 500 almshouses, and helped
establish 6 hospitals. In 1854 Congress passed her bill granting federal
land for asylums — and President Franklin Pierce vetoed it, saying the
federal government had no such power. Dix shows both the strength and the
limit of the petition method: a documented investigation can move a
legislature, but it can still be stopped by one veto.

**Women's rights — organization and argument.** The women who organized
Seneca Falls in 1848 (next lesson) came out of abolitionism and
temperance, where they had learned to organize — and learned that even
reform movements could shut women out. Lucretia Mott had been barred
from the floor of the 1840 World Anti-Slavery Convention in London
because she was a woman. Their method was a convention: gather publicly,
adopt a declaration, and argue in the open. Organization is the method
you use when the people affected are the ones who must lead.

Four movements, four methods — but notice what they share: every one of
them used **print** (newspapers, journals, memorials, declarations) and
every one of them asked the state to do something it had not done before.
The reformers were not all allies — temperance leaders and abolitionists
quarreled, and Garrison's abolitionism split over women's rights — and
that is part of the lesson: people who agree that society should change
can still disagree fiercely about how.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *Moral suasion* is changing minds by
argument and example — the temperance pledge. A *petition* is a written
request to a government body — Dix's Memorial was a petition with
evidence attached. *Institution-building* is creating a lasting
organization — a school system, a hospital — rather than winning a single
argument.

## Worked example 1 — Match the method to the problem

Problem: "In our county, mentally ill people are chained in the jail
basement."

- Step 1: Persuasion alone? You could preach that chaining people is
  wrong — but the jailer would say he has nowhere else to put them.
- Step 2: What Dix actually did: she visited, documented, and petitioned
  the legislature with evidence — investigation plus petition.
- Step 3: Why that method fits: the problem is a government practice, so
  the fix must come from the government, and the government needs proof
  before it spends money.
- Conclusion: match the method to who must act. If the actor is a
  legislature, you need evidence and a petition. If the actor is a
  neighbor, persuasion may be enough.

## Worked example 2 — When persuasion hits its limit

The temperance society's membership grew to 170,000 by 1831, and
drinking fell. Yet Maine still passed a law in 1851.

- Step 1: persuasion reached the willing — 170,000 people signed pledges.
- Step 2: it did not reach the unwilling — drinkers who would not sign,
  and sellers who profited from selling.
- Step 3: the movement changed method: from persuading drinkers to
  banning sellers, by law.
- Conclusion: a method's limit is as important as its strength. When you
  judge a reform, ask: who could not be reached by this method, and what
  did the reformers do about them?

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Name the four movements and give each one's main method in one
   phrase. (Temperance: persuasion, then law. Schools: institution-
   building. Asylums: investigation and petition. Women's rights:
   organization and argument.)
2. Dix visited 300 jails and 500 almshouses. Why did she travel before
   she petitioned? (Evidence first: a petition without documented facts
   is easy for a legislature to ignore.)
3. Mann built schools and taxed everyone to pay for them. Why could
   persuasion not have built the school system? (Families who saw no use
   for school would not pay voluntarily; a system needs steady public
   money.)
4. Why did the Maine Law of 1851 ban *manufacture* as well as sale?
   (Banning sale alone left sellers supplied; banning manufacture cut
   the supply itself.)

## Application

Choose one of the four movements. Write a five-sentence plan as if you
are its organizer in 1840: one sentence naming the problem, one sentence
naming your method, one sentence naming who must act, one sentence
naming what your method cannot reach, and one sentence saying what you
will do when you hit that limit. Label the plan as a modern exercise.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) the year the American Temperance
Society was founded; (2) the year Mann became secretary of the
Massachusetts Board of Education; (3) one sentence explaining what a
memorial is in Dix's sense.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the Dix quotation aloud while the
  learner underlines "suffering humanity" and "the outcast," then states
  in one sentence who Dix is speaking for.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the table orally or
  by sorting movement/method/result cards.
- **Extension:** research the Washington Societies (workingmen's
  temperance groups of the early 1840s) and compare them with the
  American Temperance Society: who joined each, and why did the
  Washingtonians fade by 1843? Write three sentences with a citation.

## Teacher note

Misconception alert: learners often flatten all reformers into one
"reform movement." Correct it explicitly: these were separate movements
with separate methods that sometimes cooperated and sometimes quarreled.
The Dix material mentions chaining and beating of mentally ill people;
keep the discussion factual and brief, name the conditions once, and do
not ask learners to imagine themselves in a cell. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 3 items.
