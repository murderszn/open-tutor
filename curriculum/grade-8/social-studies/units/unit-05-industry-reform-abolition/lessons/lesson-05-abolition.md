# Lesson 5 — Abolition: Moral Argument, Black Leadership, and the Press

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can distinguish gradualism from
immediatism, name three Black leaders' roles in abolition, and explain
the backlash the movement met.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) If a law is unjust, should it be ended
at once, or ended slowly so nobody is disrupted? (2) Who should lead the
fight against slavery — the people held in slavery, or sympathetic
outsiders? Keep your answers; the abolitionists argued over both
questions, and the answers shaped the whole movement.

## Explicit teaching

Slavery was old; abolitionism as an organized demand to end it *now*
was new. Before 1830, most white Americans who opposed slavery favored
**gradualism**: free enslaved people slowly, over decades, and send freed
people to Africa — the American Colonization Society's plan, called
colonization. Black abolitionists and their white allies rejected both
parts: gradualism kept millions in bondage for a generation, and
colonization told free Black Americans that their home was somewhere else.
The new position was **immediatism**: immediate, uncompensated
emancipation and full civil rights, demanded now.

The movement's center was Black leadership. **David Walker**, a free
Black businessman in Boston, published his *Appeal to the Coloured
Citizens of the World* in 1829 — a pamphlet that called on enslaved
people to resist, warned slaveholders that God would judge them, and
frightened Southern legislatures so badly that they banned its
distribution and tightened laws against Black literacy. **Nat Turner**,
an enslaved preacher in Virginia, led a rebellion in August 1831 that
killed about 55 white people before it was crushed; Virginia answered
with the execution of Turner and more than fifty others, and with harsher
laws against Black assembly, preaching, and reading. Turner's rebellion
was used by slavery's defenders as proof that abolitionist talk caused
violence — and by abolitionists as proof of what slavery drove people to.

**William Lloyd Garrison**, a white printer, launched *The Liberator* in
Boston on January 1, 1831, with Isaac Knapp, and printed it every week
for 35 years, until December 29, 1865. Its first issue declared: "I will
not equivocate — I will not excuse — I will not retreat a single inch —
AND I WILL BE HEARD." Garrison's circulation was small — about 3,000 —
but his readers included every abolitionist leader in the country, and
the paper became the movement's bulletin board: letters, sermons, and
reports from across the nation. In 1832 he founded the New England
Anti-Slavery Society, and in December 1833 he helped found the American
Anti-Slavery Society in Philadelphia. Garrison's method was moral
suasion: change minds by argument, and refuse any compromise with
slavery — including refusing to work through politics, which split the
movement when others wanted to vote against slavery.

**Frederick Douglass** showed why Black voices had to lead. Born enslaved
in Maryland, he escaped in 1838, published his *Narrative* in 1845, and
on December 3, 1847, began publishing the *North Star* in Rochester with
Martin Delany — because, as he wrote, those who had suffered injustice
must be the ones to demand its end. He broke with Garrison in the early
1850s, arguing that the Constitution could be used *against* slavery
through politics — the position Garrison rejected. On July 5, 1852, in
Rochester, Douglass delivered his most famous speech, "What to the Slave
Is the Fourth of July?" — the source task in this unit's assessment uses
its opening passage.

The backlash was organized and national. Southern states banned
abolitionist mail and put bounties on activists. In 1836 the U.S. House
of Representatives adopted the **gag rule**, which automatically tabled
every antislavery petition without reading it — a rule that stayed in
place until 1844. The gag rule's target was not only the enslaved: it
struck at the petition itself, the right of citizens to ask their
government for redress. John Quincy Adams, the former president now
serving in the House, fought the rule every year until it fell. And in
1852, Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel *Uncle Tom's Cabin* carried the
abolitionist argument into millions of homes that no newspaper had
reached — a reminder that a story can do what an editorial cannot.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *Immediatism* is "end it now, all of it";
*gradualism* is "end it slowly." *Moral suasion* is persuasion by
argument and example. A *petition* is a citizen's written request to a
government body — and the gag rule was an attack on petitioning itself.

## Worked example 1 — Gradualism or immediatism?

Sort each position. Ask: does it end slavery now, or later?

- "Free the children of enslaved people when they turn 25." —
  Gradualism. It ends slavery for a future generation, not for the people
  living in it.
- "Emancipate everyone immediately, with no payment to owners." —
  Immediatism. Garrison's position from 1831.
- "Send freed people to Liberia." — Colonization, the gradualists'
  companion plan: it assumed Black Americans could not belong here.
- Conclusion: the labels are about *time* and *belonging*, not about
  kindness. Gradualists often believed they were being kind. The
  immediatists' argument was that kindness delayed is still bondage.

## Worked example 2 — Why did the gag rule matter?

- Step 1: The Constitution protects the right to petition the government
  (First Amendment).
- Step 2: The House gag rule (1836–1844) did not ban petitions — it
  tabled them unread, automatically, so they never reached debate.
- Step 3: That meant the petitions' *senders* — including thousands of
  women, who could not vote but could petition — lost their one formal
  way to be heard.
- Conclusion: the gag rule was aimed at slavery's critics, but its
  weapon was silence about procedure. When you judge a rule, ask not
  only who it punishes but whose voice it removes from the room.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. In one sentence each, state gradualism and immediatism. (Gradualism:
   end slavery slowly, over time. Immediatism: end it at once, with full
   rights.)
2. Name three Black leaders in this lesson and one thing each did.
   (Walker: the 1829 *Appeal*. Turner: the 1831 rebellion. Douglass:
   the *Narrative* and the *North Star*.)
3. What was Garrison's method, and how did it differ from Douglass's
   later position? (Moral suasion through the press, outside politics;
   Douglass came to argue for using the Constitution and politics.)
4. What did the gag rule do, and whose voice did it remove? (It
   auto-tabled antislavery petitions unread, 1836–1844; it removed
   petitioners — including women who could not vote.)

## Application

Imagine you are an editor in 1836 deciding whether to print an
antislavery petition the House has just gagged. Write a five-sentence
editorial: one sentence stating what the gag rule did, one sentence
stating why petitioning matters, one sentence quoting or paraphrasing
Garrison's "I will not equivocate" line, one sentence naming one risk of
printing it, and one sentence stating your decision and why. Label the
editorial as a modern reconstruction.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) the year of the *Liberator*'s first
issue and its editor; (2) the years of the gag rule; (3) one sentence
explaining immediatism.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult reads Garrison's "I will not
  equivocate" sentence aloud while the learner underlines each "I will
  not," then states in one sentence what Garrison refuses to do.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the table orally or
  by sorting person/date/role cards.
- **Extension:** research Maria W. Stewart — the Black abolitionist
  whose 1832–1833 *Liberator* essays and public lectures made her one
  of the first American women to lecture before mixed-race,
  mixed-gender audiences. Write three sentences with a citation.

## Teacher note

Misconception alert: learners often retell abolition as a white rescue
story. Correct it explicitly and early: Black people led the movement —
as writers, organizers, speakers, and escapers — and white allies like
Garrison joined a fight Black Americans were already waging. The Nat
Turner material names killings on both sides; keep the numbers factual
(about 55 white people killed in the rebellion; Turner and more than
fifty others executed afterward), do not dramatize the violence, and do
not ask learners to role-play either side. Answer-key reference:
[answer-key.md](../answer-key.md), Lesson 5 items.
