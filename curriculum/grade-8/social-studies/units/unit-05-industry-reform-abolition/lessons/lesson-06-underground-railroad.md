# Lesson 6 — The Underground Railroad: Resistance as a System

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can explain how the Underground
Railroad worked, why enslaved people's resistance and abolitionist
organizing reinforced each other, and how to judge a historical source
whose exact words are disputed.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) If escaping meant traveling at night,
on foot, through a state where you could be seized and returned, what
would you need most — a map, a friend, or a law? (2) Who knew the roads
and dangers best — the person escaping, or the people waiting at the far
end? Keep your answers; the Railroad's answer was: friends, and the
person escaping.

## Explicit teaching

The Underground Railroad was not a railroad, and it was not mostly
underground. It was a secret network of safe houses ("stations"), guides
("conductors"), and routes ("tracks") that helped enslaved people escape
to free states and, after 1850, to Canada. It had existed since the late
1700s, built by free Black people and white allies working together. What
changed in the 1840s and 1850s was scale and organization: abolitionist
societies, newspapers like the *North Star*, and conductors like Harriet
Tubman turned scattered help into a system.

**Harriet Tubman** is the Railroad's most famous conductor, and the facts
bear repeating carefully. Born enslaved in Maryland (about 1820–1822),
she escaped to Philadelphia in 1849 — and then went back. Over about a
decade she made about 13 trips into Maryland and led about 70 people to
freedom, while giving instructions that helped 50–60 more escape on their
own. She traveled by night, in extreme secrecy, and she was never caught
and never lost a passenger — her own words, spoken in 1896: "I never ran
my train off the track and I never lost a passenger." William Lloyd
Garrison called her "Moses," for leading her people out of bondage. The
Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 — passed by Congress as part of the Compromise
of 1850 — made her work far more dangerous: it let slaveholders seize
suspected fugitives in free states with few legal protections, so Tubman
began guiding people all the way to Canada. In 1858 she met John Brown
and helped him plan and recruit for his 1859 raid on Harpers Ferry. She
did not create the Railroad; she made it work at a scale and with a
courage no one else matched.

**Sojourner Truth** shows a different kind of resistance: testimony.
Born enslaved in New York, she won her freedom when New York abolished
slavery, and in 1851 she spoke at the women's-rights convention in Akron,
Ohio. Her speech is famous under the title "Ain't I a Woman?" — but here
is a historian's caution the unit wants you to learn. Two versions of
the speech survive: a newspaper report from the *Anti-Slavery Bugle*,
published June 21, 1851, one month after she spoke; and a retelling in
the 1875 *Narrative of Sojourner Truth*, written by others, which gives
her the famous repeated line "Ain't I a woman?" and a Southern dialect
she may not have used. The 1851 version is shorter and plainer. The 1875
version is the one everyone quotes. **This unit quotes neither version as
Truth's exact words.** The honest statement is: Truth spoke at Akron in
1851, demanding both abolition and women's rights; the famous wording
comes from a later retelling. When a quotation's wording is disputed,
say so — do not pick the prettier version and call it exact.

The deepest point of this lesson is the connection between the two halves
of the movement. Enslaved people's resistance — escapes, slowdowns,
sabotage, revolts, petitions — proved that slavery could not hold people
who refused it. Abolitionist organizing — newspapers, societies, the
Railroad's stations — gave that resistance somewhere to go. Neither half
worked alone: without escapers, the Railroad had no passengers; without
the Railroad, most escapers had nowhere safe to run. And both halves
faced the same backlash: the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 was Congress's
answer to the Railroad's success.

**Key vocabulary in context:** A *conductor* guides; a *station* hides;
a *passenger* escapes. The Fugitive Slave Act (1850) let owners reclaim
suspected fugitives in free states. A *retelling* is a later account of a
speech or event, written from memory or notes — it may be faithful, or
it may be polished, and the reader must know which.

## Worked example 1 — Why travel at night?

- Step 1: An escaping person has no papers proving freedom, and slave
  catchers are paid to seize people and ask questions later.
- Step 2: Daylight travel on roads means being seen — by patrols, by
  neighbors, by anyone who might claim a reward.
- Step 3: Night travel means slower going and worse navigation, but the
  North Star gives direction, and stations are spaced a night's walk
  apart.
- Conclusion: every Railroad choice trades one risk for another. Night
  is slower but safer; speed is faster but deadlier. Resistance is not
  bravery alone — it is calculation under pressure.

## Worked example 2 — Which version of Truth's speech do you trust?

- Step 1: The 1851 *Bugle* report was written one month after the
  speech, by people who heard it — close in time, but a newspaper
  summary, not a transcript.
- Step 2: The 1875 *Narrative* version was written 24 years later, by
  other people, in a dialect Truth — who grew up speaking Dutch — may
  not have used.
- Step 3: The 1875 version is more famous and more dramatic; the 1851
  version is closer to the event.
- Conclusion: "famous" is not the same as "accurate." A historian uses
  the 1851 version for what Truth said, and treats the 1875 version as
  evidence of how later Americans *remembered* her. Both versions are
  useful — for different questions.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Explain the Railroad's three terms — conductor, station, passenger —
   in one sentence each. (Conductor: guide. Station: safe house.
   Passenger: person escaping.)
2. How did the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 change Tubman's work? (It let
   owners seize fugitives in free states, so she guided people on to
   Canada.)
3. Why did the Railroad need both escapers and organizers? (Without
   escapers there were no passengers; without organizers there were no
   safe routes or stations.)
4. Why does this unit refuse to quote Truth's "Ain't I a Woman?" as her
   exact words? (Two versions survive; the famous wording comes from an
   1875 retelling, not a transcript.)

## Application

Imagine you are a station keeper in 1852 — a free Black farmer in
Pennsylvania — and a conductor arrives at midnight with four passengers.
Write a six-sentence plan: one sentence saying what you do first, one
sentence saying how you hide them, one sentence saying what the 1850 Act
means for your risk, one sentence saying who else must help, one
sentence saying what you tell the passengers about the road to Canada,
and one sentence saying why you take the risk. Label the plan as a
modern reconstruction. Do not role-play an escape or a capture.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) what a conductor, a station, and a
passenger are; (2) Tubman's approximate trips and people led; (3) one
sentence explaining why two versions of Truth's speech matter.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult reads Tubman's 1896 words aloud
  ("I never ran my train off the track and I never lost a passenger")
  while the learner writes the two things she claims — no derailment,
  no lost passenger — then states what each claim means.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the table orally or
  by sorting figure cards into the table's rows.
- **Extension:** research the Christiana Resistance of 1851 — when a
  Maryland slaveholder's attempt to seize fugitives in Pennsylvania was
  met by armed free Black neighbors. Write three sentences with a
  citation, and one sentence on what it shows about the 1850 Act's
  limits.

## Teacher note

Misconception alert: learners often picture the Railroad as a formal
organization with a headquarters. Correct it explicitly: it was a loose,
secret network of local people, and its strength was that it had no
center to destroy. Sensitive note: escape stories involve real danger;
keep the discussion factual, do not ask learners to imagine being hunted
or captured, and do not role-play escapes. The Truth two-versions point
is a source lesson, not a challenge to her importance — say so plainly.
