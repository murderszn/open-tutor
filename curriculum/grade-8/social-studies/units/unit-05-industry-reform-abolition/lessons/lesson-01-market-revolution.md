# Lesson 1 — The Market Revolution: Canals, Steam, and the Factory

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can state four ways transport and
communication changed between 1820 and 1850, give one verified figure for
each, and explain a supply-or-demand shift with a period example.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, the [Erie
Canal schematic](../assets/erie-canal-schematic.svg) (used in Week 20 S3).

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) In 1800, how long did it take a letter or
a barrel of flour to travel from Buffalo to New York City — days or weeks?
(2) Who made most American cloth in 1800 — a factory worker or a farm
family at home? Keep your answers; both answers change in this lesson.

## Explicit teaching

In 1800, most Americans lived on farms and made most of what they used.
A family spun its own wool, wove its own cloth, and sold only a small
surplus nearby, because moving goods overland was slow and expensive: a
wagon trip from Buffalo to New York City took about two weeks and cost
about $100 a ton. Between 1820 and 1850 that world flipped into the
**market revolution**: Americans increasingly worked for wages, bought
what they needed in shops, and sold what they made to distant buyers.
Four changes drove the flip.

**Canals.** The Erie Canal, built by the state of New York from 1817 to
1825, cut a 363-mile waterway from Albany on the Hudson River to Buffalo
on Lake Erie — 40 feet wide, 4 feet deep, with 83 locks to lift boats over
the hills. It cost $7,143,000 to build. When it opened on October 26,
1825, freight from Buffalo to New York fell by roughly 90% — from about
$100 a ton to about $10 — and the trip shrank from about two weeks to
about five days. Suddenly Ohio wheat and western timber could reach
Atlantic markets cheaply, and New York City became the nation's busiest
port. Other states rushed to dig their own canals.

**Steamboats.** Robert Fulton's *Clermont* made its first commercial run
in 1807. A steamboat could go upstream against a river's current, which
sailing ships and flatboats could not do reliably. That turned the
Mississippi and Ohio rivers into two-way highways: farm goods floated
down, manufactured goods steamed back up. River towns like Cincinnati and
St. Louis grew fast.

**Railroads and factories.** Railroads spread in the 1830s and 1840s,
running mostly east–west and tying Midwestern grain to Northeastern ports
and factories — a pattern that mattered later, because it linked the North
and West more closely than either was linked to the South. Factories
gathered workers and machines under one roof. Samuel Slater had opened
the first American water-powered cotton-spinning mill at Pawtucket, Rhode
Island, in 1790; by the 1820s, Lowell, Massachusetts, was a planned mill
city on the Merrimack River. Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) let one
worker clean far more cotton than by hand, and his interchangeable parts
let muskets — and later many machines — be built from identical pieces
that could be swapped when one broke. The gin made cotton enormously
profitable, which is why it also made slavery bigger: planters bought
more western land and forced more enslaved people to work it. A machine
can make life easier and make an injustice worse at the same time.

**The telegraph.** On May 24, 1844, Samuel F. B. Morse sent the first
public telegraph message from Washington to Baltimore — "What hath God
wrought," a line from the Bible (Numbers 23:23) chosen by Annie
Ellsworth, the patent commissioner's daughter. The wire ran about 38–40
miles, and Congress had paid $30,000 to build it the year before. News
that once traveled at the speed of a horse or a ship now traveled at the
speed of electricity. Within a few years, telegraph wires followed the
railroads, and merchants could learn a price in another city the same day.

Economists describe the price effects with **supply and demand**: when
more of a good becomes available (supply rises) and nothing else changes,
its price tends to fall; when more people want it (demand rises), its
price tends to rise. The canal was mostly a supply story — cheaper
transport meant more western wheat could reach eastern buyers, so flour
got cheaper in the East. The cotton gin was a demand story in reverse:
cheaper cleaning made cotton cloth cheaper, more people bought it, and
the demand for raw cotton — and for the enslaved labor that grew it —
rose sharply. Keep both directions in mind: the same invention can lower
one price and raise the demand for something else, including something
cruel.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *Wage labor* is work paid in cash — the
mill girls of Lowell earned wages, while a farm daughter's work earned no
cash at all. A *corporation* is a business chartered as a legal body; the
Lowell mills were corporations, which could raise large sums to build
mills and canals. *External costs* are costs paid by people who did not
choose them: a mill's noise and dust fell on its workers, and a canal's
debt fell on taxpayers, even when the profits went to owners.

## Worked example 1 — How much did the canal cut freight?

A merchant in Buffalo wants to ship 10 tons of wheat to New York City.

- Before the canal: about $100 a ton × 10 tons = about $1,000, and about
  two weeks on the road.
- After the canal (1825): about $10 a ton × 10 tons = about $100, and
  about five days on the water.
- Saving: about $900 on this one shipment — a cut of about 90%.
- Conclusion: the canal did not just save money; it changed what was
  worth selling. Wheat that was too expensive to ship by wagon suddenly
  paid to ship by canal, so western farmers planted more wheat and eastern
  bakers bought it. When transport gets 90% cheaper, the map of who trades
  with whom is redrawn.

## Worked example 2 — A supply shift, step by step

Use the labeled practice dataset in Task A (fictional numbers, made for
practice — not historical observations). In the dataset, a town's cloth
price is $4 a yard when 100 yards a week are available.

- Step 1: A new canal opens, so cloth can arrive from two more mills.
  Supply rises from 100 to 160 yards a week.
- Step 2: More cloth is chasing the same buyers, so sellers cut prices to
  move it. The price falls to $3 a yard.
- Step 3: At $3, more buyers can afford cloth, so more is sold — but the
  price is lower than before.
- Conclusion: a supply increase pushes the price down. Now flip it: if a
  fashion changes and everyone wants cloth (demand rises) while supply
  stays at 100 yards, the price rises instead. Always ask first: did
  supply move, or did demand move?

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Name the four changes in this lesson and give one verified figure for
   each. (Canal: 363 miles / $7,143,000 / 1825; steamboat: 1807; railroad:
   1830s–40s east–west; telegraph: May 24, 1844.)
2. A farmer in Ohio can now ship wheat to New York for $10 a ton instead
   of $100. Is that a supply change or a demand change for New York flour
   buyers? (Supply: more wheat can reach them cheaply.)
3. The cotton gin made cleaning cotton far faster. Did the price of raw
   cotton rise or fall, and what happened to the demand for enslaved
   labor? (Raw cotton got cheaper to produce; demand for the labor that
   grew it rose.)
4. Why did the telegraph matter to a merchant even though it carried no
   goods at all? (It carried prices and news instantly, so merchants could
   buy and sell the same day.)

## Application

Imagine you are a shopkeeper in Albany in 1826, a year after the canal
opened. Write a five-sentence letter to a cousin in Ohio: one sentence
saying what changed, one sentence with a verified canal figure, one
sentence saying what you can now buy or sell, one sentence saying who
you think loses from the change, and one sentence saying what you would
ask the state to do next. Label the letter as a modern reconstruction.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) the year the Erie Canal opened and its
length in miles; (2) the date and first message of the telegraph;
(3) one sentence explaining supply and demand in your own words.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the canal figures aloud while the
  learner writes each number on a timeline (1817–1825). Say each figure
  twice; the numbers are the lesson's anchors.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the table orally or by
  sorting pre-written change/figure cards into matched pairs.
- **Extension:** research the sewing machine (Elias Howe's 1846 patent
  and Isaac Singer's improvements) as the Week 20 S3 innovation case:
  who invented it, who used it, and who profited? Write three sentences
  with a citation.

Misconception alert: learners often treat "the market revolution helped
everyone" as the lesson's point. Correct it explicitly: the unit's

that a productivity gain can deepen slavery. Answer-key reference:
