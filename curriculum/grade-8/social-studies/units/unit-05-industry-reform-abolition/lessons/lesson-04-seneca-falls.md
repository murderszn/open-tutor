# Lesson 4 — Seneca Falls: Reading the Declaration of Sentiments

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can paraphrase the opening of
the Declaration of Sentiments, explain how it borrows from the
Declaration of Independence, and explain why its suffrage resolution was
the hardest fight at the convention.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) In 1848, could a married woman in New
York own property, sign a contract, or vote? (2) The Declaration of
Independence says "all men are created equal." Who was left out of that
sentence in practice? Keep your answers; the Seneca Falls convention was
built on exactly these questions.

## Explicit teaching

On July 19–20, 1848, about 300 people — including about 40 men — gathered
at the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, New York, for the first women's
rights convention in the United States. It had been organized in about ten
days by five women: Elizabeth Cady Stanton, Lucretia Mott, Martha Wright,
Mary Ann M'Clintock, and Jane Hunt. Stanton and Mott had met at the 1840
World Anti-Slavery Convention in London, where the women delegates were
barred from the floor because they were women. Eight years later they
decided to hold a convention of their own.

Their central document, the **Declaration of Sentiments**, was drafted
mainly by Stanton, working with M'Clintock at the M'Clintock family's tea
table on July 16. It was modeled, deliberately and openly, on the
Declaration of Independence — and that borrowing was the argument. Read
its opening (public domain, 1848):

> "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are
> created equal; that they are endowed by their Creator with certain
> inalienable rights; that among these are life, liberty, and the pursuit
> of happiness."

Stanton changed four words — she added "and women" — and the whole
American promise suddenly had to answer for itself. The Declaration of
Independence had listed the king's abuses; the Declaration of Sentiments
listed men's: the denial of the vote, of property rights for married
women, of education, of a place in the church, of equal pay for equal
work. Its final demand was blunt: women must be admitted "to all the
rights and privileges which belong to them as citizens of these United
States." One hundred people signed it — 68 women and 32 men.

The hardest fight in the room was over one resolution: the demand for
women's **suffrage**, the right to vote. Many delegates, including Lucretia
Mott herself, thought demanding the vote was too radical — it would make
the whole convention look ridiculous, they warned, and sink the other
demands. Stanton insisted. The resolution passed only because Frederick
Douglass — the abolitionist, the only African American present — rose and
argued that the right to vote was the foundation of all other rights, and
that women were as entitled to it as the Black men for whom he was
fighting. His speech carried the day. Douglass's own newspaper, the
*North Star*, gave the convention one of its few sympathetic accounts.

Stop and notice what happened here. The convention's most respected
leaders disagreed with each other in public, argued it out, and voted.
Mott lost the argument and stayed. Stanton won it with help from an
ally who had his own fight. That is how a movement works: not by
everyone agreeing in advance, but by arguing honestly and letting the
better argument win. Also notice the borrowing strategy: Stanton did not
invent a new language of rights. She took the nation's most famous
language and showed, word by word, that it had left women out. That is
why the document is powerful — it uses the reader's own principles
against the reader's own practice.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *Suffrage* is the right to vote. A
*grievance* is a formal complaint of an injustice. A *resolution* is a
formal statement a meeting adopts. The *Declaration of Sentiments*
borrows its name from the Declaration of Independence's "sentiments" —
the shared beliefs a people declares.

## Worked example 1 — Paraphrase the opening

Original (1848): "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men
and women are created equal; that they are endowed by their Creator with
certain inalienable rights."

- Step 1: Find the borrowed part. "We hold these truths to be
  self-evident" and "created equal" and "inalienable rights" come straight
  from Jefferson's Declaration of Independence (1776).
- Step 2: Find the change. Stanton adds "and women" after "all men."
- Step 3: State the effect in your own words: *Some truths are so
  obvious they need no proof — one is that women and men are equally
  human, and that equality gives both the same basic rights, which no
  government may take away.*
- Conclusion: a good paraphrase keeps the idea and changes the words.
  Notice what the paraphrase must not do: it must not claim Stanton
  *quoted* Jefferson — she rewrote him, and the rewrite is the point.

## Worked example 2 — Why was suffrage the hardest resolution?

- Step 1: The other demands — property, education, church membership —
  asked men to share things they already had.
- Step 2: The vote asked men to share power itself: the power to make
  the laws that governed property, education, and the church.
- Step 3: Even allies feared it. Mott worried the demand would discredit
  the convention; only Douglass's argument — that the vote underpins
  every other right — moved the room.
- Conclusion: when you judge a demand, ask what power it touches. The
  demands that touch power are always the hardest to win, and the ones
  that matter most to the people making them.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Paraphrase the Declaration of Sentiments' opening sentence in your
   own words, keeping the idea and changing the words. (See worked
   example 1.)
2. Name two grievances the Declaration listed. (Any two of: no vote; no
   property rights for married women; no equal education; no place in
   the church; unequal pay; no legal standing apart from a husband.)
3. Why did Mott oppose the suffrage resolution? (She thought it too
   radical and feared it would make the convention look ridiculous and
   sink the other demands.)
4. What did Douglass argue, and why did it matter that *he* said it?
   (The vote is the foundation of all other rights; as the convention's
   only African American and a leading abolitionist, his support linked
   the women's cause to the antislavery cause.)

## Application

Imagine you are a delegate at Seneca Falls who is undecided on the
suffrage resolution. Write a six-sentence speech: one sentence stating
your doubt, one sentence quoting or paraphrasing the Declaration's
opening, one sentence giving Mott's worry fairly, one sentence giving
Douglass's argument fairly, one sentence stating how you will vote, and
one sentence saying why. Label the speech as a modern reconstruction.

## Exit check

Without looking back, write: (1) the dates and place of the convention;
(2) the Declaration's opening words as Stanton wrote them; (3) one
sentence explaining why the suffrage resolution was the hardest fight.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading support:** the adult reads the opening sentence aloud twice
  while the learner follows along, then the learner reads it aloud once.
  Underline "and women" — the two words that change everything.
- **Alternate response mode:** learners may answer the table orally or
  by sorting fact cards into the table's rows.
- **Extension:** read the National Park Service "Women's Rights in
  Seneca Falls" page (resource pack, reference 2) and find the five
  organizers' names. Write two sentences: why did it take five
  organizers, not one? Cite the page.

## Teacher note

Misconception alert: learners often say Seneca Falls "gave women the
vote." Correct it explicitly: the convention demanded the vote; American
women did not win it nationally until the 19th Amendment in 1920 — 72
years later — and even then the fight over who could actually vote
continued. The lesson's point is the argument and the organizing, not an
instant victory. Answer-key reference: [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 4 items.
