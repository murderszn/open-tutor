# Answer Key — Unit 03: Constitution, Ratification, Rights, Institutions

For the guiding adult only. Every question in the lessons, practice tasks, quiz,
and assessment is solved here. Open-response items give a model answer and the
acceptable variation; do not require the learner's wording to match the model.

## Lesson 1 — A League That Could Not Tax, Enforce, or Amend

**Guided practice:**

1. The states supply the soldiers and the money. Congress can only ask; the states
   kept sovereignty, so each legislature decides.
2. No. Congress had no power to regulate interstate or foreign commerce, so it
   could not stop a state from taxing a neighbor's goods.
3. Money (no tax power) and an executive/army to enforce a response (no executive,
   no national army under Congress's direct control).
4. Small states liked equal votes because population did not matter; large states
   worried because their larger populations would count the same as a small
   state's few people.

**Independent practice:**

1. A requisition is a request a state may refuse; a tax is a compulsory levy the
   government collects directly.
2. Open: any of the three with a reason and a fact (e.g., no tax power → unpaid
   war debts and no army for Shays' Rebellion). Accept reasoned choices.
3. The error is explaining a structural failure by character. Rewrite: "The
   Articles failed because Congress could only request money rather than tax, so
   payment depended on thirteen separate legislatures, several of which paid
   little or nothing."
4. Timeline: 1781 Articles in force; 1786–1787 Shays' Rebellion; May 1787
   convention called. Cause-effect arrow example: "Because Congress could not tax
   or raise an army (rule), Massachusetts had to suppress Shays' Rebellion with a
   state militia (effect)."

**Application:** Score with project §6: rule named, breakdown with a fact, causal
link, an objecting group with a reason, an answer to the objection.

## Lesson 2 — The Convention's Bargains

**Guided practice:**

1. The Senate gives every state two votes regardless of population, so a small
   state cannot be outvoted on sheer numbers in that chamber.
2. Yes, mathematically: in the Senate, one small-state voter carries more weight
   per person than one large-state voter, because seats are equal while
   populations differ.
3. Counting enslaved people for representation gained seats; refusing to count
   them for taxation avoided cost. It treated enslaved people as persons for power
   and as property for burden — whichever benefited the slave states.
4. It postponed the slavery question — whether slavery would end, and when — at
   the expense of enslaved people and, eventually, the union.

**Independent practice:**

1. Virginia Plan: bicameral, by population, favored by large states. New Jersey
   Plan: unicameral, one vote per state, favored by small states. Great
   Compromise: bicameral — House by population, Senate two per state.
2. State C: 80,000 + (3/5 × 50,000) = 80,000 + 30,000 = 110,000. State D: 100,000.
   State C has more counted population by 10,000, though it has fewer free people.
3. Model: "The Three-Fifths Compromise increased slave-state power because it
   added three-fifths of each state's enslaved population to its apportionment
   count, giving it more House seats and Electoral College votes — while freeing
   no one and giving enslaved people no vote."
4. "Solved" is wrong because the representation argument continued: the Senate's
   equal representation remained contested, and the three-fifths rule itself was
   only removed by the 14th Amendment (1868). "Managed" or "postponed" is accurate.

## Lesson 3 — Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists

**Guided practice:**

1. Madison: factions are inevitable in liberty; a large republic controls their
   effects by multiplying competing interests. Brutus: a republic this large
   cannot represent its people, and the necessary-and-proper and supremacy
   clauses will let the national government swallow the states.
2. The pamphlets show the debate reached ordinary readers through print. The
   image cannot show what any pamphlet said, who agreed, or how anyone voted.
3. A delegate who feared the Constitution but also feared disunion could vote yes
   while demanding amendments — ratify-and-recommend satisfied both fears at once.
4. Open, but each side needs one reason: secrecy allowed candid bargaining and
   changed minds; publicity allowed public scrutiny and legitimacy. Accept
   reasoned verdicts either way.

**Independent practice:**

1. Sequence: Delaware, December 7, 1787 (30–0); Massachusetts, February 6, 1788
   (187–168, with recommended amendments); New Hampshire, June 21, 1788 (57–47),
   the ninth; Virginia, June 25, 1788 (89–79); New York, July 26, 1788 (30–27);
   Rhode Island, May 29, 1790 (34–32), last.
2. The sentence is too simple because the Anti-Federalists' central demand — a
   bill of rights — was adopted: Congress proposed twelve amendments in 1789 and
   ten were ratified in 1791. Losing ratification did not mean losing the argument.
3. Three caption errors: (a) it is not a photograph — photography did not exist
   in 1788, and this image is a modern AI-generated reconstruction; (b) it is not
   the New York convention — the scene is imagined, not a specific meeting;
   (c) it is not evidence of any vote or speech — it shows no words, no decision.
4. Open: one convincing Federalist argument and one reasonable Anti-Federalist
   worry, each tied to a fact. Accept reasoned pairs.

## Lesson 4 — Three Branches, Checks, Federalism

**Guided practice:**

1. National law prevails, by the supremacy clause (Article VI).
2. No. Treaties need the consent of two-thirds of the Senate.
3. Delegated: coining money is an enumerated national power (Article I, §8).
4. Article III authorizes Congress to create inferior courts; the president
   nominates judges and the Senate confirms them.

**Independent practice:**

1. Veto chain: bill passes both houses → president vetoes → House reconsiders
   (two-thirds) → Senate reconsiders (two-thirds) → law despite veto.
2. Life tenure ("during good Behaviour") means judges need not please the
   president or Congress to keep their jobs, so they can rule against either.
3. Delegated: declare war, coin money. Concurrent: levy taxes. Reserved: run
   public schools, establish local police, license drivers.
4. Correction: the Supreme Court did not strike down the Alien and Sedition Acts
   in 1798 — they expired or were repealed without a Court ruling. Judicial
   review was established in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803). The date matters because
   the 1787 debate cannot be read as if judicial review already existed.

## Lesson 5 — The Bill of Rights

**Guided practice:**

1. 3rd Amendment: no quartering of soldiers in private homes in peacetime
   without the owner's consent — violated on these facts.
2. 5th Amendment: no double jeopardy — a person acquitted cannot be tried again
   for the same federal offense.
3. The 1st Amendment as ratified in 1791 bound Congress (the federal
   government), not a town. A town ban is not decided by the 1st Amendment as
   ratified; its coverage of state and local action came later, through the 14th
   Amendment — a U07 topic.
4. 8th Amendment: no excessive bail — bail set beyond any plausible amount for a
   minor offense violates it.

**Independent practice:**

1. One-line summaries: 1 speech/religion/press/assembly/petition; 2 arms; 3 no
   peacetime quartering; 4 warrants on probable cause; 5 grand jury, no double
   jeopardy, no self-incrimination, due process, just compensation; 6 speedy
   public jury trial with counsel and confrontation; 7 civil jury over $20;
   8 no excessive bail/fines or cruel and unusual punishments; 9 unlisted rights
   retained; 10 reserved powers.
2. The 9th Amendment prevents the list from being read as exhaustive: it says
   the people retain rights beyond those enumerated, so a right is not
   unprotected merely because it is unlisted.
3. Brutus would care most about the 1st Amendment's explicit limits and the
   10th Amendment's reservation of powers — both answer his demand for written
   limits. Madison the Federalist thought a bill of rights least necessary,
   because he argued the Constitution's structure (separation of powers,
   federalism) was itself the protection.
4. Error: the 1st Amendment binds government action, not private actors. A
   private club's expulsion is not a 1st Amendment violation.

## Lesson 6 — A Contested Settlement

**Guided practice:**

1. Senate equality: settled (structure). 1808 importation clause: postponed.
   Meaning of "due process": left open (to courts and amendment). Nine-state
   ratification rule: settled (Article VII).
2. "Adopted unanimously" is wrong at every stage: only 39 of 42 delegates present
   signed; ratification needed nine of thirteen, not all; Rhode Island's voters
   rejected the Constitution in a 1788 referendum and ratified only in 1790.
3. Relevancy order (highest first): a state's vote tally (direct evidence of how
   close and how decided); a delegate's private letter (evidence of motives, one
   person's view); a Federalist essay (elite argument, not popular opinion);
   a modern textbook summary (secondary, no new evidence). Accept reasoned
   reorderings with justification.
4. Groups barred: women, enslaved people, most free Black people, Native peoples
   (rules varied by state). Consequence: the record shows the views of those
   allowed to vote, not of the whole population the government would rule.

**Independent practice:**

1. Model thesis: "The Constitution's greatest postponement was slavery, because
   the Three-Fifths Compromise and the 1808 clause bought union by deferring the
   question — and the deferred question returned as the sectional crisis (U06)."
   Accept other postponements with a clause and a consequence.
2. Score: maker/side, date/place, audience/purpose, specific relevancy, one limit
   plus a follow-up source.
3. Three facts: Mason, Randolph, and Gerry refused to sign; Massachusetts
   ratified only 187–168; Rhode Island waited until May 29, 1790.
4. Model limit sentence: "This argument cannot establish what people barred from
   voting would have chosen, because the surviving record was made by those who
   could vote."

## Practice Tasks

**Task A:**

1. Paraphrase: each state keeps supreme authority and every power not explicitly
   given to Congress. (Accept accurate paraphrases.)
2. One vote each — largest and smallest alike.
3. No. Five is fewer than the nine required for important laws.
4. "Expressly delegated" means only listed powers belong to Congress; unlisted
   powers stay with the states, so Congress cannot claim implied powers.

**Task B:** Score with project §6: rule named, breakdown with a fact, causal link,
rule change that would prevent it. Model: "The tax rule let Congress only request
money. When states paid little, Congress could not pay soldiers or service debts;
veterans and farmers faced taxes and foreclosures without relief, feeding Shays'
Rebellion. A power to tax directly would have let Congress pay its debts and fund
a response."

**Task C:** Virginia Plan — bicameral, by population; gained: large states;
gave up: nothing yet (it was a proposal); postponed: small-state equality.
New Jersey Plan — unicameral, one vote per state; gained: small states; gave up:
a strong executive/judiciary design; postponed: proportional fairness. Great
Compromise — bicameral, House by population, Senate equal; gained: both sides got
one chamber; gave up: each side's principle in the other chamber; postponed: the
fairness of Senate equality. Three-Fifths — enslaved counted at 3/5 for
representation and direct taxation; gained: slave states (seats and electoral
votes); lost: enslaved people (counted, not enfranchised) and free-state weight;
postponed: slavery itself. Electoral College — president chosen by electors, not
direct popular vote; gained: small states (Senate-based electors) and slave
states (three-fifths in the count); gave up: direct popular choice; postponed:
democratic legitimacy of presidential selection.

**Task D:** (1) Secrecy advantage: delegates could bargain candidly and change
their minds without public pressure. Publicity advantage: decisions made in the
open can be scrutinized and carry public legitimacy. (2) Open verdict with one
Lesson 2/3 argument and one vote fact (e.g., Massachusetts 187–168 shows a
genuinely contested public decision).

**Task E:**

1. Chain: president vetoes → House reconsiders (two-thirds) → Senate reconsiders
   (two-thirds) → law. At 60%, the override fails: 60% is below two-thirds
   (66.7%), so the veto stands.
2. Chain: president nominates → Senate confirms by majority → president
   appoints. The Senate blocks by refusing confirmation.
3. Congress holds the purse (appropriations); the president executes spending.
   The check is congressional control of money: the president may not spend what
   Congress has not appropriated.
4. Correction: the Supreme Court never struck down the Alien and Sedition Acts;
   judicial review was established in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), five years
   later.

**Task F:**

1. 4th Amendment — violated: no warrant, no emergency; private papers seized.
   Fact changing it: a valid warrant on probable cause describing the barn and
   papers.
2. Not decided by the 1st Amendment as ratified in 1791: the amendments bound the
   federal government, and this is a state law. (Later, 14th Amendment
   incorporation changed this — U07.) Fact changing it: if Congress, not a
   state legislature, passed the ban.
3. 5th Amendment, double jeopardy — violated. Fact changing it: a genuinely
   different offense, or a civil action rather than a second criminal trial for
   the same act.
4. 8th Amendment, excessive bail — violated on these facts. Fact changing it:
   bail proportionate to the offense and flight risk.
5. 3rd Amendment, quartering — violated: peacetime quartering without consent.
   Fact changing it: wartime quartering "in a manner to be prescribed by law."

**Task G:** Score: maker/side, date/place, audience/purpose, specific relevancy,
one limit plus a follow-up source. See Lesson 6 worked example 2 for the model.

**Task H:** Score with project §6. A meeting essay has: a claim in paragraph one;
a genuine counterclaim answered in its own paragraph; three named sources with
what each proves; voter-inclusion analysis (who could/could not vote, with the
property rule and at least two excluded groups); one stated limit; a conclusion
on what would strengthen or weaken the claim.

## Formative Quiz

**Part A:**

1. One vote per state; nine of thirteen for important laws.
2. False: Congress could only request ("requisition") money from the states; it
   could not tax citizens directly.
3. Any two: tax, regulate commerce, enforce its laws, create national courts,
   raise an army directly, amend its own rules without unanimity.
4. All thirteen. One refusal killed the amendment.
5. Model: "Shays' Rebellion exposed the enforcement and money gap: Congress had
   no tax power and no army of its own, so it could not respond when Massachusetts
   farmers rose, and the state had to rely on a privately funded militia."
6. Officially to revise the Articles; actually it wrote an entirely new
   Constitution.

**Part B:**

1. Virginia Plan: bicameral, by population. New Jersey Plan: unicameral, one vote
   per state. Great Compromise: bicameral — House by population, Senate two per
   state.
2. 100,000 + (3/5 × 50,000) = 100,000 + 30,000 = **130,000**.
3. Delaware, December 7, 1787, 30–0.
4. New Hampshire, June 21, 1788 (57–47). The ninth ratification met Article VII's
   threshold, so the Constitution legally took effect.
5. Model: "A faction is a group united by an interest hostile to others' rights or
   the common good. A large republic controls factions' effects by multiplying
   competing interests, so no single faction easily becomes an oppressive
   majority."
6. Model: "Brutus feared that a republic as large as the United States could not
   represent its people, and that the necessary-and-proper and supremacy clauses
   would let the national government swallow the states. He demanded a bill of
   rights before ratification."

**Part C:**

1. Legislative, Article I — makes laws. Executive, Article II — carries out laws.
   Judicial, Article III — interprets laws.
2. Two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate.
3. The Senate, by majority.
4. 4th Amendment. Warrants require probable cause and must particularly describe
   the place to be searched and the things to be seized.
5. 5th Amendment; the rule is double jeopardy.
6. Ten became Amendments 1–10, ratified December 15, 1791. Proposed Article 2
   became the 27th Amendment in 1992; proposed Article 1 was never ratified.

## Unit Assessment (44 points)

**§1 (16 points, 2 each):**

1. Any three: tax, regulate commerce, enforce laws, create national courts, raise
   an army directly, amend without unanimity.
2. A bicameral Congress: House apportioned by population, Senate two per state.
3. Enslaved people counted as three-fifths of a person, for representation and
   for direct taxation.
4. First: Delaware, December 7, 1787. Ninth: New Hampshire, June 21, 1788. Last:
   Rhode Island, May 29, 1790.
5. Federalists supported ratification and a stronger national government
   (Hamilton, Madison, Jay). Anti-Federalists opposed ratification, feared a
   distant national government, and demanded a bill of rights (Brutus, Mason,
   Henry).
6. Two-thirds of each house.
7. 10th Amendment: powers not delegated to the national government, nor prohibited
   to the states, are reserved to the states or the people.
8. December 15, 1791; Congress originally proposed twelve.

**§2 (10 points):**

1. (2) Both agree factions and abuses of power are real dangers to liberty.
2. (3) Madison's remedy: control factions' effects through size and structure —
   many competing interests plus separated powers. Brutus's remedy: keep government
   small and close, and write explicit limits (a bill of rights) before ratifying.
3. (3) Brutus's worry was answered by the Bill of Rights — but only partly: the
   amendments listed limits, yet they originally bound only the federal
   government, and the scope of national power (necessary-and-proper vs. 10th
   Amendment) remained contested. Accept reasoned "complete"/"partial" verdicts
   with a fact.
4. (2) Source A is an elite argument written to persuade New York ratifiers; it
   shows what supporters argued, not what ordinary farmers, artisans, or
   laborers thought.

**§3 (6 points):**

1. (3) Yes, the bill becomes law. House: 290/435 = exactly two-thirds. Senate:
   68/100 = 68%, above the two-thirds threshold (66.7%). Both houses cleared the
   override bar, so the veto is overridden.
2. (2) The president nominates; the Senate confirms by majority.
3. (1) Any one: appropriations (funding the courts), impeachment of judges
   (House charges, Senate tries), or creating/abolishing lower courts.

**§4 (10 points, 5 each):**

1. 4th Amendment. Rule: searches require a warrant on probable cause describing
   the place and items, unless an exception applies. Decision: lawful — the
   warrant is valid and the seizure matches it. Fact changing it: a warrantless
   search, or seizing items not listed in the warrant.
2. 1st Amendment. Rule: "Congress shall make no law… abridging the freedom of
   speech, or of the press." Decision: the law violates the 1st Amendment.
   Fact changing it: if the actor were private (a private publisher refusing to
   print), not the government.

**§5 (10 points):** Score: thesis answering the question (3); two pieces of
evidence from the unit — e.g., the necessary-and-proper clause vs. the 10th
Amendment, the ratify-and-recommend model, or the postponed slavery clauses (4);
one stated limit (2); clear writing (1). Model thesis: "No — the Constitution did
not settle the scope of national power; it framed the argument. The
necessary-and-proper clause points toward expansion while the 10th Amendment
points toward reservation, and the ratifying states' recommended amendments show
the question was deferred, not decided. The limit: this argument uses the 1787–1791
record only; later amendments and court decisions reshaped the answer."
