# Unit 03 — Constitution, Ratification, Rights, Institutions

**Grade 8 Social Studies · Weeks 11–14 · 20 sessions (~45 minutes each)**
**Track:** [Grade 8 Social Studies](../../README.md) · [Scope and sequence](../../scope-and-sequence.md) · Issue #41

## Essential question

Who got to decide what the Constitution meant — and who was left out of the decision?

## Overview

Winning independence did not settle how Americans would govern themselves. The first
attempt, the Articles of Confederation (in force March 1, 1781), made the states a "firm
league of friendship" with a Congress that could ask for money but not tax, declare war
but not enforce its own laws, and change its rules only if all thirteen states agreed.
When Massachusetts farmers rose in Shays' Rebellion (1786–1787) and the Confederation
could not respond, even many skeptics of strong government agreed the system had to change.

The Constitutional Convention met in Philadelphia from May 25 to September 17, 1787 —
55 delegates from twelve states (Rhode Island sent none), behind closed doors, with
George Washington presiding. The delegates did not simply revise the Articles; they
wrote a new plan, then fought over it in public for ten months. The Virginia Plan
(proportional representation) and the New Jersey Plan (equal state votes) collided over
who counts and who decides; the Great Compromise split the difference, and the
Three-Fifths Compromise counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for
representation and taxation — boosting slave-state power without freeing anyone.

Ratification was itself a contest. Article VII required nine of thirteen states, and each
state held a special convention. Federalists (Hamilton, Madison, Jay — 85 *Federalist*
essays) argued that a large republic with separated powers could control factions;
Anti-Federalists (notably "Brutus") warned that a distant national government would
swallow the states and demanded a bill of rights. Delaware ratified first (December 7,
1787, 30–0); New Hampshire's vote on June 21, 1788 (57–47) made nine; Virginia (89–79)
and New York (30–27) followed narrowly; North Carolina (November 21, 1789) and Rhode
Island (May 29, 1790) came last. The price of agreement was a Bill of Rights: Congress
proposed twelve amendments on September 25, 1789, and ten were ratified on
December 15, 1791.

Learners in this unit:

- explain why the Articles failed, using the tax, enforcement, and amendment rules —
  not just the label "weak";
- compare the Virginia and New Jersey plans and explain each compromise as a bargain
  with winners, losers, and postponed problems;
- close-read paired Federalist and Anti-Federalist arguments and judge which fears
  were answered by the Constitution's structure and which by the Bill of Rights;
- trace how the three branches check one another and how federalism divides power;
- apply the first ten amendments to new scenarios and explain what each protects;
- argue, with claim, counterclaim, evidence, and stated limits, whether ratification
  was democratic — including who could vote and who could not.

This unit assumes the U02 habits (claim + evidence + limit; maker/audience/purpose
source criticism) and adds institutional reasoning: rules, incentives, and who benefits
from a rule.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this unit, learners will be able to:

1. **Explain the Articles' failure** — name at least three structural weaknesses
   (taxation, enforcement, amendment) and connect each to a concrete breakdown,
   including Shays' Rebellion (D2.Civ.3.6-8; D2.His.14.6-8).
2. **Explain the Convention's compromises** — compare the Virginia and New Jersey
   plans, explain the Great Compromise and the Three-Fifths Compromise with a
   worked counting example, and state what each compromise postponed
   (D2.Civ.5.6-8; D2.His.4.6-8).
3. **Analyze the ratification debate** — paraphrase one Federalist and one
   Anti-Federalist argument, explain how the Massachusetts "ratify and recommend"
   model broke the deadlock, and sequence ratification with dates and votes
   (D2.Civ.8.6-8; D2.Civ.9.6-8).
4. **Explain the Constitution's structure** — map the three branches' powers, trace
   at least two checks (veto override; appointment confirmation or impeachment)
   through a full chain, and distinguish delegated, concurrent, and reserved powers
   (D2.Civ.4.6-8; D2.Civ.5.6-8).
5. **Apply the Bill of Rights** — state what each of the first ten amendments
   protects and decide at least four new scenarios with reasons tied to specific
   amendments (D2.Civ.3.6-8; D2.Civ.12.6-8).
6. **Argue about ratification's democratic character** — write an argument on
   whether ratification was democratic that states a claim and a genuine
   counterclaim, cites at least three sources, names who could and could not vote,
   and states one limit of the evidence (D3.3.6-8; D3.4.6-8; D4.1.6-8; D4.4.6-8).

## Prerequisites

From U02: sequencing events on a timeline; claim + evidence + one stated limit;

contexts. Learners should be comfortable with the U01/U02 vocabulary of primary
sources, evidence limits, and perspective.

## Vocabulary

**Confederation** — a league of states that keep most of their sovereignty while
acting together on limited matters. **Sovereignty** — supreme authority to govern.
**Unanimity** — agreement by everyone; under the Articles, amendments needed all
thirteen states. **Supermajority** — a vote threshold above a simple majority (nine
of thirteen under the Articles; two-thirds to override a veto). **Ratification** —
formal approval that makes a document legally binding. **Federalist** — a supporter
of ratifying the 1787 Constitution and a stronger national government. **Anti-Federalist** —
an opponent of ratification who feared a distant national government and wanted
explicit rights protections. **Faction** — a group united by an interest that works
against the rights of others or the common good (Madison's term). **Bicameral** —
having two legislative chambers. **Proportional representation** — seats assigned by
population. **Compromise** — a settlement in which each side gives something up.
**Enumerated powers** — powers specifically listed for Congress in Article I, §8.
**Necessary and proper clause** — the Article I, §8 power to make laws needed to carry
out the enumerated powers (called the "elastic clause" by critics). **Supremacy clause** —
Article VI: the Constitution and national laws are the "supreme Law of the Land."
**Separation of powers** — dividing government into legislative, executive, and
judicial branches. **Checks and balances** — powers each branch holds over the others.
**Veto** — the president's refusal to sign a bill into law. **Override** — Congress
passing a vetoed bill again with two-thirds of both houses. **Impeachment** — the
House charging a federal official; the Senate then tries the case. **Federalism** —
dividing power between a national government and state governments. **Reserved powers** —
powers kept by the states or the people (10th Amendment). **Due process** — fair legal
procedures the government must follow before depriving someone of life, liberty, or
property. **Amendment** — a formal change to the Constitution (Article V process).

**Factual claims verified 2026-10-27 for this unit:** Articles of Confederation —
approved by the Second Continental Congress November 15, 1777; in force March 1, 1781
(Maryland last) until the Constitution took effect in 1789; one vote per state; nine
of thirteen states needed for major laws; amendments required unanimous consent; no
power to tax, no executive, no national courts (National Constitution Center;
U.S. House Ben's Guide background; University of Wisconsin Center for the Study of
the American Constitution). Shays' Rebellion — Massachusetts farmers' uprising,
August 1786–February 1787, including the January 1787 attack on the Springfield
arsenal; put down by state militia, not by Congress. Convention — met May 25–
September 17, 1787, in Philadelphia; 55 delegates attended from twelve states
(Rhode Island sent none); Washington presided; 39 of the 42 still present signed on
September 17, 1787; Mason, Randolph, and Gerry refused (National Park Service;
Britannica). Virginia Plan introduced May 29, 1787 (Randolph); New Jersey Plan
(Paterson) followed; Great Compromise adopted July 16, 1787 (House by population,
Senate two per state); Three-Fifths Compromise counted three-fifths of enslaved
persons for representation and direct taxation; first House apportionment in
Article I, §2 gave Virginia 10 seats and Delaware 1. Ratification (Article VII,
nine of thirteen) — Delaware December 7, 1787 (30–0); Pennsylvania December 12,
1787 (46–23); New Jersey December 18, 1787 (38–0); Georgia January 2, 1788 (26–0);
Connecticut January 9, 1788 (128–40); Massachusetts February 6, 1788 (187–168, with
recommended amendments); Maryland April 28, 1788 (63–11); South Carolina May 23,
1788 (149–73); New Hampshire June 21, 1788 (57–47), the ninth; Virginia June 25,
1788 (89–79); New York July 26, 1788 (30–27); North Carolina November 21, 1789
(194–77); Rhode Island May 29, 1790 (34–32) (Ben's Guide, U.S. Government
Publishing Office). New government commenced March 4, 1789. *Federalist* essays —
85 essays by Hamilton, Madison, and Jay as "Publius," 1787–1788; No. 10 (Madison,
November 22, 1787, on factions and the large republic); No. 51 (Madison, February
1788, "ambition must be made to counteract ambition"); "Brutus" No. 1 (October 18,
1787, attributed to Robert Yates) warned that a large republic and the
necessary-and-proper and supremacy clauses threatened liberty and the states.
Bill of Rights — Congress proposed twelve amendments September 25, 1789;
Articles 3–12 were ratified December 15, 1791, becoming Amendments 1–10; proposed
Article 2 became the 27th Amendment in 1992; proposed Article 1 was never ratified
(National Archives). Judicial review was established later, in *Marbury v. Madison*
(1803), not by the 1787 text. All quoted primary sources are public domain
(pre-1929 documents). The town-hall illustration is a labeled AI reconstruction,
not evidence. The Three-Fifths and representation arithmetic in Lessons 2 and
practice Task B uses simplified fictional numbers, labeled as such — it is not
census data.
