# Lesson 2 — The Convention's Bargains: Representation, Slavery, and Power

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can compare the Virginia and New Jersey
plans, explain the Great Compromise and the Three-Fifths Compromise, and state what
each compromise postponed.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, a calculator for the
counting example.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Recall from Lesson 1: under the Articles, how many votes did each state get, and how
many states had to agree to amend? Now predict: at a convention to replace the
Articles, would a large state and a small state want the same voting rule? Why?

## Explicit teaching

The convention met in Philadelphia from May 25 to September 17, 1787. Fifty-five
delegates attended from twelve states — Rhode Island sent none — and George
Washington presided. The doors were closed and the windows shut: delegates could
argue freely and change their minds without daily public pressure. (That secrecy
later became an Anti-Federalist complaint — see Lesson 3.)

Two plans framed the fight over representation:

- **The Virginia Plan** (introduced May 29, 1787, by Edmund Randolph from James
  Madison's draft): a strong national government with a **bicameral** legislature
  (two chambers), both apportioned by **population**. Large states loved it; it
  would give them the most seats.
- **The New Jersey Plan** (William Paterson, June 15, 1787): keep a **one-chamber**
  legislature with **one vote per state**, as under the Articles, but give Congress
  real powers to tax and regulate commerce. Small states loved it; it protected them
  from being outvoted.

The deadlock broke with the **Great Compromise** (also called the Connecticut
Compromise, from Roger Sherman's proposal, adopted July 16, 1787): a two-chamber
Congress — the **House of Representatives** apportioned by population, and the
**Senate** with **two senators per state**, regardless of size. Both plans' supporters
got half of what they wanted, and both gave something up. The Constitution's own
first apportionment (Article I, §2) shows the split in practice: Virginia got 10
House seats, Delaware got 1 — but in the Senate they were equal at 2 each.

The second bargain was darker. Southern delegates wanted enslaved people counted
for representation (more seats) but not for taxation; northern delegates argued that
if enslaved people had no rights, they should not count at all. The **Three-Fifths
Compromise** settled it: for representation and direct taxation, each enslaved
person counted as **three-fifths** of a free person. It did not free anyone, and it
did not give enslaved people a vote. It gave slave states extra seats in the House
and extra votes in the Electoral College — political power built on people who were
denied every political right. Two related bargains postponed the slavery question
further: Congress could not ban the importation of enslaved people before 1808, and
a fugitive-slave clause required the return of people who escaped to free states.

The convention also created a single executive — a president, chosen not by popular
vote but by an **Electoral College** — and a federal judiciary, with the Supreme
Court named and lower courts left for Congress to create. On September 17, 1787,
thirty-nine of the forty-two delegates still present signed. Three refused, including
George Mason of Virginia, who objected above all to the lack of a bill of rights.
Their refusal is the bridge to Lesson 3: the fight moved from Philadelphia to the
states.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *bicameral* = two chambers. *Proportional
representation* = seats by population. A *compromise* is not a perfect solution; it
is a deal each side can live with — and it always leaves someone out.

## Worked example 1 — Why the Great Compromise split the difference

Use simplified fictional numbers (labeled practice numbers, not census data):

- Big State: 400,000 free people. Small State: 60,000 free people.
- Virginia Plan (population only): seats in proportion, roughly 400:60 — the big
  state has about 7 seats for every 1 of the small state's.
- New Jersey Plan (one vote per state): 1:1 — the small state's vote counts as much
  as the big state's, even though it has one-seventh the people.
- Great Compromise: House ~7:1 (population), Senate 1:1 (equal). A bill needs both
  chambers, so neither the big states nor the small states can pass a law alone.

Conclusion: the compromise did not decide which principle — population or state
equality — was right. It built *both* principles into the structure, which is why
the Senate still gives small states disproportionate power today.

## Worked example 2 — Counting three-fifths: who gains?

Use simplified fictional numbers (labeled practice numbers, not census data):

- State A: 120,000 free people + 90,000 enslaved people.
- State B: 170,000 free people + 0 enslaved people.

Under the Three-Fifths rule, State A's counted population is
120,000 + (3/5 × 90,000) = 120,000 + 54,000 = **174,000**.
State B's counted population is **170,000**.

So State A gets *more* representation than State B even though it has *fewer* free
people — and the 90,000 enslaved people whose presence creates those extra seats
cannot vote, cannot testify in most courts, and cannot leave. Now compare with the
alternatives the delegates rejected: counting enslaved people as whole persons would
have given State A 210,000 (even more slave-state power); counting them as zero
would have given it 120,000 (far less). Three-fifths was a bargain between
slave-state and free-state *delegates* — the enslaved people themselves were never
at the table.

## Guided practice

1. A delegate from a small state says, "Population rule means we will never matter."
   Answer her with one sentence using the Senate's design.
2. A delegate from a large state says, "Equal Senate votes mean a voter in a small
   state counts more than a voter in mine." Is he mathematically right? Explain.
3. Why did southern delegates want enslaved people counted for representation but
   *not* for taxation? What does that double position reveal about how they viewed
   enslaved people? (Hint: property for one purpose, persons for another.)
4. The convention also agreed not to ban slave importation before 1808. What problem
   did that postpone, and for whom?

   representation argument that continued after 1787.

## Application

Write a six-sentence delegate's diary entry dated July 17, 1787, the day after the
Great Compromise passed. You may write as a large-state delegate, a small-state
delegate, or an enslaved person's advocate observing from outside (the adult
previews this option — see the teacher guide). Include: what was decided, what you
gained, what you gave up, and one worry about the future. (This entry becomes the
seed of your project §2 delegate brief.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: (1) What does the House apportion by, and what does the Senate
apportion by? (2) What fraction did the Three-Fifths Compromise use, and for what two
purposes? (3) Name one thing the convention postponed rather than decided.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the counting examples may be done with a calculator or with
  manipulatives (counters grouped in fives, three counted per five enslaved).
- **Alternate response mode:** the diary entry may be dictated or drawn as a
  four-panel sequence (decided / gained / gave up / worry).
- **Extension:** compare the Electoral College bargain to the Great Compromise:
  what two principles does it combine, and who benefits from each?

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** "Three-fifths meant enslaved people were considered
  three-fifths of a human being." Correct carefully: the clause was a *counting rule
  for apportionment and taxation*, not a philosophical statement about humanity — but
  its moral meaning is exactly what students should debate: it treated enslaved
  people as a basis for someone else's power. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).
- **Sensitive preview:** slavery is central to this lesson; the adult previews the
  diary-entry options and the language ("enslaved people" rather than "slaves"
  where the lesson models it) before assigning.
