# Lesson 1 — A League That Could Not Tax, Enforce, or Amend

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can name three structural weaknesses of the
Articles of Confederation and explain one breakdown each weakness caused.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook, the timeline from U02
(ends 1783) to extend to 1789.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

Answer in one sentence each: (1) What did the United States win in 1783? (2) What
does a government need in order to pay soldiers and service a debt? Keep your answers;
you will test them against the Articles' actual rules.

## Explicit teaching

After independence, Americans feared a strong central government — they had just
fought one. So the Second Continental Congress wrote a deliberately weak first
constitution, the **Articles of Confederation**: approved November 15, 1777, and in
force from March 1, 1781, when Maryland, the last state, ratified. It called the
union "a firm league of friendship." Each state kept "its sovereignty, freedom, and
independence, and every power… which is not by this confederation expressly delegated."

Congress under the Articles had one chamber. Each state had **one vote**, no matter
how large. It could declare war, make treaties, coin money, and admit new states. But
three missing powers broke it:

1. **No power to tax.** Congress could only *ask* states for money ("requisitions").
   States often paid little or nothing. The national government could not pay its war
   debts, fund an army, or keep its promises to veterans.
2. **No power to enforce.** There was no executive to carry out laws and no national
   court system to settle disputes. If a state ignored Congress, Congress had no
   lawful way to make it comply.
3. **No workable way to change the rules.** Passing an important law needed **nine of
   thirteen** states. Amending the Articles needed **all thirteen** — unanimity. Any
   single state could veto a fix.

Add a fourth weakness: Congress could not regulate interstate or foreign commerce,
so states taxed each other's goods and trade policy was chaos.

Then came the test. Massachusetts farmers, many of them war veterans, were crushed by
debt and taxes after the war; courts were foreclosing their farms. In August 1786 they
began closing courts by force, and in January 1787 a force led by Daniel Shays, a
former Continental Army captain, attacked the federal arsenal at Springfield,
Massachusetts. Congress could not raise an army to respond — it had no money and no
tax power — and Massachusetts had to rely on a state militia largely funded by private
donations. The rebellion was put down by February 1787, but the lesson stuck: a
government that cannot act in a crisis is not a government. Within months, Congress
called a convention in Philadelphia — officially to *revise* the Articles.

**Key vocabulary in context:** *sovereignty* is supreme authority — the Articles left
it with the states. *Unanimity* means everyone must agree; one "no" kills an amendment.
A *supermajority* (nine of thirteen) is a threshold above half; it protects minorities
but makes action hard.

## Worked example 1 — The tax request that failed

Suppose Congress needs $3,000,000 to pay war debts and asks each of the thirteen
states for a share. Under the Articles, Congress can request but cannot compel.

- Step 1: Congress votes the request (needs nine states to pass it).
- Step 2: Each state legislature decides whether — and how much — to pay.
- Step 3: Suppose five states pay in full, four pay half, and four pay nothing.
  Congress receives far less than $3,000,000 and has no court or officer to collect
  the rest.

Conclusion: the problem is not that the states were selfish; it is that the *rule*
made payment voluntary. A tax power would have let Congress collect directly. This is
why the Constitution later gave Congress the power "to lay and collect taxes"
(Article I, §8).

## Worked example 2 — The amendment that one state could kill

Suppose twelve states agree the Articles should be amended to let Congress tax imports.
Under the unanimity rule:

- Step 1: Twelve states approve.
- Step 2: The thirteenth state refuses — perhaps because it profits from its own
  import duties.
- Step 3: The amendment fails. Twelve-to-one is not enough.

Conclusion: unanimity gives every state a veto over reform. That is why, when the
Constitution was written, its framers set a different bar — nine of thirteen states
to ratify, and later a two-thirds/three-fourths amendment process — instead of
unanimity. Notice the trade-off: a lower bar makes change possible, but also lets a
minority of states be bound by a decision they opposed. That trade-off is the whole
story of ratification in Lesson 3.

## Guided practice

With an adult or partner, work through these. Hints are in parentheses.

1. Congress wants to raise an army. Under the Articles, who actually supplies the
   soldiers and the money? (Think: who kept sovereignty?)
2. A state taxes goods from its neighbor. Can Congress stop it? Why or why not?
   (Check the commerce power.)
3. Shays' men attack the Springfield arsenal. List the two things Congress lacks
   that a normal government would use to respond. (Money and…?)
4. Explain in your own words why "one state, one vote" pleased small states and
   worried large ones.

## Independent practice

1. In two sentences, explain the difference between a requisition and a tax.
2. Which weakness — no tax power, no enforcement, or unanimity — do you think was
   most damaging? Give one reason and one piece of evidence from this lesson.
3. Error analysis: A classmate says, "The Articles failed because Americans were too
   lazy to pay taxes." What is wrong with that explanation? Rewrite it as a claim
   about *rules*, not character.
4. Extend your U02 timeline: add 1781 (Articles in force), 1786–1787 (Shays'
   Rebellion), and May 1787 (convention called). Write one cause-effect arrow
   between two of the new events.

## Application

You are a delegate in 1787 asked to fix *one* Articles rule. Write a five-sentence
proposal: name the rule, state the breakdown it caused, propose the replacement rule,
name one group that would object and why, and state how your rule answers the
objection. (Keep this proposal — project §1 revisits it.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: name the three structural weaknesses and, for each, one
breakdown it caused. If you miss one, re-read the "three missing powers" section
before Week 11 S2.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the adult reads the Articles excerpts aloud in S2; key terms
  are defined in context above. Learners may answer orally or by dictation.
- **Alternate response mode:** the exit check may be given as a three-row table
  (weakness | breakdown) instead of sentences.
- **Extension:** research the Northwest Ordinance of 1787 — the one major
  achievement under the Articles — and explain why a weak Congress could still pass
  it (it organized western land, which did not require taxing the states).

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** "The Articles failed because the founders were stupid."
  Redirect to rules and incentives: the Articles did exactly what their designers
  intended (keep power in the states) and failed at what the country then needed
  (coordinated action). See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md) and
  [answer-key.md](../answer-key.md) Lesson 1.
- **Sensitive preview:** Shays' Rebellion involved armed protest and foreclosure;
  frame it as debt and courts, not as a battle narrative.
