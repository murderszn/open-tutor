# Lesson 5 — The Bill of Rights: Ten Amendments, Written Down

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can state what each of the first ten
amendments protects and decide new scenarios with reasons tied to specific amendments.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, a notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

From Lesson 3: what did the Anti-Federalists demand, and what deal made the
Constitution ratifiable despite that demand? Write two sentences.

## Explicit teaching

The Bill of Rights was the price of ratification. During the state conventions,
several states ratified with recommended amendments; James Madison, who had once
thought a bill of rights unnecessary, drafted the response in the First Congress.
On **September 25, 1789**, Congress proposed **twelve** amendments. Ten of them —
proposed Articles 3 through 12 — were ratified by three-fourths of the states on
**December 15, 1791**, and became Amendments 1–10. (Proposed Article 2 waited until
1992 to become the 27th Amendment; proposed Article 1, on the size of the House,
was never ratified.)

Read each amendment as a *limit on government*, not a gift from it. The First
Amendment, for example, begins "Congress shall make no law…" — it binds the
government, not the citizen.

**The ten, in one line each:**

1. **Religion, speech, press, assembly, petition** — no established national
   religion; free exercise; no law abridging speech, press, peaceful assembly, or
   petitioning the government.
2. **Arms** — the right of the people to keep and bear arms, in the context of a
   "well regulated Militia."
3. **Quartering** — no soldiers housed in private homes in peacetime without the
   owner's consent.
4. **Search and seizure** — no unreasonable searches; warrants require probable cause
   and must describe the place and items.
5. **Rights of the accused (I)** — grand-jury indictment for serious crimes; no
   double jeopardy; no compelled self-incrimination; no deprivation of life,
   liberty, or property without **due process**; no taking of private property for
   public use without just compensation.
6. **Rights of the accused (II)** — speedy, public trial by an impartial jury;
   notice of charges; confrontation of witnesses; compulsory process for favorable
   witnesses; assistance of counsel.
7. **Civil jury** — jury trial preserved in federal civil cases over $20.
8. **Punishment** — no excessive bail, no excessive fines, no cruel and unusual
   punishments.
9. **Unenumerated rights** — listing some rights does not deny others retained by
   the people.
10. **Reserved powers** — powers not delegated to the national government, nor
    prohibited to the states, are reserved to the states or the people.

Two reading rules matter. First, the amendments originally bound only the *federal*
government; their application to the states came much later, through the 14th
Amendment's due-process clause — a U07 topic, so do not assume it here. Second, no
right is absolute in practice: courts balance rights against one another and
against public safety. Your job in the scenarios is to *argue* from the text, not
to announce a final verdict — just as real courts argue.

## Worked example 1 — A search without a warrant

*Scenario:* A federal officer, with no warrant and no emergency, opens a citizen's
locked desk and reads private letters to find evidence of tax evasion.

- Step 1: Identify the government action — a search of private papers.
- Step 2: Find the amendment — the 4th: "The right of the people to be secure…
  against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated," and warrants
  require probable cause and particular description.
- Step 3: Apply — no warrant, no emergency, private papers: the search violates the
  4th Amendment's rule.
- Step 4: State the limit — if the officer *had* a valid warrant based on probable
  cause, the same search could be lawful. The amendment regulates *how* government
  searches, not whether it ever may.

## Worked example 2 — Punishing speech

*Scenario:* Congress passes a law making it a crime to publish a newspaper editorial
criticizing the president.

- Step 1: Identify the government action — a law punishing published criticism.
- Step 2: Find the amendment — the 1st: "Congress shall make no law… abridging the
  freedom of speech, or of the press."
- Step 3: Apply — the law directly abridges press freedom; it violates the 1st
  Amendment.
- Step 4: State the limit — the amendment binds *Congress* (the government), not a
  private newspaper owner who refuses to print a letter. Government action is the
  trigger for the whole Bill of Rights.

## Guided practice

Decide each scenario. Name the amendment, quote or paraphrase its rule, and state
one limit or complication.

1. A soldier demands a family house him during peacetime without consent.
2. A defendant is tried twice for the same federal crime after an acquittal.
3. A town meeting bans all residents from petitioning the government about a new
   road. (Whose action matters here — and is it covered? Think about *which*
   government the amendments bound in 1791.)
4. A court sets bail at an amount no one could possibly pay for a minor offense.

## Independent practice

1. Write one line for each amendment from memory, then check against the lesson.
   Mark any you missed and re-study only those.
2. A classmate says the 9th Amendment is "empty because it doesn't list anything."
   Explain what work it does: why would the framers add an amendment that lists no
   rights?
3. Which amendment would Brutus (Lesson 3) have cared about most, and why? Which
   would Madison (as a Federalist) have thought least necessary, and why?
4. Error analysis: A scenario says, "A private social club expels a member for
   criticizing the club president; the 1st Amendment is violated." What is wrong?
   (Hint: who acted?)

## Application

Write a five-sentence "citizen's brief" for one of these new cases (adult assigns
one, or choose): (a) federal agents search a traveler's phone at the border without
a warrant; (b) a federal agency fines a speaker for a protest speech; (c) a court
denies a defendant a lawyer in a serious criminal trial. State the amendment, the
rule, your decision, one counterargument, and what further fact would change your
mind. (Briefs feed project §3's scenario set.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: (1) How many amendments did Congress propose in 1789, and
how many became the Bill of Rights? (2) Which amendment covers search warrants?
(3) Which covers double jeopardy? (4) What does the 10th Amendment reserve, and to
whom? (5) Name one limit of the Bill of Rights as ratified in 1791.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** amendment summaries are given in plain language in the
  lesson; the adult may read the original First Amendment text aloud and unpack it
  clause by clause.
- **Alternate response mode:** scenario decisions may be given as a table
  (scenario | amendment | decision | reason) instead of paragraphs.
- **Extension:** compare the Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776) with the Bill of
  Rights: which phrases match, and what did Madison add or change?

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** "The Bill of Rights gives us our rights." Correct: the
  founding-era theory was that rights pre-exist government; the amendments *protect*
  listed rights against government action. The 9th Amendment exists precisely to say
  the list is not exhaustive. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).
- **Sensitive preview:** scenarios touch policing, punishment, and protest; the
  adult previews them and keeps discussion on the constitutional rule, not on any
  learner's personal experience.
