# Lesson 6 — A Contested Settlement: What It Settled, Postponed, and Left Open

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can sort the Constitution's outcomes into
settled, postponed, and left open; evaluate a source's relevancy for a ratification
inquiry; and assemble the claim, counterclaim, and limits of the unit's argument.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, your project portfolio, a notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

List three things the Constitution decided and three things it did not decide, from
memory. Compare with a partner: where do your lists disagree? Disagreement is the
lesson — the document's silences were themselves decisions.

## Explicit teaching

The Constitution was a settlement, not a solution. Sort its outcomes three ways:

**Settled (for its time):**

- A national government that can tax, regulate commerce, raise armies, and make
  laws that bind the states (supremacy clause).
- A bicameral Congress (House by population, Senate equal), a single president, and
  a federal judiciary.
- Ratification by nine of thirteen states, not unanimity — a new rule for making
  fundamental law.
- A written list of individual protections, once the Bill of Rights was added.

**Postponed:**

- Slavery. The Three-Fifths Compromise, the 1808 importation clause, and the
  fugitive-slave clause bought union by deferring the question. Every postponement
  made the eventual reckoning larger — the Civil War is U06's story, but its roots
  are here.
- The scope of national power. The necessary-and-proper clause and the 10th
  Amendment point in opposite directions; the fight over which wins has never
  ended.
- Who counts as a citizen with rights. The Constitution used the word "citizen"
  without defining national citizenship for everyone — that definition waited for
  the 14th Amendment (U07).

**Left open (to politics, courts, and amendment):**

- How the amendment process itself would be used — 27 amendments so far, the first
  ten in 1791.
- Whether courts could strike down laws (answered in 1803, not 1787).
- The practical meaning of each right — what "unreasonable" searches, "cruel and
  unusual" punishments, and "due process" mean in new cases. Those meanings are made
  over time, by argument.

Now the unit's hard question: **was ratification democratic?** Consider the evidence
both ways. For "yes": the Constitution was debated in public for ten months, in
newspapers anyone literate could read; it was approved by conventions chosen for
that purpose; several states attached recommended amendments, and the Bill of
Rights followed. For "no": in nearly every state, only adult men meeting property
requirements could vote for convention delegates — women, enslaved people, most
free Black people, and Native peoples did not vote, though the government would
rule them all; Rhode Island's voters rejected the Constitution in a 1788 referendum
(2,708–237) and were effectively coerced by the threat of being treated as a
foreign country before ratifying in 1790; and the Philadelphia convention itself met
in secret. A good answer holds both sides and names what the evidence cannot settle
— for example, we cannot know what people who were barred from voting would have
chosen, because the record was made by those who could.

**Evaluating a source for this inquiry** (D2.His.13.6-8): ask maker, date, place,
audience, and purpose — then ask *relevancy for this question*. A Federalist essay
is highly relevant to "what did supporters argue?" but weak evidence for "what did
most ordinary people think?" A ratification vote count is strong evidence for "how
close was it?" but silent on "why did delegates vote that way?" No single source
answers the democratic question; the argument must triangulate.

## Worked example 1 — Sorting an outcome

Claim: "The Constitution settled the slavery question."

- Test: does the text end slavery, or does it manage it? It counts enslaved people
  for representation, bars Congress from banning importation before 1808, and
  requires returning fugitives. None of these ends slavery.
- Sort: **postponed**, not settled. Evidence: the 1808 clause itself names a future
  date — a built-in postponement.
- Lesson: a clause that sets a future date is a confession that the issue was too
  hard to decide now. Learners should flag future dates as postponement markers.

## Worked example 2 — Evaluating a source's relevancy

Question: "Did ordinary farmers support ratification?"

- Source A: *Federalist No. 10* (Madison, 1787). Maker: a supporter, writing to
  persuade New Yorkers. Relevancy: low for this question — it shows elite argument,
  not farmer opinion.
- Source B: the Massachusetts ratification vote, 187–168. Maker: the convention
  record. Relevancy: medium — it shows a close division among delegates, but
  delegates were not farmers at large.
- Source C: a petition from Massachusetts farmers against the Constitution (adult
  selects a public-domain example). Relevancy: high for farmer opinion — but one
  petition is not all farmers.
- Conclusion: the honest answer combines B's closeness with C's voice and notes the
  gap: no referendum of all farmers exists. State the limit in your argument.

## Guided practice

1. Sort these into settled / postponed / left open, with one reason each: the
   Senate's equal representation; the 1808 slave-importation clause; the meaning of
   "due process"; the nine-state ratification rule.
2. A source says, "The Constitution was adopted unanimously." What is wrong, at
   which stage? (Hint: distinguish signing, ratification, and the people's vote.)
3. For the question "Was ratification democratic?", rank these by relevancy and
   justify the order: a state's vote tally; a Federalist essay; a modern textbook
   summary; a delegate's private letter.
4. Name one group barred from voting and one consequence of that exclusion for how
   we read the ratification record today.

## Independent practice

1. Write a paragraph: "The Constitution's greatest postponement was ___ because
   ___." Use one clause as evidence and one later consequence (through 1877 only —
   do not import later history you have not studied).
2. Evaluate one new source the adult selects (a public-domain ratification-era
   document) for the democratic question: maker, date, audience, purpose,
   relevancy, one limit.
3. Error analysis: A classmate writes, "Everyone agreed to the Constitution in
   1787." Correct it with three facts: one refusal at signing, one close state
   vote, and one state that waited until 1790.
4. Draft your argument's limit sentence: "This argument cannot establish ___,
   because the evidence ___." (You will use it in practice Task H.)

## Application

Assemble the unit argument skeleton (full essay in practice Task H): compelling
question ("Was ratification democratic?"), your claim in one sentence, your
strongest counterclaim in one sentence, three sources with one line each on what
they prove, the voter-inclusion analysis in two sentences, and your limit sentence.
Exchange skeletons with a partner and critique one another's counterclaims: is the
counterclaim genuine, or a straw man?

## Exit check

Without looking back: (1) Name one settled outcome, one postponed outcome, and one
left-open outcome. (2) What makes a source relevant to a *particular* question?
(3) Name two groups who could not vote in the ratifying conventions and one
consequence for the record.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** sorting may be done with cards (one outcome per card, three
  labeled piles) instead of a written list.
- **Alternate response mode:** the argument skeleton may be dictated or presented
  orally.
- **Extension:** compare ratification's democratic character with the Articles'
  unanimity rule: which rule is more democratic — one that lets one state block
  everyone, or one that lets nine states bind four? Defend your answer with a
  principle, not just a preference.

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** "The founders were hypocrites, so the Constitution
  doesn't matter." Redirect: holding both truths — the document created a durable
  republic *and* excluded most people from its making — is the historical skill
  itself. Dismissal is not analysis. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).
- **Sensitive preview:** voter exclusion covers enslavement, gender, race, and
  Native dispossession; the adult frames this as who held power, previews the
  Rhode Island referendum figures, and keeps the discussion on evidence.
