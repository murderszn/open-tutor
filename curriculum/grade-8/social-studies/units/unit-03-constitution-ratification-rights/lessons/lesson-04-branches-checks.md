# Lesson 4 — Three Branches, Checks and Balances, Federalism

**Goal:** By the end of this lesson, you can map the three branches' powers, trace
at least two checks through a full chain, and distinguish delegated, concurrent,
and reserved powers.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** this lesson, the branches diagram
(`../assets/branches-checks-diagram.svg`), a notebook.

## Readiness check (3 minutes)

From Lesson 3: what did Madison mean by "Ambition must be made to counteract
ambition"? Write one sentence. Then predict: if Congress passes a law the president
hates, what can each side do?

## Explicit teaching

The Constitution divides national power three ways — **separation of powers** — and
then gives each branch tools to resist the others — **checks and balances**. The
diagram in `assets/` shows the layout; this lesson teaches you to trace it.

**Article I — Legislative (Congress).** A bicameral Congress: the **House of
Representatives** (apportioned by population, two-year terms) and the **Senate**
(two per state, six-year terms, staggered). Congress's **enumerated powers**
(Article I, §8) include taxing, borrowing, regulating commerce, coining money,
raising armies, and declaring war — plus the **necessary-and-proper clause**: the
power to make laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out the listed powers. That
clause is why the government's powers could grow: it is a tool, and its scope was
exactly what Brutus feared.

**Article II — Executive (the President).** A single president, four-year term,
chosen by the Electoral College. The president executes the laws, is commander in
chief, negotiates treaties (with two-thirds Senate consent), appoints judges and
officers (with Senate confirmation), and can **veto** bills.

**Article III — Judicial (the Courts).** "The judicial Power" rests in a Supreme
Court and lower courts Congress creates. Judges serve "during good Behaviour" —
effectively life tenure — so they need not please the president or Congress to keep
their jobs. Note carefully: the 1787 text does **not** say courts may strike down
laws. That power, **judicial review**, was established later, in *Marbury v. Madison*
(1803). Do not project it backward into the founding debate.

**Federalism** divides power a second way: between the national government and the
states. **Delegated (enumerated)** powers belong to the national government (coin
money, declare war). **Reserved powers** belong to the states or the people (the
10th Amendment) — running elections, local police powers, schools. **Concurrent
powers** are shared — both levels may tax, build roads, and establish courts. And
when national and state law conflict, the **supremacy clause** (Article VI) says
national law wins.

## Worked example 1 — Trace a veto override

Congress passes a bill funding a road. The president vetoes it. What happens?

1. The bill returns to Congress with the president's objections.
2. The House reconsiders. Override requires **two-thirds** of the House.
3. If the House reaches two-thirds, the Senate reconsiders. Override requires
   **two-thirds** of the Senate.
4. If both reach two-thirds, the bill becomes law *despite* the veto.

Why two-thirds, not a simple majority? Because the first vote already passed with a
majority — the override bar must be higher, or the veto would be meaningless. Count
the actors: president → House (2/3) → Senate (2/3). Miss one step and the chain
breaks.

## Worked example 2 — Trace an appointment

The president nominates a Supreme Court justice. What happens?

1. The president nominates.
2. The Senate holds hearings and votes. Confirmation requires a **majority** of the
   Senate.
3. If confirmed, the president appoints (commissions) the justice, who serves
   during good behavior.

Now the check runs the other way too: the Senate can *refuse* confirmation, and the
House can later impeach a justice (majority) with the Senate trying the case
(two-thirds to convict). Appointment is shared power, not presidential command.

## Guided practice

Use the diagram for these.

1. Congress passes a tax bill; the president signs it. A state refuses to collect
   it. Which level's law prevails, and which clause says so?
2. The president negotiates a treaty. Can it take effect without the Senate? What
   fraction of the Senate must consent?
3. A governor claims her state may coin its own money. Which power category answers
   her — delegated, reserved, or concurrent — and why?
4. Congress creates a new federal court. Which article authorizes that, and which
   branch staffs it?

## Independent practice

1. Draw the veto-override chain from memory (actors + thresholds), then check it
   against worked example 1.
2. Explain in two sentences why life tenure for judges is itself a check on the
   other branches.
3. Sort these powers into delegated / concurrent / reserved: declare war; run
   public schools; levy taxes; establish local police; coin money; license drivers.
   (One is tricky: taxing is concurrent — both levels levy taxes, on different
   bases.)
4. Error analysis: A classmate says, "The Supreme Court could always strike down
   unconstitutional laws — that's in the Constitution." Correct the error with the
   *Marbury* date and one sentence about why the distinction matters for reading
   the 1787 debate honestly.

## Application

A new problem: Congress wants a national rule about river pollution, but a state
already has its own stricter rule. Write a six-sentence analysis: (a) which level
may act, (b) what happens if the two rules conflict, (c) which branch would decide
a lawsuit about it, (d) one check another branch holds over that decision, and
(e) one question the Constitution's text does not settle. (This is the kind of
open-textured question the Bill of Rights scenarios in project §3 also raise.)

## Exit check

Without looking back: (1) Name the three branches and one core power of each.
(2) What fraction of each house overrides a veto? (3) What does the 10th Amendment
reserve, and to whom? (4) When was judicial review established, and why is that
date important?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** the diagram may be printed large or described aloud; all
  chains are also written as numbered steps in the lesson.
- **Alternate response mode:** chains may be drawn as flowcharts instead of
  sentences.
- **Extension:** research one real veto override (e.g., Congress overriding
  President Andrew Johnson's vetoes during Reconstruction — a U07 preview) and
  trace its chain.

## Teacher note

- **Likely misconception:** "Checks and balances means the branches are equal."
  Correct: they are *balanced*, not equal — Congress has the purse and the lawmaking
  power, the president has execution and the veto, courts have judgment but "neither
  force nor will" (Hamilton, *Federalist No. 78*). The point is mutual restraint, not
  symmetry. See [teacher-guide.md](../teacher-guide.md).
