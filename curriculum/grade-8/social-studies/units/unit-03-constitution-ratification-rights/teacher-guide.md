# Teacher Guide — Unit 03: Constitution, Ratification, Rights, Institutions

This guide is for the guiding adult. It is not student material. Keep it separate
from student pages when assigning work.

## Week-by-week notes

### Week 11 — Why the Articles failed

- **S1 (Lesson 1):** Teach the three missing powers as *rules*, not character
  flaws. If the learner says "the states were selfish," redirect: the Articles
  made payment voluntary by design. The worked examples are the lesson's core;
  do not skip the arithmetic of the amendment example (12–1 still fails).
- **S2 (Task A):** Read the Article II and V excerpts aloud. Learners often
  paraphrase "sovereignty" as "freedom" — press for "supreme authority kept by the
  states."
- **S3 (project §1):** The failure chain must have four links; the most common
  gap is link 3 (the failed response — Congress asking, states refusing). Check
  it before S4.
- **S4 (Task B):** Accept any of the three rules; score the causal link, not the
  choice.
- **S5 (quiz Part A):** Re-teach the amendment rule if Q4 is missed; it returns
  in Lessons 3 and 6 (nine-of-thirteen vs. unanimity).

### Week 12 — Compromises and ratification

- **S1 (Lesson 2):** Do the counting example on paper with counters if needed:
  group enslaved-person counters in fives and count three per five. The moral
  point — power built on people denied rights — must be stated explicitly by the
  adult, not left for the learner to infer.
- **S2 (Task C):** The table's "postponed" column is the point of the unit's
  Lesson 6; do not accept "nothing" for any compromise row.
- **S3 (project §2):** For the Mason position, note that Mason owned enslaved
  people while objecting to the lack of a bill of rights — a contradiction worth
  naming honestly if the learner raises it. The debate is assessed on fairness to
  both sides.
- **S4 (Lesson 3):** Show the reconstruction image with its caption and the
  "not a primary source" label every time. If the learner asks why women appear in
  the audience, explain: public meetings could include women listeners, but
  convention delegates and voters were, in nearly every state, adult men meeting
  property requirements. Do not let the image imply women voted.
- **S5 (quiz Part B):** The Three-Fifths arithmetic (100,000 + 30,000 = 130,000)
  is the most-missed item; re-teach with the Lesson 2 example if needed.

### Week 13 — Structure and rights

- **S1 (Lesson 4):** Print the branches diagram large or project it. Trace both
  worked chains aloud before assigning Task E. Stress the *Marbury* point twice:
  learners routinely backdate judicial review into 1787.
- **S2 (Task E):** Q4's correction: the Alien and Sedition Acts were never struck
  down by the Supreme Court; they expired or were repealed. Judicial review came
  in 1803.
- **S3 (Lesson 5):** Read the First Amendment's opening words aloud ("Congress
  shall make no law…") — the "binds government, not citizens" rule is the
  lesson's hinge.
- **S4 (Task F / project §3):** Task F Q2 is the hardest: in 1791 the Bill of
  Rights bound only the federal government, so a state newspaper ban is not
  decided by the 1st Amendment as ratified. Do not accept "the 1st Amendment
  bans it" without the federal-action analysis. Preview all scenarios for
  appropriateness before assigning.
- **S5 (quiz Part C):** If Q2 (veto fractions) is missed, re-trace the Lesson 4
  chain before Week 14.

### Week 14 — Synthesis

- **S1 (Lesson 6):** The settled/postponed/open sort works best as cards on a
  table. Insist that "postponed" items name *what was postponed and for whom*.
- **S2 (Task G):** Select the source in advance from a public-domain collection
  (see the resource pack). Check that the source's maker and side are identifiable;
  an unattributed excerpt cannot be evaluated for relevancy.
- **S3 (project §4):** The organizer needs two sources and one open question;
  a single-source organizer is incomplete.
- **S4 (Task H):** The essay's counterclaim must be genuine. If the learner's
  counterclaim is a straw man ("some might say the Constitution was bad"), send
  it back with the Lesson 6 both-sides evidence.
- **S5 (unit assessment):** §3's House figure (290 of 435) is exactly two-thirds;
  credit learners who show the division. Total: 44 points; see the answer key for
  the scoring bands.

## Sensitive-content preview

- **Enslavement** is central to Lessons 2 and 6 and to the Three-Fifths
  Compromise. Use "enslaved people" as the lesson models. Frame the compromise as
  a bargain *about* enslaved people made *without* them. Do not ask the learner to
  role-play an enslaved person; the Lesson 2 diary option for an outside observer
  is adult-previewed and optional.
- **Armed rebellion (Shays')** is taught as debt, courts, and government
  capacity — not as battle narrative. No graphic detail is included.
- **Exclusion from voting** (Lesson 6) covers gender, race, enslavement, and
  Native peoples. Keep the discussion on evidence: who could vote, what the record
  therefore shows and hides.
- **Policing, punishment, protest scenarios** (Lesson 5, Task F, project §3)
  stay on the constitutional rule. Do not connect scenarios to any learner's
  personal experience.

## Reteaching moves

- **Amendment thresholds confused (9/13 vs. unanimity vs. 2/3 vs. 3/4):** make a
  four-row table with the learner: Articles laws (9/13), Articles amendments
  (13/13), Constitution ratification (9/13), Constitution amendments (2/3 of
  Congress + 3/4 of states). Drill with one example per row.
- **Three-Fifths as "three-fifths of a person":** re-teach as a counting rule for
  apportionment and taxation, then discuss its moral meaning separately.
- **Judicial review backdated:** put 1787 and 1803 on the timeline and ask, "What
  did the 1787 text actually say about courts striking down laws?" (Nothing.)
- **Bill of Rights applied to states in 1791:** re-teach the federal-action rule
  with Lesson 5's worked example 2 and Task F Q2.
- **One-sided argument:** return the Lesson 6 skeleton and require the
  counterclaim paragraph to cite a source the learner disagrees with.

## What success looks like

By Week 14 S5, the learner can: name the Articles' three missing powers with a
breakdown each; work a Three-Fifths counting example; sequence ratification with
dates and at least two close votes; trace a veto override and an appointment;
decide four Bill of Rights scenarios with amendments cited; and argue the
democratic question with a genuine counterclaim, voter-inclusion analysis, and a
stated evidence limit. Anything less is a reteaching target, not a failure — the
portfolio rubric (project §6) plans the follow-up.
