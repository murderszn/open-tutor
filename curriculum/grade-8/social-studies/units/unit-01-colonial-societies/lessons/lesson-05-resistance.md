# Lesson 5 — Resistance: From Everyday Acts to Revolt

**Unit 01 · Week 5 S2 · ~45 minutes · Session type: close reading and investigation**

> **Adult preview note:** Covers violent resistance and violent punishment. Excerpts are brief and public domain; see [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) preview notes. Discussion roles are assigned, never forced.

## Goal

I can identify at least four forms of resistance to enslavement and support each with evidence from a primary or secondary source.

## Materials

- Germantown petition excerpt (below)
- Resistance evidence cards (6 cards; texts in [practice.md Task F](../practice.md))

## Readiness check (5 min)

From Lesson 4: the system needed demand, supply, law, and violence. If you were trapped in such a system, what could you do? Brainstorm freely — no wrong answers yet.

## Explicit teaching (15 min)

Enslaved people resisted constantly. Historians sort resistance on a spectrum from **everyday** to **organized**:

- **Everyday resistance:** work slowdowns, breaking tools, feigning illness, preserving African languages, music, and religious practices, learning to read in secret. These acts were hard to punish all at once — and they asserted humanity inside a system designed to deny it.
- **Escape:** running away, sometimes for days (to maroon communities in swamps and mountains), sometimes permanently. Colonial newspapers printed runaway advertisements — which are now evidence of how many people fled.
- **Revolt:** organized armed uprisings. The **Stono Rebellion** (South Carolina, 1739) — the largest in the British mainland colonies — saw dozens of enslaved people march toward Florida, where the Spanish promised freedom; it was suppressed with great violence, and South Carolina answered with harsher slave codes. An earlier revolt in **New York City (1712)** likewise ended in executions and tighter laws.
- **Protest in writing and law:** petitions, lawsuits, and published arguments. In **1688** — the same decade William Penn's colony was founded — four German Quakers in Germantown, Pennsylvania, signed the first formal anti-slavery protest in the colonies.

### Worked example 1 — Close reading the Germantown petition

*Excerpt — the 1688 Germantown Quaker Petition Against Slavery, signed by Francis Daniel Pastorius, Garret Hendericks, Derick and Abraham op den Graeff. Public domain; transcription via the Historical Society of Montgomery County.*

> "There is a saying, that we shall do to all men like as we will be done ourselves; making no difference of what generation, descent or colour they are. And those who steal or rob men, and those who buy or purchase them, are they not all alike? ... But to bring men hither, or to rob and sell them against their will, we stand against."

*Question: Who wrote this, to whom, and what is the argument?*

*Reasoning — maker and audience (D2.His.11):* Four German immigrant Quakers, writing to their own Quaker monthly meeting — fellow believers, not the colonial government. Purpose: persuade their religious community that slaveholding contradicts its own principles. Date and place: Germantown, Pennsylvania, April 1688 — only six years after Penn's colony was founded, when slavery was already present there.

*Reasoning — the argument:* It applies the Golden Rule universally ("no difference of what generation, descent or colour") and then asks a piercing question: is the *buyer* any better than the *stealer*? The logic makes everyone in the supply chain responsible. Note the limit (D2.His.10): the petition was ignored for decades — it proves *some* colonists objected early, not that colonial society agreed.

### Worked example 2 — Classifying resistance with evidence

*Question: A South Carolina law of 1740 bans enslaved people from gathering in groups, learning to write, and leaving plantations without passes. What does this law prove about resistance?*

*Reasoning:* Laws are evidence of what lawmakers *feared* — and fear reveals what was actually happening. A law banning gatherings proves people were gathering; a ban on writing proves some were learning to write; pass requirements prove people were moving without permission. This is reading a hostile source *against the grain*: the law's purpose was control, but its existence is evidence of resistance. Each ban maps to a resistance form: gathering → organizing; writing → literacy as resistance; passes → escape.

## Guided practice (10 min)

1. With the adult, place each evidence card on the spectrum (everyday / escape / revolt / protest): *a runaway advertisement from a 1740s newspaper; the Stono Rebellion; a broken hoe found at a plantation site; the Germantown petition; a secretly kept African drum; the 1712 New York revolt.*
2. For the Germantown petition, name maker, audience, and purpose in one sentence each.
3. Discuss: which form of resistance was easiest for an individual to attempt? Which required the most coordination — and carried the greatest risk?

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Choose **two** forms of resistance and write a short paragraph for each: what the act was, who did it, and what evidence historians use to know about it.
2. **Perspective check:** Write 3–4 sentences from the viewpoint of a South Carolina assemblyman in 1740 explaining *why* the new restrictions are needed. Then write 2–3 sentences as a historian explaining what the restrictions reveal about the assemblyman's fears. Keep the two voices clearly labeled.
3. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "The Stono rebels failed, so their revolt changed nothing." Agree or disagree with evidence: what changed *because* of Stono (hint: the law), and what does that tell us about how resistance worked even when revolts were crushed?

## Application

Find a modern example of "everyday resistance" — small acts by people with little formal power (workers, students, customers). Describe the act and what it asserts. One paragraph.

## Exit check

Name four forms of resistance to enslavement, each with one piece of evidence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Read the petition excerpt aloud together; paraphrase each clause before analyzing.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may sort the evidence cards physically and narrate the spectrum instead of writing paragraphs.
- **Extension:** Research one more resistance event (e.g., the Haitian Revolution's beginnings, 1791 — just beyond this unit's period but the most consequential; or a local maroon community). Present: who, where, what happened, what changed.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"If conditions were that bad, why didn't everyone just revolt?"* Surveillance, terror, division by design (mixed origins and languages), and the near-certainty of death made revolt rare; everyday resistance was constant precisely because it was survivable.
- *"White colonists all supported slavery."* The Germantown petition proves dissent existed from the 1680s — a minority, but a real one, and worth naming.
