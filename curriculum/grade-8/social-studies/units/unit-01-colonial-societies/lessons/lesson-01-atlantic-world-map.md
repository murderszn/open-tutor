# Lesson 1 — The Atlantic World on the Map

**Unit 01 · Week 3 S1 · ~45 minutes · Session type: concept launch**

## Goal

I can locate the three continents of the Atlantic world on a map and explain how wind patterns made regular ocean crossings possible.

## Materials

- A world map or globe (printed or on screen) showing the Atlantic Ocean
- Blank Atlantic-basin outline map (learner draws three continents' coastlines; Europe, Africa, the Americas)
- Ruler, colored pencils
- Learner notebook

## Readiness check (5 min)

Point to the Atlantic Ocean on a world map. Name one ocean besides the Atlantic. Name one continent that touches the Atlantic Ocean. (Accept any correct answers; this lesson teaches the rest.)

## Explicit teaching (15 min)

Before about 1500, the Atlantic Ocean was a barrier. People on its three surrounding continents — Europe, Africa, and the Americas — knew little or nothing about each other. After 1500, the Atlantic became a **highway**: ships crossed it regularly, carrying goods, people, crops, animals, diseases, and ideas. Historians call this connected system the **Atlantic world**.

Three facts make the map readable:

1. **Three continents, one ocean.** Europe sits to the northeast of the Atlantic, Africa to the east and southeast, and the Americas to the west. Everything this unit studies moves between these three landmasses across this one ocean.

2. **Winds are the engine.** Sailing ships could not motor against the wind. In the North Atlantic, the **trade winds** blow steadily from east to west near the equator, while the **westerlies** blow from west to east farther north. A ship leaving Europe sailed south to catch the trade winds west to the Americas; a ship returning sailed north to catch the westerlies back to Europe. That is why the crossing routes form a great clockwise loop — sailors called it the "volta," the turn.

3. **Coastlines are evidence.** The eastern coastline of the Americas and the western coastline of Africa and Europe face each other across the ocean. Every port that grew rich in this system — Seville, Lisbon, London, the Gold Coast ports, Havana, Charleston, Boston — sits on these facing coastlines.

### Worked example 1 — Reading the wind loop

*Question: A merchant ship leaves Lisbon, Portugal, carrying wine and cloth, and wants to reach the Caribbean. Which way does it sail first — north or south?*

*Reasoning:* The ship needs a wind pushing it west. Near the equator, the trade winds blow east→west. Lisbon is far north of those winds. So the ship sails **south** along the African coast first, then turns west with the trade winds toward the Caribbean. Sailing straight west from Lisbon would mean fighting headwinds the whole way.

*Check:* The return trip reverses the logic — the ship sails **north** from the Caribbean to catch the westerlies blowing west→east back toward Europe. The full loop is clockwise.

### Worked example 2 — Why ports face each other

*Question: Why did the busiest Atlantic ports all sit on coastlines facing the ocean, rather than inland?*

*Reasoning:* Before railroads, moving heavy goods over land was slow and expensive — a barrel of sugar could cost more to haul 50 miles inland than to ship 3,000 miles across the ocean. Ports on facing coastlines were the transfer points where ocean cargo met land transport. Charleston faced the Atlantic because that is where the ships were; tobacco moved down rivers to Charleston, then across the ocean.

## Guided practice (10 min)

Work through these with the adult, thinking aloud:

1. On your blank Atlantic outline, label **Europe**, **Africa**, **North America**, **South America**, and the **Atlantic Ocean**. Use the classroom map to check coastlines.
2. Draw arrows for the **trade winds** (east→west near the equator) and the **westerlies** (west→east in the north). Label each.
3. Trace the clockwise sailing loop: Europe → south along Africa → west to the Americas → north → east back to Europe. Mark one port on each of the three continents along your loop.

## Independent practice (10 min)

In your notebook:

1. Explain in 2–3 sentences why a ship sailing from England to Virginia would sail south first instead of heading straight west.
2. Name two facing-coastline port cities (one European, one American) and explain in one sentence each why the port's location mattered.
3. **Error analysis:** A classmate says, "Sailors crossed the Atlantic in straight lines because that's the shortest distance." What is wrong with this reasoning? What actually determined their routes?

## Application

Look at a modern world map (or globe). Find one present-day country on each of the three Atlantic continents. Write one sentence for each explaining what crosses the Atlantic to or from it today (goods, people, ideas — any true example).

## Exit check

On your outline map, draw and label the clockwise wind loop from memory. Then answer: what powered Atlantic crossings before steam engines?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Read the three numbered facts aloud together; learner repeats the bold terms. Pre-teach *prevailing* (the usual, steady pattern).
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may trace the loop with a finger on a globe and narrate it instead of drawing.
- **Extension:** Research one real port (e.g., Seville, Elmina, Havana, Charleston) and write a half-page on what it exported and imported around 1700. What does its cargo list reveal about the Atlantic system?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"Columbus discovered an empty ocean route anyone could sail."* Sailors needed the wind knowledge; the first crossings were slow, dangerous experiments, and the loop pattern took decades to work out.
- *"Maps then looked like ours."* Early maps had real gaps and errors — the lesson's blank outline is a modern teaching tool, not a period map.
