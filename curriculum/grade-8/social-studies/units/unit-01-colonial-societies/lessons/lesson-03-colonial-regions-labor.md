# Lesson 3 — Three Colonial Regions: Environment, Economy, Labor

**Unit 01 · Week 4 S1 · ~45 minutes · Session type: concept launch**

## Goal

I can compare New England, the Middle colonies, and the Southern colonies by environment, economy, and labor system — and explain how environment shaped the labor each region used.

## Materials

- Outline map of England's North American colonies (13 colonies region)
- Three-column region chart (environment / economy / labor)
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 min)

From Lesson 2: which exchange crop became a Southern colonial staple? (Tobacco, rice, indigo — any one.) Why would a crop's needs shape what kind of labor a region uses?

## Explicit teaching (18 min)

By the mid-1700s, England's North American colonies had sorted into three regions. They shared a language and a king, but their **environments** — soil, climate, coastline — pushed them toward different economies, and different economies demanded different **labor**.

**New England** (Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New Hampshire): rocky soil, short growing season, long coastline, deep harbors, thick forests. Farming fed families but rarely produced surpluses. The economy turned to the sea and the forest: fishing, whaling, shipbuilding, timber and lumber, and trade. Labor was mostly **family labor** on small farms plus **wage labor and apprenticeships** in ports and shipyards. Enslaved labor existed in New England — especially in port cities — but it was never the economic foundation.

**Middle colonies** (New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware): fertile soil, moderate climate, broad rivers. Called the "breadbasket" for their **wheat and grain** exports. Mixed economy: farming, artisans, ironworks, trade. Labor was mixed too: family farms, **indentured servants** (workers under 4–7-year contracts exchanged for Atlantic passage), some enslaved laborers, and a large free workforce. Pennsylvania's religious tolerance also drew diverse European immigrants, including the Germans who signed the 1688 Germantown petition.

**Southern colonies** (Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia): long growing season, fertile coastal plains, rivers navigable deep inland. Ideal for **staple crops** grown in bulk for export: **tobacco** (Virginia, Maryland), **rice and indigo** (South Carolina, Georgia). Staple crops needed huge amounts of stoop labor on large plantations. Early on, planters used indentured servants — but servants' contracts ended, and freed servants wanted their own land. Planters shifted decisively to **lifelong, hereditary enslaved labor**, and colonial laws hardened around it (by the 1660s–1700s, laws in Virginia and Maryland made enslavement permanent and inherited through the mother). By 1750 the Southern economy rested on enslaved labor.

The causal chain runs **environment → crop → labor system → law**: it was not an accident that enslaved labor concentrated where staple crops grew, and it was not "natural" either — it was a series of human choices, each responding to profit.

### Worked example 1 — Environment to labor, step by step

*Question: Why did South Carolina use enslaved labor while Massachusetts mostly did not?*

*Reasoning:* Step 1 — environment: South Carolina's Lowcountry had a long, hot growing season and swampy coastal plains. Step 2 — crop: those conditions grew **rice**, a hugely profitable export crop. Step 3 — labor demand: rice cultivation needed large gangs of workers doing skilled, grueling labor in malarial conditions. Step 4 — labor supply: planters first used enslaved Indigenous people, then shifted to enslaved Africans, many from West Africa's "Rice Coast," whose agricultural knowledge planters deliberately exploited. Massachusetts, by contrast, had rocky soil and a short season — no staple crop, no plantation labor demand, so the economy ran on family farms and wage labor. The difference starts with environment but ends with human decisions about whom to force to do the work.

### Worked example 2 — Sorting a labor system with evidence

*Question: A 1750 record describes a Pennsylvania farmer, his wife, three children, and one hired hand harvesting wheat. Which labor system is this, and how do you know?*

*Reasoning:* The evidence points to **family labor plus wage labor**: the workers are the farmer's own household (family labor), and the extra hand is *hired* — paid wages, free to leave. There is no mention of a multi-year contract (indenture) or of people held as property (enslavement). Region check: Pennsylvania is a Middle colony, the wheat "breadbasket" — consistent with the mixed-labor pattern. The method: match each clue (family, hired, wheat, Pennsylvania) to the region chart before deciding.

## Guided practice (8 min)

1. Complete the three-column chart for the **Middle colonies** together: environment (one phrase), economy (two items), labor (two systems).
2. Sort these into regions with one evidence phrase each: *shipbuilding; rice; tobacco; wheat exports; whaling; indigo*.
3. Discuss: which region's labor system gave workers the most freedom to leave? Which gave the least? What made the difference?

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. In your notebook, write the environment → crop → labor chain for **one** region in your own words (3–4 sentences).
2. **Error analysis:** A classmate claims, "The Southern colonies used enslaved labor because the climate made white people unable to work." Identify two errors: one factual (indentured servants *did* do field labor early on) and one in reasoning (confusing a profit-driven choice with a natural necessity).
3. Classify: was **indentured servitude** closer to family labor or to enslavement? Give two reasons, using the definitions from the vocabulary list.

## Application

Find one present-day example of "environment shapes the economy" (e.g., a fishing town, a wheat region, a tech hub near universities). Write 3–4 sentences explaining the chain. Does a similar labor question arise today?

## Exit check

Name the three regions and, for each, one staple product and the dominant labor system.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Color-code the three regions on the map (one color each) and keep the chart visible all week.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may build the chain as a labeled diagram (boxes and arrows) instead of a paragraph.
- **Extension:** Research the shift from indentured servitude to enslaved labor in Virginia (Bacon's Rebellion, 1676, is the usual turning point historians cite). Write a half-page: what did planters fear, and what did they change?

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"Slavery existed only in the South."* Enslavement existed in all 13 colonies, including New England ports — the difference was economic centrality, not presence.
- *"Indentured servants were basically slaves."* Indenture was brutal and exploitative, but it was temporary and contractual; conflating the two erases what made racialized, hereditary enslavement distinct.
