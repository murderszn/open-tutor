# Lesson 2 — The Columbian Exchange: Two Directions

**Unit 01 · Week 3 S2 · ~45 minutes · Session type: close reading and skills practice**

## Goal

I can explain the Columbian exchange as a two-direction transfer and trace one cause and one effect in each direction.

## Materials

- Two-direction exchange chart (T-chart: "Americas → Europe/Africa" and "Europe/Africa → Americas")
- Notebook; colored pencils

## Readiness check (5 min)

From Lesson 1: what powered ships across the Atlantic before steam? Name one thing you think crossed the ocean in each direction. (Accept guesses — this lesson checks them.)

## Explicit teaching (15 min)

When sustained contact began after 1492, the two hemispheres swapped living things at a scale never seen before. Historians call this the **Columbian exchange**. The key word is *exchange*: it ran **both directions**, and each direction reshaped the receiving continent.

**Americas → Europe and Africa:** maize (corn), potatoes, tomatoes, cacao (chocolate), tobacco, peanuts, pumpkins, turkeys. The potato and maize mattered most: they grew where European grains struggled, yielded far more calories per acre, and helped feed a European population boom in the 1700s.

**Europe and Africa → the Americas:** wheat, rice, sugarcane, coffee, citrus fruit — and animals: horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, chickens. Horses transformed Indigenous life on the Plains; cattle ranching reshaped whole economies. But the deadliest arrivals were invisible: **smallpox, measles, and influenza**. Native populations had no prior exposure and no immunity. Epidemics killed a huge share of Indigenous peoples — in many regions, most of the population — which in turn made European conquest and colonization far easier than it would otherwise have been.

Notice the pattern: **the same exchange that fed Europe devastated the Americas.** Any honest account of the exchange keeps both directions — and both consequences — in view. A one-direction story ("Europe gave the Americas new crops") hides the cost; a one-consequence story ("the exchange was all disaster") hides the real changes in diet and population.

### Worked example 1 — Tracing one item both ways (potato)

*Question: How did the potato move, and what changed because of it?*

*Reasoning:* Direction: Americas → Europe (the potato is native to the Andes). Cause of spread: it grew in poor soils and cold climates where wheat failed, and an acre of potatoes fed far more people than an acre of grain. Effect: by the 1700s it was a staple across northern Europe, supporting population growth — Ireland's population, for example, grew enormously on a potato-based diet. That growth itself later became a vulnerability (the 1840s blight), which is a second-order effect worth noting but not confusing with the first.

### Worked example 2 — Disease as an exchange item

*Question: Why did European diseases kill so many Indigenous Americans while American diseases did not devastate Europe the same way?*

*Reasoning:* Smallpox, measles, and influenza came from long contact between humans and domesticated animals in Europe, Asia, and Africa — thousands of years of exposure built partial population immunity. The Americas had far fewer domesticated herd animals, so these "crowd diseases" had never circulated there. When they arrived, they met populations with **no prior exposure**. The result was not just illness but demographic collapse — whole towns emptied — which weakened resistance to invasion and forced labor. This is a cause-and-effect chain: *no immunity → catastrophic mortality → weakened societies → easier conquest.*

## Guided practice (10 min)

Complete the first rows of your two-direction chart together with the adult:

1. Add **maize** to the correct side. What was one effect in Europe/Africa? (Hint: think calories and population.)
2. Add **horses** to the correct side. What was one effect in the Americas?
3. For **smallpox**: which direction did it travel, and what was the effect on Indigenous societies beyond the deaths themselves?

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Finish your chart: at least **three items per direction**, each with one stated effect.
2. Write a cause/effect paragraph (4–6 sentences): *"The Columbian exchange helped Europe's population grow AND devastated Indigenous populations. Explain how both happened, naming at least one item moving in each direction."*
3. **Explain-your-reasoning:** A classmate writes, "The Columbian exchange was good because it gave everyone new foods." Using your chart, explain what this claim leaves out and why the missing part matters.

## Application

Look at your own dinner table (or a grocery list). Identify three foods that exist in your kitchen because of the Columbian exchange, and name which direction each traveled. Write one sentence for each.

## Exit check

Without looking at your chart: name two things that moved Americas→Europe/Africa and two that moved the other way, plus one effect for each direction.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Pair each item with a quick sketch (potato, ship, horse, microbe symbol). Say direction aloud as you draw the arrow.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may dictate the paragraph while the adult scribes, then read it back and correct one sentence.
- **Extension:** Investigate one exchange item in depth (e.g., sugarcane): where it was grown, who grew it, who profited, who labored. Connect it to Lesson 3's labor systems — sugar plantations were among the first places to shift decisively to enslaved labor.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"Exchange means fair trade."* In history, "exchange" just means movement both ways — it says nothing about fairness or consent. Disease and enslaved people also "moved."
- *"Only Europeans benefited."* Maize and potatoes transformed diets in Africa and Europe too; some Indigenous groups adopted horses and new crops strategically. Benefits and harms were unevenly distributed, not one-sided — which is exactly what the evidence chart teaches.
