# Lesson 6 — The Atlantic System: Who Benefited, What It Cost

**Unit 01 · Week 6 S1 · ~45 minutes · Session type: concept launch and synthesis**

## Goal

I can trace who profited at each leg of the triangular trade, explain one trade barrier and its costs, and frame the unit's compelling question for argument.

## Materials

- [Triangular trade schematic](../assets/triangular-trade-schematic.svg) (labeled schematic, not to scale)
- Notebook

## Readiness check (5 min)

From Lesson 4: name the four system requirements. From Lesson 5: name two forms of resistance. Today we assemble the whole system and ask who it served.

## Explicit teaching (15 min)

The **triangular trade** is the textbook diagram of the Atlantic economy: three legs, each profitable. Study the schematic below (deterministic diagram, not to scale — positions are not geographic).

![Schematic diagram of the triangular trade: three labeled boxes — Europe, West Africa, the Americas — with arrows for Leg 1 (Europe to West Africa: cloth, guns, iron, rum), Leg 2 / Middle Passage (West Africa to the Americas: enslaved Africans), and Leg 3 (the Americas to Europe: sugar, tobacco, rice). Footer: profit was made at every leg except by the enslaved people whose labor created the value.](../assets/triangular-trade-schematic.svg)

*Caption: Schematic of the triangular trade (not to scale). Text alternative: Leg 1 carried manufactured goods from Europe to West Africa; Leg 2 (the Middle Passage) carried enslaved Africans to the Americas; Leg 3 carried plantation goods from the Americas to Europe.*

- **Leg 1 — Europe to Africa:** European ships carried manufactured goods — cloth, guns, iron, rum — to West African ports, trading them for captives (and gold, ivory, pepper).
- **Leg 2 — Africa to the Americas (the Middle Passage):** Captives were transported and sold in the Caribbean, Brazil, and mainland colonies. This was the most profitable and most lethal leg.
- **Leg 3 — Americas to Europe:** Ships returned loaded with plantation goods — sugar, tobacco, rice, cotton, indigo — produced by enslaved labor, to be sold and consumed in Europe.

At **each leg, different people profited**: European manufacturers and merchants on Leg 1; ship captains, investors, and traders on Leg 2; plantation owners and European consumers (who got cheap sugar and tobacco) on Leg 3. African traders and states profited too — participation was widespread, though the terms were set by European demand and naval power.

The system was also shaped by **barriers to trade** (D2.Eco.14.6-8). England's **Navigation Acts** required that colonial goods ship only on English vessels to English ports. That was a barrier: it raised costs for colonists (who couldn't sell to the highest bidder) while guaranteeing profits for English merchants. Every trade policy has **benefits and costs** distributed unevenly (D2.Eco.15.6-8): the Navigation Acts benefited English shippers and the crown's tax revenue; they cost colonial planters and consumers, who paid more and earned less than in an open market. Colonists' anger at these costs would later feed the Revolution — a thread Unit 02 picks up.

Now frame the **compelling question** (D1.1.6-8): *Was the Atlantic system primarily economic or political?* An economic answer points to profit driving every leg; a political answer points to empires, laws, and naval power making the trade possible and protecting it. The unit assessment asks learners to argue one side while steelmanning the other.

### Worked example 1 — Tracing profit at each leg

*Question: A barrel of Jamaican sugar sells in London. Who profited along the way?*

*Reasoning:* Leg 3: the Jamaican planter profits from selling the sugar — but the sugar was produced by enslaved laborers who were paid nothing, so the planter's profit *is* the workers' stolen wages. The London merchant profits on resale; the crown collects customs duties; the consumer gets cheap sugar. Working backward: Leg 2 — the trader who sold the enslaved workers to the planter profited from their sale price; Leg 1 — the manufacturer who sold trade goods to the slaver profited too. Everyone in the chain profited except the enslaved workers, whose labor created the value. That asymmetry is the system's moral core, and any honest profit-tracing must show it.

### Worked example 2 — Evaluating a trade barrier

*Question: How did the Navigation Acts act as a barrier, and who paid the cost?*

*Reasoning:* The barrier: colonial tobacco *had* to go to England on English ships — planters could not sell directly to, say, Dutch buyers offering higher prices. The effect on trade: reduced competition among buyers → lower prices paid to planters; reduced competition among shippers → higher freight rates charged to planters. Who benefited: English merchants (guaranteed cargo) and the crown (customs revenue). Who paid: colonial planters and consumers. This is D2.Eco.14–15 in one case: name the barrier, trace the cost to specific people. Note the limit: smuggling was rampant, so the Acts' *intended* effect exceeded their *actual* effect — evidence of limits again.

## Guided practice (10 min)

1. Using the triangular-trade schematic, label each leg with **what moved** and **who profited**. Mark with an X the people in the chain who created value but did not profit.
2. Explain the Navigation Acts as a barrier in two sentences: what was restricted, and who paid the cost?
3. With the adult, draft two supporting questions under the compelling question (e.g., "Who financed the voyages?" "What did the laws protect?").

## Independent practice (10 min)

1. Write a paragraph: *"A London consumer in 1750 enjoys cheap sugar. Trace the sugar's path back through all three legs, naming who profited at each point and who did not."*
2. **Counterclaim practice:** Write the strongest one-paragraph case that the Atlantic system was *primarily political* (empires, laws, navies). Then write one sentence identifying the strongest *economic* reply to it. (This is rehearsal for the assessment.)
3. **Explain-your-reasoning:** "The triangular trade diagram makes the system look neat and balanced." What does the neat triangle hide? Name two things.

## Application

Find a modern three-party supply chain (e.g., phone: mined minerals → factory → store). Sketch its triangle and mark who profits at each point and who does the work. One paragraph: is the distribution fair? What would make it fairer?

## Exit check

State the compelling question in your own words, and give one piece of evidence for the economic side and one for the political side.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Walk the schematic leg by leg with a finger before writing; say "who profits?" at each stop.
- **Alternate response mode:** Learner may narrate the sugar's path aloud as a recorded explanation.
- **Extension:** Research the Navigation Acts' dates (first: 1651; major: 1660, 1663) and write a half-page on colonial smuggling as resistance to trade barriers — connecting economic policy to the coming Revolution.

## Teacher note — likely misconceptions

- *"The triangle was equal — everyone traded fairly."* The diagram's symmetry is visual, not moral: one leg carried human beings in chains.
- *"Mercantilism helped the colonies."* Mercantilism enriched the mother country *by design*; colonial prosperity happened within — and often despite — that design.
