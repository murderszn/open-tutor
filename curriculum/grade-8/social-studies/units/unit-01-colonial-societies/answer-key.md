# Answer Key — Unit 01: Colonial Societies, Atlantic Exchange, Labor, Resistance

**For the adult/educator only. Never place beside student questions.**
Scoring: award partial credit for partially correct reasoning; the notes show what "complete" looks like.

## Lesson 1 — Independent practice

1. Ships needed westward wind; near the equator the trade winds blow east→west, but England is far north of them, so ships sailed south to the trade-wind belt, crossed west, then returned north to the westerlies. Straight west from England meant headwinds.
2. Accept any two true facing-coastline ports, e.g., **London** (financed and outfitted voyages; collected customs) and **Charleston** (exported rice/indigo; imported manufactured goods and enslaved people). One sentence each on the port's role.
3. Error: distance is not the constraint — **wind** is. Square-rigged ships could barely sail into the wind, so routes followed the wind loop even though it was longer.

## Lesson 2 — Independent practice

1. Chart (accept equivalents): Americas→Europe/Africa: maize (effect: calorie-rich staple supported population growth), potatoes (effect: fed northern Europe's 1700s population boom), tomatoes/cacao/tobacco (effects: new diets, plantation cash crops). Europe/Africa→Americas: horses (effect: transformed Plains Indigenous life and warfare), wheat/sugarcane (effects: new plantation economies), smallpox/measles (effect: demographic collapse with no prior immunity).
2. Paragraph must name one item each direction with a cause and an effect — e.g., potatoes spread because they yielded more calories on poor soils → European population growth; smallpox spread with no Indigenous immunity → catastrophic mortality that weakened resistance to conquest.
3. The claim omits the **disease direction and its human cost**, and treats "new foods" as the whole story. Missing part matters because the mortality reshaped who held power in the Americas.

## Lesson 3 — Independent practice

1. Example chain (Southern): long hot growing season and fertile coastal plain → rice/tobacco as profitable export staples → huge demand for stoop labor → planters' shift from indentured servants to lifelong hereditary enslaved labor, locked in by law.
2. Factual error: Europeans (indentured servants) *did* do plantation field labor in the early decades. Reasoning error: treats a profit-driven choice as climate destiny — planters chose enslaved labor because contracts ended and freed servants wanted land; the climate explains the *crop*, not the *coercion*.
3. Indenture was closer to neither exactly, but structurally nearer **family/wage labor** in being temporary and contractual — while nearer **enslavement** in its brutality and unfreedom. Accept either position with two accurate reasons (temporary vs. lifelong; contract vs. hereditary property; could seek legal redress vs. legally a thing).

## Lesson 4 — Independent practice

1. Middle Passage: the forced Atlantic crossing of enslaved Africans from Africa to the Americas; ships carried captive Africans in lethal overcrowding; ~12.5 million embarked, ~10.7 million landed (1501–1866), so ~1.8 million died on the crossing (12.5 − 10.7 = 1.8).
2. Accept any accurate pair, e.g., "helps me see how much infrastructure (ships, warehouses, wharves) the trade needed" / "cannot answer who was forced to do the labor or where the profits went."
3. "Just business" describes only the **demand/profit** requirement. It omits **supply by force** (kidnapping, not hiring), **law** (people defined as hereditary property), and **violence** (the system's operating method). Each adds coercion the word "business" hides.

## Lesson 5 — Independent practice

1. Accept any two well-evidenced forms, e.g., **escape**: runaway advertisements prove people fled and owners paid to recover them; **revolt**: Stono (1739) — organized march toward Florida, suppressed by militia, answered with harsher codes.
2. Planter paragraph should show interests (property, order, fear) in his own reasoning; enslaved person's paragraph should show *their* reasoning (family, risk assessment) — not a single generic voice. Historian's paragraph: together they reveal the same event as simultaneously a property crime (to the planter) and a freedom bid (to the enslaved) — perspective is evidence of position.
3. **Disagree.** Stono failed as an escape but *changed the law*: South Carolina's 1740 code banned gatherings, literacy, and unsupervised movement — proof the assembly feared repetition. Resistance worked by forcing the system to spend resources on control, even when revolts were crushed.

## Lesson 6 — Independent practice

1. Sugar's path: Jamaican cane cut by enslaved laborers (unpaid — Leg 3 producers); sold to the planter who profits; shipped to London (Leg 3 freight profits); merchant resells; crown takes customs; consumer buys cheap sugar. Backward: the trader who sold the workers (Leg 2) and the manufacturer who sold trade goods (Leg 1) also profited. Only the enslaved workers created value without pay.
2. Political case: empires chartered the companies, navies protected the routes, colonial assemblies wrote enslavement into law, and the Navigation Acts directed trade by statute — no law, no system. Strongest economic reply: every law served profit — the system existed because sugar, tobacco, and rice made fortunes; without demand, the laws would have had nothing to protect.
3. The neat triangle hides: (1) the Middle Passage's human cost — a line on the diagram was months of lethal overcrowding; (2) the asymmetry — two legs moved goods, one moved human beings in chains, and the "equality" of the triangle is visual only.

## Practice tasks

**Task A:** Check structure — one named item, stated direction, one cause of spread, one effect on the receiving continent, one honest limit sentence. Deduct if direction or effect is missing.

**Task B:** 1 New England (cod/nets — fishing economy); 2 Southern (rice, driver, sunrise-to-sunset gang labor); 3 Middle (German immigrant, passage paid by years of labor — indenture); 4 New England (Boston workshop apprenticeship); 5 Southern (people listed as property with prices); 6 Middle (wheat harvest, barn-raising — family/neighbor labor). Each needs one evidence phrase.

**Task C:** Six markers correctly placed per the key list; two pattern sentences must connect products to labor (e.g., "staple-crop markers cluster in the South with the 'enslaved labor dominant' note; grain/fishing markers cluster north with family/wage notes").

**Task D:** Three differences each with an environmental or economic reason (e.g., growing season length → staple crops possible or not; coastline/harbors → maritime economy; labor demand → labor system). Summary sentence names the most important difference with justification — accept any defended choice.

**Task E:** 1. Any two: "scarcely room to turn himself"; "the whole ship's cargo were confined together"; chains, necessary tubs, crowding by design. 2. "Cargo" shows traders categorized captives as goods — property, not people. 3. Maker: Olaudah Equiano, formerly enslaved West African; date: published 1789 (crossing decades earlier); audience: British reading public; purpose: abolitionist argument against the slave trade. 4. Limit: one person's experience, written decades later for a political purpose — cannot alone establish typical conditions; needs corroboration (ship logs, database).

**Task F:** Card 1 escape; Card 2 revolt; Card 3 everyday; Card 4 protest; Card 5 everyday (read against the grain); Card 6 revolt. Three cards need specific evidence cited from the card text.

**Task G:** See Lesson 5 scoring — interests visible, voices distinct and labeled, historian's paragraph names what the pairing reveals.

**Task H:** 1. Restriction: colonial goods had to ship on English ships to English ports; colonists couldn't sell to the highest foreign bidder. 2. Benefited: English merchants/shippers (guaranteed cargo) and the crown (customs revenue). Paid: colonial planters (lower prices, higher freight) and colonial consumers (fewer, pricier imports). 3. Widespread smuggling shows the *actual* effect was smaller than intended — enforcement was incomplete, so the barrier leaked.

**Task I:** 1. Ledger — maker: merchant/clerk; purpose: track profit and inventory; audience: the merchant firm (private business record). Petition — maker: four Germantown Quakers; purpose: persuade their meeting that slaveholding violates Quaker principle; audience: fellow Quakers. 2. Ledger reveals scale, prices, and how people were commodified (line items); it cannot reveal moral argument or lived experience. Petition reveals principled dissent and the Golden-Rule argument; it cannot reveal the trade's scale or mechanics. 3. Ledger's limit: dehumanizes by design — it records prices, not people. Petition's limit: a minority protest, ignored for decades — it proves dissent existed, not that the system was widely opposed.

**Task J:** Paragraph 1: clear claim + two unit evidence citations. Paragraph 2: genuine counterclaim (not a strawman) + one evidence citation. Final sentence: one honest limit (e.g., "my sources show the colonial period, not whether the same logic held everywhere in the Atlantic world").
