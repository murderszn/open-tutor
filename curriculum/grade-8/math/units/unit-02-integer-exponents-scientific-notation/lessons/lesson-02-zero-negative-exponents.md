# Lesson 02 — Zero and Negative Exponents

**Student goal:** I can explain why a⁰ = 1 and a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ, and evaluate
expressions with zero and negative exponents.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 3 of 16**

## Materials

- Notebook, pencil, calculator (for checking only)
- Yesterday's "pattern museum" predictions (from Lesson 01's extension)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Continue the pattern: 10³ = 1,000; 10² = 100; 10¹ = 10; 10⁰ = __. Then
predict the next value down. (Expected: 1; then 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1.) If the
dividing-down pattern is shaky, re-chant the ladder before continuing.

## Teaching

Yesterday the pattern forced 10⁰ = 1. Today we walk the pattern one step
further — and give the result a name.

| Power | Value |
|---|---|
| 10² | 100 |
| 10¹ | 10 |
| 10⁰ | 1 |
| 10⁻¹ | 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1 = 1/10 |
| 10⁻² | 0.1 ÷ 10 = 0.01 = 1/100 |
| 10⁻³ | 0.01 ÷ 10 = 0.001 = 1/1,000 |

Two new rules appear:

1. **Zero exponent:** a⁰ = 1 for any nonzero a. Every nonzero base,
   multiplied "zero times," leaves the multiplicative identity. Note the
   guard: 0⁰ is *undefined* — there is no pattern to extend there, and
   this course never evaluates it.

2. **Negative exponent:** a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ for any nonzero a and positive n.
   A negative exponent means "put the positive power in the denominator."
   So 10⁻³ = 1/10³ = 1/1,000 = 0.001.

Why does the zero rule hold for *every* base, not just 10? Run the
dividing-down pattern on powers of 3: 3⁴ = 81; 3³ = 27; 3² = 9; 3¹ = 3;
3⁰ = 3 ÷ 3 = 1. The pattern is base-independent. Then 3⁻¹ = 1/3 ≈ 0.333,
3⁻² = 1/9 ≈ 0.111. Same rules, any base.

**Key vocabulary:** *zero exponent* (a⁰ = 1, a ≠ 0), *negative exponent*
(a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ), *reciprocal* (1/a — the "flipped" fraction).

## Worked example 1 — evaluating a negative exponent

Evaluate 5⁻².

Step 1: flip it — 5⁻² = 1/5². Step 2: evaluate the positive power —
5² = 25. Step 3: write the fraction — 1/25 = 0.04.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** "The minus sign does not make the answer
negative — it moves the power to the denominator. 5⁻² is positive and
small: one twenty-fifth."

## Worked example 2 — mixed zero and negative exponents

Evaluate 4⁻¹ + 7⁰.

First term: 4⁻¹ = 1/4 = 0.25. Second term: 7⁰ = 1. Sum: 1/4 + 1 = 1.25
(or 5/4).

**Reasoning to name aloud:** "Evaluate each piece by its own rule, then
add. The exponent rule changes each power's *form*; addition happens
afterward, in ordinary arithmetic."

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. 10⁻⁴ as a fraction and as a decimal. *(Hint: "1 over ten-to-the-fourth;
   then write it decimal.")*
2. 2⁻³. *(Hint: "Flip first: 1/2³ = 1/8.")*
3. 9⁰ + 9⁻¹. *(Hint: "Each piece gets its own rule.")*
4. Error analysis: "Jordan says 3⁻² = −9." What is wrong, and what is
   3⁻² really? *(Hint: "Is the minus sign a subtraction? Where did the
   power move?")*
5. Write 0.00001 as a power of 10, then evaluate 10⁻⁵ × 10² without a
   calculator. *(Hint: "Add the zeros one rung at a time, or use the
   pattern twice.")*
6. Is (−2)⁻² positive or negative? Evaluate it. *(Hint: "Flip it, then
   count the minus signs in the expansion.")*

## Independent practice

1. 6⁰, (−8)⁰, 100⁰ — evaluate each; then explain in one sentence why
   they are all equal.
2. 2⁻⁴ and (1/2)⁴ — evaluate; what do you notice?
3. 10⁻¹ + 10⁻² + 10⁻³ — evaluate exactly as a fraction, then as a decimal.
4. Error analysis: "Priya rewrites 5⁻³ as 1/(−5³)." Find the error and
   fix it.
5. A calculator shows 4⁻³ as `2.5E-2`. Write the exact fraction and
   explain whether the display is reasonable. (Preview of Lesson 06.)

## Application

A grain of salt is about 0.0003 meters wide. Write this width as a
fraction over a power of 10, then as 3 × 10⁻ⁿ for the right n. (Expected:
3/10,000 = 3 × 10⁻⁴ m. Small things live in negative-exponent land.)

## Exit check

"Explain to a friend why 2⁰ = 1 but 2⁻¹ = 1/2." Expected: the
dividing-down pattern 8 → 4 → 2 → 1 → 1/2. Record: secure / developing /
not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "reciprocal" as "the flipped version" with a
  physical flip gesture. Keep "the minus sign moves it downstairs"
  (denominator) as the anchor phrase.
- **Alternate response mode:** oral pattern-chant for powers of 2 and 3,
  with the adult recording the rung values.
- **Calculator boundary:** the calculator may check 3⁻² but must not be
  used to *discover* the rule — expansion and the pattern come first.

## Extension

Prove the zero-exponent rule a second way: 5³ ÷ 5³ = 1, and dividing
same-base powers subtracts exponents (preview of Lesson 03) — so
5³ ÷ 5³ = 5⁰. Hence 5⁰ = 1. Test the argument with base 7.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "A negative exponent makes a negative
answer" — the minus relocates, never negates. (2) "Anything to the zero
power is zero" — pattern says 1. (3) Evaluating 0⁰ — undefined; do not
allow the calculator's answer (if any) as authority. (4) Dropping the
parentheses: −2⁻² vs (−2)⁻² give different results.
