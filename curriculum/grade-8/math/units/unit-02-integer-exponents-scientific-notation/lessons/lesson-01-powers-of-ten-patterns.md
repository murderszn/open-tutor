# Lesson 01 — Powers of Ten and the Dividing-Down Pattern

**Student goal:** I can write any power of 10 as a decimal, and I can
continue the pattern past 10⁰ to predict what comes next.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Unit:** [U02](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- Notebook, pencil, calculator (for checking only)

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Write 10³, 10², and 10¹ as decimals. (Expected: 1,000; 100; 10.) Then ask:
"Each time the exponent drops by 1, what happens to the value?" If powers
feel shaky, re-evaluate 2³, 5², and 4¹ quickly before continuing.

## Teaching

A **power** like 10⁴ is shorthand for repeated multiplication:

10⁴ = 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000

The **base** (10) tells you *what* is multiplied; the **exponent** (4)
tells you *how many times*.

Line up the powers of ten from 10⁴ down to 10¹ and watch what happens at
each step:

| Power | Meaning | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 10⁴ | 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 | 10,000 |
| 10³ | 10 × 10 × 10 | 1,000 |
| 10² | 10 × 10 | 100 |
| 10¹ | 10 | 10 |

Each row is the row above **divided by 10**. Moving down one rung divides
by 10; moving up one rung multiplies by 10. The pattern is perfectly
regular — and regular patterns can be extended.

So the next rung down is 10¹ ÷ 10. We don't need a new definition for
10⁰; the pattern *gives* it to us: 10⁰ = 1. (Lesson 02 proves this and
names it the zero-exponent property.)

**Key vocabulary:** *base* (the repeated factor), *exponent* (the count),
*power* (base^exponent, and also the value itself).

## Worked example 1 — writing powers of ten

Write 10⁶ as a decimal.

Count the zeros: the exponent says how many zeros follow the 1. 10⁶ has 6
zeros → 1,000,000 (one million).

**Reasoning to name aloud:** "10 to a positive power is a 1 followed by
that many zeros. The exponent is a zero-counter." Check: 10¹ = 10 (one
zero), 10² = 100 (two zeros), so 10⁶ = 1 followed by six zeros.

## Worked example 2 — spotting the dividing-down pattern

Fill the missing value: 10³ = 1,000; 10² = 100; 10¹ = 10; 10⁰ = __.

Each step divides by 10: 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100; 100 ÷ 10 = 10; 10 ÷ 10 = 1.
So 10⁰ = 1.

**Reasoning to name aloud:** "I did not memorize 10⁰ = 1 — I *derived*
it. The pattern forced it. Whenever the pattern and a memorized fact
disagree, re-check the pattern: the pattern is the authority."

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

1. Write 10⁵ as a decimal. *(Hint: "The exponent counts the zeros.")*
2. Write 10⁷ as a decimal and name the number. *(Hint: "1 followed by how
   many zeros? Seven — that's ten million.")*
3. Complete: 10² = 100 → 10¹ = 10 → 10⁰ = __ → predict the next value
   down the pattern. *(Hint: "Keep dividing by 10. What is 1 ÷ 10 as a
   decimal?")*
4. Error analysis: "Riley says 10³ = 3 × 10 = 30." What is wrong?
   *(Hint: "Read the exponent aloud: '10 multiplied by itself 3 times.'")*
5. Write each as a power of ten: 100,000; 10,000,000. *(Hint: "Count the
   zeros.")*
6. A million is 10⁶. Write a billion as a power of ten, then as a
   decimal. *(Hint: "A billion has nine zeros.")*

## Independent practice

1. 10⁴ × 1,000 — write as a power of ten, then as a decimal.
2. (10³)² — evaluate. (Expansion allowed: (10×10×10)×(10×10×10).)
3. 10⁸ ÷ 10² — predict using the pattern, then verify by writing both
   decimals and dividing.
4. Explain in two sentences why the exponent in 10ⁿ counts zeros when
   n is positive.
5. 2 × 10³ — evaluate without writing the full expansion first; explain
   your shortcut.

*Alternate response mode:* chant the ladder aloud — "ten to the five is a
hundred thousand…" — while pointing at a written rung list.

## Application

A light-year is about 9,460,000,000,000 km. Write it as a product of a
small number times a power of ten (like 9.46 × 10¹²). Which digits came
from the number and which from the exponent? (Expected: 9.46 × 10¹²;
the digits 946 describe the "shape," the exponent 12 describes the size.
This is a preview of Lesson 05's scientific notation.)

## Exit check

"10⁵ ÷ 10 = ? Write the answer two ways." Expected: 10⁴ and 10,000.
Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "power" as "repeated multiplication written
  short," and "pattern" as "what keeps happening." Pair with the ladder
  chant: one rung down = divide by 10.
- **Alternate response mode:** oral rung-reciting with finger counting
  instead of written expansions.
- **Calculator boundary:** the calculator may verify a finished rung but
  may not generate the pattern — the point is *seeing* the regularity.

## Extension

Start a "pattern museum": predict what 2⁰ and 2⁻¹ should be by running
the dividing-down pattern on powers of 2 (16, 8, 4, 2, __, __). Bring
your prediction to Lesson 02.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "10³ = 30" — confusing exponent with a
multiplier. Expansion fixes it: 10 × 10 × 10 is a hundred groups of ten.
(2) "10⁰ = 0" — the *pattern* says 1; nothing "becomes zero." (3) Counting
zeros incorrectly for large powers — always tie the zero count back to
the exponent, aloud.
