# Lesson 01 — Rational Numbers and Their Decimal Expansions

**Student goal:** I can explain what makes a number rational and predict
whether its decimal expansion terminates or repeats.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Unit:** [U01](../README.md) · **Session 1 of 16**

## Materials

- Notebook, pencil, calculator (for checking only)
- Long-division reference from the diagnostic weeks

## Readiness check (5 minutes)

Write 1/4, 3/5, and 7/10 as decimals. (Expected: 0.25, 0.6, 0.7.) Then ask:
"What did the division do in each case — did it ever stop?" If fraction →
terminating-decimal conversion is shaky, spend 10 minutes re-dividing 1/8
and 3/4 before continuing; note it for re-teaching.

## Teaching

A **rational number** is any number that can be written as a fraction a/b,
where a and b are integers and b is not zero. The integers themselves
count: 5 = 5/1, −3 = −3/1.

Every rational number has a **decimal expansion** — the digits you get when
you divide a by b. And something remarkable happens every time: the digits
either **stop** or **start repeating forever**. They never wander
aimlessly.

Why? Watch the remainders. When you divide by 7, the only possible
remainders are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. If a remainder ever hits 0, the decimal
**terminates** (it stops). If it never hits 0, then with only six nonzero
remainders available, some remainder must come back — and the moment a
remainder repeats, the whole digit pattern repeats from there. The digits
have nowhere else to go.

**Key vocabulary:** *rational number* (a/b with integers, b ≠ 0), *decimal
expansion* (the digits from the division), *terminating decimal* (digits
stop), *repeating decimal* (a block of digits repeats forever), *repetend*
(the repeating block).

## Worked example 1 — a terminating expansion

Convert 7/8 by long division:

- 8 into 70 → 8, remainder 6 → first digit 8
- 8 into 60 → 7, remainder 4 → next digit 7
- 8 into 40 → 5, remainder 0 → next digit 5

7/8 = 0.875. The remainder hit 0, so the digits stopped. **Terminating.**

**Reasoning to name aloud:** a zero remainder is the "stop" signal. Once it
appears, every further digit would be 0, so we stop writing.

## Worked example 2 — a repeating expansion

Convert 4/11 by long division:

- 11 into 40 → 3, remainder 7 → 0.3…
- 11 into 70 → 6, remainder 4 → 0.36…
- 11 into 40 → 3, remainder 7 → 0.363…

The remainder 7 came back — the same remainder we had after the first step.
So the digits "36" will repeat forever: 4/11 = 0.363636… The repetend is
"36".

**Reasoning to name aloud:** the repeat of a remainder is the "loop" signal.
We don't need to divide forever — once a remainder repeats, the digits are
locked into their cycle. Write repeating decimals with a bar over the
repetend: 0.3̄6̄.

## Guided practice (adult stays close, hints allowed)

Do 6 rounds. For each fraction: predict (terminate or repeat?), then divide
to 5–6 decimal places to check.

1. 3/4 *(Hint: "Divide it out — does a remainder hit 0?")*
   Expected: 0.75, terminating.
2. 2/3 *(Hint: "What remainder keeps coming back?")*
   Expected: 0.666…, repetend "6".
3. 5/6 *(Hint: "Watch the remainders after the first digit.")*
   Expected: 0.8333…, repetend "3" (starts after the 8).
4. 7/11 *(Hint: "Same game as 4/11 in the example.")*
   Expected: 0.636363…, repetend "63".
5. −9/5 *(Hint: "Handle the sign first, then divide 9 by 5.")*
   Expected: −1.8, terminating.
6. Error analysis: "Sam says 5/6 = 0.83 because his calculator showed
   0.8333333 and he stopped." What is wrong, and what is the exact decimal?
   Expected: the calculator truncated; the 3s never stop — 0.83̄. Stopping
   early changes the value.

## Independent practice

Predict, then verify by division (calculator checks allowed after the
hand division):

1. 1/5
2. 8/9
3. 13/20
4. 2/7 (go to 7 decimal places — find the full repetend)
5. −11/8
6. Is 0.101001000100001… (pattern: one more zero each time) the expansion
   of a rational number? Explain.

*Alternate response mode:* dictate the repeating block aloud ("thirty-six,
thirty-six") instead of writing the bar notation.

## Application

A recipe calls for 1/3 cup of oil. The measuring cup is marked in decimals:
0.30, 0.31, 0.32, … Which mark is closest to 1/3 cup, and is any mark
exactly right? (Expected: 1/3 = 0.333…; 0.33 is closest; no mark is exact —
a terminating mark can never equal a repeating decimal exactly.)

## Exit check

"Is 0.454545… rational or irrational? How do you know?" Expected:
rational — the block "45" repeats forever, so it equals 45/99 (taught
formally in Lesson 03). Record: secure / developing / not yet.

## Supports

- **Language access:** teach "terminate" as "the digits end" and "repeat"
  as "the digits loop," with hand gestures (flat hand = stop; circling
  finger = loop). Accept home-language equivalents alongside English.
- **Alternate response mode:** oral prediction + pointing to the repeated
  remainder in the long-division work instead of writing the full bar
  notation.
- **Calculator boundary:** the calculator may *check* a hand division but
  may not *replace* it — calculator displays truncate repeating decimals.

## Extension

Find the repetend of 1/7 (0.1̄42857̄ — six digits) and of 1/13
(0.0̄76923̄ — six digits). Conjecture: dividing by a prime other than 2
or 5 gives a repetend whose length divides (prime − 1). Test with 1/11 and
1/17 (1/17 = 0.0̄588235294117647̄ — sixteen digits).

## Teacher note

**Likely misconceptions:** (1) "The calculator's digits are the whole
decimal" — calculators truncate; the math decides. (2) "A long decimal is
irrational" — length is irrelevant; only the pattern matters. (3) Reading
0.8333… as "zero point eight-three" and stopping.
