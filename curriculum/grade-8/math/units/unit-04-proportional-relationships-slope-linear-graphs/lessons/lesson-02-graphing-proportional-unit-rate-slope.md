# Lesson 02 — Graphing Proportional Relationships; Unit Rate as Slope

**Goal (student-readable):** I can graph a proportional relationship,
read its unit rate as the steepness of its graph, and compare two
proportional relationships shown in different ways.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, ruler,
graph paper.

## Readiness check

Plot the points (0, 0), (1, 4), (2, 8). Do they lie on one straight
line? (Answer: yes.) What do you notice about how much `y` rises
each time `x` goes up by 1? (Answer: `y` rises by 4 each time.)
If plotting feels shaky, plot (0, 0), (1, 2), (2, 4) first and check
the same pattern.

## Teaching

Graphing `y = kx` is quick because you already know two things:
the graph passes through the origin (0, 0) — the zero test from
Lesson 01, now visible as a point — and every step of 1 across
(`x` up by 1) steps up by exactly `k`. So plot (0, 0) and one more
point, join them with a ruler, and you have the whole graph. A third
point is your check: if it misses the line, something is wrong.

That "up by `k` for each 1 across" is the graph's steepness. The
steepness has a name you will use for the rest of the unit:
**slope**. For a proportional relationship, the slope *is* the unit
rate. A line with unit rate 8 climbs twice as steeply as a line with
unit rate 4, because each step across gains twice as much height.
Steeper line, bigger unit rate — and for distance-time graphs,
steeper line, greater speed.

Comparing two proportional relationships in different
representations is the skill the standard names explicitly. The
method never changes: reduce each relationship to its unit rate,
then compare the unit rates. A graph gives its unit rate from any
point except the origin: divide `y` by `x`. A table gives it from
any row. An equation wears it openly: in `y = kx`, the unit rate
is `k`.

### Worked example 1

**Graph `y = 4x` for `x` from 0 to 3, and describe what the graph
shows.**

Make a table: `x = 0 → y = 0`; `x = 1 → y = 4`; `x = 2 → y = 8`;
`x = 3 → y = 12`. Plot (0, 0), (1, 4), (2, 8), (3, 12) and join them.
The points line up exactly — they must, because the equation adds
the same amount, 4, for every step of 1.

Reading the graph: from (0, 0) to (1, 4) the line rises 4 while
running 1 across. That rise-over-run, 4/1 = 4, is the unit rate.
From (1, 4) to (3, 12) it rises 8 while running 2 across: 8/2 = 4
again. Same unit rate everywhere — that sameness *is* what makes
the graph straight. Lesson 03 proves it with triangles.

### Worked example 2

**Runner A is shown by a distance-time graph that passes through
(2, 16): after 2 hours she has run 16 miles. Runner B is shown by
the equation `d = 6t`, where `d` is miles and `t` is hours. Who is
faster, and by how much? How far does each run in 5 hours?**

Reduce each to a unit rate. Runner A: 16 ÷ 2 = 8 miles per hour.
Runner B: the equation is `d = 6t`, so the unit rate is 6 miles per
hour, readable directly. Runner A is faster by 8 − 6 = 2 miles per
hour.

In 5 hours: Runner A covers `8 × 5 = 40` miles; Runner B covers
`6 × 5 = 30` miles. The gap after 5 hours is 10 miles, which is
exactly 5 hours × the 2-mile-per-hour difference — a good check
that both routes (rates, then totals) agree.

Notice what was *not* needed: the full graph of Runner A, or a table
for Runner B. One point plus the origin, or one equation, is enough
once you know the relationship is proportional. Always confirm
proportionality first, though — a graph that misses the origin is a
different animal (Lesson 04).

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult; draw every graph on graph paper.

1. Graph `y = 3x` for `x` from 0 to 4. Name the rise for each step
   of 1 across, and check a third point on your line.
2. A graph passes through the origin and (3, 21). What is its unit
   rate? Write its equation and predict `y` when `x = 8`.
3. Compare: a table shows 2 hours → 14 miles; an equation reads
   `d = 9t`. Which runner is faster, and by how much per hour?
4. Two graphs both pass through the origin. One passes through
   (4, 12) and the other through (4, 20). Which is steeper? What
   are the two unit rates?
5. Explain why the point (1, `k`) always lies on the graph of
   `y = kx`, and how that fact lets you read a unit rate straight
   off a graph.
6. A graph passes through (0, 0) and (5, 35), but a learner plots
   (2, 15) and says the point "does not fit, so the graph is not a
   straight line." Who is wrong, and what is the correct value at
   `x = 2`?

## Independent practice

1. Graph `y = 6x` for `x` from 0 to 3. What is the slope (rise per
   1 across)? Predict `y` when `x = 7` from the equation, then
   check it against the steepness you see.
2. A distance-time graph passes through the origin and (6, 48).
   Find the speed, write the equation, and find the distance after
   9 hours.
3. Compare: Shop A charges by the equation `C = 4n` (dollars for `n`
   notebooks). Shop B's graph passes through (5, 25). Which shop is
   cheaper per notebook, and what does each charge for 12 notebooks?
4. True or false, with a reason: "If a graph is a straight line
   through the origin, its equation is `y = kx`, where `k` is the
   `y`-value at `x = 1`."
5. A learner graphs `y = 2x` but plots (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4) — adding
   1 instead of doubling. What does the plotted line actually show,
   and where did the method go wrong? (Hint: check the ratio test.)
6. **Application.** Two water pumps fill tanks proportionally.
   Pump P's graph passes through (4, 60) gallons in minutes. Pump
   Q's equation is `G = 12m`. Which pump is faster? How many gallons
   does each deliver in 10 minutes?

## Application

A charity walkathon tracks two walkers. Walker's graph passes
through (3, 12) miles in hours; the other's equation is `d = 5t`.
After 6 hours, how far has each walked, and who is ahead by how
much? Draw both lines on the same axes (label each line with its
unit rate) and explain how the picture shows the answer before any
multiplication is done.

## Exit check

A graph passes through the origin and (2, 18). What is its unit
rate, what is its equation, and is its line steeper or less steep
than the graph of `y = 7x`? Answer all three, with one reason each.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** "steep" is a picture word — hold a hand at a
  shallow angle, then a steep angle, and match each to a small and a
  large unit rate.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the graph with a table of
  values only, and read the unit rate from the table's `y ÷ x`
  column; the drawn line is optional if the table is complete.
- **Extension:** a graph passes through (0, 0) and (4, 10). Its unit
  rate is 2.5. Graph it, and explain why a unit rate need not be a
  whole number — and why the point (2, 5) must lie on the line.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: reading the unit rate from a point's `y`-value
alone (for example, calling the rate 16 from the point (2, 16)).
Drill "divide `y` by `x`" until it is automatic, and always ask
"per *one* what?" Watch also for graphs drawn freehand through
scattered points without checking a third point. Answer-key
reference: [answer key, Lesson 02](../answer-key.md#lesson-02).
