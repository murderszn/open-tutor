# Lesson 06 — Comparing Linear Relationships

**Goal (student-readable):** I can compare two linear relationships
— proportional and non-proportional — from tables, equations, and
graphs, and explain which wins, where they cross, and why.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, ruler,
graph paper.

## Readiness check

Two plans: A costs `12h` dollars, B costs `8h + 20` dollars. Which
is cheaper at 2 hours? At 10 hours? (Answers: at 2 hours, A: $24
vs. B: $36 — A is cheaper. At 10 hours, A: $120 vs. B: $100 — B is
cheaper.) If that feels shaky, re-read Lesson 05, Worked example 2,
before starting.

## Teaching

Comparing linear relationships is the whole unit in one skill. The
method, in order:

1. **Write each relationship in the same form** — two equations,
   two tables with the same `x` values, or two lines on the same
   axes. Different forms hide the comparison; same forms reveal it.
2. **Compare slopes first, then intercepts.** The slope decides
   who pulls away over time; the intercept decides who starts
   ahead. A relationship can win early and lose late — that is
   normal, not a contradiction.
3. **Find the crossing point from the table or graph** (same value
   at the same `x`). On one side of it, one relationship wins; on
   the other side, the other wins — unless the slopes are equal,
   in which case the lines never cross and the starting advantage
   lasts forever (parallel lines).
4. **Say what "wins" means in the story.** Cheaper is not always
   better, and faster is not always better: name the criterion
   (cost, distance, time) before declaring a winner.

The trap of the lesson: **steeper does not mean "more" in every
sense.** A steeper line grows faster, but if it starts lower it may
still be behind for a long time. And a line with a head start can
look like the leader while actually losing ground every hour. The
graph shows both facts at once; the equation splits them into
`slope` and `intercept` so you can talk about them separately.

### Worked example 1

**Maya rides from the start line at a steady 12 miles per hour:
`M = 12t`. Jon starts 6 miles ahead of the start line and rides at
a steady 9 miles per hour: `J = 9t + 6`. Here `t` is hours and the
distances are miles from the start line. Build a table for 0, 1, 2,
and 3 hours. When does Maya catch Jon, and who is ahead after that?**

| `t` (hours) | Maya: `12t` | Jon: `9t + 6` |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 6 |
| 1 | 12 | 15 |
| 2 | 24 | 24 |
| 3 | 36 | 33 |

At `t = 0` Jon leads by 6 miles — his intercept, his head start.
But Maya's slope is bigger (12 vs. 9), so she gains 3 miles every
hour: the gap goes 6, 3, 0, then −3 (meaning Maya is now 3 miles
ahead). **Maya catches Jon at 2 hours, at 24 miles**, and after
that Maya stays ahead, pulling away by 3 more miles each hour.

In graph language: Maya's line is steeper and starts at the origin;
Jon's line is gentler and starts at 6. The lines cross at (2, 24).
Before the crossing, Jon's line is on top; after it, Maya's is.
Neither "steeper wins" nor "starts ahead wins" is the whole story
— you need both numbers, which is why the unit taught them
separately.

### Worked example 2

**Three movie-rental plans charge by the month for `n` movies:
A: `A = 4n`; B: `B = 3n + 6`; C: `C = 5n`. Which is cheapest for
2 movies? For 6 movies? Explain with slopes and intercepts.**

Compute both cases:

| `n` | A: `4n` | B: `3n + 6` | C: `5n` |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 8 | 12 | 10 |
| 6 | 24 | 24 | 30 |

For 2 movies, A is cheapest ($8). For 6 movies, A and B tie at
$24, and C is most expensive. Why does B catch A? B's slope is
smaller (3 vs. 4) but its intercept is bigger (6 vs. 0): B starts
behind by $6 and gains $1 per movie, so it needs 6 movies to catch
up. C is steepest (5) and starts at 0 — steepest here means most
expensive at every count, because nothing offsets its slope. The
moral: compare the *pair* (slope, intercept), never one number
alone.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult; build the table before judging.

1. Two walkers: W: `W = 4t`, V: `V = 3t + 5` (miles, hours).
   Table for 0, 2, and 5 hours. When does W catch V? Who is ahead
   at 2 hours? At 5 hours?
2. Two savings plans: S: `S = 10w`, T: `T = 6w + 20` (dollars,
   weeks). Which saves more after 3 weeks? After 8 weeks? Where
   do they tie?
3. A proportional plan (`y = 9x`) and a plan with a fee
   (`y = 7x + 8`). At what `x` do they cost the same? (Hint:
   build the table at 0, 2, 4 — the tie is at one of those rows.)
4. Two lines have the same slope, 10, but intercepts 0 and 15.
   Do they ever cross? Which is always ahead, and by how much?
   What does "always" mean in a story about two prices?
5. Explain why the picture above cannot tell you who is faster,
   and what extra information (from the text) you need before you
   can say.
6. A learner says "Jon starts ahead, so Jon wins the race." Use
   Worked example 1's table to correct the claim in two sentences.

## Independent practice

1. Runners: R: `R = 8t`, Q: `Q = 6t + 10` (miles, hours). Table
   for 0, 3, and 5 hours. When does R catch Q? Who leads at 3
   hours?
2. Phone plans: A: `A = 9g`, B: `B = 5g + 24` (dollars, gigabytes).
   Find the tie point from a table (try 0, 3, 6) and say which
   plan is cheaper for a light user (2 gigabytes) and a heavy user
   (10 gigabytes).
3. Two tanks drain: X: `X = −6t + 120`, Y: `Y = −4t + 80`
   (gallons, minutes). Which empties first? (Hint: "empties" means
   reaches 0 — solve each equation for `t`, Unit 03 style.)
4. A graph shows two lines crossing at (4, 28). One line passes
   through the origin. What is its equation? If the other line has
   intercept 12, what is its slope? (Hint: use the crossing point
   and the intercept as your two points.)
5. **Error analysis.** A learner compares `y = 6x` and `y = 4x + 5`
   and says "the first is steeper, so it is always greater." Test
   `x = 1` and `x = 10`, then correct the claim.
6. **Application.** A fair stall offers: pay-per-ride `P = 3r`, or
   a wristband `W = 2r + 12` (dollars, rides). A learner plans 5
   rides; a friend plans 15 rides. What should each choose, and
   where do the plans tie? Show the table.

## Application

Design a one-paragraph "which plan?" advice note for a customer
choosing between a proportional plan and a plan with a starting
fee, using numbers from any example in this lesson. Your note must
name the crossing point, say who wins on each side of it, and
explain the result once with slopes and once with intercepts. The
adult checks that both explanations use the same numbers.

## Exit check

Plans `P = 11x` and `Q = 7x + 16` (dollars). At `x = 3`, which
costs more, and by how much? Do the plans ever cost the same? Use
a table row to justify the second answer — do not solve a system.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** "catch up" = the gap shrinks to zero;
  "pull away" = the gap grows after the crossing. Say the gap at
  each table row out loud.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw both lines on one set of axes
  and read the crossing off the graph instead of building the
  table; the graph must be labeled with both equations.
- **Extension:** find the crossing of `M = 12t` and `J = 9t + 6`
  by setting the expressions equal and solving (optional preview
  of Unit 05), and confirm the table's answer of 2 hours.

## Teacher note

Likely misconceptions: "steeper always wins" (Independent practice
item 5), "starts ahead always wins" (Guided practice item 6), and
reading a crossing point off a graph without checking the table
values. Parallel lines (Guided practice item 4) deserve emphasis:
same slope, different intercept means the gap never changes — a
fact Unit 05 will use for "no solution" systems. Answer-key
reference: [answer key, Lesson 06](../answer-key.md#lesson-06).
