# Lesson 01 — Proportional Relationships and Unit Rate

**Goal (student-readable):** I can tell whether a relationship is
proportional, find its unit rate, and write its equation as `y = kx`.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** notebook, pencil, calculator
for checking only.

## Readiness check

A shop sells 3 notebooks for $9. What is the price of 1 notebook?
(Answer: $9 ÷ 3 = $3 per notebook.) If the price stays fair, what
should 5 notebooks cost? (Answer: 5 × $3 = $15.) If that feels
shaky, practise dividing a total into equal shares before starting.

## Teaching

Two quantities are in a **proportional relationship** when their ratio
stays the same no matter how much you have. Double one, and the other
doubles. Triple one, and the other triples. The fixed ratio has a
name: the **unit rate**, also called the **constant of
proportionality** and written `k`. It is the value of the second
quantity when the first quantity is 1.

Every proportional relationship can be written as an equation:

```
y = kx
```

Here `x` is the first quantity, `y` is the second, and `k` is the
unit rate. Two quick tests, both worth doing:

1. **Ratio test.** Divide `y` by `x` for several pairs. If you get
   the same number every time, the relationship is proportional,
   and that number is `k`.
2. **Zero test.** When `x = 0`, a proportional relationship gives
   `y = 0`. Nothing in, nothing out. If `x = 0` gives something
   else — a joining fee, a starting amount — the relationship is
   **not** proportional, even if it grows steadily.

A common error is to see "goes up by the same amount each step" and
call it proportional. Steady growth is not enough: the ratio must
stay constant, and the line must start at zero. Lesson 04 will name
the not-proportional steady kind (`y = mx + b`).

### Worked example 1

**A lemonade stand charges $6 for 2 cups, $9 for 3 cups, and $15 for
5 cups. Is the price proportional to the number of cups? Write the
equation.**

Apply the ratio test: $6 ÷ 2 = 3$; $9 ÷ 3 = 3$; $15 ÷ 5 = 3$. The
ratio is $3 every time, so the relationship is proportional and the
unit rate is $3 per cup. The equation is `y = 3x`, where `x` is cups
and `y` is dollars.

Check with the zero test: 0 cups cost $0 — no joining fee, no
starting charge. That agrees.

Now use the equation: 7 cups cost `y = 3 × 7 = $21`. Notice that
the equation answers a question the table never asked — that is the
point of writing it.

### Worked example 2

**4 litres of paint cover 32 square metres. What area does 1 litre
cover, and how much area do 10 litres cover?**

The unit rate is area per litre: 32 ÷ 4 = 8 square metres per litre.
So `k = 8`, and with `x` litres and `y` square metres, `y = 8x`.

For 10 litres: `y = 8 × 10 = 80` square metres.

Check by scaling: 10 litres is 2.5 times 4 litres, and 2.5 × 32 = 80.
Both routes agree. If they disagreed, one of them would be wrong —
that disagreement is a signal, not a mystery.

**A non-example, so the boundary is sharp.** A taxi charges a $2
pickup fee plus $3 per mile. For 1 mile the total is $5; for 2 miles,
$8; for 3 miles, $11. The ratio test fails: 5 ÷ 1 = 5, 8 ÷ 2 = 4,
11 ÷ 3 ≈ 3.67. And the zero test fails: 0 miles still cost $2. The
growth is steady (+$3 per mile) but the relationship is **not**
proportional. It is the kind Lesson 04 will write as `y = 3x + 2`.

## Guided practice

Work these with the adult; say the ratio test out loud each time.

1. A cyclist rides 15 miles in 1 hour, 30 miles in 2 hours, 45 miles
   in 3 hours. Proportional? Unit rate? Equation?
2. A recipe uses 2 cups of flour for 12 muffins, 3 cups for 18
   muffins, 5 cups for 30 muffins. Proportional? Unit rate? Equation?
3. A data plan charges $5 per gigabyte with no monthly fee.
   4 gigabytes cost $20. Write the equation and find the cost of
   9 gigabytes.
4. Table: `x`: 1, 2, 4; `y`: 6, 12, 24. Proportional? Find `k` and
   write the equation.
5. Table: `x`: 0, 1, 2, 3; `y`: 4, 7, 10, 13. Proportional? Explain
   with both tests.
6. Explain in one sentence why `y = 5x` is proportional but
   `y = 5x + 1` is not, using the zero test.

## Independent practice

1. A runner covers 8 miles in 2 hours at a steady pace. Find the
   unit rate, write the equation, and find the distance in 7 hours.
2. 6 kilograms of apples cost $18. Find the unit rate, write the
   equation, and find the cost of 11 kilograms.
3. Table: `x`: 3, 5, 7; `y`: 18, 30, 42. Proportional? Equation?
   Predict `y` when `x = 10`.
4. Table: `x`: 0, 2, 4; `y`: 3, 9, 15. Proportional? If not, say
   which test fails and what the pattern actually is.
5. A printer prints 25 pages per minute. Write the equation (pages
   in terms of minutes) and find how many pages it prints in
   12 minutes. Is the relationship proportional? Why?
6. **Error analysis.** Sam says the table `x`: 2, 4, 6; `y`: 5, 9,
   13 is proportional "because `y` goes up by 4 each time." Is Sam
   right? Show the ratio test and correct the claim.

## Application

A school fair sells ride tickets. Stall A: 4 tickets for $12.
Stall B: 10 tickets for $25, with no extra fees at either stall.
Are both proportional? Which stall is cheaper per ticket, and by how
much? If a learner buys 20 tickets, how much does each stall charge?
Show the ratio test for both stalls and write both equations.

## Exit check

Write the equation for a proportional relationship with unit rate 7,
and use it to find `y` when `x = 6`. Then name one relationship
from your own day that is proportional, and one steady relationship
that is not, and say which test separates them.

## Supports and extension

- **Language access:** say "per" as "for each one" every time
  ("$3 per cup" = "$3 for each one cup") until the phrase is
  automatic.
- **Alternate response mode:** answer with a ratio table (columns
  `x`, `y`, `y ÷ x`) instead of sentences; the table *is* the
  explanation if every row is filled.
- **Extension:** find a proportional relationship where the unit
  rate is a fraction (for example, 3 miles in 4 hours → `k = 3/4`
  miles per hour) and explain why `y = (3/4)x` still passes both
  tests.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "adds the same amount" confused with
"proportional" (see Independent practice item 6 and the taxi
non-example). Insist on both tests for the first week. Also watch
for unit-rate inversion (dividing `x` by `y`): always ask "per
*what*?" — the unit rate's denominator is the `x` quantity.
