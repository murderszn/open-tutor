# Lesson 02 — Strongest Evidence: Quoting, Paraphrasing, Lead-Ins

**Goal (student-readable):** I can rank evidence by strength and weave it
into my own sentences with quotations, paraphrases, and lead-ins —
punctuated correctly.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** this page, Lesson 1 notes ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L2](../answer-key.md#lesson-02)

**Readiness check (3 min):** From Lesson 1, pick your best inference about
Mara. Write the exact words from "The Lantern" that support it. Today you
learn how to put those words inside your own writing.

## Not all evidence is equal

Three details can all point the same way — and still not be equally
strong. The **strongest evidence** is the detail that most directly proves
the claim: specific beats vague, and on-point beats loosely related.

**Claim:** Mara was worried about Tomas.

| Evidence | Strength | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Mara stood at the kitchen window." | Weak | Standing at a window could mean anything — daydreaming, waiting for a delivery. |
| "She had reheated [the casserole] twice already." | Better | Reheating twice proves she waited a long time and kept dinner ready — waiting with care. |
| "She pretended she hadn't been watching the window at all." | Strongest | Only someone self-conscious about how much they care pretends not to watch. It proves both the watching *and* the feeling about it. |

Ranking is a thinking move, not a guessing move: ask, *"If this were the
only detail I had, how much would it prove?"*

## Three ways to bring evidence into your sentences

1. **Quotation** — the author's exact words in quotation marks. Use it
   when the wording itself matters.
2. **Paraphrase** — the same idea in your own words. Use it when the idea
   matters more than the wording. A paraphrase still needs attribution —
   it is the author's idea.
3. **Lead-in** — the sentence (or fragment) that introduces the evidence
   and connects it to your claim. Evidence never appears alone; it always
   arrives with a guide.

## Worked example 1 — Quote, paraphrase, and lead-in on one sentence

Original sentence from "The Lantern":

> She checked her phone for the fourth time — no messages, which was
> strange, because Tomas texted about everything, even the weather at
> truck stops.

**Quotation with lead-in:** Mara checks her phone "for the fourth time,"
and the narrator explains that Tomas "texted about everything, even the
weather at truck stops." The fragments blend into the writer's sentence —
no comma needed before a blended fragment.

**Paraphrase with lead-in:** Mara keeps checking for messages, which is
unusual because Tomas normally texts constantly. The idea is the author's;
the words are the writer's. Notice the lead-in ("which is unusual
because") tells the reader *why this evidence matters*.

**Punctuation rules that matter here:**

- Full-sentence quote after a lead-in: use a comma or colon.
  *The narrator states it plainly: "She checked her phone for the fourth time."*
- Blended fragment: no comma.
  *Mara checks "for the fourth time."*
- Periods and commas go **inside** the closing quotation mark in American
  English: *"for the fourth time,"* not *"for the fourth time",*.

## Worked example 2 — Ranking, then writing

**Claim:** Tomas's lateness was unusual, not routine.

- Weak: "Her brother's truck was supposed to be in the driveway by six."
  (Sets the schedule, but says nothing about whether lateness is normal.)
- Better: "At seven-fifteen, the casserole had gone from steaming to
  merely warm." (An hour-plus of waiting is concrete.)
- Strongest: "no messages, which was strange, because Tomas texted about
  everything." (The narrator *labels* it strange — the text itself flags
  the break in pattern.)

**Written as a claim with evidence:**

> Tomas's lateness was unusual for him. Dinner had been ready since six,
> but "at seven-fifteen, the casserole had gone from steaming to merely
> warm," and more tellingly, there were "no messages, which was strange,
> because Tomas texted about everything, even the weather at truck
> stops." The narrator's word *strange* does the ranking for us: this is
> not an ordinary late arrival.

## Guided practice (with a partner)

**A. Rank the evidence.** Claim: *The bus-stop teenager came prepared for
bad weather.* Rank these 1 (strongest) to 3 (weakest) and say why in one
sentence each:

1. The teenager is wearing a yellow raincoat.
2. A closed black umbrella hangs on the bench.
3. Dark gray clouds fill the sky.

**B. Fix the integration.** Each sentence breaks a quotation or
punctuation rule. Rewrite it correctly:

1. Mara "stood at the kitchen window long after the streetlights buzzed
   on".
2. The narrator says "She checked her phone for the fourth time."
3. Tomas texted about everything "even the weather at truck stops" which
   was strange.
4. Mara pretended she hadn't been watching, "she was already at the door
   with his plate covered in foil."

## Independent practice

Return to the two inferences you wrote in Lesson 1. For **each**
inference, write two versions:

1. A **quotation version**: one sentence with a lead-in and a blended
   fragment quote.
2. A **paraphrase version**: one sentence with a lead-in and the idea in
   your own words.

Then circle the version you think is stronger for that inference, and
write one sentence explaining your choice.

## Application

Write one paragraph (5–7 sentences): *Was Mara's worry reasonable?*
Take a position and support it with **two pieces of evidence** — one as a
quotation, one as a paraphrase — each with a lead-in. End with one
sentence explaining which piece is stronger and why.

## Exit check

1. What is the difference between a quotation and a paraphrase, and what
   do they have in common?
2. Punctuate correctly: *The narrator notes that Mara checked her phone
   for the fourth time* (turn the quoted part into a blended fragment).
3. Which is stronger evidence that Mara waited a long time: "she had
   reheated it twice already" or "the wind rattled the loose screen
   door"? Why?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Keep a "quote bank" of five copy-ready fragments
  from "The Lantern" on an index card; build sentences from the bank.
- **Alternate response mode:** Dictate the paragraph; the adult scribes
  while the learner directs the punctuation.
- **Extension:** Find one place in the guided practice where a paraphrase
  would be *stronger* than a quotation, and rewrite it.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners drop a quotation into a paragraph with
no lead-in ("quote bombing") or paraphrase without attribution. Insist
that every piece of evidence arrives with a sentence that says what it is
doing there. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-02).
