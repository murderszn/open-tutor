# Lesson 03 — Theme vs. Topic: Tracking a Theme

**Goal (student-readable):** I can state a theme as a full-sentence claim
and show how it develops across the beginning, middle, and end of a text.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (3 min):** In one or two words, what is "Mother to Son"
*about*? Now try to say what it *claims* about that subject in a full
sentence. Notice how much harder the second one is — that gap is today's
lesson.

## Topic is not theme

- A **topic** is the subject in a word or two: *perseverance*,
  *hardship*, *grief*. Most readers can name the topic in seconds.
- A **theme** is what the text *claims* about the topic, stated as a
  complete sentence: *Hardship does not excuse quitting; a life is built
  by climbing through it.* A theme must be supportable by the *whole*
  text, not just one line.

Test: if it is not a complete sentence, it is a topic. If the text's
details do not all point toward it, it is not this text's theme.

## Mother to Son — Langston Hughes (1922; U.S. public domain)

> Well, son, I'll tell you:
> Life for me ain't been no crystal stair.
> It's had tacks in it,
> And splinters,
> And boards torn up,
> And places with no carpet on the floor —
> Bare.
> But all the time
> I'se been a-climbin' on,
> And reachin' landin's,
> And turnin' corners,
> And sometimes goin' in the dark
> Where there ain't been no light.
> So boy, don't you turn back.
> Don't you set down on the steps
> 'Cause you finds it's kinder hard.
> Don't you fall now —
> For I'se still goin', honey,
> I'se still climbin',
> And life for me ain't been no crystal stair.

*(Text-rights note: published 1922 in* The Crisis; *in the U.S. public
domain. Quoted in full here so every learner works from the same words.)*

## Worked example 1 — Topic or theme?

| Statement | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "The theme is perseverance." | Topic, not theme | One word, not a claim. It names the subject but says nothing about it. |
| "The theme is that mothers always know best." | Not this poem's theme | Too broad, and the poem never claims it — the mother speaks from her own hard experience, not from authority over everything. |
| "The theme is that the speaker climbed stairs in a house with no carpet." | Plot summary, not theme | Retells details without claiming anything about them. |
| "Endurance through hardship is possible and worthwhile — the speaker's own climbing proves it, so the son should keep climbing too." | Theme | Full sentence, and every part of the poem supports it (see below). |

## Worked example 2 — Tracking the theme across the poem

A theme is not found in one line; it is **tracked** — built up as the
text adds layers.

- **Beginning (the setup):** "Life for me ain't been no crystal stair."
  The central metaphor arrives in line 2: life as a rough staircase, and
  the word *crystal* sets up ease and luxury as the thing life is *not*.
- **Middle (the proof):** the catalog of hardships — "tacks," "splinters,"
  "boards torn up," "places with no carpet on the floor — / Bare" — makes
  the metaphor concrete and painful. Then the turn: "But all the time /
  I'se been a-climbin' on" — hardship listed *in order to show* it did
  not stop her. The dark stretches ("sometimes goin' in the dark / Where
  there ain't been no light") raise the stakes: she climbed even without
  light.
- **End (the transfer):** the direct address — "So boy, don't you turn
  back... Don't you fall now" — hands the theme to the son as advice.
  The closing return to the stair image, now with "I'se still goin',
  honey, / I'se still climbin'," proves the claim with a living example:
  she is *still* climbing as she speaks.

Notice the method: **detail → what it adds → running claim.** Each
section of the poem contributes one layer, and the theme is the sum.

## The Empty Chair (original story for this lesson)

Every Sunday, Elena set five plates on the table, though only four
people ate. The fifth plate went in front of the empty chair by the
window — the one her grandfather had claimed with a joke about
"supervising the street." He had died in March, and the chair had been
empty for four months, but Elena still set the plate, still poured a
glass of water beside it, still caught herself glancing over when her
mother laughed at something. At first her mother had moved the plate
away, gently, and Elena had moved it back, gently. Now nobody moved it.
Last Sunday, Elena's little brother asked why the chair got a plate if
nobody sat there, and her mother said, "Because someone did." Elena
thought about that all through dinner, and afterward she washed the extra
plate by hand, carefully, the way she used to watch him do it — slowly,
like the plate mattered.

## Guided practice (with a partner)

**A. Repair the theme statements.** Each is broken in a labeled way.
Rewrite each as a true theme of its text:

1. "The theme of 'Mother to Son' is hard work." *(topic, not theme)*
2. "The theme of 'The Empty Chair' is that Elena's family eats dinner
   on Sundays." *(plot summary, not theme)*
3. "The theme of 'Mother to Son' is that life is unfair and nobody can
   change it." *(contradicted by the text — the speaker keeps climbing)*

**B. Start the track.** For "The Empty Chair," fill the first row of a
three-column chart together: **Beginning detail** (the fifth plate) →
**what it adds** → **running theme claim**. You will finish the chart
alone below.

## Application

Choose one: "Mother to Son" or "The Empty Chair." Write one paragraph
(5–7 sentences) that states the theme and **tracks its development**
through at least three moments in the text, citing each moment. This is
the core move of the unit project — practice it here.

## Exit check

1. In your own words: what is the difference between a topic and a
   theme?
2. Write a theme statement for "Mother to Son" as a full sentence.
   (Any defensible statement supported by the whole poem earns credit —
   see the answer key for acceptable range.)
3. Name one detail from the *end* of "Mother to Son" and explain what
   layer it adds to the theme.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Use the tracking-chart columns as sentence starters:
  "In the beginning… This shows… By the end…"
- **Alternate response mode:** Build the chart on index cards (one card
  per section) and arrange them before writing.
- **Extension:** Compare the two texts' themes: both are about enduring
  hardship, but one is spoken advice and the other is a quiet ritual.
  Write three sentences on how the *form* changes the *theme's* feel.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners write themes as morals ("always be
kind") detached from the text. Require the theme to *need* this text:
ask, "Could you say that about any story? Then it is not this story's
theme." See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-03).
