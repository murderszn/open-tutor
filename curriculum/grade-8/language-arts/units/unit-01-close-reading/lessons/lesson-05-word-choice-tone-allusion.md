# Lesson 05 — Word Choice, Tone, and Allusion

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain how a writer's specific word
choices — including connotation and allusion — create tone, and I can
figure out unknown words with context, word parts, and references.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** this page, dictionary/thesaurus
(print or digital) · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (3 min):** Which sounds worse: "He is *stubborn*" or
"He is *resolute*"? Both describe someone who does not give up — so why
does one sting? That difference is today's whole lesson.

## If We Must Die — Claude McKay (1919; U.S. public domain)

> If we must die, let it not be like hogs
> Hunted and penned in an inglorious spot,
> While round us bark the mad and hungry dogs,
> Making their mock at our accursed lot.
> If we must die, O let us nobly die,
> So that our precious blood may not be shed
> In vain; then even the monsters we defy
> Shall be constrained to honor us though dead!
> O kinsmen! we must meet the common foe!
> Though far outnumbered let us show us brave,
> And for their thousand blows deal one death-blow!
> What though before us lies the open grave?
> Like men we'll face the murderous, cowardly pack,
> Pressed to the wall, dying, but fighting back!

*(Text-rights note: published July 1919 in* The Liberator; *in the U.S.
public domain. Quoted in full so every learner works from the same
words. Context note for the guiding adult: McKay, a Jamaican-born Black
poet, wrote this during the "Red Summer" of 1919, a wave of anti-Black
violence in the United States. The poem answers degrading violence with
a demand for dignity. Preview for mature themes — violence, death —
before assigning.)*

## Denotation vs. connotation

A word's **denotation** is its dictionary definition. Its
**connotation** is the feeling and association it carries. Writers choose
words for both.

## Worked example 1 — "hogs" is not "pigs"

Denotation: a hog is a pig. But McKay does not write "let it not be like
pigs." *Hogs* drags in the slaughterhouse: animals "hunted and penned,"
driven to killing. The connotation does the poem's first job — naming
the death the speaker refuses: a degraded, helpless one. Then line 5
answers with the opposite charge: "O let us **nobly** die." *Nobly*
connotes honor, choice, and dignity — the exact reversal of *hogs*. Two
word choices, one line apart, frame the poem's whole argument: not
*whether* we die, but *how*.

More connotation pairs from the poem:

- **"kinsmen"** (line 9) vs. "people": *kinsmen* connotes family bond
  and shared blood — the fighters are not strangers; they belong to
  each other.
- **"monsters we defy"** (line 7) vs. "enemies": *monsters* connotes
  inhuman cruelty — it justifies defiance and makes the "honor us though
  dead" claim feel earned.
- **"precious blood"** (line 6) vs. "blood": *precious* insists a life
  has worth even when the attackers treat it as worthless.

## Worked example 2 — Tone is built, not declared

**Tone** is the writer's attitude toward the subject. The poem never says
"I feel defiant." Instead, defiance is *built* from word choices:
"nobly," "defy," "brave," "death-blow," "fighting back." Imagine the
same situation in a despairing tone: "hopelessly," "doomed," "surrender,"
"weeping." Same facts (outnumbered, facing death), opposite tone —
proof that tone lives in word choice.

And notice the **allusion** — a reference that borrows meaning from
outside the text. The poem's pattern (outnumbered fighters, a common foe,
honor in a last stand) echoes the heroic tradition of dignified final
battles found in epic stories across cultures. You do not need to name
one specific epic to feel the echo: "Like men we'll face the murderous,
cowardly pack" borrows the *shape* of every last-stand story, and the
borrowed shape lends the speaker's demand its gravity. (Similarly, many
readers hear a biblical echo in Hughes's "crystal stair" — a stairway
image with heaven at the top. Allusions are interpretations: name the
echo, then show the words that create it.)

## Three vocabulary strategies (L.8.4)

When a word is unknown, work in this order:

1. **Context:** read the whole sentence and ask what meaning the
   sentence *needs*. "Penned in an **inglorious** spot" sits opposite
   "let us **nobly** die" — the sentence needs "without glory, shameful."
2. **Word parts:** break it. *in-* (not) + *glorious* → "not glorious."
   *ac-* (intensive) + *cursed* → "under a curse, doomed."
3. **Reference:** check a dictionary or thesaurus to **verify** — never
   to replace thinking. Confirm the part of speech and the exact shade.

## Guided practice (with a partner)

**A. Connotation sort.** Arrange these five words from most negative to
most positive connotation. They share one denotation — *someone who does
not give up* — so the order comes entirely from feeling:

*bullheaded · willful · firm · persistent · resolute*

Then answer: which two would the speaker of "If We Must Die" use for his
kinsmen, and which would he use for the "murderous, cowardly pack"?

**B. Tone builders.** Find three more words in the poem (beyond "nobly,"
"defy," "brave," "fighting back") that build the defiant tone. For each,
write what the line would feel like with a neutral synonym.

**C. Strategy drill.** Use all three strategies on **"constrained"**
(line 8): (1) what does the sentence need it to mean? (2) break it into
parts; (3) verify with a reference and write the dictionary definition
in your own words.

## Independent practice

Choose three words from the poem you did not fully know before today
(suggestions: *inglorious, accursed, kinsmen*). For each, show your work
in three labeled steps — **Context guess → Word parts → Reference
check** — and end with a one-sentence definition in your own words.

## Application

Write one paragraph (5–7 sentences): *How does McKay's word choice in
the first four lines create a tone of refused degradation?* Quote or
reference at least three specific words, explain each word's connotation,
and connect the choices to the tone. This is RL.8.4 in one paragraph:
word choice → tone, proven with evidence.

## Exit check

1. In your own words: what is the difference between denotation and
   connotation? Give one example from the poem.
2. Name two words from "If We Must Die" that build its defiant tone and
   explain what each contributes.
3. What is an allusion, and what earlier tradition does this poem's
   last-stand pattern echo?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Read the poem aloud twice — once flat, once with
  the defiant tone. Mark the words your voice naturally stresses; those
  are your tone builders.
- **Alternate response mode:** Do the connotation sort as a physical
  line-up of word cards across the table, from most negative to most
  positive.
- **Extension:** Write four lines of your own in the poem's *pattern*
  (outnumbered, dignity demanded) but about a nonviolent situation —
  then explain which of McKay's word-choice moves you borrowed.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners define connotation as "the hidden
meaning" and hunt for secret codes. Correct gently: connotation is not
hidden — it is the *felt* meaning, and it is public. If the class cannot
agree whether "willful" is positive or negative, that disagreement *is*
the lesson: connotation varies, which is why writers choose carefully.
