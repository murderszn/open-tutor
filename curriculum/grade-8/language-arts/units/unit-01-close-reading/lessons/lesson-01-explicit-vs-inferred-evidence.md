# Lesson 01 — Explicit vs. Inferred: The Evidence Test

**Goal (student-readable):** I can tell the difference between what a text
says outright and what it suggests, and I can point to the exact words
that support an inference.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** this page, two highlighter colors,
the bus-stop illustration below · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer-key.md § L1](../answer-key.md#lesson-01)

**Readiness check (3 min):** Look at the illustration below for 30 seconds.
Name one thing you can *see* and one thing you *think is happening*. Keep
both — you will test them against each other.

## The two kinds of meaning

Every text carries two kinds of meaning.

- **Explicit meaning** is stated outright. You can put your finger on the
  sentence. *The casserole had gone from steaming to merely warm* is
  explicit: the words say it.
- **An inference** is meaning the text suggests but does not state. You
  build it from clues in the text plus what you already know. *Mara was
  worried about her brother* is an inference: no sentence says "Mara was
  worried," but the clues point there.

The rule for this whole unit: **an inference is only as good as its
evidence.** "I just think so" is not evidence. "These exact words" is.

## Worked example 1 — Reading the picture like a text

Study the illustration. First, list what is **observable** (you can point
to it):

1. Dark gray clouds fill the sky.
2. A teenager in a yellow raincoat stands at a bus stop.
3. A closed black umbrella hangs on the bench.
4. A backpack with a book sticking out sits on the bench.
5. A bus with its headlights on approaches in the distance.
6. The pavement is wet, with puddles reflecting the sky.

Now build **inferences** — and notice how each one names its evidence:

- **Inference:** It has recently rained, or is about to.
  **Evidence:** wet pavement and puddles (4, 6), dark clouds (1), the
  raincoat (2), the umbrella (3). Four separate clues converge — that is
  what "strongest evidence" starts to look like.
- **Inference:** The teenager is waiting for that bus.
  **Evidence:** standing at the bus stop (2), facing the approaching bus
  with headlights on (5).
- **Inference:** The teenager was probably reading while waiting.
  **Evidence:** the open backpack with a book pulled out (4). This one is
  weaker — the book might just be packed badly — so a careful reader says
  *probably* and keeps looking for more clues.

## The Lantern (original story for this lesson)

Mara stood at the kitchen window long after the streetlights buzzed on.
Her brother's truck was supposed to be in the driveway by six. At
seven-fifteen, the casserole had gone from steaming to merely warm, and
she had reheated it twice already. She checked her phone for the fourth
time — no messages, which was strange, because Tomas texted about
everything, even the weather at truck stops. The wind rattled the loose
screen door, and she latched it, then unlatched it again. When headlights
finally swept across the ceiling at eight-oh-two, she was already at the
door with his plate covered in foil, and she pretended she hadn't been
watching the window at all.

## Worked example 2 — Explicit or inferred?

**Claim A:** "Mara reheated the casserole twice."
This is **explicit**. The sentence says it word for word: *"she had
reheated it twice already."* Finger on the sentence — done.

**Claim B:** "Mara was worried about Tomas."
This is an **inference**. No sentence says she was worried. But the
evidence stacks up: she watched the window past dark, reheated dinner
twice, checked her phone four times, and fussed with the screen door.
Four clues, one conclusion. And the last line — she *pretended she
hadn't been watching* — is the strongest single piece: people only
pretend not to watch when they care about being seen watching.

**Claim C:** "Tomas is a careless driver."
This is **not supported**. There is no evidence about his driving. A
careful reader rejects it — not because it is impossible, but because the
text gives it nothing to stand on.

## Guided practice (with a partner)

For each statement about the bus-stop illustration, mark **O**
(observable) or **I** (inference). For every I, name the observation it
rests on.

1. The sky is full of dark clouds.
2. It rained earlier today.
3. The teenager is wearing a yellow raincoat.
4. The teenager expects rain.
5. A bus is coming.
6. The teenager missed the bus.
7. The pavement is wet.
8. The teenager brought something to read.

## Independent practice

Re-read "The Lantern." In your notebook:

1. List **three explicit facts** (quote the exact words or give the
   sentence number).
2. Write **two inferences** about Mara or Tomas. For each, write the
   **exact words from the story** that support it.
3. Write one **unsupported claim** — something a careless reader might
   say that the story does not support — and explain in one sentence why
   the story does not support it.

## Application

Write one paragraph (4–6 sentences): *What is Mara feeling in the last
two sentences of the story, and how do you know?* Use at least two
pieces of quoted or clearly referenced evidence.

## Exit check

1. Which is an inference about "The Lantern": (a) "Mara reheated the
   casserole twice" or (b) "Mara was worried about Tomas"? Explain your
   choice in one sentence.
2. Quote one piece of evidence from the story that supports your answer
   to #1.
3. From the bus-stop illustration: state one observation and one
   inference, and name the observation your inference rests on.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Read "The Lantern" aloud with the adult first;
  highlight explicit facts in one color before hunting inferences.
- **Alternate response mode:** Answer the guided practice orally, or sort
  O/I cards instead of writing.
- **Extension:** Find the *weakest* inference you made today and argue
  against it — what other explanation could fit the same clues?

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners treat any personal reaction as an
inference ("I think Tomas is nice, so that's my inference"). Redirect:
an inference must be *built from the text's clues*, not from feelings
about the topic. If they cannot point to words, it is a guess, not an
inference. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-01) for
discussion moves.
