# Lesson 04 — Objective Summary and Central Idea in Informational Text

**Goal (student-readable):** I can write an objective summary of an
informational text and state its central idea with supporting details.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** this page, notebook ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L4](../answer-key.md#lesson-04)

**Readiness check (3 min):** Summarize "The Lantern" in two sentences for
a friend who will never read it. Now check: did you sneak in any opinion
("sad," "boring," "should")? Circle it. Summaries report; they do not
judge.

## What "objective" means

An **objective summary** retells a text's main ideas in your own words —
shorter than the original, in the text's order, with **no opinions, no
outside facts, and no "I think."** The test: a reader who disagrees with
you about everything should still agree your summary is accurate.

A **central idea** is the informational text's equivalent of a theme: the
main point the whole text develops, stated as a full sentence.

## Why Bridges Sway (original informational passage for this lesson)

On the morning of November 7, 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in
Washington state twisted itself apart and fell into the water below. The
bridge was only four months old. From the day it opened, drivers had
noticed something strange: on windy days, the long, narrow deck rippled
up and down like a ribbon, earning it the nickname "Galloping Gertie."

Engineers at the time understood that wind pushes on structures. What
they had not fully understood was that wind can also set up a rhythm. On
that November morning, steady winds of about 40 miles per hour began
pushing the deck sideways at just the wrong pace. Each push arrived at
the same point in the bridge's natural swing, the way a parent times
pushes on a playground swing. The twists grew larger with every cycle — a
process engineers now call aeroelastic flutter. Within an hour, the deck
was heaving in waves several feet high, suspenders snapped, and a
600-foot section dropped into Puget Sound.

Remarkably, no people died. The bridge had been closed to traffic as the
twisting worsened. The only fatality was a dog named Tubby, whose owner
could not be persuaded to leave the car.

The collapse changed bridge design worldwide. Engineers learned that a
bridge must be tested against the rhythm of wind, not just its force.
Modern long bridges are studied in wind tunnels before they are built,
and their decks are shaped to let wind slip past instead of catching it
— some even have open grating down the center. "Galloping Gertie"
taught the profession a humbling lesson: a structure can be strong enough
to stand and still be shaped wrong for the wind.

*(Factual note: the dates, wind speed, "Galloping Gertie" nickname,
aeroelastic flutter, and the bridge's design legacy are the standard
historical account of the 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse.)*

## Worked example 1 — Good summary vs. broken summary

Paragraph 2, summarized well:

> Engineers knew wind pushes on bridges, but they had not understood
> that steady wind can build a destructive rhythm. On November 7, 1940,
> 40-mile-per-hour winds pushed the Tacoma Narrows deck at the pace of
> its natural swing, and the twisting grew until a 600-foot section fell.

Why it works: shorter than the original, main ideas in order, no opinion
words, no new facts.

Broken version of the same paragraph:

> The engineers were foolish not to realize that wind is incredibly
> dangerous, and I think the bridge was badly built. The wind was
> terrifying and destroyed the whole bridge in minutes.

Three violations: **opinion** ("foolish," "badly built," "terrifying" —
the text says none of these), **outside claim** ("destroyed the whole
bridge" — the text says a 600-foot section), and **wrong detail** ("in
minutes" — the text says "within an hour").

## Worked example 2 — Finding the central idea

Candidates:

1. "The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940." — True, but only a
   fact from paragraph 1. A central idea must cover the *whole* text.
2. "Wind is dangerous." — Too broad; you could say it about any storm
   story. It does not need *this* text.
3. "The 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse showed engineers that wind's rhythm,
   not just its force, can destroy a bridge — and changed how long
   bridges are designed." — **Central idea.** It connects the collapse
   (paragraphs 1–3) to the lesson (paragraph 4), using only the text's
   ideas.

Method: look for the idea that **repeats** (rhythm/pace appears in
paragraphs 2 and 4), check the **opening and closing** (both frame the
collapse as a lesson), and reject anything one paragraph could carry
alone.

## Guided practice (with a partner)

**A. Purge the opinion.** Each sentence is trying to be a summary
sentence but smuggles something in. Rewrite it objectively:

1. "The stupid engineers ignored the obvious danger of the wind."
2. "Amazingly, the heroic officials closed the bridge just in time."
3. "The bridge fell because it was the worst-designed bridge in
   American history."
4. "In my opinion, the most interesting part is the poor dog."

**B. Rank the central-idea candidates** for "Why Bridges Sway" 1–3 and
say what each one is missing or getting wrong:

1. "Dogs should not be left in cars on bridges."
2. "The Tacoma Narrows Bridge was nicknamed 'Galloping Gertie.'"
3. "A bridge can survive wind's force and still fail against wind's
   rhythm, which is why engineers now test designs in wind tunnels."

## Independent practice

Write an **objective summary** of the whole passage "Why Bridges Sway"
in 5–7 sentences. Checklist before you finish:

- [ ] Covers all four paragraphs' main ideas, in order
- [ ] Shorter than the original (about one quarter the length)
- [ ] No opinion words (*foolish, amazing, should, unfortunately…*)
- [ ] No facts from outside the passage
- [ ] Written in your own words (no copied sentences)

## Application

Write one paragraph (4–6 sentences): state the passage's **central
idea** as a full sentence, then support it with **two details** from
different paragraphs, each introduced with a lead-in. This is the
informational version of the theme-tracking paragraph from Lesson 3 —
same skill, different text type.

## Exit check

1. List the four rules of an objective summary.
2. Why is "The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940" not a central
   idea, even though it is true?
3. Rewrite objectively: "The brilliant engineers quickly learned their
   lesson after the tragic collapse."

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Number the passage's paragraphs; write one
  "main-idea phrase" per paragraph first, then expand into sentences.
- **Alternate response mode:** Build the summary as a 4-box comic
  (one box per paragraph, caption only, no opinion words) before writing
  sentences.
- **Extension:** Compare this lesson's central-idea method with Lesson
  3's theme-tracking method: write three sentences on what is the same
  and what is different when the text is informational instead of
  literary.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners believe "objective" means "boring"
and drain all specific detail, producing summaries so vague they fit any
text ("It was about a bridge that fell"). Push back the other way: an
objective summary is *precise but neutral* — dates, numbers, and names
stay; judgments go. See the [teacher
guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-04).
