# Answer Key — Unit 01

**For the guiding adult only.** Every learner-facing question in the
five lessons, practice sets, project, quiz, and unit assessment is
solved here. Open-response items show **sample reasoning and the
acceptable range** — credit any response that meets the stated criteria,
not just these wordings. Misconception notes and reteaching moves are in
the [teacher guide](teacher-guide.md).

## Lesson 01 {#lesson-01}

**Guided practice (O/I sort):**

1. O — directly visible: dark clouds fill the sky.
2. I — rests on the wet pavement and puddles (it may have rained; the
   picture does not show rain falling).
3. O — directly visible: yellow raincoat.
4. I — rests on the raincoat (3) and the umbrella; preparation implies
   expectation.
5. O — directly visible: a bus approaches with headlights on.
6. I — and a **weak, likely wrong** one: the bus is still approaching,
   so "missed" contradicts the evidence. Use this to teach that
   inferences can be *wrong* when they ignore evidence.
7. O — directly visible: wet pavement, puddles.
8. I — rests on the book sticking out of the backpack (labeled
   *probably* in the lesson — the book might just be packed badly).

**Independent practice (sample):**

1. Explicit facts (any three): "Her brother's truck was supposed to be
   in the driveway by six." / "she had reheated it twice already." /
   "She checked her phone for the fourth time."
2. Inferences (samples): *Mara was worried about Tomas* — evidence:
   "she had reheated it twice already," "checked her phone for the
   fourth time," "she pretended she hadn't been watching the window at
   all." / *Tomas is usually punctual and communicative* — evidence:
   "no messages, which was strange, because Tomas texted about
   everything."
3. Unsupported claim (sample): "Tomas was in a car accident." The story
   never says why he is late — no evidence supports this; it is a guess.

**Application (sample paragraph):** *In the last two sentences Mara
feels embarrassed about how obvious her worrying has been. When the
headlights finally come, "she was already at the door with his plate
covered in foil" — she was so ready that she cannot hide it — and then
"she pretended she hadn't been watching the window at all." People only
pretend not to have been watching when they care about being seen
caring.*

**Exit check:**

1. (b) — no sentence states that Mara was worried; the feeling is built
   from clues (window-watching, reheating, phone-checking).
2. Any quoted clue, e.g. "she pretended she hadn't been watching the
   window at all."
3. Sample: Observation — "A closed black umbrella hangs on the bench."
   Inference — "The teenager expects rain," resting on the umbrella
   (plus raincoat and clouds).

## Lesson 02 {#lesson-02}

**Guided A (ranking for "came prepared for bad weather"):**

1. **Strongest — the yellow raincoat.** It is worn preparation on the
   teenager's own body: the most direct proof of expecting bad weather.
2. **Middle — the closed black umbrella.** Brought for rain, but unused
   and hanging on a bench — preparation one step removed.
3. **Weakest — dark gray clouds.** A weather clue, not evidence about
   the *teenager's* preparation at all.

**Guided B (fixes — one error each):**

1. Error: comma between the blended quote and the rest of its sentence.
   Fix: *Mara "stood at the kitchen window" long after dark.*
2. Error: missing comma before a full-sentence quotation. Fix: *The
   narrator says, "She checked her phone for the fourth time."*
3. Error: "which was strange" dangles — it was the *lack of messages*
   that was strange, not the texting. Fix: *The narrator calls the lack
   of messages "strange," since Tomas "texted about everything, even
   the weather at truck stops."*
4. Error: comma splice before a full-sentence quotation. Fix: *Mara
   pretended she hadn't been watching, but "she was already at the door
   with his plate covered in foil."*

**Independent practice (sample):** Inference — *Tomas is usually
reliable.* Quotation version: *Tomas is usually reliable: the narrator
notes the silence was "strange, because Tomas texted about everything."*
Paraphrase version: *Tomas is usually reliable, since the narrator finds
it strange that someone who texts constantly has sent nothing.*
Stronger: the quotation version — the word *strange* is the narrator's
own flag, and quoting preserves it.

**Application (sample paragraph):** *Mara's worry was reasonable.
Tomas's silence broke a clear pattern: there were "no messages, which
was strange, because Tomas texted about everything, even the weather at
truck stops." She also "had reheated [dinner] twice already," which
means she had been waiting over an hour past six. The first detail is
stronger — waiting proves lateness, but the broken texting pattern
proves the lateness was* unusual.

**Exit check:**

1. A quotation uses the author's exact words in quotation marks; a
   paraphrase restates the idea in your own words. Both need attribution
   (a lead-in) — a paraphrase is still the author's idea.
2. *The narrator notes that Mara checked her phone "for the fourth
   time."*
3. "She had reheated it twice already" — it directly measures how long
   she waited; the rattling screen door is atmosphere, not a clock.

## Lesson 03 {#lesson-03}

**Guided A (repairs — samples; accept any defensible full-sentence
theme):**

1. *"Endurance through hardship is possible and worthwhile — the
   speaker's own lifelong climbing is the proof she offers her son."*
2. *"Rituals of remembrance keep the absent present: Elena's family
   stays whole by continuing to set the grandfather's plate."*
3. *"Hardship is certain, but quitting is a choice — the speaker urges
   her son to keep climbing because she herself never stopped."*
   (The original is contradicted by "I'se still climbin'.")

**Guided B (first chart row, sample):** Beginning detail — *"Elena set
five plates on the table, though only four people ate."* What it adds —
establishes the ritual and the absence before explaining either. Running
claim — *The family maintains a place for the grandfather.*

**Independent practice (sample chart):**

| Section | Key detail | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| Beginning | Five plates for four people | The ritual exists; absence is built into the family's routine |
| Middle | "At first her mother had moved the plate away, gently, and Elena had moved it back, gently" | The ritual was contested, then accepted — it is chosen, not automatic |
| End | "Because someone did" + Elena washing the plate "carefully… like the plate mattered" | The mother's sentence names the reason; Elena inherits the care |

Theme statement (sample): *Love outlasts absence when a family turns
remembrance into ritual.* Strongest two details: the contested plate
(middle) and "Because someone did" (end).

**Application (sample paragraph, "Mother to Son"):** *The poem's theme
is that endurance through hardship is possible and worthwhile, and the
speaker's own life is the proof. It opens with the central metaphor —
"Life for me ain't been no crystal stair" — defining hardship as the
norm, not the exception. The middle catalogs the proof: "tacks,"
"splinters," "boards torn up," and floors "Bare," followed by the turn,
"But all the time / I'se been a-climbin' on." The end transfers the
theme as direct advice — "don't you turn back… Don't you fall now" —
and closes with the living example: "I'se still climbin'." Each section
adds a layer: the claim, the evidence of survival, the instruction.*

**Exit check:**

1. A topic is the subject in a word or two; a theme is a full-sentence
   claim the text makes *about* that topic.
2. Sample: *Hardship does not excuse quitting; the speaker's lifelong
   climbing proves endurance is possible, so the son should keep
   climbing too.* (Accept any full sentence the whole poem supports.)
3. Sample: *"I'se still climbin'"* — it adds living proof: the advice
   is backed by ongoing action, turning description into a claim the
   son can trust.

## Lesson 04 {#lesson-04}

**Guided A (purged sentences — samples):**

1. *The engineers did not understand that steady wind could build a
   destructive rhythm in the bridge deck.* (Removes "stupid," "obvious
   danger.")
2. *Officials closed the bridge to traffic as the twisting worsened.*
   (Removes "amazingly," "heroic," "just in time.")
3. *A 600-foot section of the bridge fell after wind-driven twisting
   grew beyond what the structure could withstand.* (Removes the
   superlative judgment and the outside claim "worst-designed.")
4. *The only fatality was a dog named Tubby.* (Removes "in my opinion,"
   "most interesting," "poor.")

**Guided B (ranking):** 1 → (c) the central idea (covers collapse +
lesson); 2 → (b) a true fact from one paragraph; 3 → (a) an opinion
about a minor detail. (a) is weakest: it is both a judgment and about
the passage's smallest fact.

**Independent practice (sample summary, 6 sentences):** *On November 7,
1940, the four-month-old Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington collapsed
into Puget Sound after wind set its deck twisting. From opening day the
narrow deck had rippled in wind, earning the name "Galloping Gertie."
That morning, 40-mile-per-hour winds pushed the deck at the pace of its
natural swing, and each cycle's twisting grew — a process now called
aeroelastic flutter — until suspenders snapped and a 600-foot section
fell. No people died; the bridge had been closed, though a dog named
Tubby did. Engineers concluded that bridges must be tested against
wind's rhythm, not just its force, and modern long bridges are studied
in wind tunnels with decks shaped to let wind pass through.*

**Application (sample paragraph):** *The passage's central idea is that
the 1940 Tacoma Narrows collapse taught engineers to design against
wind's rhythm, not just its force. The collapse itself proves the gap
in understanding: steady 40-mile-per-hour winds "began pushing the deck
sideways at just the wrong pace," and the twisting grew "with every
cycle" until the bridge fell. The lesson follows directly — the text
explains that modern bridges are tested in wind tunnels and shaped so
wind "slips past instead of catching." The disaster and the design
reform are one idea: rhythm, not force, was the missing variable.*

**Exit check:**

1. Shorter than the original; main ideas in order; no opinions; no
   outside facts; your own words.
2. It is a true fact from one paragraph, not the main point the *whole*
   text develops.
3. *The engineers changed bridge design worldwide after the 1940
   collapse.* (Removes "brilliant," "tragic.")

## Lesson 05 {#lesson-05}

**Guided A (sort — accept reasoned variation):** Most negative →
most positive: **bullheaded → willful → firm → persistent → resolute.**
*Willful* is the hinge — negative toward the "murderous, cowardly pack"
("willful cruelty"), neutral-positive for the kinsmen only if recast as
*resolute*. The speaker would choose **resolute / persistent** for his
kinsmen (honorable endurance) and **bullheaded / willful** for the pack
(blind, cruel stubbornness).

**Guided B (three more tone builders — samples):**

- *"monsters"* (line 7): neutral "enemies" would mean mere opposition;
  *monsters* connotes inhuman cruelty, which justifies defiance and
  makes "honor us though dead" feel earned.
- *"precious blood"* (line 6): neutral "blood" states a fact; *precious*
  insists each life has worth the attackers deny.
- *"kinsmen"* (line 9): neutral "allies" states alliance; *kinsmen*
  connotes family bond — they fight for their own.

**Guided C ("constrained"):** (1) Context needs "forced, compelled" —
"shall be constrained to honor us" means even the monsters will *have
to* honor them. (2) Parts: *con-* (intensive) + *strained* (bound
tight) → "bound, compelled." (3) Dictionary: "to compel or force into
a course of action."

**Independent practice (samples):**

- *inglorious*: Context — "penned in an inglorious spot" contrasts with
  "nobly die," so it means "without glory, shameful." Parts — *in-*
  (not) + *glorious*. Reference check — "shameful, dishonorable."
  Final: *shameful; lacking honor.*
- *accursed*: Context — "our accursed lot" alongside "hogs" and "dogs"
  suggests a doomed, despised fate. Parts — *ac-* (intensive) + *cursed*.
  Final: *under a curse; doomed to suffering.*
- *kinsmen*: Context — "O kinsmen! we must meet the common foe!" is a
  rallying address to fellow fighters. Parts — *kin* (family) + *men*.
  Final: *male relatives; here, fellow fighters bound like family.*

**Application (sample paragraph):** *The first four lines refuse
degradation through word choices that make the rejected death feel
subhuman. "Hogs" — not "pigs" — drags in the slaughterhouse: animals
"hunted and penned," driven and killed without dignity. "Inglorious"
strips the death of honor before it happens, and the "mad and hungry
dogs" barking "round us" complete the picture of prey surrounded. Each
word's connotation does argumentative work: by making this death sound
like butchery, McKay makes the alternative — "let us nobly die" — the
only honorable option. The tone is not despair but refusal.*

**Exit check:**

1. Denotation is the dictionary definition; connotation is the feeling
   and associations a word carries. Example: *hogs* denotes pigs but
   connotes slaughter and degradation.
2. Any two with contributions, e.g. *"nobly"* (lends honor and choice
   to death) and *"fighting back"* (ends the poem on action, not
   victimhood).
3. An allusion is a reference that borrows meaning from outside the
   text. The poem's outnumbered-fighters pattern echoes the heroic
   tradition of dignified last stands.

## Practice sets {#practice-sets}

**A.** Sample chart rows: *Observation — "A closed black umbrella hangs
on the bench" | Inference — "The teenager prepared for rain" | Strength
3 — the umbrella is direct preparation, on the teenager's bench.*
Trading rule: any inference that cannot name its observation returns
for more evidence — no exceptions.

**B.** Lead-in ladder (samples): *Mara's vigil lasted for hours: "she
had reheated it twice already."* / *The silence was out of character —
there were "no messages, which was strange."* / *Her worry showed
despite her act: "she pretended she hadn't been watching the window at
all."* Paraphrase swaps keep the lead-ins; only the evidence form
changes. Proofread fixes: (1) *Mara "stood at the kitchen window" long
after dark.* — no comma splits a blended quote from its sentence. (2)
*The narrator explains: Tomas "texted about everything."* — periods go
inside closing quotation marks. (3) *"She checked her phone for the
fourth time," the narrator reports.* — comma before the closing quote
when the sentence continues.

**Vocabulary (S5).** Sample definitions: *inference — a conclusion built
from text clues plus background knowledge; explicit — stated directly in
the text's own words; evidence — the details that prove a claim;
citation — a reference to the exact source of evidence; claim — the
statement you are trying to prove; strongest evidence — the detail that
most directly proves the claim.* *Cite*: forms — cite, cites, cited,
citing; fitting definition — "to quote or refer to as evidence"; wrong
for this context — "to summon to appear in court" (no legal context
here). *Infer* synonyms: *deduce* — works: "She deduced from the
reheated dinner that Mara had waited hours"; fails: "I deduce he's a
nice person" (no evidence named — that is guessing). *Gather* — works:
"I gather from the phone-checking that she was anxious"; fails: "I
gather it will rain" said with no clues at all.

**C.** See Lesson 03 key; the stress-test detail (sample): *"her mother
had moved the plate away, gently"* — fits the theme because the ritual
had to be *chosen* over forgetting; revise the theme only if the detail
truly contradicts it.

**D.** Sample summary (52 words): *Every Sunday Elena sets five plates
for four people, placing one before her late grandfather's empty chair.
Her mother once removed the plate; Elena restored it, and now no one
moves it. When her brother asks why, their mother answers, "Because
someone did." Elena then hand-washes the extra plate with care.*
Purged words typically include *sweet, sad, loving, touching* — facts
stay, judgments go.

**E.** Sorts (defenses): *cunning* (negative — implies deceit) <
*shrewd* (neutral-positive — implies sharpness) < *clever* (positive);
*cheap* (negative — implies low quality) < *economical* (neutral —
implies efficiency) < *thrifty* (positive — implies wise saving);
*arrogant* (negative) < *proud* (hinge — positive about achievement,
negative about superiority) < *confident* (positive). Allusion hunt
(sample): *The "crystal stair" may echo the biblical stairway to heaven;
"crystal" suggests a shining, easy ascent the speaker never had — the
echo makes her rough stair feel like the opposite of paradise. A reader
might reject it: the poem never names heaven, and the stair could be
purely domestic.* Upgrade reflection (sample): *"resolute" for "firm"
improved the tone — it added moral weight; "pulchritudinous" for
"beautiful" just sounded fancier and broke the tone.*

**Vocabulary (S15).** *predictable — pre- (before) + dict (say) +
-able → "able to be said beforehand"; transport — trans- (across) +
port (carry) → "carry across"; reunion — re- (again) + union → "a
coming together again"; disagreeable — dis- (not) + agree + -able →
"not able to be agreed with, unpleasant"; circumnavigate — circum-
(around) + navigate → "sail/fly around."* *Track* meanings: (1) a path
or rails; (2) to follow the course of; (3) a song on an album; (4) a
mark left behind. In "track a theme," context (following an idea
through a text) selects meaning (2). Word bank (sample): *inglorious,
kinsmen, constrained, flutter, objective, allusion* — each with an
original sentence.

**Synthesis block (S16 — "Salt").** Sample chart: *Inference — "Mrs.
Abernathy is the neighborhood's anchor": details — "she did what she
always did" (routine hospitality, strength 2) and "half the block had
drifted over" (people come to her, strength 3 — strongest: it proves her
role through others' behavior). Inference — "The gathering mattered to
everyone": "the ice melted, the lemonade went warm, and still nobody
left" (strength 3), "people stood up slowly, the way you leave a good
movie" (strength 2).* Theme (sample): *Shared hardship, met with
generosity, turns neighbors into community.* Track: beginning — the
chairs and lemonade (hospitality as habit); middle — "still nobody left"
(the gathering becomes its own reason); end — "Same time next blackout"
(hardship reframed as occasion). Word choice: *"drifted over"*
(connotes easy, unforced movement — no one was summoned); *"the way you
leave a good movie"* (simile — reluctant, warm departure). Synthesis
paragraph (sample): *The passage's theme is that generosity in hard
moments turns neighbors into community. It begins as habit — Mrs.
Abernathy "did what she always did," setting out chairs and lemonade —
but the middle shows the habit becoming something larger: "the ice
melted, the lemonade went warm, and still nobody left." By the end the
blackout itself has been reframed as an occasion: "Same time next
blackout," she jokes, and everybody laughs. The warm tone is carried by
word choices like "drifted over," which makes the gathering feel
effortless rather than organized — community as something that happens,
not something planned.*

## Project sample responses {#project-sample-responses}

**Sample chart rows ("Mother to Son"):**

| # | Evidence | Form | What it proves | Strength + why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Life for me ain't been no crystal stair" | quote | Names the theme's central metaphor: hardship as the norm | 3 — the whole poem hangs on this line |
| 2 | The catalog: "tacks," "splinters," "boards torn up," "Bare" | paraphrase + referenced | Makes hardship concrete; proves the metaphor is earned | 2 — vivid but illustrative rather than argumentative |
| 3 | "I'se been a-climbin' on" / "I'se still climbin'" | quotes | The speaker's ongoing action proves endurance is possible | 3 — the living proof the advice rests on |
| 4 | "don't you turn back… Don't you fall now" | quote | The theme transferred as direct instruction to the son | 2 — states the theme's demand plainly |

**Sample synthesis (abridged):** *P1: The poem claims that endurance
through hardship is possible and worthwhile — and the speaker is the
evidence. P2: It opens by defining life as "no crystal stair," then
proves the hardship is real with "tacks," "splinters," and floors
"Bare"; the turn — "But all the time / I'se been a-climbin' on" — shows
the hardship never stopped her, and the closing "I'se still climbin'"
hands the son a living example with the instruction "don't you turn
back." P3: The connotation of "crystal" (ease, luxury, shine) makes the
rough stair feel like the opposite of an easy life, and the repeated
"-in'" endings ("climbin'," "goin'") keep the tone intimate and spoken —
a mother's voice, not a lecture.*

**Sample presentation Q&A:** Q: *"What is the weakest row on your chart,
and why did you keep it?"* A (sample): *"Row 2, the catalog — it is
illustrative, not argumentative. I kept it because without the tacks
and splinters, 'climbin'' would be abstract; the concrete hardships
make the endurance believable."*

## Unit assessment {#unit-assessment}

**Scoring: 30 points.**

**A1. (2 pts)** Sample: *Jonah is learning to honor family tradition —
evidence: he "refolded the map carefully, the way his grandmother had
taught him — thirds, then in half."* / *The grandmother was practical
and trusted — evidence: her note "Don't trust the shortcut" proved
correct when the shortcut "had washed out."* (1 pt per inference +
evidence; the grandmother must be inferred from others' actions.)

**A2. (3 pts)** 1 — **(a)** "Her map, her rules": the family's explicit
rule of deference — directly on-point for respecting her *judgment*.
2 — **(c)** refolding as taught: internalized respect shown through
careful action. 3 — **(b)** same route every summer: shows tradition
and habit, weakest link to *judgment* specifically. (1 pt ranking + 2
pts justifications; accept (a)↔(c) swaps with genuine reasoning.)

**A3. (3 pts)** Sample: *The father treats the grandmother's wishes as
binding — he enforces them with a shrug: "Her map, her rules."* (1 pt
claim + 1 pt blended fragment + 1 pt punctuation.)

**B4. (3 pts)** Sample theme: *Honoring the wisdom of those who came
before us protects us in ways efficiency cannot.* Moments: beginning —
the hand-drawn map with "Don't trust the shortcut. — G."; middle —
"Her map, her rules" chosen over the GPS's twenty saved minutes; end —
"Some things, you don't update." (1 pt theme + 2 pts tracked moments
with citations.)

**B5. (4 pts)** Sample summary: *Every summer Jonah's family drives six
hours to his grandmother's lake house, and sixteen-year-old Jonah now
drives the middle stretch. This year his father hands him navigation
too, using the grandmother's hand-drawn map, which warns, "Don't trust
the shortcut." The GPS favors the shortcut, but the family follows the
map's rule instead; they later learn the shortcut washed out in the
spring rains. Jonah refolds the map as his grandmother taught him,
thinking that some things should not be updated.* (Deduct for opinion
words, outside facts, or missing main ideas.)

**B6. (2 pts)** Sample: *The GPS was not wrong — the shortcut would
have saved twenty minutes; the problem was safety, which the GPS did
not address. And the claim covers one detail, not the whole text's
point about inherited wisdom versus efficiency.*

**C7. (3 pts)** Sample: *Literally, the note warns against the shortcut
road — which indeed "had washed out." On the larger level, "shortcut"
means any easy, efficient path chosen over proven wisdom; the echo is
"Some things, you don't update" — the story's closing claim that some
inherited ways are worth keeping.* (1 pt literal + 1 pt larger + 1 pt
echo quote.)

**C8. (3 pts)** (a) Context — it describes handwriting in a margin
note, so it suggests rounded, curling script. (b) A *loop* is a curve
that crosses itself; *-ing* gives the quality — like the loops in
cursive writing. (c) *"Having rounded, curling strokes, like cursive
handwriting."*

**C9. (2 pts)** Sample: *Tone — warm (accept "affectionate,"
"tender"). Word choices: "tossed him the keys" (casual, trusting ease)
and "looping handwriting" (fond, specific attention to her).*

**D10. (5 pts)** Sample paragraph: *By the end, the map represents
inherited wisdom that deserves trust. Jonah begins by simply unfolding
"the paper map his grandmother had drawn decades ago," but the middle
turns the map into a test: against the GPS's promise of twenty saved
minutes, the family chooses the note's warning — "Her map, her rules."
The test resolves in the map's favor when the shortcut turns out to
have "washed out in the spring rains." Jonah's final act — refolding it
"the way his grandmother had taught him — thirds, then in half" — shows
he has internalized the lesson, and his closing thought, that "some
things… you don't update," connects the map to the theme: some
inherited wisdom should be kept, not optimized away.* Score with the
[project rubric](project.md#rubric): claim (1), two evidence forms with
lead-ins (2), theme connection (1), mechanics (1).
