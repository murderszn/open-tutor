# Project — The Case for a Theme: Evidence Portfolio

**Sessions:** 13–14 (build), 17 (finish and present) · **Type:**
investigation/portfolio · **Answer-key reference:**
[answer key](answer-key.md#project-sample-responses)

## The task

You are building a **case** — the way a lawyer builds one. Your client
is a theme. Your evidence is the text. By the end, anyone who reads your
portfolio should be convinced that your theme is really in the text, not
just in your head.

**Choose one** of the unit's core texts:

- "The Lantern" (Lesson 1)
- "Mother to Son" — Langston Hughes (Lesson 3)
- "The Empty Chair" (Lesson 3)
- "Why Bridges Sway" (Lesson 4 — argue for its *central idea* instead of
  a theme)
- "If We Must Die" — Claude McKay (Lesson 5)

## Materials

Notebook or paper, two highlighter colors, index cards or sticky notes
for the evidence chart, the generated bus-stop illustration (optional:
use it as a model for your chart layout), dictionary/thesaurus access.

## Steps

1. **Re-read with a highlighter (Session 13, ~15 min).** Mark every
   detail that could support a theme — more than you need. Aim for 10+.
2. **Draft three candidate theme statements** (full sentences). For each,
   ask: does the *whole* text support it? Cross out the weakest two, or
   combine the best parts of two into one stronger claim.
3. **Build the evidence chart** (Session 13, ~20 min). Six rows minimum:

   | # | Evidence (quoted or precisely referenced) | Form (quote / paraphrase / observation) | What it proves about the theme | Strength (1–3) + why |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | 1 | | | | |
   | … | | | | |

   Requirements: at least **two quotations** (blended, punctuated
   correctly), at least **two paraphrases** (attributed), and at least
   **two ranked pairs** (say which of two is stronger and why).
4. **Draft the synthesis** (Session 14, ~30 min). Three paragraphs:
   - **P1 — Claim:** state the theme and why it matters (2–3 sentences).
   - **P2 — Track:** show the theme developing across beginning, middle,
     and end, weaving in **three** pieces of chart evidence with
     lead-ins.
   - **P3 — Language:** analyze **two** word choices (connotation,
     figurative language, or allusion) and explain how they shape the
     theme's tone or force.
5. **Revise** (Session 14, ~15 min). Run the checklist: every quotation
   punctuated? Every paraphrase attributed? Any opinion words in the
   summary parts? Any claim without evidence? Fix them.
6. **Present** (Session 17, ~10 min per learner). Present the case
   aloud to the guiding adult (or record it): theme statement, the two
   strongest evidence rows, and one word-choice insight. The adult asks
   one challenging question; answer with evidence.

## Deliverables

1. The evidence chart (6+ rows, meeting the form requirements).
2. The three-paragraph synthesis (handwritten or typed).
3. The presentation (live or recorded) + the adult's challenging
   question and your answer, noted on the chart's back.

## Rubric

| Criterion | Exemplary (4) | Proficient (3) | Developing (2) | Beginning (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Theme statement** | Full-sentence claim, specific to this text; whole text supports it | Full sentence; mostly text-specific; minor overreach | Topic-length or partly contradicted by the text | Missing, or a plot summary |
| **Evidence quality** | 6+ rows; strongest evidence identified and ranking justified; no unsupported claims | 6 rows; forms met; rankings present but thin | Fewer than 6 rows, or a form missing, or an unsupported claim | Evidence absent or unrelated to the claim |
| **Integration mechanics** | Quotations blended and punctuated correctly; paraphrases attributed; every piece has a lead-in | Correct with at most one punctuation slip; lead-ins present | Quote-bombing (no lead-ins) or repeated punctuation errors | Evidence pasted without integration |
| **Word-choice analysis** | Two choices analyzed for connotation/allusion and tied to tone or theme | Two choices identified with connotation explained | Choices named but not explained | Missing |
| **Presentation** | Clear claim, two strongest rows cited from memory or notes, answers challenge with evidence | All parts present; challenge answered | Reads the paper aloud; challenge unanswered | Missing or incoherent |

**Scoring:** 17–20 = exceeds; 13–16 = meets (ready for U02); 9–12 =
revise one criterion and resubmit; below 9 = reteach Lessons 2–3, then
retry with a different text. See the [teacher
guide](teacher-guide.md#project-facilitation) for facilitation and the
[answer key](answer-key.md#project-sample-responses) for annotated sample
responses.

## Accessibility and alternatives

- The presentation may be live, recorded audio, or a written
  "presentation script" — same rubric, same challenge question in
  writing.
- The chart may be built with cards, a table, or a voice-recorded
  walkthrough (transcribed by the adult).
- If the chosen poem's themes feel too heavy, the adult may steer the
  learner to "The Lantern," "The Empty Chair," or "Salt" — the rubric
  is text-neutral.
