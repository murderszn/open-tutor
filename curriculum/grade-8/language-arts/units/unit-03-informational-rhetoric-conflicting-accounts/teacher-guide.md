## Pacing notes

- **Week 11** is the heaviest reading week: Douglass's diction
  ("boasted liberty," "swelling vanity") needs a read-aloud pass
  before any analysis. Do not assign the Douglass excerpts as silent
  homework on first encounter.
- **Lesson 5 needs two sessions** (S11–S12 in the README pacing).
  Rushing Account B into the same session as Account A produces
  summaries instead of comparisons.
- **Do not run Lesson 6's discussion** until the Lesson 5
  fact/interpretation table is complete. Unprepared discussion becomes
  opinion trading, and the counterclaim paragraphs come out as
  straw men.
- **Week 14, session 18** (review + practice P6–P10) is the last
  reteach window before the quiz and assessment. Use it for the
  weakest skill in the class's practice results, not for new material.

## Misconceptions and corrections

| Misconception | Correction (say this) |
|---|---|
| Pathos means "manipulative"; logos means "true." | An appeal is a tool, not a verdict. Pathos can carry a sound argument (Douglass, L1); logos can be built on false premises. Judge the reasoning, not the label. |
| Summarizing a paragraph is analyzing its structure. | Structure analysis names what each sentence *does* (denies, replaces, concedes, concludes). Require a verb in every answer. |
| Any true statement counts as evidence. | Evidence must attach to a reason for *this* claim. Run the attachment test: "Show me the reason, or set it aside." |
| More numbers = stronger argument. | Numbers need relevance and sufficiency. A small or narrow sample (Cedar Falls survey, L5) can be accurately quoted and still too thin. |
| A disagreement means one side is lying. | First classify: fact (settle it with a record) or interpretation (weigh the reasoning). Most real disagreements are the second kind. |
| Acknowledging the other side weakens your argument. | Fair acknowledgment is what makes an argument credible (Douglass's praise of the founders, L4; Lincoln's concession, L4). A hidden counterclaim is a weakness, not a strength. |
| The author's purpose is the topic. | Purpose needs a verb of intended effect: persuade, move, redefine, warn. "Slavery" is a topic; "move citizens from celebration to action" is a purpose. |

## Discussion facilitation (Lesson 6)

- Seat the discussion so no one speaks twice before everyone speaks
  once. Use the protocol's sentence starters ("Building on what …
  said") as required openers for the first round.
- When a speaker states a claim without evidence, do not correct the
  claim — ask the delineation question: "What evidence supports that
  reason?" Let the map do the work.
- If the discussion turns personal ("you just don't care about
  parking"), stop and reframe to the argument: "Restate their point
  fairly, then answer the point."
- Close every discussion with the synthesis question: "What evidence
  would change your mind?" A discussion that ends without that
  question has produced positions, not thinking.

## Reteach paths

- **Appeals confused (L1):** re-run the appeals sort with index
  cards; require the proving quotation in every answer, not just the
  label.
- **Structure answers are summaries (L2):** give the sentence-verb
  list (introduce, explain, contrast, concede, conclude) and require
  one verb per sentence analyzed.
- **Irrelevant evidence missed (L3):** use the card-sorting version
  of the argument map (cards that will not attach are set aside
  physically).
- **Fact/interpretation swapped (L5):** use the settling-evidence
  question: "Could a record, measurement, or observation decide this?
  If yes, it is a fact disagreement."
- **Straw-man counterclaims (L6):** require the learner to write the
  other side's paragraph first, and have the adult check it for
  fairness before the learner answers it.

## Project scoring notes

Use the rubric in [project.md](project.md) (12 points). Two cautions:

- Do not reward the side you agree with. Score fairness and evidence
  only; a well-argued parking brief outscores a sloppy garden brief.
- The adult fairness check (Step 5) is a requirement, not a courtesy.
  If a supporter of the other side would call the summary unfair, the
  brief is not finished, whatever its other scores.

## Answer-key use

The answer key gives one strong response per item plus acceptable
variation notes for open responses. It is not a script: accept any
response that meets the same standard (correct label, proving
quotation, honest reasoning). For the assessment, partial credit
follows the point splits printed in the assessment itself.
