# Unit 03 — Informational Rhetoric: Argument and Conflicting Accounts

**Grade:** 8 · **Subject:** Language Arts · **Track weeks:** 11–14
**Backlog:** [issue #40](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/40), section U03
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Two writers can look at the same facts and argue opposite conclusions —
and both can sound convincing. This unit teaches the learner to slow that
moment down: name the appeals a writer uses, take an argument apart into
claim, reasoning, and evidence, judge whether the evidence is relevant and
enough, and compare two accounts of one topic to find exactly where they
disagree — on a fact, or only on what a fact means. The unit's anchors are
two short American speeches in the public domain, plus two original
practice accounts written for this unit about a fictional town decision,
so the learner can practice disagreement analysis without needing any
outside article.

## Essential question

When two accounts disagree, how do you decide what each one actually
proves — and what it only claims?

## Prerequisites

From U01, U02, and the grade-7 track:

- Cite the strongest textual evidence for explicit and inferential
  claims (U01)
- Determine a central idea and write an objective summary (U01)
- Analyze word choice, including figurative and connotative meaning,
  for its effect on meaning and tone (U01, U02)
- Write a multi-paragraph response with quoted or clearly referenced
  evidence (U01, U02)
- Discuss a text with preparation, building on others' ideas (U02)

**Adult note (read first):** Frederick Douglass's 1852 speech describes
enslavement and its violence plainly, including the internal slave trade
and the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850. Preview the excerpts in Lessons 1
and 4, and use the trimmed passages provided here rather than the full
10,000-word speech. Biblical devotional study is not part of this unit.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. Identify ethos, pathos, and logos in an informational passage and
   explain, with a quotation, how each appeal supports the author's
   purpose (RI.8.1, RI.8.4, RI.8.6).
2. Analyze a paragraph's structure — including the role of particular
   sentences in developing and refining a key concept — and explain
   how a text connects individuals, ideas, or events (RI.8.5, RI.8.3).
3. Delineate an argument's claims, reasoning, and evidence; judge
   whether the reasoning is sound and the evidence relevant and
   sufficient; and recognize irrelevant evidence (RI.8.8).
4. Determine an author's point of view or purpose and analyze how the
   author acknowledges and responds to conflicting evidence or
   viewpoints (RI.8.6).
5. Analyze a case in which two texts give conflicting information on
   the same topic, identifying where they disagree on matters of fact
   and where they disagree on interpretation (RI.8.9).
6. Take part in a structured discussion with prepared evidence, evaluate
   a speaker's argument (SL.8.1, SL.8.3), and write a short argument
   that acknowledges and distinguishes a counterclaim (W.8.1.b, W.8.9).

## Core texts (this unit)

All learner-facing texts are either original to this unit or U.S. public
domain. No copyrighted books, poems, or worksheets are reproduced.

- **Public domain:** Frederick Douglass, "What to the Slave Is the
  Fourth of July?" (address delivered July 5, 1852, Corinthian Hall,
  Rochester, New York, at the invitation of the Rochester Ladies'
  Anti-Slavery Sewing Society). Short excerpts are quoted in Lessons 1
  and 4; the full text is free from the Library of Congress. The adult
  previews the excerpts (see the adult note above).
- **Public domain:** Abraham Lincoln, Gettysburg Address (delivered
  November 19, 1863, at the dedication of the Soldiers' National
  Cemetery, Gettysburg, Pennsylvania). The full text (Bliss copy) is
  quoted in Lesson 2. Full text is free from the Smithsonian and from
  Learning for Justice; the National Archives' former lesson page has
  moved and returned "Page Not Found" when checked 2026-10-26.
- **Original (written for this unit):** "Two Accounts of the Cedar
  Falls Lot" (Lesson 5) — two short practice accounts of a fictional
  town decision. All people, places, numbers, and events in them are
  invented for practice and are labeled as such wherever they appear.
  They are not real news reports.

## Materials

Notebook or paper, pen/pencil, the six lesson pages, two highlighters
(one for claims, one for evidence), the generated illustration, a print
or digital dictionary, and index cards for the appeals sort and the
conflict table. For the project: the Lesson 5 accounts and the
comparison chart from Lesson 5.
