# Lesson 3 — Delineating an Argument: Claims, Reasoning, Evidence

**Goal:** by the end of this lesson you can take an argument apart into
claim, reasoning, and evidence, and judge whether the reasoning is sound
and the evidence relevant and sufficient.
**Time:** about 45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, two highlighters
(claims in one color, evidence in the other), this lesson page,
[`assets/argument-anatomy.svg`](../assets/argument-anatomy.svg).

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** write one claim you believe ("school
should start later" or any claim of your own) and one fact that
supports it. Label which is the claim and which is the evidence. If you
cannot tell them apart yet, this lesson fixes that.

## Teaching

To **delineate** an argument means to draw its outline: separate what
is claimed from what is offered as proof. Every argument has three
parts:

- **Claim** — the conclusion the writer wants you to accept. It is
  debatable: someone reasonable could disagree. ("Our school should
  keep the art room open during lunch.")
- **Reasoning** — the logical link: *why* the evidence should lead you
  to the claim. Often it is unstated, and your job is to supply it and
  test it. ("Calmer students learn better, so a calming break supports
  learning.")
- **Evidence** — the checkable support: facts, numbers, quotations,
  examples, expert findings. ("A survey of 40 students found 31 said
  drawing at lunch helped them concentrate afterward.")

Then judge the argument with three tests:

1. **Is the reasoning sound?** Does the evidence, if true, actually
   lead to the claim — or is there a gap? (A survey about *liking*
   art does not prove art *improves concentration*.)
2. **Is the evidence relevant?** Does it bear on this claim, or is it
   merely interesting? Evidence that does not touch the claim is
   **irrelevant evidence**, even if it is true.
3. **Is the evidence sufficient?** Is there *enough* of it, from good
   enough sources, to carry the claim? One small survey is a start, not
   a proof.

Use the diagram: claim at the top, reasons beneath it, evidence beneath
each reason. Anything that cannot be attached to a reason is suspect —
highlight it and ask what it is doing there.

## Worked example 1 — mapping a complete argument

Original practice paragraph (written for this unit):

> "Our school should keep the art room open during lunch. Students who
> make art during breaks return to class calmer and more focused, and a
> survey of 40 students found 31 said drawing at lunch helped them
> concentrate afterward. The art room also gives students who do not
> play sports a place to belong, and the school's own attendance notes
> show art-room regulars miss fewer afternoon classes."

**Map, step by step.** Claim: the art room should stay open at lunch.
Reason 1: art breaks calm students and improve focus. Evidence for
Reason 1: the 40-student survey (31 of 40). Reasoning link: calmer,
more focused students learn better, so the break serves learning.
Reason 2: the room gives non-athletes belonging. Evidence for Reason
2: attendance notes showing fewer missed afternoon classes. Reasoning
link (weaker — note it): belonging may explain attendance, but the
notes do not prove belonging caused the attendance; they only show the
two go together. A careful reader marks Reason 2 "plausible, not
proved" rather than rejecting the whole argument. Delineation is not
demolition: most real arguments are partly strong and partly thin.

## Worked example 2 — spotting irrelevant evidence and thin evidence

Add this sentence to the paragraph above: "The art teacher once won a
county painting prize."

**Reasoning, step by step.** (1) *Does it support the claim?* The
claim is about lunch opening hours. A prize says the teacher is a good
painter; it says nothing about whether lunch opening helps students.
It cannot attach to either reason. It is **irrelevant evidence** —
true, flattering, and useless here. (2) *Is the survey sufficient?*
31 of 40 is a real majority, but 40 students in one school, answering
about their own feelings, is thin support for a policy claim. It would
be stronger with a second source: another school, a term of attendance
records, or a comparison with students who did not use the room.
(3) *Verdict:* the argument is reasonable but not yet sufficient. The
honest summary is "promising, needs one more source" — not "proved"
and not "worthless."

## Guided practice

Original practice paragraph (written for this unit):

> "The town should build a skate park. Teenagers need safe places to
> spend time after school, and the police log shows after-school
> disturbances near the mall fell by half in the year after the
> neighboring town opened its park. Skateboarding is also an Olympic
> sport, and our mayor once skateboarded as a teenager."

1. State the claim, then list each reason with its evidence (if any).
   Which sentence has no evidence at all?
2. Which sentence is irrelevant evidence? Explain in one sentence why
   it cannot attach to any reason.
3. Judge sufficiency: is the police-log evidence enough, on its own,
   to carry the claim? What one additional source would strengthen it
   most, and why?

## Independent practice

1. A writer argues: "Homework should be abolished because my cousin
   hates homework and because Finland has shorter school days."
   Delineate it: claim, reasons, evidence. Then name the flaw in each
   reason in one sentence each.
2. Error analysis: a classmate says, "This argument has three pieces
   of evidence, so it must be strong." In three sentences, explain why
   counting evidence is the wrong test, using the words *relevant* and
   *sufficient*.
3. Rewrite the skate-park paragraph's weakest sentence so that it
   becomes relevant evidence for the claim. (You may invent a clearly
   labeled practice fact, and you must label it as invented.)

## Application

Find one argument in yesterday's news, a school announcement, or a
family discussion. Delineate it in a small map: claim, reasons,
evidence, one judgment (sound / relevant / sufficient) for each
reason. Bring the map to Lesson 6's discussion.

## Exit check

1. In your own words, what is the difference between reasoning and
   evidence?
2. Give one example of irrelevant evidence for the claim "our school
   needs a new gym" — something true that does not support the claim.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** "delineate" means to outline precisely;
  "sufficient" means enough to carry the weight of the claim.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the argument map with index
  cards (one card per claim, reason, or evidence) and physically set
  aside any card that will not attach.
- **Extension:** take Worked example 1's Reason 2 and write the
  additional evidence that would turn "plausible, not proved" into
  "well supported." Name the source you would need.

**Teacher note:** learners often accept any true statement as evidence.
Drill the attachment test: "show me the reason this evidence attaches
to, or set it aside." Full solutions are in the
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
