# Lesson 5 — Conflicting Accounts: Fact or Interpretation?

**Goal:** by the end of this lesson you can compare two accounts of
one topic and say exactly where they disagree — on a checkable fact,
or only on what a fact means.
**Time:** about 45 minutes across two sessions if needed.
**Materials:** notebook, this lesson page, the image below.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** two friends watch the same game and
one says "we dominated" while the other says "we got lucky." Did they
disagree about what happened, or about what it meant? Hold that
distinction — it is the whole lesson.

## Teaching

When two texts conflict, do not ask first "which one is right?" Ask
first "what kind of disagreement is this?"

- **Fact disagreement:** the accounts cannot both be true as stated.
  One says the lot sat empty six years; the other says three. A record,
  a measurement, or an observation can settle it.
- **Interpretation disagreement:** both accounts agree on the facts
  but draw different conclusions from them. Both cite the same survey;
  one reads it as support for a garden, the other as too small a
  sample to decide anything. No single record settles this — you must
  weigh reasoning, relevance, and sufficiency (Lesson 3).

A careful comparison also asks how each account **handles the other
side**: does it mention the opposing evidence fairly (Lesson 4), or
does it pretend it does not exist? An account that hides the other
side's best point is weaker, even when its facts are right.

## The accounts (original practice texts — read both)

*The two accounts below were written for this unit. Cedar Falls, its
people, its survey, and its lot are fictional. All numbers are invented
practice data, not real town records. The accounts are for practicing
comparison, not for deciding a real case.*

**Account A — Mara Ellison, Cedar Falls Garden Committee:**

> "The empty lot on Elm Street has sat unused for six years. It is time
> to turn it into a community garden. In the town survey this spring,
> 214 residents answered; 62 percent said they would use green space
> weekly, compared with 38 percent who said they would use more
> parking. That is a clear majority for a garden. The lot drains well:
> after heavy rain last May, our committee watched the ground dry
> within an hour. Donated soil and lumber worth $18,000 are already
> pledged, so the garden can be built without raising taxes."

**Account B — Tom Okafor, Cedar Falls Shop Owners' Association:**

> "The empty lot on Elm Street has sat unused for three years, not six
> — the hardware store used it for storage until 2023. A parking lot
> there would serve everyone, not only gardeners. The spring survey
> asked only 214 people in a town of 9,000, and it asked what people
> would *use*, not what they would pay to maintain. Sixty-two percent
> of 214 is just 133 people — too few to commit the town to years of
> watering, weeding, and repairs. And the lot does not drain well: after
> heavy rain, standing water stays for days. The $18,000 in donations
> covers soil and lumber only, not water lines or upkeep."

## Worked example 1 — a fact disagreement and how to settle it

**Disagreement:** Account A says the lot sat empty six years;
Account B says three years, with storage use until 2023. **Kind:**
fact — both cannot be true. **How to settle it:** check a record that
neither writer controls, such as town property or rental records for
the lot, or dated photographs. **What not to do:** decide by which
writer sounds more confident. Confidence is an appeal (ethos/pathos),
not a record. Note also the second fact disagreement hiding in the
same pair: drainage — "dry within an hour" vs. "standing water for
days." That one is settled by observation: watch the lot after the
next heavy rain, or ask for dated photos from both sides.

## Worked example 2 — an interpretation disagreement over the same numbers

**Shared fact:** 214 residents answered the survey; 62 percent chose
green space, 38 percent parking. Neither account disputes those
numbers. **Disagreement:** what the numbers mean. Account A reads 62
percent as "a clear majority for a garden." Account B reads the same
numbers as "133 people in a town of 9,000, asked about use rather than
cost — too few to decide." **Kind:** interpretation. **How to weigh
it:** apply Lesson 3. Is Account B's reasoning sound? Yes, in part:
a sample of 214 from 9,000 is small, and "would use" is not "would
pay for." Is Account A's reading wrong? Not entirely: 62 percent is a
real majority *of respondents*. The honest summary names both truths:
a majority of a small sample favored green space, and the sample is
too small and too narrow to settle the funding question. Also note how
each account handles the other side: Account A never mentions the
parking need or the maintenance cost; Account B answers the garden
case directly. On acknowledgment, Account B is stronger here — which
does not automatically make its conclusion right.

## Guided practice

1. Find the cost disagreement: what does each account claim the
   $18,000 covers, and is this a fact disagreement, an interpretation
   disagreement, or both? Explain in two sentences.
2. Write the fact/interpretation table for this pair so far: two rows
   for fact disagreements (with the settling evidence for each) and
   two rows for interpretation disagreements (with the shared fact in
   each row).
3. Which account acknowledges the other side more fairly? Quote one
   phrase from each account that proves your judgment.

## Independent practice

1. Write an objective summary of Account A in three sentences (no
   praise, no blame — RI.8.2). Then do the same for Account B.
2. Suppose the town records show the lot was last rented in 2023.
   Which account's fact claim does that support, and what — if
   anything — does it prove about whether to build a garden? Answer in
   three sentences.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says, "Account B is right because it
   gives more numbers." In three sentences, explain why more numbers
   do not automatically win, using one number from Account B that
   strengthens it and one way numbers can mislead.

## Application

Ask two people who attended the same school event, game, or meeting to
describe it in three sentences each (or write two descriptions
yourself from opposite seats, if no one is available). Compare them:
one fact disagreement (if any), one interpretation disagreement, and
one sentence on which account acknowledges the other side better.

## Exit check

1. Give one example of a fact disagreement and one of an
   interpretation disagreement, using the Cedar Falls accounts.
2. Why is "which writer sounds more confident?" the wrong first
   question when two accounts conflict?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** "interpretation" means what someone concludes
  from facts; two honest people can interpret the same facts
  differently.
- **Alternate response mode:** build the fact/interpretation table
  with index cards — one card per disagreement, sorted into two piles
  — before writing anything.
- **Extension:** write a third, neutral paragraph (five sentences)
  that both Mara Ellison and Tom Okafor could agree is fair. It must
  state the shared facts, name both disagreements and their kinds, and
  say what evidence would settle each fact disagreement.

**Teacher note:** the most common error is treating every disagreement
as "one side is lying." Insist on the settling-evidence question for
facts and the reasoning-weighing question for interpretations. Full
solutions are in the [answer key](../answer-key.md).
