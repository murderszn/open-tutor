# Lesson 6 — Discussion and Synthesis: Arguing Fairly, in Speech and Writing

**Goal:** by the end of this lesson you can take part in a structured
discussion with prepared evidence, evaluate a speaker's argument as
you hear it, and write a short argument paragraph that acknowledges a
counterclaim.
**Time:** about 45 minutes across two sessions if needed.
**Materials:** notebook, your Lesson 5 fact/interpretation table, this
lesson page.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** write the strongest point *against*
your own view on the Cedar Falls lot. If you cannot state the other
side's best point fairly, you are not ready to argue — this lesson
gets you ready.

## Teaching

Discussion is argument at speaking speed, so it needs rules that
writing gets for free. Use this protocol:

1. **Prepare:** come with two quotations and one question. No one
   speaks from memory alone.
2. **Build, don't just wait:** begin at least once with "Building on
   what [name] said …" or "That evidence makes me reconsider …"
3. **Delineate live:** when a speaker makes a claim, silently map it —
   claim, reason, evidence — and ask the question the map suggests:
   "What evidence supports that reason?" or "Is that evidence about
   this claim?"
4. **Acknowledge before answering:** restate the other side's point
   fairly before you respond to it. ("You are right that 214 is a
   small sample — and I think it still matters because …")
5. **Close with synthesis:** end by stating what you now believe and
   what evidence would change your mind.

In writing, the same fairness appears as a **counterclaim paragraph**:
state your claim, acknowledge the strongest opposing claim, distinguish
it from yours (show where you agree and where you part), and answer it
with reasoning and evidence — not with dismissal. A counterclaim
paragraph is not a weakness in your argument; it is the part that proves
you have thought about the other side.

## Worked example 1 — a modeled discussion exchange

**Speaker A:** "The survey is decisive: 62 percent of respondents chose
green space. That is a clear majority for a garden."
**Speaker B (building + delineating):** "Building on that number — 62
percent of 214 is 133 people, in a town of 9,000. So my question is:
does a majority of a small sample carry a decision about years of
maintenance costs, which the survey never asked about?"
**Speaker A (acknowledging + answering):** "You are right that the
survey did not ask about cost, and that weakens it. But 133 people
answering a town survey is still a real signal — the garden side
should now answer your cost question with a maintenance estimate,
rather than pretending the survey settled it."

**Why this exchange works:** B delineates (claim → reason → missing
evidence) instead of merely disagreeing; A acknowledges the gap
instead of defending the survey as perfect; both leave with a sharper
question than they started with. Nobody "won" — the *argument* got
better, which is the point of discussion.

## Worked example 2 — a modeled counterclaim paragraph

> "Cedar Falls should build the community garden on the Elm Street
> lot. The survey majority and the pledged donations show real demand
> and a funded start. Critics rightly point out that 214 respondents
> cannot speak for 9,000 residents, and that the $18,000 covers
> materials, not upkeep. That objection matters, but it argues for a
> maintenance plan, not against a garden: the town should publish a
> yearly upkeep estimate and a volunteer rota before breaking ground,
> rather than abandoning a project most respondents want. The parking
> need is real too — but a garden and a smaller parking strip can share
> the lot, while a full parking lot would end the garden option for
> good."

**Why this paragraph works:** the claim is stated first; the
counterclaim is stated fairly ("critics rightly point out …"); the
writer distinguishes agreement (the sample is small) from disagreement
(that this kills the project); and the response is a concrete next
step, not an insult. Note the structure you can reuse: claim →
acknowledge → distinguish → answer → conclude.

## Guided practice

1. Delineate this spoken claim: "A garden will bring the neighborhood
   together because gardens always build community." Name the claim,
   the reason, and the missing evidence, each in one phrase.
2. Rewrite Speaker A's first line from Worked example 1 as an
   acknowledgment-first response: begin by fairly restating B's sample
   objection, then answer it in one sentence.
3. Write one counterclaim sentence for the claim "Cedar Falls should
   build a parking lot." It must fairly state the garden side's best
   point — not a weak version of it.

## Independent practice

1. Write a full counterclaim paragraph (5–7 sentences) for the
   parking-lot claim, following the worked example's structure: claim
   → acknowledge → distinguish → answer → conclude.
2. Script a three-turn discussion exchange on a topic of your choice
   (school uniforms, homework, phone rules). Each turn must either
   build on the previous speaker or delineate their argument; label
   which move each turn makes.
3. Error analysis: a classmate's paragraph says, "Some people disagree,
   but they are wrong because gardens are nice." In three sentences,
   name two faults (a straw-man counterclaim and an unsupported
   reason) and rewrite the faulty sentence fairly.

## Application

Hold a 10-minute structured discussion (with an adult, a classmate, or
recorded alone in turns) on the Cedar Falls lot, following the
protocol. Then write your own counterclaim paragraph for whichever side
you favor, using your Lesson 5 table as evidence. Both pieces feed the
project: the paragraph may become your brief's argument section.

## Exit check

1. What are the two things a counterclaim paragraph must do besides
   state your own claim?
2. In discussion, what is the difference between "building on" an idea
   and merely waiting for your turn?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** "synthesis" means combining separate ideas or
  pieces of evidence into a new, fuller conclusion.
- **Alternate response mode:** discuss with an adult who plays the
  opposing side, then write the paragraph together from notes.
- **Extension:** take your Independent practice 1 paragraph and
  revise it after a real discussion: add one sentence that concedes a
  point your discussion partner made, and note what changed.
