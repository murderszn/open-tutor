# Lesson 1 — Rhetorical Appeals: Ethos, Pathos, Logos

**Goal:** by the end of this lesson you can name the appeal at work in a
passage — ethos, pathos, or logos — and explain in one or two sentences
how it supports the writer's purpose.
**Time:** about 45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, two highlighters,
this lesson page.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** in one sentence, what is the difference
between "I feel this is true" and "here is a reason this is true"? Keep
your answer — you will test it against the three appeals below.

## Teaching

**Rhetoric** is the art of persuading through language. The ancient
Greek thinker Aristotle named three appeals — ways a writer earns
belief. Most real passages mix them; your job is to find which appeal
is doing the heaviest work in a given sentence, and to say how.

- **Ethos** — appeal to credibility. The writer shows, or reminds you,
  why they are worth trusting: experience, fairness, honesty, or
  expertise. Signal words: "I have seen," "as someone who," a fair
  admission about the other side.
- **Pathos** — appeal to feeling. The writer chooses words and images
  that stir an emotion — anger, pity, pride, shame — so the audience
  cares enough to listen. Signal words: loaded adjectives, vivid
  images, repeated contrasts.
- **Logos** — appeal to reason. The writer offers a claim backed by
  facts, definitions, comparisons, or a chain of reasoning the reader
  can check. Signal words: "because," "therefore," numbers, "if … then."

A strong passage often layers them: ethos gets you to listen, logos
gives you a reason, pathos makes you care. A weak passage leans on only
one — usually pathos alone, with no reason behind the feeling.

Our anchor text is Frederick Douglass's address "What to the Slave Is
the Fourth of July?", delivered July 5, 1852, in Rochester, New York.
Douglass had escaped enslavement and become a leading abolitionist; he
was invited to speak at a Fourth of July celebration and chose to speak
the next day. The passages below are quoted from the public-domain
speech; spelling of "4th" follows the original printing.

## Worked example 1 — pathos carrying a logical contrast

> "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July? I answer: a day
> that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross
> injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim. To him, your
> celebration is a sham; your boasted liberty, an unholy license; your
> national greatness, swelling vanity; your shouts of liberty and
> equality, hollow mockery."

**Reasoning, step by step.** (1) *What does Douglass want?* He wants his
audience — free citizens celebrating independence — to feel the gap
between their celebration and an enslaved person's life. (2) *What
does he do?* He repeats "your" — your celebration, your liberty, your
greatness — and pairs each proud word with a deflating one: sham,
unholy license, swelling vanity, hollow mockery. (3) *Which appeal
leads?* **Pathos**: the loaded words are chosen to sting, especially
"hollow mockery" aimed at "liberty and equality," the celebration's own
language. (4) *Is there logos underneath?* Yes — the contrast itself is
a reason: a celebration of liberty cannot honestly include people held
in bondage. Pathos delivers the point; the contrast supplies the logic.
Calling this "only emotional" would miss the argument inside the
feeling.

## Worked example 2 — ethos from lived experience and fairness

> "This Fourth of July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must
> mourn. … The blessings in which you, this day, rejoice, are not
> enjoyed in common."

**Reasoning, step by step.** (1) *Who is speaking, and why does it
matter?* Douglass speaks as a man who was himself enslaved. He does not
have to argue that enslavement is cruel — he lived it. That lived
experience is **ethos**: a reason to trust that he knows what he
describes. (2) *What else builds trust?* Earlier in the speech Douglass
praises the founders and the principles of the Declaration of
Independence. Praising what deserves praise, even while attacking what
is wrong, shows fairness — another form of ethos. A writer who only
attacks is easier to dismiss. (3) *What about pathos?* "You may rejoice,
I must mourn" is also deeply felt, and that feeling is real. But the
sentence's special force is positional: only someone standing outside
the celebration could say "yours, not mine" with this authority. When
two appeals are present, name both and say which one leads — here,
ethos leads, pathos supports.

## Guided practice

Read each item, name the leading appeal, and quote the words that prove
it. One sentence of reasoning each.

1. Original practice passage (written for this unit): "I have coached
   youth soccer for eleven seasons, and I have watched the same ankle
   injuries repeat every year on our worn field. I am asking the council
   to resurface it before another child is carried off." Which appeal
   leads, and which words prove it?
2. Same practice passage, second sentence: "Maya, age twelve, sat on
   the sideline with an ice pack, crying, while her team played on
   without her." Which appeal leads here?
3. Douglass again: "The blessings in which you, this day, rejoice, are
   not enjoyed in common." Is this mainly ethos, pathos, or logos?
   Defend your answer in one sentence — two answers can be defended
   if the reasoning is honest; say which you chose and why.

## Independent practice

1. Write one original sentence that uses **ethos** to argue that your
   school library should stay open later. Underline the words doing the
   credibility work.
2. Write one original sentence that uses **logos** for the same
   argument. Underline the reason or fact.
3. Read this original sentence: "If we close the library at four,
   students with no quiet room at home will simply stop reading — and a
   town that stops reading stops thinking." Name the leading appeal
   and explain, in two sentences, whether the reasoning is sound or
   only sounds sound. (Hint: is "stops thinking" proved, or asserted?)

## Application

Pick any advertisement, speech clip, or announcement you have seen this
week. In four sentences: (a) quote or closely paraphrase one line,
(b) name its leading appeal, (c) explain how that appeal serves the
writer's purpose, and (d) say what appeal is missing that would make
the argument stronger.

## Exit check

1. In your own words, what is the difference between ethos and logos?
2. Which appeal did Douglass lean on most in Worked example 1, and what
   single word best proves it?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** read the Douglass passages aloud with an adult
  first; "boasted" means proudly claimed, "vanity" means empty pride.
- **Alternate response mode:** instead of writing, record your answers
  or sort index cards labeled ETHOS / PATHOS / LOGOS under each
  quotation.
- **Extension:** find a second appeal hiding inside Worked example 1
  and write three sentences on how the passage would change if that
  second appeal were removed.

**Teacher note:** the common misconception is that pathos means
"manipulative" and logos means "true." Correct it directly: an appeal
is a tool, not a verdict. Misconception notes and full solutions are in
the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and [answer key](../answer-key.md).
