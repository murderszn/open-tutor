# Lesson 4 — Author's Point of View and Purpose

**Goal:** by the end of this lesson you can state an author's point of
view and purpose in one sentence each, and explain how the author
acknowledges and responds to a conflicting view.
**Time:** about 45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, this lesson page,
Lessons 1 and 2.

**Readiness check (2 minutes):** Douglass was invited to celebrate the
Fourth of July and instead delivered an attack on American hypocrisy.
Why would a speaker accept an invitation and then disappoint the
hosts? Write one guess. You will test it below.

## Teaching

Every informational writer stands somewhere. **Point of view** is the
position the writer writes from — their situation, loyalties, and
assumptions. **Purpose** is what the writer wants the audience to
think, feel, or do after reading. Point of view shapes purpose: a
writer who stands outside a celebration will not write a celebration
speech.

Strong writers do not pretend opposing views do not exist. They
**acknowledge** them — name them fairly — and then **respond**: agree
in part, answer the objection, or show why it does not change the
conclusion. Watch for the moves:

- Concession: "It is true that … but …"
- Anticipation: answering an objection before the audience raises it.
- Praise of the other side's best point, then a turn: "The founders'
  principles are noble — and that is exactly why slavery condemns us."

A writer who never acknowledges another view is easier to distrust:
either they have not thought about it, or they hope you will not.

## Worked example 1 — Douglass: an outsider's purpose

**Point of view:** Douglass writes as a formerly enslaved Black man
addressing free white citizens celebrating independence — an outsider
to the celebration, invited in as a guest. **Purpose:** to make his
audience feel the contradiction between American liberty and American
slavery, and to move them from celebration to action against slavery.
**How he acknowledges a conflicting view:** he knows his audience
could object, "We invited you to honor our holiday — why attack us?"
He answers before they can ask, in two moves. First, praise: he honors
the founders and the Declaration's principles, showing he is not
attacking the ideals. Second, the turn: "This Fourth of July is yours,
not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn." The praise makes the attack
harder to dismiss, because Douglass has shown he understands — and
shares — the principles he says America betrays. Acknowledgment here
is not surrender; it is what makes the argument credible.

## Worked example 2 — Lincoln: conceding to redirect

**Point of view:** Lincoln writes as president, at a cemetery
dedication, speaking to a grieving nation mid-war. **Purpose:** to
dedicate the ground and, more importantly, to redefine what the war is
for — not only preserving the Union, but giving the nation "a new
birth of freedom." **How he acknowledges a conflicting view:** he
concedes the strongest version of the objection to his own ceremony:
"in a larger sense, we can not dedicate — we can not consecrate — we
can not hallow — this ground." A listener might think, "Then why are we
here?" Lincoln answers by redirecting dedication from the ground to
the living: "It is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here to
the unfinished work." Conceding the small point (words cannot make
ground sacred) wins trust for the large point (the living must take up
the work). Notice the pattern from Lesson 2's deny-and-replace: the
concession *is* the acknowledgment.

## Guided practice

1. State Douglass's purpose in your own words in one sentence. Then
   quote the single phrase from Worked example 1 that best proves it.
2. Lincoln's purpose has two layers: the stated one (dedicate the
   cemetery) and the deeper one (redefine the war). In two sentences,
   explain which layer the sentence "government of the people, by the
   people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth" serves, and
   why.
3. A writer who wants school uniforms writes: "Critics say uniforms
   limit self-expression. They are right that students express
   themselves through clothes — and that is exactly why a school
   should not let clothing decide who belongs." Name the
   acknowledgment move (concession, anticipation, or praise-and-turn)
   and explain in one sentence how it strengthens the argument.

## Independent practice

1. Take the skate-park paragraph from Lesson 3. Write one sentence
   stating its author's point of view (who is writing, to whom, from
   what position) and one sentence stating its purpose.
2. Write a two-sentence acknowledgment for the skate-park argument:
   fairly state the strongest objection to a skate park, then answer
   it without insulting the objector.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says, "Douglass's purpose was to ruin
   everyone's Fourth of July." In three sentences, correct this using
   the distinction between point of view and purpose: what did
   Douglass actually want his audience to do, and what in the speech
   proves celebration was not his target?

## Application

Choose any opinion piece, editorial, or announcement you can access.
Write: (a) the author's point of view in one sentence, (b) the
author's purpose in one sentence, (c) one quotation where the author
acknowledges a conflicting view — or, if there is none, one sentence
explaining what acknowledgment the piece is missing and how adding it
would change its credibility.

## Exit check

1. What is the difference between point of view and purpose, in your
   own words?
2. Why does acknowledging an opposing view usually make an argument
   stronger rather than weaker?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** "acknowledge" means to admit something exists
  or has force; it is not the same as agreeing.
- **Alternate response mode:** state point of view and purpose aloud
  to an adult, then write only the quotation that proves each.
- **Extension:** compare the two acknowledgment moves in the worked
  examples. In five sentences, argue which move is more effective for
  a hostile audience and which for a friendly one, and why.

**Teacher note:** learners often confuse "the author's purpose" with
"the topic" ("his purpose was slavery"). Require a verb of intended
effect: persuade, move, redefine, warn. Full solutions are in the
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
