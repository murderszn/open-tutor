# Answer Key — Unit 03: Informational Rhetoric and Conflicting Accounts

For the guiding adult only. Do not give this file to the learner
before they attempt the work. Open responses: accept any answer that
meets the same standard as the sample (correct label, proving
quotation, honest reasoning). Where two answers are defensible, the
key says so — do not mark a defended alternative wrong.

## Lesson 1 — Rhetorical appeals

**Guided practice**

1. **Ethos** leads. Proving words: "I have coached youth soccer for
   eleven seasons, and I have watched the same ankle injuries repeat
   every year." Experience is the credibility claim; the injury
   observation is the evidence that follows from it.
2. **Pathos** leads. Proving words: "Maya, age twelve, sat on the
   sideline with an ice pack, crying." A named child, an injury, and
   exclusion are chosen to stir feeling.
3. Defensible answers: **pathos** leads (the sting of exclusion —
   "not enjoyed in common" — is felt before it is reasoned), with
   **logos** acceptable (the unequal distribution is itself the
   reason: shared celebration, unshared blessing). Ethos alone is
   weak here because the sentence claims no credential. Accept either
   pathos or logos with the reasoning above.

**Independent practice**

1. Sample ethos sentence: "As a student who studies in the library
   every afternoon because home is too noisy, I know how much the
   late hours matter." Credibility words: "every afternoon,"
   "because home is too noisy." Accept any sentence whose
   credibility comes from the writer's position or experience.
2. Sample logos sentence: "Last term, 68 of 90 surveyed students
   said they finished homework in the library after four o'clock
   (invented practice figure, labeled here)." Accept any sentence
   with a checkable reason or figure.
3. Leading appeal: **logos in form** (an if–then chain), but the
   reasoning is **unsound as stated**. "Students will stop reading"
   is plausible, not proved; "a town that stops reading stops
   thinking" is asserted, not shown. The chain needs evidence at its
   second link before it counts as sound logos.

**Exit check**

1. Ethos = credibility (why trust this writer); logos = reason
   (facts, comparisons, or a checkable chain leading to the claim).
2. **Pathos**; best proving word: "sham" (accept "hollow mockery").

**Application:** accept any real example with (a) a quotation or
close paraphrase, (b) a correct leading appeal, (c) an explanation of
how it serves the writer's purpose, and (d) a named missing appeal.

## Lesson 2 — Paragraph structure and key sentences

**Guided practice**

1. "It is altogether fitting and proper that we should do this" is a
   **bridge/endorsement**, not the paragraph's key sentence. It
   affirms the dedication before the next paragraph reverses it.
   The paragraph's key sentence is "We have come to dedicate a
   portion of that field, as a final resting place …" — it states
   the ceremony's purpose, which the next paragraph then redefines.
2. The chain refines **what the living owe** — the "great task
   remaining." Stopping after the first "that" clause (devotion) would
   lose the purposes that follow: that the dead "shall not have died
   in vain," that the nation gains "a new birth of freedom," and that
   democratic government "shall not perish."
3. Sample connection: soldiers — "gave their lives that that nation
   might live"; the living — "for us the living, rather, to be
   dedicated here to the unfinished work"; the future — "government
   of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish
   from the earth." One quoted phrase per link is required.

**Independent practice**

1. Accept any sentence with (a) a verb for its job (introduce,
   explain, contrast, concede, conclude) and (b) a concrete statement
   of what the paragraph loses without it.
2. "Measure" turns a life into something counted; "full" means total,
   nothing held back. The tone is solemn and elevated: devotion is
   measured not in words but in a life given completely.
3. Correction: the cemetery is the occasion, not the subject. The
   speech is about whether a nation founded on equality can endure
   and what the living owe the dead. Proving sentence: "that this
   nation, under God, shall have a new birth of freedom" (accept the
   closing "shall not perish" clause).

**Exit check**

1. A key sentence is the sentence a paragraph would collapse without
   — the one that states, turns, or concludes its concept.
2. Deny-and-replace: Lincoln denies the literal meaning ("we can not
   dedicate … this ground") and replaces it with the living's
   dedication to "the unfinished work."

## Lesson 3 — Delineating arguments

**Guided practice (skate-park paragraph)**

1. Claim: the town should build a skate park. Reason 1: teenagers
   need safe after-school places — evidence: police log showing
   after-school disturbances near the mall fell by half after the
   neighboring town opened its park. "Skateboarding is also an
   Olympic sport" is a reason with **no evidence** (and weak
   relevance: Olympic status does not show local need).
2. Irrelevant: "our mayor once skateboarded as a teenager." A
   personal history cannot attach to any reason about teen safety or
   need.
3. Not sufficient on its own: one town, one year, and correlation is
   not causation (other changes could explain the fall). Best
   addition: before-and-after disturbance data from several towns
   with parks, or a survey of local teenagers' after-school
   whereabouts — either tests the safety claim directly.

**Independent practice**

1. Claim: homework should be abolished. Reason 1: "my cousin hates
   homework" — flaw: one person's preference is not evidence of
   harm. Reason 2: "Finland has shorter school days" — flaw: no
   outcome is given and no link to homework is shown; a different
   country's schedule proves nothing alone.
2. Counting is the wrong test because evidence must be **relevant**
   (attached to a reason for this claim) and **sufficient** (enough,
   from good sources). Three irrelevant or thin pieces weigh less
   than one relevant, well-supported piece.
3. Sample rewrite (invented practice fact, labeled): "The
   neighboring town's park recorded no after-school loitering
   complaints in its first year (invented practice figure)." The
   rewrite is relevant because it bears directly on the safety
   reason.

**Exit check**

1. Reasoning is the link (why the evidence should lead to the claim);
   evidence is the checkable support (facts, numbers, quotations).
2. Sample irrelevant evidence for a new gym: "The gym teacher won a
   coaching award last year." True, and it says nothing about whether
   the school needs a new gym. Accept any true statement that cannot
   attach to a need/reason.

## Lesson 4 — Point of view and purpose

**Guided practice**

1. Purpose: to move free citizens from celebrating independence to
   acting against slavery. Proving phrase: "You may rejoice, I must
   mourn" (accept "gross injustice and cruelty").
2. The closing sentence serves the **deeper** layer: it defines what
   must survive — democratic government itself ("of the people, by
   the people, for the people") — which is the redefinition of the
   war's meaning, not the cemetery ceremony.
3. Move: **concession-and-turn**. The writer concedes the objection
   ("They are right …") and then turns it: the admitted point
   becomes the reason for the proposal rather than against it.

**Independent practice**

1. Point of view: a skate-park supporter (likely a resident or young
   person) writing to the town council. Purpose: to persuade the
   council to build the park. Accept any fair one-sentence versions.
2. Sample acknowledgment: "Neighbors worry about noise and late
   crowds, and that worry is fair — a good plan would set evening
   hours and keep the park away from houses." The objection must be
   stated fairly, not mocked.
3. Correction: Douglass's purpose was not to ruin a holiday but to
   move his audience from celebration to action against slavery.
   Proof: he praises the founders' principles and then shows the
   celebration excludes the enslaved ("yours, not mine"). The target
   is hypocrisy, not the holiday.

**Exit check**

1. Point of view = the position the writer stands in; purpose = what
   the writer wants the audience to think, feel, or do.
2. Acknowledgment shows fairness and answers objections before they
   harden; a writer who hides the other side looks afraid of it,
   which costs credibility.

## Lesson 5 — Conflicting accounts

**Image activity**

(a) Two books (red and blue) side by side show comparison, and the
magnifying glass shows close checking — the task is to compare and
verify, not merely to read. (b) The magnifying glass stands for
checking facts closely: records, measurements, observations.
(c) The notebook is there because each disagreement and its kind must
be written down; an unrecorded comparison cannot be checked or used
in the project.

**Guided practice**

1. Cost disagreement: Account A says the $18,000 in donations means
   the garden "can be built without raising taxes"; Account B says
   the $18,000 covers soil and lumber only, not water lines or
   upkeep. This is **both**: a fact part (what the pledge actually
   covers — settled by the pledge letters) and an interpretation
   part (whether that sum suffices to avoid future taxes).
2. Table rows (accept equivalent wording): Fact — empty years
   (settle: town property/rental records); fact — drainage (settle:
   observation or dated photos after heavy rain); fact — donation
   scope (settle: pledge letters). Interpretation — survey meaning
   (shared fact: 214 respondents, 62% green space); interpretation —
   cost sufficiency (shared fact: $18,000 pledged; A reads it as
   enough, B as materials-only).
3. **Account B** acknowledges more fairly. B engages A's number
   directly ("Sixty-two percent of 214 is just 133 people") and
   answers its drainage and cost claims. A never mentions parking
   need or maintenance cost; its "That is a clear majority" asserts
   without answering the sample-size question.

**Independent practice**

1. Sample summaries: A — "The Garden Committee proposes a community
   garden on the Elm Street lot, citing a survey majority and
   $18,000 in pledged materials." B — "The Shop Owners' Association
   proposes a parking lot, arguing the survey sample is small, the
   lot drains poorly, and the donations do not cover upkeep."
   Neither summary may contain praise or blame.
2. The records support **B's** three-year claim. They prove nothing
   about which future use is better: past storage use does not
   decide whether a garden or parking serves the town now.
3. More numbers do not win: numbers must be relevant and sufficient.
   B's "133 people" strengthens B because it honestly shows the
   sample's size. Numbers mislead when denominators are hidden (62%
   without 214) or when a count is offered in place of relevance
   (a big number about the wrong question).

**Exit check**

1. Fact disagreement: the lot sat empty six years vs. three years.
   Interpretation disagreement: the survey's 62% means "a clear
   majority for a garden" vs. "too small a sample to decide."
2. Confidence is an appeal (ethos/pathos), not a record. First ask
   what record would settle a fact, and what reasoning weighs an
   interpretation.

## Lesson 6 — Discussion and synthesis

**Guided practice**

1. Claim: a garden will bring the neighborhood together. Reason:
   "gardens always build community" — circular and unsupported.
   Missing evidence: examples or data of community outcomes from
   real gardens (attendance, shared work, surveys).
2. Sample: "You are right that 133 people is a small sample — and a
   small sample can still signal demand when combined with the
   pledged donations." The restatement must be fair before the
   answer.
3. Sample: "Garden supporters rightly note the survey majority and
   the donated materials; the question is whether the town can fund
   upkeep, not whether residents want a garden."

**Independent practice**

1. Sample parking paragraph: "Cedar Falls should build a parking lot
   on the Elm Street lot. Shoppers and workers need nearby parking,
   and the lot already sits beside the shops. Garden supporters
   rightly note the survey majority, but 214 respondents cannot
   commit 9,000 residents to years of watering and repairs. That
   objection argues for testing demand with a trial garden bed,
   not for giving up the parking the shops need now. A lot that
   serves everyone, every day, is the fairer use." Accept any
   paragraph with claim → fair acknowledgment → distinction →
   answer → conclusion.
2. Accept any three-turn script in which each turn is labeled as
   building or delineating and actually does that work.
3. Faults: (a) straw man — "they are wrong" without stating what
   they actually argue; (b) unsupported reason — "gardens are nice"
   is taste, not evidence. Sample rewrite: "Some residents worry a
   garden will attract pests; that concern is fair, and a
   maintenance rota with locked compost bins answers it."

**Exit check**

1. Besides stating its claim, a counterclaim paragraph must fairly
   acknowledge the strongest opposing claim and answer it
   (distinguish, then respond).
2. "Building on" uses the previous speaker's idea or evidence as a
   step; waiting only prepares your own reply without listening.

## Practice (P1–P10)

**P1.** 1. Ethos — "As a nurse who has worked night shifts for nine
years." 2. Pathos — the porch floating away "with their dog still
barking on it." 3. Logos — "4,000 cars a day," "three cracked
supports," and the if–then chain to closure.

**P2.** 1. The "say" half minimizes the speakers' words; the "did"
half elevates the soldiers' deeds — antithesis of saying vs. doing.
2. It is a key sentence because it states the speech's turn: words
cannot dedicate the ground, so the living must be dedicated to the
work. Without it, the dedication stays literal and the redefinition
never happens.

**P3.** 1. Claim: buy more graphic novels. Reason: inviting books
make students read more. Evidence: circulation rose 40% after the
display. 2. Irrelevant: "The librarian also has a degree in
literature" — a credential that says nothing about student reading.
3. Not sufficient alone: one year, one library, no reading-outcome
data. Best addition: reading-level or borrowing-continuation data,
or multi-year circulation figures.

**P4.** 1. Point of view: the principal, writing from school
authority to students and families. Purpose: to keep classrooms calm
and students safe by controlling hallway movement. 2. Missing view:
students with legitimate reasons to be late (a locker problem, a
teacher kept them). Sample acknowledgment: "We know some students
are delayed for good reasons, so teachers will excuse late passes
when they kept you."

**P5.** It could be either. If the two accounts describe different
counts, it is a fact disagreement; if they describe the same crowd
with different words ("huge" vs. "modest"), it is interpretation.
Settling evidence: a ticket count, a seat count, or a dated
photograph of the seats at noon.

**P6.** 1. **Pathos** leads — "immeasurable distance" stings; accept
logos (the contrast itself is a reason). 2. "Distance" works as an
image (a physical gap between two groups) and as an argument (the
moral and political gap between celebration and enslavement). The
image carries the reasoning.

**P7.** 1. Claim: video games rot your brain. Reason: the brother
plays for hours and his grades dropped. Evidence: one anecdote
(the brother). 2. Flaws: sufficiency — a single case proves nothing
about games in general; reasoning — correlation is not causation
(grades may have dropped for other reasons), and "rot your brain"
is undefined, so the claim cannot be tested.

**P8.** 1. Faults: the counterclaim is a straw man ("they just like
mornings" misrepresents objectors), and "helps everyone" is
unsupported. 2. Sample rewrite: "School should start later. Some
argue that later starts disrupt bus schedules and after-school
jobs. That scheduling concern is real, but it argues for staggered
routes, not for keeping a start time that research links to student
sleep loss. A later start with adjusted transport serves students
without abandoning activities." (The "research links" phrase must
be supported by a cited source in a real filing; here it is a
practice claim for the rewrite exercise.)

**P9.** Sample summary of B: "The Shop Owners' Association proposes
a parking lot on the Elm Street lot. It argues the survey sample is
small, the lot drains poorly, and the donations cover materials
only." Deliberately left out: praise or blame words (for example,
calling either side "selfish" or "wise") — a summary reports what
each side claims, not what the summarizer thinks of them.

**P10.** 1. Map: claim — fund a free summer lunch program. Reason 1:
summer food insecurity — evidence: county health survey, one in
five local children. Reason 2 (link): children who eat regularly
return ready to learn — evidence: none stated (an assumed link;
flag it). Cost objection acknowledged: program would use the empty
school kitchen — evidence: volunteer staffing kept a nearby town's
program under budget for three years. 2. Move: concession-and-
answer. The response is partly sound (it addresses cost with a
real precedent) but incomplete (a nearby town is not this town).
3. Not yet sufficient: still needed are a local cost projection, a
committed volunteer roster, and the number of children the program
would actually serve.

## Week-14 quiz (20 points)

1. (2) Ethos — credibility/trust; pathos — feeling; logos — reason.
   One phrase each; 1 point for all three names, 1 for all three
   phrases.
2. (2) **Ethos** — "I have taught middle school for fourteen years."
   (The survey-like second clause is supporting evidence, not the
   leading appeal.)
3. (2) "Our principal once ran a marathon" is irrelevant: a personal
   athletic feat says nothing about school start times. 1 point for
   identifying it, 1 for the reason.
4. (2) The sentence **concedes/denies** the ceremony's literal
   purpose, clearing the way for the redefinition that follows.
   Accept "denies" or "concedes" with the explanation that the
   paragraph then rebuilds dedication as the living's task.
5. (2) Point of view: a formerly enslaved man addressing free
   citizens at their celebration, as an outsider. Purpose: to make
   them feel the contradiction and move them to act against slavery.
   1 point each.
6. (2) **Fact** disagreement (40 vs. 90 cannot both be true).
   Settling evidence: a headcount, ticket count, or sign-in record.
7. (2) **Interpretation** disagreement: both agree on 55% but draw
   different conclusions ("mandate" vs. "barely half") from the
   same figure.
8. (2) Move: **acknowledge-and-answer**. It strengthens the argument
   because it shows the writer has heard the strongest objection
   and answered it with a concrete plan, which builds credibility.
9. (2) Sample: "The Cedar Falls lot has sat empty for years, and two
   groups disagree about its future — one favoring a garden, the
   other parking — while citing the same town survey differently."
   Deduct 1 point for any praise/blame word.
10. (2) More numbers do not win: numbers must be relevant to the
    claim and sufficient to carry it. A larger count of weak or
    off-topic numbers weighs less than one relevant, well-supported
    figure. Accept any two-sentence version of this reasoning.

## Unit assessment (33 points)

**A1. (6)** Claim (1): the school should start a composting program.
Reasons and evidence (2 for reasons, 2 for evidence): R1 — food
waste is a large share of trash; evidence: science club audit, 62
kilograms in one week. R2 — composting teaches environmental
responsibility; evidence: **none stated**. R3 — composting cuts
hauling trips; reasoning given (charges by the bin) but no measured
evidence stated — accept "reasoning without evidence" or "evidence:
the hauler's per-bin charge" with a note that the charge itself is
asserted, not documented. Irrelevant evidence (1): "our science
teacher once grew prize-winning tomatoes." Reason with no evidence
(1, included in the split above): the responsibility reason. Partial
credit: 1 point per correctly mapped element up to the split.

**A2. (4)** Soundness (1): partly sound — the audit supports the
waste claim, but the responsibility and hauling claims lack measured
support. Relevance (1): the audit is relevant; the tomato prize is
not. Sufficiency (1): one week's audit from one club is a start, not
enough for a program decision; a longer audit and a cost estimate
are needed. Verdict (1): promising but not yet sufficient. Accept
equivalent judgments with reasons.

**A3. (5)** (a) **Fact** disagreement: $1,200 vs. $900 cannot both be
the net total (2 points: 1 for the label, 1 for the explanation).
(b) Settling evidence: the ticket-stub count against the refund
record — specifically, whether the $300 was refunded (1 point).
(c) **Account D** handles the other side better: it names and
explains the $300 ("counts $300 of ticket money that was refunded"),
while C asserts a double count without addressing refunds (2 points:
1 for the judgment, 1 for the quotation).

**A4. (3)** The accounts do not directly disagree about success in
the quoted lines — C calls $1,200 "our best total ever" while D
disputes the total, not the judgment. Whether $900 (or $1,200) counts
as a "success" is an **interpretation** disagreement: it depends on
the standard (past totals, costs, attendance), not on a single
count. Award 1 point for noting the success judgment is separate
from the total, 2 for the interpretation classification with a
reason. Accept a defense that C's "best total ever" implies a
success claim D would dispute — if the learner explains that the
dispute is still about the standard, not the count.

**A5. (5)** (a) **Deny-and-replace**: "They are not strict; they are
clear" denies one label and replaces it (1 point). (b) Key sentence:
"They are not strict; they are clear." Without it, the paragraph
has no thesis to support — the later sentences would describe rules
without saying what they amount to (2 points). (c) Appeal: **logos**
— the before/after comparison (wandering unsure vs. same short list,
enforced the same way) gives a reason for "clear"; accept **ethos**
if the learner argues the even-handed enforcement claim builds
trust, with the quotation "enforced … the same way for everyone"
(2 points: 1 for the appeal, 1 for the quotation).

**A6. (3)** Sample: "The writer proposes a school composting
program. A science club audit counted 62 kilograms of food waste in
one week, and the writer argues composting would teach
responsibility and reduce hauling trips." Deduct 1 point for any
praise/blame word, 1 for adding a new claim.

**A7. (7)** Rubric — 1 point each: (1) claim stated; (2) at least one
reason with evidence (an invented practice fact must be labeled as
invented); (3) counterclaim fairly stated (not a straw man);
(4) distinction between the writer's view and the counterclaim;
(5) answer to the counterclaim with reasoning or evidence;
(6) conclusion that follows from the argument; (7) coherent
paragraph structure (sentences connect; no contradiction). A
paragraph that insults the other side loses points (3) and (5)
even if it is otherwise fluent.

## Project — sample responses (illustrative, not a learner submission)

**Sample chart rows:** Fact — empty years: A says six, B says
three; settle with town rental/property records. Fact — drainage:
A says dry within an hour, B says standing water for days; settle
with observation or dated photos after heavy rain.
Interpretation — survey: shared fact 214 respondents, 62% green
space; A reads "clear majority," B reads "133 of 9,000, asked about
use not cost." Interpretation — $18,000: shared figure; A reads it
as enough to build without tax rises, B as materials only.

**Sample brief excerpt (argument section):** "Cedar Falls should
build the garden, with a published maintenance plan. The survey
majority and pledged materials show real demand, and critics rightly
note the sample is small and upkeep unfunded. That argues for a
costed upkeep plan before construction, not for abandoning the
garden: a trial bed and a volunteer rota would test both demand and
upkeep within one season."

**Scoring note:** a brief that picks either side can score full
marks. Score the chart's labels, the settling evidence, the
fairness of the counterclaim paragraph, and the objective summary —
not the recommendation.
