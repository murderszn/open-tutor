# Lesson 03 — Dialogue and Incidents That Move the Story

**Goal (student-readable):** I can explain how a specific line of dialogue
or incident propels the action, reveals character, or provokes a
decision — and how dramatic irony builds suspense.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, two highlighters ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L3](../answer-key.md#lesson-03)

**Readiness check (3 min):** In "The Stairwell," which *single line*
changes what happens next? Write the line and what it causes.

## Three jobs of a line

Every strong line of dialogue in a drama does at least one of three
jobs (often all three):

1. **Propels the action** — makes something happen that would not have
   happened otherwise. A challenge, a confession, a refusal.
2. **Reveals character** — shows who the speaker is under pressure. Word
   choice, rhythm, and what the character *avoids* saying all count.
3. **Provokes a decision** — forces another character to choose. The
   decision, not the line, is usually the plot's turning point.

**Dramatic irony** is the engine underneath: the audience knows something
a character does not, and the gap creates suspense or humor. You cannot
analyze a line's full power until you ask, *"Who knows what, and who
doesn't?"*

## The scene: *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 3, Scene 1 (excerpt, public domain)

*Verona, a public street, on a hot afternoon. Benvolio (Romeo's cousin,
the peacemaker) and Mercutio (Romeo's witty, hot-tempered friend) meet
Tybalt (Juliet's cousin, spoiling for a fight). Line numbers are
approximate; the adult's school text may differ by a line or two.*

BENVOLIO: I pray thee, good Mercutio, let's retire.
The day is hot, the Capels are abroad,
And if we meet we shall not 'scape a brawl,
For now, these hot days, is the mad blood stirring.

MERCUTIO: Thou art like one of those fellows that, when he enters the
confines of a tavern, claps me his sword upon the table and says "God
send me no need of thee!" — and by the operation of the second cup draws
it on the drawer, when indeed there is no need.

*Enter Tybalt.*

TYBALT: Mercutio, thou consort'st with Romeo —

MERCUTIO: Consort! What, dost thou make us minstrels? An thou make
minstrels of us, look to hear nothing but discords. Here's my
fiddlestick — *[draws his sword]* — here's that shall make you dance.

BENVOLIO: We talk here in the public haunt of men. Either withdraw unto
some private place and reason coldly of your grievances, or else depart;
here all eyes gaze on us.

MERCUTIO: Men's eyes were made to look, and let them gaze. I will not
budge for no man's pleasure, I.

*Enter Romeo.*

TYBALT: Romeo, the hate I bear thee can afford no better term than this:
thou art a villain.

ROMEO: Tybalt, the reason that I have to love thee doth much excuse the
appertaining rage to such a greeting. Villain am I none. Therefore
farewell; I see thou know'st me not.

TYBALT: Boy, this shall not excuse the injuries that thou hast done me.
Therefore turn and draw.

ROMEO: I do protest I never injured thee, but love thee better than thou
canst devise, till thou shalt know the reason of my love. And so, good
Capulet — which name I tender as dearly as mine own — be satisfied.

MERCUTIO: O calm, dishonourable, vile submission! *Alla stoccata*
carries it away. *[Draws.]* Tybalt, you ratcatcher, will you walk?

**Word help:** *'scape* = escape · *consort* = keep company with (Mercutio
puns on "consort" = a band of musicians) · *discords* = clashing notes ·
*fiddlestick* = violin bow (his sword) · *appertaining* = belonging ·
*tender* = hold dear · *ratcatcher* = Mercutio's insult: Tybalt is "king
of cats" in the old Reynard tales, so a cat-chaser of rats.

*What happens next (plot summary, not excerpt): the fight breaks out.
Romeo throws himself between the fighters; Tybalt stabs Mercutio under
Romeo's arm. Mercutio dies cursing both houses. Romeo, enraged and
guilty, kills Tybalt — and is banished from Verona. This scene is the
play's turning point: comedy becomes tragedy here.*

## Worked example 1 — A line that propels the action

**Line:** Mercutio: "Tybalt, you ratcatcher, will you walk?"

**Analysis:** This line does all three jobs. It **propels the action**:
until now Tybalt wanted Romeo, not Mercutio; Mercutio's insult hands
Tybalt a new opponent and the fight becomes unavoidable. It **reveals
character**: Mercutio would rather pick a deadly fight than watch his
friend submit — pride and loyalty twisted together. It **provokes a
decision**: Tybalt must now answer the challenge ("I am for you"). Remove
this line and Romeo keeps refusing, Tybalt keeps demanding — the scene
stalls. The insult is the spark.

## Worked example 2 — Dramatic irony inside a line

**Line:** Romeo: "the reason that I have to love thee… till thou shalt
know the reason of my love."

**Analysis:** The audience knows what Tybalt does not: Romeo married
Juliet — Tybalt's cousin — *that very morning* (Act 2, Scene 6). So
Romeo's strange gentleness is not cowardice; it is a secret. The irony
creates **suspense**: we watch Tybalt mistake love for insult, and we
know the misunderstanding is steering everyone toward the fight. A line
that looks weak becomes heartbreaking once you track who knows what.

**Foil check:** Benvolio and Tybalt open the scene as foils — Benvolio
("let's retire… reason coldly") against Tybalt (who arrives looking for
a quarrel). Same hot street, same feud, opposite choices. And Mercutio's
mockery of Benvolio ("thou art like one of those fellows…") shows that
even friends foil each other: Mercutio hears caution as cowardice.

## Guided practice (pairs — read the excerpt aloud)

1. Find one line (not used above) that **provokes a decision**. Name the
   speaker, quote the line, and state the decision it forces.
2. Benvolio says "here all eyes gaze on us." In one sentence, explain how
   this line reveals character rather than propelling action.
3. **Dramatic irony hunt:** list two things the audience knows in this
   scene that at least one character on stage does not. For each, name
   the effect (suspense or humor) in one phrase.

## Independent practice

In your notebook:

1. Choose **one** line from the excerpt. Write a three-job analysis:
   does it propel the action, reveal character, provoke a decision — or
   more than one? Quote the line and give one sentence of reasoning per
   job it performs.
2. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "Romeo is a coward in this
   scene because he won't fight Tybalt." Using the dramatic-irony
   example above, write 3–4 sentences correcting the reading. Quote at
   least one line.
3. Foil paragraph: explain how Benvolio and Tybalt function as foils in
   this scene's opening. Quote one line from each and name the theme
   their contrast sharpens (honor, peace, pride — defend your choice).

## Application

Return to "The Stairwell." Identify the scene's turning-point line (your
readiness-check answer or a revised choice) and analyze it with the
three-job frame in 4–5 sentences. Then add one sentence: is there any
dramatic irony in "The Stairwell"? Who knows what?

## Exit check

1. Name the three jobs a line of dialogue can do.
2. In one sentence, explain why Romeo's gentleness toward Tybalt is an
   example of dramatic irony.
3. Quote one line from the excerpt that propels the action and state what
   it causes in one sentence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** The adult reads Tybalt and Mercutio; the learner
  reads Benvolio and Romeo. Use the word-help glosses before the first
  read, not during.
- **Alternate response mode:** Perform the three-job analysis as a
  labeled diagram (line in the center, three arrows) instead of prose.
- **Extension:** Compare this scene's structure to the Hamlet soliloquy
  in Lesson 4 *before* reading it: predict how a scene with four
  speakers arguing will differ in pacing from one speaker thinking
  alone. Write 3–4 sentences.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners summarize the fight instead of
analyzing lines ("then they fight and Mercutio dies"). Require every
claim to start from a quoted line; the three-job frame is the scaffold.
Also watch for presentism — judging Romeo's refusal by modern
fight-culture norms instead of by what the audience knows. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-03).
