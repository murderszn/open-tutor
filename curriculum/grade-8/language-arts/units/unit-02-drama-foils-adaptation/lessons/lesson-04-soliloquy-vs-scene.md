# Lesson 04 — Soliloquy vs. Scene: Structure Shapes Meaning

**Goal (student-readable):** I can compare a soliloquy's structure with a
dialogue scene's structure and explain how each structure shapes what the
audience learns — and how Shakespeare's word choices build the thinking.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, dictionary ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L4](../answer-key.md#lesson-04)

**Readiness check (3 min):** Write one sentence: why would a playwright
ever have a character talk *alone* instead of talking *to someone*?

**Adult note:** this soliloquy contemplates suicide ("To die: to sleep").
Preview it, use the excerpt below, and treat Hamlet's question as a
philosophical argument to analyze — not as advice. If the adult prefers,
substitute the teacher guide's alternate: Juliet's "Gallop apace"
soliloquy (Act 3, Scene 2), which practices the same skills.

## Two structures, two kinds of truth

| | Dialogue scene (R&J 3.1) | Soliloquy (Hamlet 3.1) |
|---|---|---|
| Speakers | Several, interrupting and answering | One, uninterrupted |
| Pace | Fast — conflict drives it forward | Slow — thought circles and returns |
| What the audience learns | Who these people are *to each other* | Who this person is *to himself* |
| Evidence type | Clashes, decisions, reactions | Images, questions, contradictions |
| Can the character lie? | Yes — to other characters | Arguably no — this is the "honest room" |

A soliloquy is a structural choice: Shakespeare removes every other voice
so we hear the mind working. The meaning of the speech *depends* on that
loneliness — the same words spoken to a friend would be a conversation,
not a confession.

## The text: *Hamlet*, Act 3, Scene 1, excerpt (public domain)

*Prince Hamlet, alone, weighing whether to go on living and avenging his
murdered father — or to end his suffering. Line numbers approximate.*

To be, or not to be, that is the question:
Whether 'tis nobler in the mind to suffer
The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune,
Or to take arms against a sea of troubles,
And by opposing end them? To die: to sleep;
No more; and by a sleep to say we end
The heart-ache and the thousand natural shocks
That flesh is heir to, 'tis a consummation
Devoutly to be wish'd. To die, to sleep;
To sleep: perchance to dream: ay, there's the rub;
For in that sleep of death what dreams may come
When we have shuffled off this mortal coil,
Must give us pause.

**Word help:** *slings and arrows* = weapons; suffering them = enduring
life passively · *take arms against a sea* = fight the ocean — a
deliberately impossible image · *consummation* = completion, fulfillment
· *the rub* = the obstacle (from bowls, where "rub" is an uneven patch
that deflects the ball) · *mortal coil* = the turmoil of mortal life
("coil" = fuss, turmoil — not a snake) · *give us pause* = make us
hesitate.

## Worked example 1 — Structure comparison

**Question:** What does the soliloquy let the audience learn that the
3.1 street scene could not?

**Model answer:** In 3.1, every line is *social* — Mercutio performs for
Tybalt, Romeo performs calm for his enemy. We never hear what anyone
privately fears. Hamlet's soliloquy does the reverse: there is no one to
perform for, so the structure permits contradiction. He calls death "a
consummation devoutly to be wish'd" and, nine lines later, dreads it
("perchance to dream"). A dialogue scene would force another character
to resolve the contradiction; the soliloquy lets it stand, and the
unresolved contradiction *is* the character: a mind that cannot stop
weighing. Structure creates the meaning — hesitation made audible.

## Worked example 2 — Diction: the sea you cannot fight

**Question:** Why does Hamlet say "take arms against a *sea* of
troubles" instead of "an *army* of troubles"?

**Model answer:** An army can be fought; a sea cannot. The mixed
metaphor is deliberate: Hamlet's first option (suffer the "slings and
arrows") is passive endurance, and his second option (fight back) is
pictured as *impossible* — you cannot stab the ocean. The diction quietly
admits that neither choice works, which is why the speech keeps going.
Notice the pattern: each beautiful image ("slings and arrows," "sea of
troubles," "undiscover'd country") makes action sound grand and then
impossible. That is how word choice builds the theme: **thought
paralyzes action.**

**Foil connection:** Hamlet's great foil is Laertes — another young man
whose father was killed. Laertes hears the news and immediately raises an
army; Hamlet hears the news and delivers a soliloquy. Same grief,
opposite responses. Shakespeare needs both: Laertes' speed makes Hamlet's
slowness visible, and the play asks which response destroys more.

## Guided practice (pairs)

1. Reread the excerpt aloud — slowly, with the pauses the punctuation
   marks (colons, semicolons, question marks). How does the punctuation
   *sound* like thinking? Give one example.
2. Find one image in the excerpt where death sounds peaceful and one
   where it sounds frightening. Quote both phrases. What does the shift
   reveal about Hamlet's mind?
3. Structure test: rewrite "To die, to sleep; / To sleep: perchance to
   dream: ay, there's the rub" as *dialogue* — Hamlet says the first
   line to a friend, the friend answers. What is lost? Write 2–3
   sentences.

## Independent practice

In your notebook:

1. **Structure comparison paragraph (6–8 sentences):** Compare the R&J
   3.1 excerpt (Lesson 3) with the Hamlet excerpt above on *two* of
   these: number of speakers, pace, what the audience learns, or whether
   a character can hide the truth. Conclude: which structure would
   better show a character *lying to himself*, and why?
2. **Diction analysis:** Choose one phrase from the word-help list
   (e.g., "mortal coil," "the rub," "slings and arrows"). In 3–4
   sentences, explain the literal meaning, the figurative meaning, and
   what it reveals about Hamlet's view of action vs. thought.
3. **Foil application:** In 3–4 sentences, explain how Laertes functions
   as Hamlet's foil. Name the shared situation, the contrast, and what
   the contrast reveals about the play's theme of revenge.

## Application

Write 6–10 lines of an original soliloquy for **Maya or Jay** from "The
Stairwell," alone in the stairwell after the blackout. Reveal one
thought the character would never say aloud to the other. Then write two
sentences: how did the soliloquy structure let you show something the
dialogue scene could not?

## Exit check

1. In one sentence, state the key structural difference between a
   soliloquy and a dialogue scene.
2. Quote one phrase from the Hamlet excerpt where death sounds peaceful
   and one where it sounds frightening (or name the phrases).
3. Why is Laertes an effective foil for Hamlet? Answer in one sentence
   using the three foil rules from Lesson 2.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** The adult reads the excerpt aloud first while the
  learner follows with a finger; paraphrase each sentence into modern
  English together before analyzing.
- **Alternate response mode:** Record the soliloquy as audio with the
  pauses exaggerated, then answer guided practice orally.
- **Extension:** Research question — the "To be" speech appears in three
  early printed versions of *Hamlet* with different wording (Q1, Q2, and
  the First Folio, 1623). Why might the "same" speech differ across
  printings? Write 3–4 sentences using the Folger's "Early printed
  texts" note as a starting point.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners treat the soliloquy as Hamlet
"being sad" and paraphrase it loosely, skipping the diction work. Anchor
every claim to a quoted phrase; the word-help glosses exist so no one
can hide behind "it's just old English." The suicide content needs a
calm, analytic frame — see the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-04)
for language.
