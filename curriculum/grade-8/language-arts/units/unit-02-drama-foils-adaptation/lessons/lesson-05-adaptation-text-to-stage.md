# Lesson 05 — From Page to Stage: Judging an Adaptation

**Goal (student-readable):** I can compare a script with a filmed or live
production of the same scene, name where the production stays faithful
and where it departs, and evaluate whether the director's choices work.
**Time:** ~50 minutes · **Materials:** this page, index cards ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L5](../answer-key.md#lesson-05)

**Readiness check (3 min):** Think of a book-to-movie change you have
noticed. In one sentence, was the change an improvement, a loss, or
neutral — and how could you tell?

## Faithful vs. departed — and why it matters

An **adaptation** moves a story from one medium to another: script to
stage, script to film. Every adaptation makes **choices**, and every
choice can be judged two ways:

- **Faithful:** the production keeps the text's words, order, setting, or
  meaning. Faithfulness is not automatically good — a word-perfect but
  lifeless staging can fail.
- **Departed:** the production changes, cuts, or adds. Departure is not
  automatically bad — a changed setting can reveal a theme the original
  staging hid.

The critic's question is never "did they change it?" It is: **"did the
change serve the story — and can you prove it with evidence from both
the text and the production?"**

Track three kinds of choices with an **adaptation choice tracker**:

| Choice type | What to watch for | Example question |
|---|---|---|
| Setting & design | Place, time period, costumes, light | Why this century, these clothes? |
| Pacing & cuts | What is cut, reordered, sped up, lingered on | What does the cut hide or highlight? |
| Body & voice | Gestures, distance between actors, volume, pauses | What does the actor's body say the words don't? |

## The text: *Romeo and Juliet*, Act 2, Scene 2, excerpt (public domain)

*Capulet's orchard, night. Romeo has climbed the garden wall. Juliet
appears above, at her window, unaware he is listening. Line numbers
approximate.*

ROMEO: But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?
It is the east, and Juliet is the sun.
Arise, fair sun, and kill the envious moon,
Who is already sick and pale with grief
That thou, her maid, art far more fair than she.

JULIET: O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?
Deny thy father and refuse thy name;
Or, if thou wilt not, be but sworn my love,
And I'll no longer be a Capulet.

ROMEO *[aside]*: Shall I speak at this moment?

JULIET: 'Tis but thy name that is my enemy.
Thou art thyself, though not a Montague.
What's Montague? It is nor hand, nor foot,
Nor arm, nor face, nor any other part
Belonging to a man. O, be some other name!
What's in a name? That which we call a rose
By any other word would smell as sweet.
So Romeo would, were he not Romeo call'd,
Retain that dear perfection which he owes
Without that title. Romeo, doff thy name,
And for that name, which is no part of thee,
Take all myself.

**Word help:** *wherefore* = why (not "where") · *doff* = take off,
discard · *thy* / *thou* = your / you · *yonder* = over there.

*Plot note:* Juliet does not know Romeo is below. When he finally
speaks, she is startled — the scene's intimacy depends on her thinking
she is alone. That is dramatic irony again: the audience watches Romeo
watching Juliet.

## Worked example 1 — A choice and its judgment

**Production fact (documented):** Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film stages
this scene on a literal stone balcony at dawn-like light, with Juliet
above and Romeo below in the garden — the version most school audiences
know. (The Folger's Shakespeare Unlimited podcast, "Olivia Hussey: The
Girl on the Balcony," discusses this production's casting and look;
verified via folger.edu 2026-10-20.)

**Choice:** The film keeps Shakespeare's words nearly intact (faithful)
but *adds* a physical balcony and plays the scene in soft morning-ish
light (a design departure — the text says night: "Arise, fair sun").

**Evaluation:** The balcony *serves* the story: it makes Juliet's height
literal, so "what light through yonder window breaks" reads as Romeo
looking *up* — worship from below, matching the sun/moon imagery.
Cost: the text's night setting ("the envious moon") loses some logic in
bright light, and the balcony itself was a later theatrical invention,
not Shakespeare's staging. Verdict: a defensible departure — it
clarifies the power dynamic (he below, she above) at the price of the
night imagery. Note the method: name the choice, cite text *and*
production, judge by what the story gains or loses.

## Worked example 2 — Comparing two productions

The Royal Shakespeare Company (RSC) publishes short staging videos for
this scene (rsc.org.uk Shakespeare Learning Zone, verified 2026-10-20):
actors discussing *how they staged the balcony scene* and *why they made
their choices*. A strong comparison answers three questions:

1. **What did Production A keep that Production B changed?** (faithful
   vs. departed, per production)
2. **Which choice better serves one specific line?** Pick a line — e.g.,
   "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" — and ask which staging makes
   its meaning land harder.
3. **What does each production assume about the audience?** A playful
   staging assumes the audience wants romance; a tense staging assumes
   they feel the danger of the feud.

## Guided practice (pairs)

1. Reread Juliet's "What's in a name?" speech. If you were the director,
   would you stage it with Juliet *pacing* or *perfectly still*? Defend
   your choice in two sentences using one quoted phrase.
2. Romeo's aside — "Shall I speak at this moment?" — is a choice point.
   Name two different actor choices for delivering it (e.g., whispered
   eagerly vs. held back fearfully) and state what each choice tells the
   audience about Romeo.
3. **Error analysis:** A classmate writes, "The 1968 film is better
   because movies are better than plays." Rewrite the judgment using the
   critic's question from this lesson (2–3 sentences, cite one line of
   the text).

## Independent practice

In your notebook:

1. Build an **adaptation choice tracker** (table) for the Zeffirelli
   example above: three rows (setting & design, pacing & cuts, body &
   voice), each with *choice → faithful or departed → serves or costs
   the story*.
2. Watch or read about one RSC staging video for this scene (see the
   [resource pack](../resource-pack.md); if video is unavailable, use the
   RSC page's written descriptions). Add a second column to your
   tracker: how does the RSC staging differ on the *same* three choice
   types?
3. Write a verdict paragraph (5–7 sentences): which production's choices
   better serve Juliet's "What's in a name?" speech? Cite at least one
   line of text and one observed production choice.

## Application

You are adapting **"The Stairwell"** for the stage (this previews the
unit project). Make three directorial choices — one per tracker row —
and write a 4–6 sentence **director's rationale**: what you changed or
kept, and what each choice serves. Example starter: "I stage the
blackout as a slow dim, not a snap to black, because…"

## Exit check

1. In your own words: what is the critic's question for judging an
   adaptation?
2. Name one faithful choice and one departed choice from the Zeffirelli
   example.
3. Why is "movies are better than plays" not a valid adaptation
   judgment? One sentence.

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Paraphrase Juliet's speech into modern English
  with the adult before any analysis; circle the metaphors (rose, sun,
  moon) together.
- **Alternate response mode:** Deliver the verdict paragraph as a spoken
  60-second review (this rehearses the project pitch).
- **Extension:** Research how Shakespeare's own company staged this
  scene — no balcony, a window or upper stage, daylight performances.
  In 4–5 sentences, argue whether the "balcony" is a faithful or
  departed choice *relative to 1597*, using the Folger's "Early printed
  texts" note or a reputable source from the resource pack.

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners judge adaptations by enjoyment
("I liked it") instead of by evidence. The choice tracker forces the
two-column habit: *what they did* beside *what it cost or gained*.
Hold the line: no verdict without a quoted line and an observed choice.
