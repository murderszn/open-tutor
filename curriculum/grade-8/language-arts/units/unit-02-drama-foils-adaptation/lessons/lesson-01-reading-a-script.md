# Lesson 01 — Reading a Script: Acts, Scenes, Dialogue, Stage Directions

**Goal (student-readable):** I can read a dramatic scene aloud and explain
what the dialogue, stage directions, asides, and soliloquies each
contribute to the story.
**Time:** ~45 minutes · **Materials:** this page, two highlighter colors ·
**Answer-key reference:** [answer-key.md § L1](../answer-key.md#lesson-01)

**Readiness check (3 min):** In your own words, what is the difference
between reading a story and watching a play? Write one sentence. You will
revisit it at the end of the lesson.

## The parts of a script

A drama is built from four tools:

- **Dialogue** — the words characters say out loud to each other. This is
  the engine: nearly everything you learn in a play arrives through what
  people say.
- **Stage directions** — the instructions printed in italics or brackets
  (*[She slams the door]*). They tell actors where to go and what to do,
  and they tell readers what the dialogue alone cannot say: pauses, tone,
  actions.
- **Act / scene** — the big divisions. An *act* is a large section of the
  play; a *scene* is a smaller unit, usually marked by a change of place
  or by characters entering or leaving.
- **Aside / soliloquy** — special speeches. In an *aside*, a character
  speaks a few words the other characters cannot hear, but the audience
  can. A *soliloquy* is a longer speech delivered alone on stage (or
  believing oneself alone) — the character's honest inner thoughts. A
  *monologue* is a long speech to other characters, who do hear it.

Rule for this unit: **read a script twice** — once for what happens, once
for how the parts of the script make it happen.

## "The Stairwell" (original scene for this unit)

*A school stairwell after a storm. The lights flicker. MAYA, 13, holds a
clipboard and a flashlight. JAY, 13, dribbles a basketball that keeps
rolling away.*

MAYA: *[checking her clipboard]* Talent show lineup. Sound check in ten
minutes. Choir is in the gym, the magician is crying in the bathroom, and
you are here losing a basketball.

JAY: *[chasing the ball]* The ball is not lost. The ball is exploring.
There's a difference.

MAYA: The stage manager said "everyone in the gym." That was eleven
minutes ago. *[She shines the flashlight down the stairs]* And the
emergency lights are doing that horror-movie flicker, which means we
should not be in a stairwell during a storm.

JAY: *[aside]* She says "we" like she didn't volunteer to hunt me down.
*[to MAYA]* Okay, okay. But listen — what if I don't go to sound check?
What if I just… show up and play? No rehearsal, no nerves. Pure
basketball jazz.

MAYA: *[stops walking]* "Basketball jazz" is not a thing. *[She taps the
clipboard]* There is a list. The list has boxes. Sound check is a box.
You check the box, or the box checks you.

JAY: *[grinning]* That doesn't even mean anything.

MAYA: It means we are going to the gym. Now. *[The lights go out
completely. A beat of silence.]*

JAY: *[quietly, no longer grinning]* …Okay. Flashlight's yours.

MAYA: *[hands it over without a word, then]* Stay close. I know every
stair in this building.

*[Blackout.]*

## Worked example 1 — Reading the parts

**Question:** What does the stage direction *[aside]* change about Jay's
line "She says 'we' like she didn't volunteer to hunt me down"?

**Model answer:** The aside means Maya cannot hear the line — only the
audience can. So the audience learns Jay knows Maya came looking for him
on purpose, while Maya keeps acting like she is just doing her job. That
gap between what Maya knows and what the audience knows is the start of
*dramatic irony*, which we study in Lesson 3.

**Question:** What does *[Blackout.]* do as the scene's ending?

**Model answer:** A blackout ends the scene without dialogue, so the
meaning lands in the silence: Jay, the loud improviser, goes quiet and
admits he needs Maya's flashlight. The stage direction carries the
emotional turn — dialogue alone could not do it as cleanly.

## Worked example 2 — Dialogue as character evidence

**Question:** Which single line best shows that Maya is the organized one?

**Model answer:** "There is a list. The list has boxes. Sound check is a
box. You check the box, or the box checks you." The repeated word *list*
and *box*, plus the rule-like rhythm, show a character who thinks in
systems — and the last phrase is a joke delivered like a rule, which
tells us she is organized *and* a little funny about it. Cite it as
evidence: it is dialogue, not a stage direction, so the trait is revealed
through what she says, not what the script tells us to see.

## Guided practice (with a partner — read aloud)

Read "The Stairwell" aloud twice, switching roles. The second time,
follow the stage directions physically (chase the ball, shine the
flashlight, stop walking).

1. Highlight **dialogue** in one color and **stage directions** in the
   other. Which takes up more space on the page? Why do you think that
   is?
2. Jay's aside is the only aside in the scene. Rewrite it as ordinary
   dialogue Maya can hear. What changes about their relationship in that
   version?
3. Find one stage direction that tells you how a line should *sound*.
   Explain the difference between reading the line with and without the
   direction.

## Independent practice

In your notebook:

1. List the four script tools (dialogue, stage directions, act/scene,
   aside/soliloquy) and write a one-sentence definition of each in your
   own words.
2. Choose one line of Maya's dialogue and one stage direction about Maya.
   For each, write one sentence: what does it reveal about her that the
   other tool does not?
3. The scene never states where the school is or what the storm is like
   outside. Write two sentences explaining how the stage directions
   about the lights give you that information anyway.

## Application

Write a 4-line mini-scene between two characters of your choice that
includes **one aside** and **one stage direction that changes the meaning
of a line**. Label each tool in the margin.

## Exit check

1. In one sentence each, define *dialogue* and *stage direction*.
2. Why is Jay's line "She says 'we' like she didn't volunteer to hunt me
   down" funnier (or more interesting) as an aside than as dialogue?
3. Revisit your readiness-check sentence. Revise it in one sentence: what
   does a script give a reader that a story does not?

## Supports and extensions

- **Reading access:** Read the scene aloud with the adult first; the adult
  takes Maya, the learner takes Jay (fewer lines, more physical comedy).
- **Alternate response mode:** Answer guided practice orally while a
  partner highlights; perform the mini-scene instead of writing it.
- **Extension:** Mark where you would divide "The Stairwell" into two
  scenes. Justify the split — what changes (place, entrances, time) to
  earn a new scene heading?

## Teacher note

**Likely misconception:** learners treat stage directions as decoration
and skip them while reading. Insist on the second read-through *with*
the directions performed; the L1 exit check only works if they felt the
blackout. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md#lesson-01) for
read-aloud moves.
