# Teacher Guide — Unit 02: Drama, Foils, Adaptation

**For the adult.** Learner pages never include these notes. Every item
is solved in the [answer key](answer-key.md).

## Unit arc and readiness

Learners arrive able to cite evidence and track theme (U01). This unit
adds *performance literacy*: scripts are incomplete texts — they wait
for bodies, voices, and choices. The arc: read the machinery (L1) →
name the contrast (L2) → trace how lines move plot (L3) → compare
structures (L4) → judge adaptations (L5) → make one (project).

**Readiness signals (Session 20 is earned when):** the learner quotes
dialogue (not plot summary) to support claims; uses *Act.Scene*
citations and literary present tense without prompting; and can state
the critic's question for an adaptation from memory.

**Reteach triggers:** if the quiz shows plot-summary instead of
line-analysis (Q5/Q11 weak), rerun L3's three-job frame on a new
excerpt before the assessment. If foil claims ignore rule 3 (Q12 weak),
redo Practice B item 2 orally. If the rationale's evidence is
paraphrase-only, require one quoted line per choice before the pitch.

## Lesson notes

### Lesson 01 {#lesson-01}

- **Read-aloud moves:** first read straight through for plot; second
  read with directions *performed*. Assign the aside as a stage whisper
  to the audience — learners feel the irony physically.
- **Misconception:** skipping stage directions. Counter: cover the
  dialogue and ask what the directions alone tell (storm, flicker,
  blackout). The scene survives as mime — directions are text too.
- **Discussion seed:** "Who has more power in the blackout moment, and
  how do you know without any dialogue?"

### Lesson 02 {#lesson-02}

- **The three rules are the unit's spine.** Post them visibly for all
  four weeks. Every later foil claim (Laertes, Benvolio/Tybalt,
  Alba/Noa) gets tested aloud against them.
- **Misconception:** "different = foil." The Practice B item-2
  error-analysis (Maya vs. the basketball) is the vaccine; do it as a
  class debate.
- **Image use:** the generated stage illustration is an *observation*
  text. Insist learners cite visual details the way they cite lines
  ("the warm light on the left figure…"). The text-only alternative
  must be offered before the image to any learner using a screen
  reader — and it fully supports the activity.

### Lesson 03 {#lesson-03}

- **Pacing:** the excerpt is dense. Read it in roles, then re-read only
  the four "load-bearing" lines (Benvolio's warning, Tybalt's
  challenge, Romeo's refusal, Mercutio's insult). Analysis lives in
  those four.
- **Mature content:** two characters die in the scene's aftermath. The
  lesson summarizes rather than excerpts the deaths; preview and decide
  whether to read further in the Folger text.
- **Presentism watch:** learners may call Romeo cowardly for refusing.
  Redirect to the irony: the audience knows about the secret marriage
  (2.6). Judging without that knowledge is exactly Tybalt's error —
  say so out loud; it lands.

### Lesson 04 {#lesson-04}

- **Suicide content:** frame Hamlet's question as philosophy to
  *analyze*, never as a model. Language: "Hamlet is arguing with
  himself about whether suffering is bearable." If the learner is
  affected, pause the lesson — the alternate soliloquy (Juliet 3.2,
  "Gallop apace") teaches identical skills: apostrophe, imagery,
  impatience made audible.
- **Diction work:** do not let "it's old English" end the discussion.
  The word-help glosses remove the vocabulary barrier precisely so the
  analysis can start.
- **Laertes foil:** learners often know the plot beat (Laertes storms
  the castle, Act 4) without connecting it. Draw the two-column
  comparison on paper: same loss → opposite speed → what the contrast
  reveals about revenge.

### Lesson 05 {#lesson-05}

- **The critic's question** ("did the change serve the story — proved
  with evidence from text *and* production?") is the single sentence to
  memorize. Reject every "I liked it" verdict until it is rephrased.
- **Video logistics:** the RSC page hosts the staging videos; the
  Zeffirelli example is described, not screened, in-unit. If screening
  a film clip, preview for age fit — the 1968 film includes brief
  nudity, which is why the unit describes rather than assigns it.
- **Project launch:** end L5 with scene selection. Learners who choose
  "The Stairwell" adapt an original; those who choose Shakespeare adapt
  public-domain text. Both are equally rigorous — judge by the
  rationale, not the source's prestige.

## Project coaching

- **Common failure:** choices without evidence ("I set it in space
  because space is cool"). Require the sentence frame: "I [changed/
  kept] ___; this is [faithful/a departure]; it serves ___ , as shown
  when [character] says '[quote].'"
- **Pitch timing:** rehearse with a phone timer. Most learners run long
  on the evidence and drop the invitation — remind them the trailer
  needs all three parts.
- **Differentiation:** a learner who struggles with writing may deliver
  the rationale orally (recorded) and the scene as a performed reading;
  the rubric's evidence and choice criteria still apply.

## Answer-key conventions

The key gives *acceptable* answers, not the only answers. For open
responses it lists required elements (quote, rule, effect) and sample
wording. Accept any defensible alternative that meets the elements —
this is stated per item where it matters.
