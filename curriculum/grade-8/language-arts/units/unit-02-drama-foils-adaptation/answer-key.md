# Answer Key — Unit 02: Drama, Foils, Adaptation

**Teacher only.** Accept defensible alternatives that meet each item's
required elements (stated per item).

## Lesson 01 {#lesson-01}

**Guided practice**
1. Dialogue takes more space; plays are mostly people talking — the
   engine is speech, directions are the chassis. (Required: the
   observation + one reason.)
2. Sample: as dialogue, Maya hears Jay mock her ("volunteer to hunt me
   down") — it becomes teasing/conflict between them; as an aside it is
   Jay confiding in the audience, which keeps Maya's authority intact
   while letting us see through it. (Required: what changes for the
   relationship.)
3. Acceptable: *[quietly, no longer grinning]* before "…Okay.
   Flashlight's yours." — without it the line reads as casual; with it
   the line reads as a genuine admission. (Required: one direction +
   the with/without difference.)

**Independent practice**
1. Dialogue = characters' spoken words; stage directions = printed
   instructions for action/tone/movement; act = large play division;
   scene = smaller unit marked by place/entrance change; aside = short
   speech unheard by other characters but heard by audience; soliloquy =
   long alone-on-stage speech of inner thought. (Paraphrases accepted.)
2. Sample: Maya's line "Stay close. I know every stair in this
   building." reveals quiet competence and care; the direction *[She
   shines the flashlight down the stairs]* reveals she acts on the
   fear she voices — action confirming words. (Required: one of each +
   what each uniquely reveals.)
3. Sample: the flickering emergency lights and the blackout tell us the
   storm has knocked out power and the building is dark — setting and
   weather delivered without narration. (Required: two sentences tying
   directions to place/weather.)

**Application** — check: 4 lines, one labeled aside, one direction that
changes a line's meaning. Sample direction: *[smiling]* turning "I'm
fine" from reassurance into a lie.

**Exit check**
1. Dialogue: words characters speak to each other. Stage direction:
   printed instructions for staging (movement, tone, action).
2. As an aside the audience shares Jay's joke while Maya stays
   dignified — the humor comes from the gap; as dialogue it would just
   be bickering.
3. Sample revision: "A script gives the reader the performance
   instructions — dialogue plus the directions that tell actors (and
   readers) how it looks and sounds — so reading it is half reading, half
   directing."

## Lesson 02 {#lesson-02}

**Guided practice**
1. Sample: "The warm light contrasts with the cool light to reveal
   boldness versus caution." (Required: the frame completed with a
   specific contrast and a revealed trait.)
2. Sample pair: Maya "There is a list. The list has boxes." / Jay "Pure
   basketball jazz." — order versus improvisation, the same opposition
   as warm/cool light. (Required: one quoted line each.)
3. It fails rule 1 (no sustained deliberate pattern — the magician is a
   one-line joke, not a developed contrast) and rule 3 (they share no
   situation or pressure). (Required: two rules named with reasons.)

**Independent practice** — matrix sample: Approach to pressure:
Maya = systematize ("There is a list"); Jay = improvise ("show up and
play"). Speech style: clipped commands vs. run-on jokes (quotes as
above). Wants: Maya = everyone in the gym on time; Jay = freedom from
the schedule. Reveals: preparation and spontaneity need each other.
Paragraph must quote one line per character, use literary present
tense, and state the revealed theme. Deduct for plot summary without a
"reveals" sentence.

**Application** — accept any real pair tested against all three rules;
if it fails a rule, the learner must name the failed rule.

**Exit check**
1. A character built to contrast with another so both become clearer.
2. It violates rule 1 (and 3): "brave vs. wears black" is a single
   surface difference with no sustained pattern or shared pressure.
3. Sample: "The lantern prop contrasts with the raised arm — the still
   figure holds light quietly while the bold figure performs, creating
   a watcher-versus-performer contrast."

## Lesson 03 {#lesson-03}

**Guided practice**
1. Sample: Tybalt: "therefore turn and draw." It provokes Romeo's
   decision — refuse (and be shamed) or fight (and break his secret
   peace). (Required: line, decider, decision.) Also acceptable:
   Mercutio's "will you walk?" forcing Tybalt's answer.
2. "Here all eyes gaze on us" reveals Benvolio as the peacemaker who
   thinks about consequences and witnesses — it characterizes rather
   than changing the plot. (Required: the reveal named.)
3. Audience knows: (a) Romeo secretly married Juliet that morning —
   effect: suspense; (b) the street has already seen two brawls, so
   Benvolio's warning is loaded — effect: suspense/dread. (Required:
   two items + effect each.)

**Independent practice**
1. Three-job analyses must quote the line and give one reasoning
   sentence per claimed job. Strong answers pick Mercutio's insult or
   Tybalt's challenge and show all three jobs.
2. Correction must cite the irony: Romeo's "reason to love thee" refers
   to the secret marriage (2.6); refusing the fight protects Juliet's
   family, not cowardice — Tybalt mistakes restraint for fear, and the
   classmate repeats Tybalt's mistake. (Required: the marriage fact +
   one quoted line.)
3. Foil paragraph: Benvolio ("let's retire… reason coldly") vs. Tybalt
   (arrives seeking quarrel); shared hot street and feud; contrast
   sharpens the theme of honor vs. peace — or pride vs. restraint with
   defense. (Required: one quote each + named theme + defense.)

**Application** — "The Stairwell" turning point: accept "…Okay.
Flashlight's yours." (Jay's admission moves them out of the stalemate)
or the blackout direction. Three-job frame required. Irony: minimal —
Jay's aside gives the audience slight superior knowledge of his
respect for Maya; accept "little to none" with the aside cited.

**Exit check**
1. Propel the action; reveal character; provoke a decision.
2. The audience knows Romeo married Juliet (Tybalt's cousin) that
   morning, so his gentleness is loyalty, not cowardice — Tybalt can't
   see it.
3. Sample: Mercutio: "Tybalt, you ratcatcher, will you walk?" — it
   causes Tybalt to accept the duel, redirecting the fight onto
   Mercutio.

## Lesson 04 {#lesson-04}

**Guided practice**
1. Sample: the colons in "To die, to sleep; / To sleep: perchance to
   dream" sound like a mind catching itself mid-thought — each mark is
   a pause to reconsider. (Required: one punctuation example tied to
   thinking.)
2. Peaceful: "a consummation devoutly to be wish'd" / "to say we end /
   the heart-ache." Frightening: "perchance to dream: ay, there's the
   rub" / "the dread of something after death." The shift reveals a
   mind arguing both sides without resolution. (Required: one quote
   each + the shift named.)
3. Sample: as dialogue the friend would answer, comfort, or argue —
   the contradiction would get *resolved* socially; alone, it stands
   unresolved, which is precisely Hamlet's paralysis. (Required: what
   the friend's presence would change.)

**Independent practice**
1. Comparison paragraph must address two of: speakers, pace, learning,
   hidden truth; conclude with the lying-to-oneself judgment. Sample
   conclusion: the soliloquy better shows self-deception because there
   is no other voice to correct it — the audience watches the lie form.
2. Diction analyses: "mortal coil" — literal: turmoil of mortal life;
   figurative: life as tangled fuss to be "shuffled off"; reveals
   Hamlet sees living itself as the burden, not just his specific
   troubles. "The rub" — literal: a bowls-term for a deflecting patch;
   figurative: the obstacle that ruins the plan; reveals his fear that
   one unknown (dreams after death) wrecks the whole argument for
   dying. "Slings and arrows" — literal: weapons; figurative: random
   sufferings of fortune; reveals passivity as his first imagined
   option. (Required per phrase: literal + figurative + revealed view.)
3. Foil: shared situation = fathers murdered, honor demands revenge;
   contrast = Laertes acts instantly, Hamlet deliberates endlessly
   ("perchance to dream… must give us pause"); reveals the play's
   question — whether thought or action serves revenge, and what each
   destroys. (Required: all three rules addressed.)

**Application** — soliloquy 6–10 lines in Maya's or Jay's voice
revealing an unspoken thought; the two-sentence reflection must name
something the dialogue scene could not show (e.g., Maya admitting she
is scared too; Jay admitting the jokes cover nerves).

**Exit check**
1. A soliloquy is one character alone speaking inner thought; a
   dialogue scene is characters speaking to each other.
2. Peaceful: "a consummation devoutly to be wish'd" (or "to say we end
   the heart-ache"); frightening: "perchance to dream: ay, there's the
   rub" (or "the dread of something after death").
3. Laertes shares Hamlet's situation (murdered father, call to revenge)
   but acts immediately where Hamlet deliberates, so the sustained
   contrast reveals the theme of thought versus action.

## Lesson 05 {#lesson-05}

**Guided practice**
1. Accept either with defense. Sample (still): "stillness lets the
   name-argument land as philosophy — 'What's in a name?' needs quiet
   to sound like thinking, and her quoted logic ('nor hand, nor foot')
   is a list, best delivered planted." (Required: choice + one quoted
   phrase + reason.)
2. Sample: (a) whispered eagerly — Romeo is swept away, the aside
   becomes confession; (b) held back fearfully — Romeo is calculating
   the danger, the aside becomes strategy. Each tells the audience a
   different Romeo. (Required: two choices + what each reveals.)
3. Sample rewrite: "Judging by enjoyment ignores the craft question.
   The critic's question is whether the change serves the story: the
   film keeps Juliet's 'What's in a name?' speech word-for-word
   (faithful), so the test is whether the staging clarifies or
   weakens her argument that names are arbitrary."

**Independent practice**
1. Tracker: setting & design — e.g., "stone balcony, dawn-like light:
   departed from the text's night; serves the visual worship (he
   below, she above), costs the moon imagery." Pacing & cuts — "words
   nearly intact: faithful; serves the poetry, costs nothing but
   runtime." Body & voice — "actors play it tender, close: departed
   from a tenser reading; serves romance, costs the feud's danger."
   (Required: three rows, each with choice → faithful/departed →
   serve/cost.)
2. RSC column: accept any three accurately described differences from
   the RSC page/videos (e.g., the cast's discussed staging choices vs.
   Zeffirelli's literal balcony). If video was unavailable, accept
   differences drawn from the RSC page's written descriptions, noted
   as such.
3. Verdict paragraph: must cite one text line and one production
   choice; either verdict accepted if argued with evidence.

**Application** — director's rationale: three choices (one per row),
each with faithful/departed label and a served purpose. Sample:
"Setting: keep the stairwell but play it in full blackout with only
the flashlight (departed — the text implies flicker, not darkness);
it serves Jay's admission, as shown when he says '…Okay. Flashlight's
yours' — the dark makes his quietness physical."

**Exit check**
1. "Did the change serve the story — and can you prove it with
   evidence from both the text and the production?"
2. Faithful: keeping the dialogue nearly word-for-word. Departed: the
   stone balcony / dawn-like light against the text's night.
3. Enjoyment is not evidence; a judgment needs a cited line and an
   observed choice tied to what the story gains or loses.

## Practice sets {#practice}

**A.** 1. Where-to-go: *[She shines the flashlight down the stairs]*;
how-to-sound: *[quietly, no longer grinning]* (before "…Okay.
Flashlight's yours."). 2. Without the opening direction we lose place
(a school stairwell), time (after a storm), and the light situation —
the dialogue alone never states them. 3. The aside gives the audience
a confidence Jay hides from Maya; a silent thought gives the audience
nothing — the aside is how the play shares secrets structurally.
4. "Stay close…" = D — reveals competence/care through speech;
*[hands it over without a word, then]* = SD — reveals trust through
action, no words; *[The lights go out…]* = SD — ends the scene and
forces the emotional turn.

**B.** 1. Fifth row sample — Maya fears: chaos/losing control ("everyone
in the gym," the clipboard as shield). Jay fears: being ordinary or
trapped ("No rehearsal, no nerves" — performing without a net proves
he's alive). 2. Passes none fully: no deliberate pattern (rule 1 —
the ball is a prop gag), no revelation (rule 2 — it reveals nothing
about either), no shared situation (rule 3 — a ball faces no
pressure). 3–4. Require two quotes + a "reveals" sentence.
4. Prediction check: learners usually predict Mercutio = improviser,
Benvolio = planner — confirm after L3; note Mercutio complicates it
(his wit *is* a kind of planning).

**Vocabulary (S5).** 1–3. Check own-word definitions and the aside vs.
soliloquy "who hears it" rule. 4. *Foil* (noun): thin sheet of bright
metal placed behind a gem to increase its brilliance — the literary
term borrows exactly this: the foil character makes the other shine
clearer.

**C.** 1. Prevent-lines: Benvolio "let's retire" / "reason coldly of
your grievances, or else depart"; Romeo "be satisfied"; Romeo "put thy
sword up" (to Mercutio). 2. Point-of-no-return: accept Mercutio's
"Tybalt, you ratcatcher, will you walk?" — propels (creates the duel),
reveals (pride over peace), provokes (Tybalt must answer); after it,
refusal is impossible. 3. Sample: "Consort! what, dost thou make us
minstrels?" — *consort/minstrels/discords/fiddlestick* turn a death
threat into a music joke; the wordplay reveals Mercutio performs wit
even facing swords. 4. The audience's memory of two prior brawls makes
Benvolio's "we shall not 'scape a brawl" a promise, not a warning —
suspense comes from knowing he is right.

**D.** 1. Paraphrases: "Is it nobler to endure life's random blows?"
/ "To sleep — but then, to dream: ah, that's the problem." 2. A sea
cannot be fought — the image admits the second option is impossible,
so both choices collapse; "pile" would be merely large, not
unconquerable. Connotation: vast, drowning, indifferent. 3. The shift
lands at "To sleep: perchance to dream: ay, there's the rub" — death
stops being relief ("a consummation devoutly to be wish'd") and
becomes a gamble with unknown dreams. 4. Laertes storms the castle;
Hamlet, given the same loss, produces "must give us pause" — speed
versus deliberation, quoted.

**E.** 1. Faithful-must: Juliet above/Romeo below (the scene's
geometry); the name-argument kept (the scene's idea). Free-to-change:
century of costumes; whether the scene plays comic, tense, or tender.
2. The cut gains pace but loses the scene's philosophical core — the
rose image ("That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell
as sweet") *is* Juliet's argument that identity outlasts labels; cut
it and her rebellion becomes mere romance. 3. (a) Before: the audience
watches Romeo discover her — irony builds as she speaks unknowingly.
(b) After: his opening lines play as pure poetry first, the irony
landing as surprise when she appears. 4. Brainstorm: check the scene
choice and one defensible "excited choice."

**Vocabulary (S15).** 1. Format: *Rom.* 3.1 (or *Ham.* 3.1); cite one
line from each correctly. 2. "Shakespeare *shows*… *is* brave";
"Juliet *does* not know…" — rule: discuss literature in the present
tense because the text exists now, each reading. 3. Definitions +
sentences using *pacing* and *characterization* about the Zeffirelli
scene. 4. Proofreading: spot-check two corrected verbs.

**Synthesis block (S16).** 1. Ren/Kai: rule 1 — sustained pattern
(log-vs-spirit across the scene); rule 2 — reveals attitudes toward
rules and truth; rule 3 — same end-of-shift, same logbook. 2. Sample:
Ren "The log is the only witness we get" vs. Kai "The forms signed
themselves in spirit" — the contrast reveals the theme: whether truth
lives in the record or in the act, and the scene refuses to let either
win cleanly. 3. Turning line: Noa's "What if I told you I trimmed the
lamp at six-fifteen, not six?" — propels (forces Alba's choice),
reveals (honesty undercutting his clowning), provokes (Alba must
decide whether to correct the book).

## Week-9 quiz {#week-9-quiz}

1. Dialogue: characters' spoken words. Stage direction: printed
   staging instructions. (1 pt each.)
2. a–SD, b–D, c–AS, d–SO. (0.5 pt each.)
3. **b** and **c**. (1 pt each; 0 if more than two chosen.)
4. Jay's improvisation ("Pure basketball jazz") makes Maya's
   system-thinking visible *by contrast* — without his looseness her
   lists would read as mere bossiness; against him they read as care.
   (Required: the contrast mechanism, not just "they differ.")
5. Sample: Mercutio: "Tybalt, you ratcatcher, will you walk?" —
   Tybalt must decide to duel Mercutio instead of Romeo. (Required:
   quote + decider + decision.)
6. The audience knows Romeo married Juliet that morning (2.6); Tybalt
   hears only a stranger's inexplicable gentleness. (Required: the
   marriage fact.)
7. A dialogue scene hides secrets socially (characters perform for
   each other); a soliloquy removes the audience of peers, so the
   secret is spoken honestly to the audience. (Required: the
   structural difference.)
8. Peaceful: "a consummation devoutly to be wish'd"; frightening:
   "perchance to dream: ay, there's the rub." (1 pt each.)
9. A sea cannot be fought — the image makes the "fight back" option
   impossible, which is the point: both options collapse. (Required:
   the impossibility insight.)
10. Adaptation: moving a story from one medium to another. Critic's
    question: "Did the change serve the story — proved with evidence
    from text and production?" (Paraphrases accepted.)
11. Departed. Sample: it serves the sun imagery ("It is the east, and
    Juliet is the sun") by literalizing dawn, but costs the night
    logic of "the envious moon." Either verdict with line + reasoning
    earns full credit.
12. Missing: the shared situation/pressure and the sustained,
    revealing contrast. Supply: both are young men whose fathers were
    murdered and honor demands revenge; Laertes charges the castle
    while Hamlet soliloquizes — the contrast reveals the play's
    thought-versus-action theme. (Required: the missing rule named +
    the supplied contrast.)

## Unit assessment {#unit-assessment}

**Part A.** 1. Sample: *[stops polishing]* before "Then the book would
be wrong…" — without it the line reads as calm principle; with it the
line lands as alarm: the hands stopping is the feeling. Also
acceptable: *[A long pause]* before "Why are you telling me this?"
2. The aside tells the audience Noa confessed *because* Alba gets angry
"at the right thing" — admiration he never voices to her; his spoken
dialogue offers only the practical reason. (Required: the hidden
motive.)

**Part B.** 3. Rules: (1) sustained pattern — Alba's every line defends
the log, Noa's every line teases it; (2) reveals — their stances on
rules, truth, and care; (3) shared — same gallery, same logbook, same
dusk shift. Quote at least one line (e.g., "The log is the only witness
we get" / "The forms signed themselves in spirit"). 4. Paragraph must:
name the theme (truth lives in records vs. in acts), quote one line per
character, use literary present tense, and end with what the contrast
reveals — sample close: "Alba's rigor makes Noa's honesty possible (he
confesses *to her*), and Noa's confession makes Alba's rigor human (she
corrects the book rather than punishing him); the scene argues that
records matter because people choose to make them true."

**Part C.** 5. Accept: "What if I told you I trimmed the lamp at
six-fifteen, not six?" — propels (Alba must respond to a false record),
reveals (Noa's clowning covers a conscience), provokes (Alba's decision
to correct the line herself). Three-job frame required.

**Part D.** 6. Minimal irony: the audience hears Noa's aside, so we know
his admiration before Alba does — small suspense (will she live up to
it?). Accept "no full dramatic irony" answers that correctly state what
would be needed (e.g., the audience knowing the lamp was never trimmed
at all while Alba trusts the book).

**Part E.** 7. Loud delivery: departed from the scene's quiet,
deliberate tone ("*[A long pause]*," "without looking up"); costs the
scene its intimacy — the confession "if I don't say it now, I'll write
it down clean tomorrow" needs quiet to land as conscience rather than
melodrama. Cutting the last two lines: departed; costs the theme —
"Everything we tend is alive" resolves the log/truth argument, and
without it the scene ends on procedure instead of meaning. (Required
per choice: faithful/departed + serve/cost + one quoted line.)

**Part F.** 8. Soliloquy 4 lines in Noa's voice; reflection must name
something dialogue couldn't show (e.g., whether he will keep telling
the truth when Alba isn't there). Check: the thought is genuinely
unspoken-able, not just narration.
