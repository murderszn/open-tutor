# Lesson 03 — Active and Passive Voice: A Choice, Not a Rule

**Goal:** you will identify active and passive voice, rewrite
sentences in both directions, and justify the choice by its effect.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook.
**Readiness check:** name the subject and the verb in "The storm
damaged the roof." (Subject: storm; verb: damaged.)

## What voice is

**Voice** tells you whether the subject of a sentence performs the
action or receives it.

- **Active voice:** the subject does the action.
  "The committee approved the plan." (Who approved? The committee
  — the subject — did it.)
- **Passive voice:** the subject receives the action. The verb is a
  form of *be* plus a past participle, and the doer — called the
  **agent** — appears in a *by* phrase or not at all.
  "The plan was approved by the committee." (The subject, *plan*,
  receives the approval.)

**The "who did it?" test.** Ask who or what performed the action.
If the answer is the subject, the sentence is active. If the
answer is someone else — or nobody named — the sentence is
passive.

## Worked example 1 — the same facts, different spotlight

> Active: "Mara painted the fence."
> Passive: "The fence was painted by Mara."

The facts are identical. What changes is emphasis: the active
version spotlights Mara, the doer; the passive version spotlights
the fence, the receiver. A writer who wants the reader to notice
the finished fence chooses passive; a writer who wants the reader
to notice Mara's work chooses active.

Rewriting active → passive, step by step: (1) move the object
(*the fence*) into the subject slot; (2) use *be* + past
participle (*was painted*); (3) move the old subject into a *by*
phrase (*by Mara*). Reverse the steps to go passive → active.

## Worked example 2 — when passive earns its place

Passive voice is not "wrong." It earns its place when:

1. **The receiver matters more than the doer.** "The winners will
   be announced at noon." The news is about the winners, not about
   who announces them.
2. **The doer is unknown or irrelevant.** "The fence was painted
   before noon." If nobody knows or cares who painted it, naming a
   doer ("Someone painted the fence") adds nothing.
3. **The writer wants a formal, impersonal tone.** Lab and
   instruction writing often does this: "The samples were measured
   twice." (Use sparingly — see the warning below.)

**The warning — passive can hide the actor.** "Mistakes were
made." Who made them? The sentence refuses to say. That is why
Unit 03's argument work matters here: when a writer hides the
actor, a careful reader asks why. In an argument, "The evidence
was ignored" should make you ask: ignored by whom? If the answer
matters and the sentence hides it, rewrite in active voice.

**Passive is not past tense.** "Mara was painting the fence" is
past progressive *active* voice — Mara is still the doer. The test
is who acts, never the tense.

## Guided practice

1. Identify the voice of each sentence and name the doer (or note
   that no doer is named):
   a. "The librarian shelved the books."
   b. "The books were shelved before lunch."
   c. "A storm damaged the roof."
   d. "The roof was damaged by a storm."
2. Rewrite sentence (b) in active voice. What, if anything, is
   lost or gained?
3. Rewrite sentence (c) in passive voice. Which version emphasizes
   the storm, and which emphasizes the roof?

Lesson 03 guided practice.)*

## Independent practice

1. Identify the voice of each sentence:
   a. "Scientists measured the rainfall."
   b. "The rainfall was measured every morning."
   c. "The principal announced the winners."
   d. "Mistakes were made."
2. Rewrite (b) and (c) in the opposite voice. For each rewrite,
   write one sentence stating what the new version emphasizes.
3. Error analysis: a classmate says, "Passive voice is always bad
   writing, so I changed 'The winners will be announced at noon'
   to 'The principal will announce the winners at noon.'" Is the
   classmate right? Explain, using the three earned-place cases
   above, and say whether the rewrite is an improvement.

## Applied task — voice in your own draft

Take one paragraph of your own earlier writing (or the paragraph
in Practice C if no draft is on hand). Find one sentence where
passive voice hides an actor who should be named, and rewrite it
in active voice. Find one sentence where passive voice is the
better choice, and write one sentence defending it. If your
paragraph has no passive sentences, write one sentence of each
voice about the same fact and defend the choice you prefer.

## Exit check

"The fence was painted by Mara." Active or passive? Who is the
agent, and what does this version emphasize that "Mara painted the
fence" does not? Then name one situation in which you would
deliberately choose passive voice.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if the *be* + past participle pattern is hard to
  spot, try inserting "by zombies" after the verb: "The fence was
  painted by zombies" makes sense, so it is passive. ("Mara painted
  by zombies the fence" does not, so the original is active.)
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may mark voice by
  moving word cards (subject / verb / object) into active and
  passive orders instead of rewriting whole sentences.
- **Extension:** find three passive sentences in a news article or
  school announcement (adult-approved source), and for each, write
  whether the passive hides an actor who matters — and what the
  active version would reveal.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "passive = weak, always rewrite." The
extension and the error-analysis task exist to break that rule
into a judgment: voice is a choice with an effect, and the effect
is what gets graded. Also watch for "was painting" mislabeled as
passive — return to the "who did it?" test, not the *was*. See
the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
