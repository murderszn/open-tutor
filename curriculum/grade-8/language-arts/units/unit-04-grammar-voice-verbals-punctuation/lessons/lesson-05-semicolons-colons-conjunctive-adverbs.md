# Lesson 05 — Punctuation for Clarity: Semicolons, Colons, Conjunctive Adverbs

**Goal:** you will punctuate semicolons, colons, and conjunctive
adverbs correctly, and catch capitalization and commonly confused
word errors in an editing pass.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook.
**Readiness check:** which of these is an independent clause — a
group of words that can stand alone as a sentence? "Because the
rain stopped" or "The rain stopped." (The second. The first is a
dependent clause and cannot stand alone.)

## The three tools

**Semicolon (;)** — joins two independent clauses that are closely
related, with no conjunction between them:

> "The garden was dry; the tomatoes still grew."

Test before using a semicolon: each side must be able to stand
alone as a sentence. "The garden was dry; because it had not
rained" fails the test — the second side cannot stand alone — so
it is an error.

**Colon (:)** — introduces a list, an explanation, or a quotation,
and it must follow an **independent clause** (a complete thought):

> "Bring three things: gloves, water, and seeds."
> "Mara had one goal: to finish before noon."

Test: cover everything after the colon. What remains must be a
complete sentence. "She bought: gloves and seeds" fails — "She
bought" is not complete — so the colon must go: "She bought
gloves and seeds."

**Conjunctive adverb** — a word like *however, therefore,
meanwhile, consequently, furthermore, nevertheless* that connects
two independent clauses and shows their relationship. The pattern
is exact: **semicolon before, comma after** when the adverb joins
two clauses in one sentence:

> "The rain stopped; however, the soil stayed wet."

At the start of a sentence, the same word takes only a comma:
"However, the soil stayed wet." A comma alone between two clauses
with *however* is a **comma splice** — two sentences wrongly
joined by a comma:

> Faulty: "The rain stopped, however the soil stayed wet."
> Repair: "The rain stopped; however, the soil stayed wet."

## Worked example 1 — three repairs, three rules

> Faulty: "Mara planted carrots, they grew quickly."
> Repair: "Mara planted carrots; they grew quickly."
> Reason: two independent clauses joined by a comma alone is a
> comma splice; a semicolon joins them correctly.

> Faulty: "She packed: a hat, sunscreen, and water."
> Repair: "She packed a hat, sunscreen, and water."
> Reason: the colon follows "She packed," which is not a complete
> thought. (Correct colon version: "She packed three things: a
> hat, sunscreen, and water.")

> Faulty: "The harvest was large, therefore, we shared it."
> Repair: "The harvest was large; therefore, we shared it."
> Reason: a conjunctive adverb joining two clauses needs a
> semicolon before it and a comma after it.

## Worked example 2 — capitalization and confused words

An editing pass also catches the small errors that change meaning:

- **Capitalization:** the first word of a sentence, the pronoun
  *I*, and proper names are capitalized. "the garden needed water"
  must start "The garden…"
- **Their / there / they're:** *their* = belonging to them ("their
  garden"); *there* = a place ("put it there"); *they're* = they
  are ("they're coming").
- **Its / it's:** *its* = belonging to it ("its leaves"); *it's*
  = it is ("it's raining"). The apostrophe test: if you can
  replace the word with "it is," use *it's*.
- **Your / you're:** *your* = belonging to you; *you're* = you
  are. Same apostrophe test.
- **To / too / two:** *to* = direction or infinitive; *too* =
  also or excessively; *two* = the number 2.

## Guided practice

Repair each sentence, and name the rule used:

1. "The soil was dry, however the tomatoes survived."
2. "We need: shovels and gloves."
3. "Their are three beds to water."
4. "Its time to water the tomatoes."
5. "Jon finished his work, he went home."
6. "The team won, therefore, they celebrated."

## Independent practice

1. Repair each sentence:
   a. "Bring: pencils, paper, and erasers."
   b. "It rained all morning, the game was canceled."
   c. "Jon is tired, nevertheless, he finished."
   d. "We need two tools a shovel and a rake."
   e. "Your going to love this book."
   f. "The team won, they celebrated."
2. Explain in two or three sentences why a semicolon cannot join
   an independent clause to a dependent clause, using one example
   of your own.
3. Write three sentences of your own: one using a semicolon
   correctly, one using a colon correctly, and one using a
   conjunctive adverb with the semicolon-comma pattern. Underline
   the punctuation mark in each.

## Exit check

Write the rule for conjunctive adverbs joining two clauses, in
your own words, and punctuate this sentence correctly: "Mara was
tired however she finished the chapter."

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** keep two tests on a card: "Can each side of the
  semicolon stand alone?" and "Is the part before the colon a
  complete sentence?" Run both tests before finalizing any
  semicolon or colon.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may repair sentences
  by moving punctuation cards into place and reading the repaired
  sentence aloud, instead of rewriting.
- **Extension:** take a paragraph from an adult-approved article,
  remove its semicolons, colons, and conjunctive-adverb
  punctuation, and repunctuate it from scratch; then compare with
  the original and explain any place where your punctuation differs
  but is still correct.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "a semicolon is a fancy comma." It is not —
it joins only independent clauses, and the stand-alone test is the
antidote. Also watch for colon overuse after verbs and
prepositions ("She packed:" / "such as:"); the complete-thought
test catches both. The applied-task paragraph's "Saturday" also
tests capitalization of days of the week — name it explicitly in
review. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
