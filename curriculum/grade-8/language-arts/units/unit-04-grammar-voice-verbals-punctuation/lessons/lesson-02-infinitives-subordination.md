# Lesson 02 — Infinitives, Verbal Phrases, and Subordination

**Goal:** you will identify infinitives and their jobs, tell an
infinitive from a prepositional phrase beginning with *to*, and use
subordination to show how ideas relate.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook; the garden-scene
illustration from Lesson 01.
**Readiness check:** in "Swimming builds stamina," what job does
*swimming* do? (Gerund, subject — Lesson 01.)

## The third verbal: the infinitive

An **infinitive** is the word *to* plus the base form of a verb:
*to plant, to read, to build*. Like the other verbals, it can do
three jobs:

- **Noun:** "To plant a garden takes patience." (Subject — what
  takes patience? To plant a garden.)
- **Adjective:** "Mara bought seeds to plant." (Which seeds? The
  seeds to plant — the infinitive describes *seeds*.)
- **Adverb:** "Mara went to the market to buy seeds." (Why did she
  go? To buy seeds — the infinitive explains the purpose of *went*.)

An **infinitive phrase** is the infinitive plus its objects or
modifiers: "to plant a garden in the spring."

**Infinitive or prepositional phrase?** The word *to* starts both,
so check what follows it. If *to* is followed by a **verb**, you
have an infinitive ("to plant"). If *to* is followed by a **noun or
pronoun**, you have a prepositional phrase ("to the shed," "to
her"). Try it: "Mara walked to the shed to fetch a spade." The
first *to* (to the shed) is a preposition; the second (to fetch)
is an infinitive of purpose.

## Subordination: showing how ideas relate

Two short sentences can be joined so that one idea becomes
**subordinate** — less important, and clearly related — to the
other. A **subordinate clause** begins with a subordinating word
(*after, although, because, when, while, if, since*) and cannot
stand alone as a sentence. The main clause carries the emphasis.

## Worked example 1 — three infinitive jobs

> "To succeed takes practice." — *To succeed* is the subject, so
> it is an infinitive doing a noun's job. Test: replace it with a
> noun — "Practice takes practice" is awkward, but "Success takes
> practice" works, and the slot is a subject slot.
>
> "Mara has a book to lend." — *to lend* describes *book* (which
> book? The book to lend), so it is an infinitive doing an
> adjective's job.
>
> "Jon stayed after school to practice." — *to practice* explains
> why Jon stayed, so it is an infinitive doing an adverb's job
> (purpose).

## Worked example 2 — combining by subordination

Short sentences: "The rain stopped. We planted the seeds."

Step 1: Decide which idea matters most. The planting is the main
event; the rain stopping is the background. Step 2: Make the
background idea subordinate with *after*: "After the rain stopped,
we planted the seeds." Step 3: Check the meaning. Nothing changed
except emphasis: the sentence now leads with the timing and lands
on the planting. Moving the subordinate clause changes emphasis
again: "We planted the seeds after the rain stopped" puts the
planting first. Both are correct; the writer chooses the emphasis.

A second pattern uses a verbal phrase instead of a clause:
"Watering the tomatoes every morning, the child kept them alive."
The participial phrase gives background; the main clause gives the
result. Check the dangling trap from Lesson 01: the phrase must
describe the subject — here *the child* is the one watering, so
the sentence is sound. "Watering the tomatoes every morning, the
tomatoes stayed alive" would dangle, because tomatoes do not water
themselves.

## Guided practice

1. Label each infinitive's job (noun / adjective / adverb):
   a. "To read widely is Mara's goal."
   b. "Jon needs a quiet place to study."
   c. "Mara rose early to water the garden."
2. Infinitive or prepositional phrase?
   a. "She gave the book to Jon."
   b. "She stopped to read the sign."
3. Combine by subordination, making the first idea subordinate:
   "Jon was tired. He finished the chapter."

## Independent practice

1. Label the job of each infinitive:
   a. "To win the race, Mara trained all winter." (Hint: the phrase
      comes first and explains purpose — whose purpose? Mara's
      training.)
   b. "His plan to build a shed took a month."
   c. "Mara wants to learn carpentry."
   d. "She walked to the store to buy paint." (Two *to* phrases —
      label each.)
2. Combine each pair by subordination. Name the subordinating word
   you used and state which idea you made main:
   a. "The soil was dry. The plants survived."
   b. "Mara checked the forecast. She planted the seeds."
3. Fix the dangling phrase, in two different ways: "Watering the
   garden, the hose leaked all over Mara's shoes." (Hint: who was
   watering? The sentence never says — so one repair must name
   the waterer.)

## Applied task — the garden scene again

Use the garden-scene illustration (Lesson 01, or its text
alternative) and write three sentences, each factually true of the
picture: one containing a gerund, one containing a participial
phrase, and one containing an infinitive. Underline the verbal in
each and label its job. Then combine any two of your three
sentences into one sentence by subordination, without changing
the facts.

## Exit check

"To the store" in "Mara walked to the store" — infinitive or
prepositional phrase? Prove it by naming what follows *to*.
Then combine "The bell rang. The class began" by subordination
in a way that emphasizes the class beginning.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** cover the *to* phrase and ask, "verb or noun next?"
  That one question settles almost every infinitive-vs.-preposition
  mix-up.
- **Alternate response mode:** for the combining tasks, the learner
  may dictate the combined sentence and point to the subordinate
  part instead of writing it.
- **Extension:** rewrite one garden-scene paragraph three ways —
  once leaning on gerunds ("Watering… Painting… Carrying…"), once
  on participles, once on infinitives — and write two sentences
  comparing their effects. Which version sounds most like a list
  of actions, and which sounds most like a description?

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "to + anything = infinitive." Drill the
verb-or-noun test until it is automatic. Also watch for learners
who subordinate everything: a paragraph of nothing but *although*
and *because* clauses buries its main ideas. The applied task's
final combination step is the place to teach restraint — combine
where the relationship matters, and leave strong short sentences
alone. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
