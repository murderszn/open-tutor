# Lesson 06 — The Style Editing Pass: Choosing on Purpose

**Goal:** you will run a full editing pass on a draft — diagnose
verbal, voice, mood, and punctuation faults, revise them, and
explain each choice for purpose and audience.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook; a draft of your
own writing (or the practice paragraph below).
**Readiness check:** name one tool from each earlier lesson:
a verbal, a voice choice, a mood, and a punctuation mark. (For
example: gerund; passive voice; subjunctive; semicolon.)

## The four-step pass

Good editors do not fix everything at once. They make four passes,
one question at a time:

1. **Verbals pass — "What job is each verbal doing?"** Circle
   every gerund, participle, and infinitive. Check each *-ing*
   word with the replacement test, and check every opening
   participial phrase for the dangling trap: does it describe the
   subject that follows?
2. **Voice pass — "Who is doing the action, and should the reader
   know?"** Underline every passive verb. For each one, decide:
   does the receiver matter more, is the doer unknown, or is an
   actor being hidden who should be named? Rewrite the hidden-
   actor cases in active voice; defend the rest.
3. **Mood pass — "Fact, command, question, possibility, or wish?"**
   Check every *were* in a wish or contrary-to-fact sentence, and
   every verb after *demand / require / suggest / recommend +
   that*. Check that each conditional sentence matches its parts
   (real possibility with *will*; contrary-to-fact with *would*).
4. **Punctuation pass — "Can each side stand alone? Is the part
   before the colon complete?"** Run the two tests from Lesson 05
   on every semicolon and colon, fix every comma splice, and run
   the apostrophe test on every *its / it's*, *their / there /
   they're*, and *your / you're*.

After the four passes, read the paragraph aloud. If a sentence
still sounds tangled, it usually contains one more fault from
steps 1–4 — find it before rewriting the whole sentence.

## Worked example — one paragraph, four passes

Flawed paragraph:

> "Running to the shed, the rain soaked Mara. The fence was
> painted by Jon, it looked new. If Jon was taller, he would
> reach the top rail. The club requires that every member brings
> gloves, the meeting starts at noon, however Mara arrived late."

Pass 1 (verbals): "Running to the shed" dangles — the rain does
not run. Repair: "Running to the shed, Mara was soaked by the
rain."
Pass 2 (voice): "The fence was painted by Jon" is passive with a
named agent and no reason to spotlight the fence, and it is also
part of a comma splice. Repair in active voice: "Jon painted the
fence, and it looked new." (The *and* now joins the clauses
correctly.)
Pass 3 (mood): "If Jon was taller" is contrary to fact, so it
needs *were*: "If Jon were taller, he would reach the top rail."
"The club requires that every member brings gloves" needs the
subjunctive base form: "…that every member bring gloves."
Pass 4 (punctuation): "the meeting starts at noon, however Mara
arrived late" is a comma splice with a conjunctive adverb.
Repair: "The meeting starts at noon; however, Mara arrived late."

Revised paragraph:

> "Running to the shed, Mara was soaked by the rain. Jon painted
> the fence, and it looked new. If Jon were taller, he would reach
> the top rail. The club requires that every member bring gloves.
> The meeting starts at noon; however, Mara arrived late."

Every change has a reason, and every reason names a tool. That is
the standard for this unit's project and assessment: a revision
without a reason is only half an answer.

## Guided practice

Run the four-step pass on this paragraph. List each fault by step
number, repair it, and give the reason in one phrase:

> "To win is Mara's goal, she practices daily. The trophy was won
> by Mara, she smiled. If Mara was faster, she would win. Their
> going to celebrate, its going to be fun."

Lesson 06 guided practice.)*

## Independent practice

1. Run the four-step pass on this paragraph. There are six faults
   to find:
   > "Swimming is fun, Mara loves it. The pool was cleaned by the
   > caretaker before noon, the water was clear. If it will rain,
   > the meet would move inside. The coach demands that Mara
   > practices every morning, she arrives early."
2. Take one paragraph of your own earlier writing and run the
   four-step pass on it. Write the revised paragraph, then list
   your changes with a reason for each. If you made no changes in
   some step, write one sentence saying what you checked and why
   it was already correct — "no change" is an answer only when
   the check is named.
3. Explain in three or four sentences why editing in four passes
   catches more faults than reading a draft once and fixing
   whatever looks wrong. Use one fault from task 1 as your
   example.

## Applied task — revise for a real audience

Choose an audience (a teacher, a club newsletter, or a friend)
and revise the Lesson 06 guided-practice paragraph for that
audience in two versions: one formal (active voice where actors
matter, subjunctive where rules are stated, semicolons where
ideas are equal) and one informal (shorter sentences, fewer
subordinate clauses). Write two sentences explaining one choice
that differs between the versions and why the audience justifies
it.

## Exit check

Name the four passes in order, and state the one test or question
that defines each pass.

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** use the four passes as a checklist on paper and
  tick each step off per paragraph; do not move to the next pass
  until the current one is complete.
- **Alternate response mode:** the learner may dictate revisions
  and reasons while the adult scribes, or mark faults with colored
  pencils (one color per pass) instead of writing a fault list.
- **Extension:** edit the same paragraph twice — once for maximum
  formality and once for maximum punch (short active sentences,
  strong verbs) — and write a paragraph comparing what each
  version gains and loses.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "editing means fixing spelling." This
lesson reframes editing as decision-making, and the project rubric
grades reasons, not just corrections. Watch for learners who
rewrite every passive sentence: the voice pass asks for a defense
of kept passives, and a kept passive with a good reason earns
full credit. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
