# Lesson 01 — Gerunds and Participles: Two Jobs for *-ing* Words

**Goal:** you will tell a gerund from a participle by testing the job
the *-ing* word does in its sentence — not by how it looks.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook, two colored pencils.
**Readiness check:** name the subject and the main verb in "Mara
watered the tomatoes." (Subject: Mara; main verb: watered.)

## What is a verbal?

A **verbal** is a verb form that stops acting as the main verb of its
sentence and takes a different job instead. This lesson covers two
kinds of verbal. Both often end in *-ing*, which is exactly why they
get mixed up. The test is never the ending. The test is the job.

- A **gerund** is an *-ing* verb form doing a **noun's job**: it can
  be a subject, an object, or the object of a preposition.
- A **participle** is a verb form doing an **adjective's job**: it
  describes a noun or pronoun. Present participles end in *-ing*;
  past participles usually end in *-ed*, *-en*, or *-d* (broken,
  chosen, baked).

A **gerund phrase** is a gerund plus its objects or modifiers
("Swimming laps every morning"). A **participial phrase** is a
participle plus its objects or modifiers ("Swimming laps every
morning, Mara grew stronger" — here the phrase describes Mara).

**The replacement test.** Cover the *-ing* word and try a plain noun
in its place. If a noun fits and the sentence still makes sense, the
word is doing a noun's job — it is a gerund. If a noun does *not*
fit, but an adjective does ("the happy child"), the word is doing an
adjective's job — it is a participle.

## Worked example 1 — the same word, two jobs

> "Swimming builds stamina."

Step 1: Find the *-ing* word: *swimming*. Step 2: What job does it
do? It is the subject — the thing that builds stamina. Step 3:
Replacement test: "Exercise builds stamina" makes sense, and
"exercise" is a noun. So *swimming* is a **gerund** (subject).

> "The swimming child waved at us."

Step 1: The *-ing* word is *swimming*. Step 2: What job does it do?
It describes *child* — which child? The swimming one. Step 3:
Replacement test: "Exercise child waved at us" makes no sense, but
"the happy child" does. So *swimming* is a **participle**
(describing *child*).

Notice: the two sentences use the identical word, and only the job
tells them apart. That is the whole lesson in one pair.

## Worked example 2 — gerund phrases and participial phrases

> "Mara enjoys reading at night."

*Reading* is the object of *enjoys* — Mara enjoys *what?* Reading.
A noun fits ("Mara enjoys music"), so *reading* is a **gerund**, and
"reading at night" is a gerund phrase acting as the object.

> "Reading the map, Mara found the trail."

Here "reading the map" describes *Mara* — it tells us what Mara was
doing when she found the trail. A noun does not fit in that slot
("Music the map, Mara found the trail" is nonsense). So *reading*
is a **participle**, and "reading the map" is a participial phrase
modifying *Mara*.

**Watch out — the dangling trap.** A participial phrase at the start
of a sentence must describe the subject that follows it. "Running
to the shed, the rain soaked Mara" is faulty: the phrase seems to
describe *the rain*, but rain does not run to sheds. The repair names
the real doer: "Running to the shed, Mara was soaked by the rain."
Lesson 02 returns to this trap; for now, always ask: *who or what is
doing the -ing action?*

## Guided practice

Label each *-ing* word **G** (gerund) or **P** (participle) and name
its job in one phrase. Say the replacement test aloud for each.

1. "Hiking is Mara's favorite weekend activity."
2. "The hiking group left at dawn."
3. "Jon tried hiking for the first time."
4. "Hiking through the woods, Jon spotted a fox."

Lesson 01 guided practice.)*

## Independent practice

Label each *-ing* word and name its job. Then write three sentences
of your own: one with a gerund as subject, one with a gerund as an
object, and one with a participial phrase at the start.

1. "Painting the fence took all afternoon."
2. "The painting teenager waved at her neighbor." (Hint: which noun
   does *painting* describe?)
3. "Mara finished painting before lunch."
4. "Painting in the heat, Mara drank a full bottle of water."
5. "His coach praised his swimming."
6. "The swimming pool opens at noon." (Hint: does *swimming* describe
   the pool, or name the pool's kind? Ask what job it does — it
   tells what kind of pool it is, so it works like an adjective
   here.)

## Exit check

Answer in one sentence each: In "Running water flooded the path,"
is *running* a gerund or a participle, and how does the replacement
test prove it? In "Running is good exercise," is *running* a
gerund or a participle, and how does the test prove it?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if labeling feels shaky, underline the noun the
  *-ing* word might describe first. If you can point to that noun,
  you are probably looking at a participle.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort the guided-practice sentences
  into two columns (gerund / participle) instead of writing labels
  in sentences; the adult checks the sort and asks for the job of
  two items.
- **Extension:** rewrite sentence 4 of the guided practice twice —
  once with the participial phrase moved to the end ("Jon spotted a
  fox, hiking through the woods" — check: does it still clearly
  describe Jon?) and once as a gerund sentence — and explain which
  version is clearest and why.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "every *-ing* word is a gerund." The
replacement test is the cure, and it must be said aloud, not just
thought. Note item 6 of independent practice: *swimming* in
"swimming pool" is a borderline classroom case; accept "participle
used like an adjective (it tells what kind of pool)" and note that
some grammars call it a noun modifier — the reasoning, not the
label alone, earns full credit. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
