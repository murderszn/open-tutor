# Answer Key — Unit 04 (Teacher Only)

Do not hand this file to the learner. Where an open answer allows
variation, the key states the reasoning that earns credit; accept
any answer that meets that reasoning.

## Lesson 01 — Gerunds and participles

**Guided practice:** 1. G — subject ("Hiking is…" names the
activity that is the favorite). 2. P — describes *group*. 3. G —
object of *tried* (Jon tried *what?* Hiking). 4. P phrase —
describes *Jon* (Jon is the one hiking).

**Independent practice:** 1. G — subject ("Painting the fence"
is what took all afternoon). 2. P — describes *teenager*.
3. G — object of *finished*. 4. P phrase — describes *Mara*.
5. G — object of *praised* (the possessive *his* before it also
signals a noun job). 6. Participle used as an adjective — it
tells what kind of pool. (Some grammars call this a noun
modifier; accept either label when the adjective reasoning is
stated.)

**Applied task:** accept any two sentences factually true of the
garden picture, one with a correctly labeled gerund and one with
a correctly labeled participial phrase.

**Exit check:** In "Running water flooded the path," *running*
is a participle: the noun test fails ("Exercise water" is
nonsense) while an adjective fits ("cold water"). In "Running is
good exercise," *running* is a gerund: it is the subject, and a
noun fits ("Exercise is good exercise").

## Lesson 02 — Infinitives and subordination

**Guided practice:** 1a. Noun — subject ("To read widely is…").
1b. Adjective — describes *place* (which place? The place to
study). 1c. Adverb — purpose (why did Mara rise early? To water
the garden). 2a. Prepositional phrase (*to* + noun *Jon*).
2b. Infinitive (*to* + verb *read*). 3. "Although Jon was tired,
he finished the chapter." Main idea: he finished the chapter;
subordinate idea: he was tired. ("Jon finished the chapter
although he was tired" is also correct, with the emphasis
reversed.)

**Independent practice:** 1a. Adverb — purpose (why did Mara
train? To win the race). 1b. Adjective — describes *plan*.
1c. Noun — object of *wants*. 1d. "To the store" is a
prepositional phrase (*to* + noun); "to buy paint" is an
infinitive doing an adverb's job (purpose). 2a. "Although the
soil was dry, the plants survived." (Main: survived.) 2b. "After
Mara checked the forecast, she planted the seeds." (Main:
planted.) Accept any correct subordination that preserves the
facts. 3. The original dangles: it implies the hose did the
watering, and no waterer is named. Acceptable repairs name a
waterer, e.g. "While Mara was watering the garden, the hose
leaked all over her shoes" or "As Jon watered the garden, the
hose leaked all over Mara's shoes."

**Exit check:** Prepositional phrase — *to* is followed by the
noun *store*. Combination emphasizing the class beginning: "When
the bell rang, the class began." (The main clause lands last,
where emphasis falls.)

## Lesson 03 — Active and passive voice

**Guided practice:** 1a. Active — doer: the librarian. 1b.
Passive — no doer named. 1c. Active — doer: a storm. 1d.
Passive — agent: a storm (in the *by* phrase). 2. "Someone
shelved the books before lunch." What is gained: a doer is named;
what is lost: the natural focus on the books, and the doer is
only the vague word *someone* — the passive was arguably the
better sentence. 3. "The roof was damaged by a storm." The
original emphasizes the storm; the rewrite emphasizes the roof.

**Independent practice:** 1a. Active. 1b. Passive (no doer).
1c. Active. 1d. Passive (doer hidden). 2. (b) "Someone measured
the rainfall every morning" — emphasizes a vague measurer, and
loses the focus on the rainfall. (c) "The winners were announced
by the principal" — emphasizes the winners rather than the
principal. 3. The classmate is wrong. Passive voice is not
always bad: it earns its place when the receiver matters more
than the doer, when the doer is unknown, or for a formal tone.
"The winners will be announced at noon" spotlights the winners,
which is the news; the rewrite spotlights the principal and
changes the emphasis. It is an improvement only if the principal
is the point.

## Lesson 04 — Verb moods

**Guided practice:** 1. Imperative (*close*; subject *you*
understood). 2. Interrogative. 3. Conditional (*would stay*;
the *if* clause verb *rained* is subjunctive — note both).
4. Error → "I wish I were taller" (contrary-to-fact wish takes
*were*). 5. Subjunctive, correct (base form *run* after
*demanded that*). 6. Mismatch → "If it rains, we will move
inside" (real possibility) or "If it rained, we would move
inside" (contrary-to-fact condition).

**Independent practice:** 1a. Imperative. 1b. Interrogative.
1c. Indicative. 1d. Conditional (*would thrive*). 1e.
Subjunctive (*were* in a wish). 1f. Subjunctive (*remain*, base
form after *insists that*). 2a. "I wish I were at the garden
today" (contrary-to-fact *were*). 2b. "The rule requires that
every visitor sign the log" (base form after *requires that*).
2c. "If Mara practiced more, she would win" or "If Mara
practices more, she will win" (match the two parts to one
pattern). 3. The classmate is wrong: *were* marks a state
contrary to fact (the speaker is not a bird), not past time.
"If I was a bird" would suggest a real past possibility, which
is not what the sentence means.

## Lesson 05 — Semicolons, colons, conjunctive adverbs

**Guided practice:** 1. "The soil was dry; however, the
tomatoes survived." (Semicolon before, comma after *however*.)
2. "We need shovels and gloves." (No colon: "We need" is not a
complete thought.) 3. "There are three beds to water."
(*There*, not *their*.) 4. "It's time to water the tomatoes."
(*It's* = it is.) 5. "Jon finished his work; he went home."
(Semicolon repairs the comma splice; a period is also correct.)
6. "The team won; therefore, they celebrated." (Semicolon
before, comma after *therefore*.)

**Independent practice:** 1a. "Bring pencils, paper, and
erasers." 1b. "It rained all morning; the game was canceled."
1c. "Jon is tired; nevertheless, he finished." 1d. "We need two
tools: a shovel and a rake." (Colon follows the complete clause
"We need two tools.") 1e. "You're going to love this book."
1f. "The team won; they celebrated." 2. Accept any explanation
stating that a semicolon joins two independent clauses, with a
correct example (e.g. "The bell rang; the class began" is
correct, while "The bell rang; when the class began" is not,
because "when the class began" cannot stand alone). 3. Accept
any three sentences that meet the stated patterns.

**Applied task:** one correct version: "The garden club met on
Saturday. The members brought gloves, water, and seeds. The soil
was dry; however, the tomatoes had survived. The club had one
rule: every member waters a bed." Decisions: (1) *Saturday*
capitalized; (2) period after *Saturday* (two sentences);
(3) commas in the list; (4) semicolon before and comma after
*however*; (5) colon after the complete clause "The club had
one rule."

**Exit check:** rule: when a conjunctive adverb joins two
independent clauses, put a semicolon before it and a comma after
it. Sentence: "Mara was tired; however, she finished the
chapter."

## Lesson 06 — Style editing pass

**Guided practice faults and repairs:** (1) Comma splice:
"To win is Mara's goal, she practices daily" → "To win is Mara's
goal. She practices daily." (A semicolon is also correct.)
(2) Voice + comma splice: "The trophy was won by Mara, she
smiled" → "Mara won the trophy, and she smiled." (Active voice
names the actor; *and* joins the clauses.) (3) Mood: "If Mara
was faster" → "If Mara were faster" (contrary to fact).
(4) Confused words + comma splice: "Their going to celebrate,
its going to be fun" → "They're going to celebrate. It's going
to be fun." (*They're* = they are; *it's* = it is; the two
clauses are separate sentences.)

**Independent practice, task 1 — six faults:** (1) Comma splice:
"Swimming is fun, Mara loves it." (2) Comma splice: "The pool
was cleaned by the caretaker before noon, the water was clear."
(3) Voice choice in the same sentence: passive with a named
agent — revise to "The caretaker cleaned the pool before noon."
(4) Conditional mismatch: "If it will rain, the meet would move
inside" → "If it rains, the meet will move inside" or "If it
rained, the meet would move inside." (5) Subjunctive: "demands
that Mara practices" → "demands that Mara practice." (6) Comma
splice: "she arrives early" joined by a comma → "…every morning;
she arrives early" or a period. One correct revised paragraph:
"Swimming is fun. Mara loves it. The caretaker cleaned the pool
before noon, and the water was clear. If it rains, the meet will
move inside. The coach demands that Mara practice every morning;
she arrives early."

## Practice R1 — Verbals in prose

*Building* — gerund, subject. *Hammering nails in the heat* —
participial phrase describing Jon. *To finish* — infinitive,
noun job (predicate nominative: his goal *was* to finish).
*Carrying lumber across the yard* — participial phrase
describing *he* (Jon). *To pace* — infinitive, object of
*learned*. *Finishing the roof* — gerund, subject. Acceptable
choice explanations include: the *hammering* phrase packs
background action into the main sentence without a second
clause; *to finish* as a noun lets the goal itself be the
subject complement; *finishing* as a subject makes the completed
action the topic of its sentence.

## Practice R2 — Voice and mood in argument

1. The passive hides who decided to cancel the trip. A reader
might want the decision-maker named to judge responsibility.
Acceptable active rewrite with a supplied actor, explicitly
noted as supplied: "The principal decided to cancel the trip."
2. *Was* is incorrect: the sentence claims the school is *not*
fair (a contrary-to-fact condition), so the verb must be
*were*: "If the school were fair, it would listen." The *were*
marks the claim as contrary to fact, not as a past event.

## Practice E — Punctuation clinic

**E1 — eight repairs:** (1) Capitalization: *the* → *The* at
the start. (2) Comma splice with *however*: "The soil was dry;
however, the tomatoes survived." (3) Comma splice: "Mara
planted carrots; they grew quickly." (4) Colon after an
incomplete clause: "She bought gloves and seeds." (5) *Their*
→ *There*. (6) Comma splice: "The fence was painted; it still
needs a second coat." (7) *Its* → *It's*. (8) Conjunctive
adverb pattern: "The harvest was large; therefore, we shared
it." Corrected paragraph: "The garden needed three things:
gloves, water, and seeds. The soil was dry; however, the
tomatoes survived. Mara planted carrots; they grew quickly. She
bought gloves and seeds. There are three beds to water. The
fence was painted; it still needs a second coat. It's time to
water the tomatoes. The harvest was large; therefore, we shared
it."

## Formative quiz (week-04)

1. Gerund phrase acting as the subject ("Swimming laps" is what
   builds endurance; a noun fits the slot).
2. Participial phrase describing *Mara* (Mara is the one
   swimming laps).
3. "Jon finished the report." The active version emphasizes Jon,
   the doer; the passive emphasized the report.
4. Passive voice. A reader might object because the sentence
   hides who made the mistakes — it avoids accountability.
5. Subjunctive, and correct: *were* marks a state contrary to
   fact (the speaker is not in charge).
6. "The principal requires that every student bring a
   notebook." (Base form after *requires that*.)
7. "The garden was wet; however, the path was dry." (Semicolon
   before, comma after *however*.)
8. "She packed a tent, a lantern, and food." (No colon: "She
   packed" is not a complete thought. Correct colon version:
   "She packed three things: a tent, a lantern, and food.")
9. Three faults: *Their* → *They're*, *its* → *It's*, and the
   comma splice → "They're going to the garden; it's open
   today." (A period is also correct.)
10. (b) is better for a report about the results: the passive
    spotlights the rainfall (the results), not the team.
11. Fact: "it opened in March" (or "holds 20,000 books").
    Interpretation: "it is the town's proudest achievement" (or
    "will transform reading forever").
12. "Libraries" is a topic — a single subject word, not an idea.
    A theme is a statement about the topic. Acceptable theme
    statement: "A new library can become a source of community
    pride." Any full-sentence idea the passage supports earns
    credit; a single word does not.
13. (a) is relevant: it bears directly on the claim that later
    starts help teens (sleep and morning performance). (b) is
    irrelevant: school colors have nothing to do with start
    times or sleep.
14. No: the passage gives only one date (March) and one size
    (20,000 books), both from Writer A, so the writers do not
    contradict each other on any fact stated here. (Naming a
    different date for Writer B is not in the passage and earns
    no credit.) Interpretive difference: Writer A states facts
    about the library, while Writer B offers praise and
    prediction — "the town's proudest achievement" and "will
    transform reading forever" — which are judgments, not
    verifiable facts.
