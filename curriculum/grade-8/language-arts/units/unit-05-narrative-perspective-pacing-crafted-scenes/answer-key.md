# Answer Key — Unit 05: Narrative Perspective, Pacing, and Crafted Scenes

**Adult / educator only.** Do not place this file beside student
questions. Open responses below give a sample response, not the
only correct one: accept any response that meets the stated
requirement and names its evidence. Where a question asks for a
reason, the reason earns the credit, not the label alone.

## Lesson 01 — Point of view

**Guided practice**

1. **1P.** The narrator is the character ("I"); only the
   speaker's thoughts are heard. The narrator cannot know
   whether the train will come, or what anyone else is thinking.
2. **3L (Jon).** Pronouns are *he/his*, and only Jon's thoughts
   are heard (his determination). The narrator cannot know what
   anyone else thinks — for example, what Mara thinks of him.
3. **3O.** The narrator reports Jon's determination *and*
   Mara's belief ("she was sure he would miss it"), plus the
   history of the three missed buses. No single character knows
   all of that.
4. **1P (plural).** "We" — the narrator is one of the people
   waiting. The narrator cannot know anyone else's private
   thoughts, or when the train will come.

**Independent practice**

1. **3L (Mara).** The narrator follows Mara's thoughts only;
   "It would have to be enough" is her judgment, not a fact the
   narrator states from outside her head.
2. **1P.** The narrator is the character counting the coins
   ("I counted").
3. **3O.** Two minds are reported: Mara's hope and the
   shopkeeper's certainty ("certain she could not afford it").
4. **1P (plural).** "We" — the narrator is in the group running
   for shelter.
5. **3O.** The narrator reports a fact Jon does not know (Mara
   already bought his present) and Mara's secret — knowledge no
   single character holds.
6. **3L (Jon).** Only Jon's perception and worry are reported
   ("worried him," "he could not say why"); Mara's smile is
   observed from outside, and her thoughts are never given.

Rewrites (samples): Passage 1 in first person — "I counted the
coins in my pocket. Eighty-seven cents. It would have to be
enough." Passage 2 in third-person omniscient — "I counted the
coins in my pocket, hoping eighty-seven cents would be enough.
Behind the counter, the shopkeeper was already wrapping the
gift I wanted, certain I could not afford it." (The facts are
the same; the omniscient version adds the shopkeeper's private
certainty, which the first-person version cannot know.)

**Applied task (sample)**

- *Version A (first person, traveler):* "I have been standing
  here so long the bench has gone cold under my bag. The clock
  says ten minutes to six, but the track is empty, and I do not
  know whether the train is late or whether I misread the
  timetable. The keeper is watching me, and I cannot tell
  whether he is worried or only waiting, the way I am."
- *Version B (third-person omniscient):* "The traveler stood
  on the platform, sure the train was late and afraid she had
  misread the timetable. The keeper watched her, lantern in
  hand, and knew the train was already an hour behind — the
  telegraph had said so, though no one had told the traveler.
  Neither of them knew the other was waiting for the same train."

**Exit check (sample)**

1. The knowledge test asks: "Whose thoughts am I hearing?
   Whose thoughts am I not hearing?"
2. Third-person limited — it follows only Mara's thoughts.
   It is not omniscient because an omniscient narrator would
   report more than one character's private thoughts.

## Lesson 02 — Narrator reliability and dramatic irony

**Guided practice**

1. **Unreliable (about their own honesty).** The narrator
   claims "I have always been honest," then admits, "I told
   Mara I had finished the homework, though I had only done
   half of it." The detail contradicts the claim.
2. **Reliable narrator; dishonest character.** The narrator
   reports the facts accurately: "There was not enough for the
   train ticket," and then reports that Jon "told Mara the
   ticket was already bought." The narrator is showing us Jon's
   lie, not endorsing it — do not confuse the narrator with
   the character.
3. **Reliable.** The narrator reports what they heard honestly
   and corrects the first impression with new evidence: "Then I
   heard the back door open, and I knew someone was inside."
   Changing your mind when the evidence changes is reliable.
4. **Reliable narrator; dramatic irony present.** The reader
   knows from the son's letter that he is not coming home
   tonight; Mrs. Alvarez does not know, and sets the table for
   two. The reader holds the fact; the character acts without it.

**Independent practice**

1. Sample: "I was perfectly calm. My hands shook as I reached
   for the door handle, and my heart was beating so hard I
   could hear it. I checked the lock twice, then a third time,
   and told myself there was nothing to be afraid of." (Claim:
   calm. Details: shaking hands, racing heart, checking the
   lock three times.)
2. Sample: "Mara was sure Jon was angry with her. He had not
   spoken all morning, and his silence felt like blame. She did
   not know he was silent because he was rehearsing the apology
   he planned to give her at the station." (The limited
   narrator follows Mara, who is wrong; the last sentence gives
   the reader the detail that proves it — Jon's silence has a
   different cause.)
3. Sample: Dramatic irony needs the reader to know something a
   character does not know. Only a narrator who knows more than
   that character can give the reader the fact — so the narrator
   must be omniscient, or a first-person narrator telling the
   reader something the other characters do not know.
4. The sentence follows Della — it reports what she does after
   crying. In this sentence the narrator shows only Della; but
   the story's narrator is omniscient overall, since it also
   reports Jim's thoughts and facts neither character knows
   (for example, that Jim sold his watch).

**Applied task (sample)**

- *Paragraph 1 (keeper, first person, unreliable):* "The train
  is never late — not on my platform, not in thirty years. The
  clock says ten minutes to six, and the track is empty, but
  that means nothing; the train is always on time. The
  traveler's suitcase has been sitting by the bench for two
  hours, and the lamps have all come on, but the train is never
  late." (Claim: never late. Details: empty track, two-hour
  wait, lamps on — all prove the train *is* late.)
- *Paragraph 2 (dramatic irony, third person):* "The traveler
  had packed for a new life in the city, with no plan to return.
  The keeper, watching her suitcase, believed she was only
  visiting her sister for the weekend. What the keeper did not
  know was that the traveler was leaving home for good."

**Exit check (sample)**

1. The three questions: Do the narrator's facts match the
   story's details? Does the narrator report what they could
   not know? Does the narrator's judgment match the evidence?
2. Dramatic irony has three parts: the reader learns a fact;
   a character does not know that fact; the reader watches the
   character act without it. The reader learning the fact comes
   first.

## Lesson 03 — Pacing: scene, summary, and the speed of time

**Guided practice**

1. **Summary / time compression.** Months of summer are covered
   in one sentence.
2. **Scene / time expansion.** A minute of reading a letter is
   stretched across a detailed sentence with repeated actions.
3. Sample scene: "The sun stayed high until eight, and the air
   smelled of cut grass. Mara lay on the blanket with a book
   she had already read twice, listening to the mower hum two
   yards over. 'One more chapter,' she said, though the light
   was already going gold at the edges. Nobody hurried; there
   was nowhere to be until dark."
4. Sample summary: "She read the letter twice."

**Independent practice**

1. Sample expansion: "The rain came down in sheets, and Mara
   stood under the narrow shelter with her suitcase at her
   feet. 'One hour,' the driver had said, but the clock on the
   shelter said two. Water ran off the edge of the roof in a
   thin, cold line and dripped onto her sleeve. She moved the
   suitcase behind her legs and kept her eyes on the road,
   though no bus was coming."
2. Sample compression: "Jon woke at six, dressed in the dark,
   ate bread standing in the kitchen, walked to the station in
   the cold, bought his ticket, and waited." (One sentence; all
   facts kept: woke at six, dressed in the dark, ate standing,
   walked in the cold, bought the ticket, waited.)
3. Sample: A difficult decision matters — it changes the story —
   so it deserves a scene, where the reader can watch the choice
   happen. The journey to get there usually changes nothing, so
   it deserves a summary; compressing it keeps the reader's
   attention on the moment that matters.
4. Sample: Scene — the final reunion, when Jim comes home and
   the two exchange gifts, told moment by moment with dialogue.
   Summary — the backstory of Jim's salary shrinking from $30 to
   $20 a week and the flat at $8 a week, compressed into a short
   passage of context. O. Henry gives the reunion a scene
   because it is the turning point (both sacrifices are revealed);
   he gives the backstory a summary because the reader only
   needs the facts of their poverty, not the months of it.

**Applied task (sample)**

- *A — summary:* "The traveler waited at the station for two
  hours. The train did not come, and the lamps came on one by
  one."
- *B — scene:* "The traveler checked the clock: ten minutes
  to six. She moved her suitcase out of the wind and sat on the
  bench, where the wood was still warm from the afternoon sun.
  The keeper lifted his lantern and looked down the track, then
  looked back at her. 'It'll come,' he said. She nodded, though
  she did not believe him, and counted the ties between the
  rails until the numbers ran out."
- *C — scene with flashback:* version B, with this inserted
  after the keeper's line: "That morning she had bought the
  ticket in a hurry, before she could change her mind; the clerk
  had asked if she was sure, and she had said yes too quickly.
  Now the lamps were coming on, and she was still deciding
  whether she had meant it." Then the scene returns to the
  platform. What the flashback adds: it explains *why* she is
  waiting and why she doubts — the reader learns the ticket was
  bought in haste, so the waiting is also a second decision.

**Exit check (sample)**

1. A scene covers a short stretch of story time in many words
   (close to real time); a summary covers a long stretch of
   story time in few words.
2. The test: does this moment change anything — a choice, a
   truth, a relationship? If yes, it deserves a scene; if time
   only passes, compress it.

## Lesson 04 — Crafted scenes

**Guided practice**

1. "'I sold the watch,' Jim said" propels the action: it reveals
   that Jim gave up his watch — the very thing Della's chain
   was bought for — so the gift exchange reverses and both
   sacrifices are exposed at once.
2. Sample showing: "Jon set his bag down hard on the bench and
   did not look at Mara. 'The tickets,' he said, very quietly.
   'I left them on the kitchen table.' He stared at the
   timetable as if it might have the answer, and his hands
   stayed in his pockets." (No *angry* — the slammed bag, the
   quiet voice, and the averted eyes show it.)
3. Sample repair: "'Is the train coming?' Mara asked, stamping
   her cold feet. Jon folded the timetable and put it away.
   'It is late,' he said. 'We should wait inside.'" (Beat:
   folding the timetable; the changed line gives a new plan —
   wait inside — so the dialogue propels action.)
4. Sample reflection: "Mara held the ticket Jon had bought her
   and understood, all at once, that he had been planning her
   leaving with her all along — she had thought she was going
   alone." (Specific to Mara and Jon, not a general moral.)

**Independent practice**

1. Sample scene (market): "The market smelled of rain on hot
   stone and fresh bread. 'The last loaf is gone,' the baker
   said, wiping her hands. Mara reached for her coins and
   counted them twice. 'Then I will take the rolls instead,'
   she said, 'and you will save me a loaf tomorrow.' The baker
   laughed, a short, warm sound under the awning. *She had come
   for bread and was leaving with a promise — and, she realized,
   with someone in this town who knew her name.*" (Dialogue
   changes the plan: rolls instead of a loaf, plus a promise
   for tomorrow. Smell: rain on stone, bread. Sound: the
   laugh. Reflection underlined.)
2. Sample showing: "Mara stood at the gate of the old house and
   did not move. The windows were dark, and the door hung open
   an inch, breathing cold air onto the step. Her hands were
   cold around the strap of her bag. 'Jon?' she called, and her
   voice came back smaller than she had meant. She stepped
   inside anyway, because his shoes were by the door." (Facts
   kept: scary house, afraid, went in anyway because Jon was
   inside — shown through the dark windows, the cold air, the
   small voice, and the shoes.)
3. Sample: Writers use beats because a beat *shows* how a line
   is said through an action the reader can picture, while an
   adverb *tells* the reader how to hear it. Beats also keep
   the scene moving — the character does something — instead
   of pausing the action to label the tone.
4. Magi opening: Precise detail — "One dollar and eighty-seven
   cents" (an exact number, not "very little money") makes
   Della's poverty concrete and countable. Showing through
   action — "Three times Della counted it" shows worry and
   disbelief through a repeated action, without saying "she was
   worried."

**Applied task (sample keeper scene)**

"The keeper set his lantern on the bench between them. 'The
last train is running late tonight,' he said. 'It will come, but
not before seven.' The traveler pulled her coat tighter against
the cold and looked down the empty track. 'Seven,' she said.
'Then I have time to decide.' The keeper picked the lantern up
again and held it out toward her. 'Then decide here,' he said,
'where it is warm.' *She had expected to wait alone; she had
not expected anyone to wait with her.*" (The keeper's line
reveals the delay; the traveler's reply changes the keeper's
plan — he decides to wait with her. Beat: setting the lantern
down. Sensory detail: the cold, the coat pulled tight.
Reflection underlined. One adverb-free scene; *said* used
throughout.)

**Exit check (sample)**

1. The test: what changes because this was said? If a line
   reveals a fact, forces a choice, or changes a relationship,
   it belongs; if nothing changes, it is filler.
2. A beat is a small action attached to dialogue that shows how
   the line was said; an adverb is a word that tells how it was
   said. Writers prefer beats because they show the tone
   through action and keep the scene moving.

## Lesson 05 — Structure, transitions, flashback, foreshadowing

**Guided practice**

1. Chronological order: Mara argues with Jon → Jon buys the
   gift → Jon gives Mara the gift → Mara boards the train.
2. Sample transition: "By morning, Mara was back in her
   kitchen, the platform lamps still buzzing in her ears."
   (Time word: *by morning*. Place word: *in her kitchen*.)
3. Sample foreshadowing: "Mara left the key on the table and
   did not look back at the house." (A hint — the key left
   behind suggests she will not need it again — not a
   statement that she will never return.)
4. The opening raises the question: why is Mara already on the
   train, and what happened before she left? The flashback to
   the argument answers what drove her to leave and what she
   and Jon said to each other.

**Independent practice**

1. Chronological order: (a) finds the old ticket → (b) argues
   → (c) packs → (d) buys a new ticket → (e) waits → (f) train
   arrives. Sample start: begin at (e), the wait — it raises
   the story's question at once (will the train come, and will
   Jon come?), and the earlier events can be told as flashback
   when the reader needs them. Starting at (a) would make the
   reader wait five events for the question to appear.
2. Sample transitions: (a) "By morning, the rain had stopped."
   (b) "Meanwhile, across town at the market, Jon was counting
   his coins." (c) "Last winter, at this same station, Mara
   had waited for a different train."
3. Sample flashback: "Mara remembered the last time she had
   stood on this platform with her grandmother. 'Hold my hand
   tight when the train comes,' her grandmother had said, 'or
   the wind will take your hat.' The wind had taken it anyway,
   and they had both laughed until the train pulled away. Now
   the platform was empty, and Mara was holding her own
   suitcase." (Dialogue: the grandmother's line. Sensory detail:
   the wind. Return sentence: "Now the platform was empty…")
4. Sample: Foreshadowing and dramatic irony are related because
   both give the reader knowledge early. They differ in what the
   reader knows: foreshadowing is a *hint* about what will
   happen (the reader suspects), while dramatic irony is a *fact*
   the reader knows for certain while a character does not
   (the reader watches the character act on a false belief).

**Applied task (sample)**

- *Version A (chronological):* "Mara packed her suitcase in
  the morning. At noon she bought her ticket, and on the way
  home she argued with Jon, who called her leaving a mistake.
  At dusk she waited on the platform alone. Then Jon arrived
  with a small wrapped gift, and together they watched the train
  come in. Mara boarded, and Jon waved from the platform."
- *Version B (reordered):* "Mara checked the clock. Ten minutes
  to six, and Jon was not there. That morning they had argued —
  he had called her leaving a mistake, and she had called him
  afraid. She had packed anyway and bought her ticket at noon.
  Now the lamps were coming on. Then she heard footsteps, and
  Jon was there, holding a small wrapped gift. She boarded the
  train, and he waved from the platform." What the reordering
  gains: the opening raises the question *will he come?* and
  the flashback answers why she is alone. What it risks:
  confusion about the order of events — prevented by the clear
  transitions "That morning" (into the flashback) and "Now"
  (back to the present).

**Exit check (sample)**

1. The structure test: cover the story and retell it in three
   sentences — what happened first, what changed, what happened
   last. If the retelling is confused, the structure is confused.
2. A flashback is a *scene* from the past, with dialogue and
   action; a summary of the past only *tells* what happened, in
   a sentence or two, without showing it.

## Lesson 06 — Revision

**Guided practice**

1. **Pacing pass:** every sentence is summary (U) except the
   last, which is told quickly rather than shown. The moment
   that deserves a scene is Jon's arrival on the train, waving
   — it resolves the whole wait. The waiting, the packing, and
   the goodbye should compress into one or two sentences.
2. **Point-of-view pass:** there is no slip. The narrator is
   first person and reports only their own experience and
   guesses: "Jon was probably not coming" is a guess, not a
   report of Jon's thoughts. Do not "fix" a slip that is not
   there.
3. **Conclusion pass:** the ending only reports (the train
   came, Jon was on it, waving). Sample reflection to replace
   the last sentence: "I had spent the whole day certain Jon
   would not come — and there he was, waving from the train,
   and I understood that I had been wrong about him all
   morning."

**Independent practice (guidance + samples)**

1. Sample pacing changes: *Expand* — the summary "Jon was
   probably not coming" becomes a scene: "I watched the road
   for Jon's coat. Twice I thought I saw him, and twice it was
   only the keeper with his lantern. The clock showed six, then
   ten past, and I stopped watching the road and started
   watching the track instead." *Compress* — "I had packed my
   suitcase that morning. I had said goodbye to my mother."
   becomes "I had packed that morning and said goodbye to my
   mother." *Cut* — "I thought about everything" (it repeats
   what the waiting sentences already show).
2. Point-of-view pass: list each paragraph's knowledge in one
   phrase ("my thoughts only" for first person; "Mara's
   thoughts only" for limited). Any paragraph reporting another
   character's secret thoughts must be rewritten inside the
   narrator's knowledge.
3. Sample reflective conclusion: see guided practice item 3 —
   the test is whether the conclusion could end a different
   story. If it could, rewrite until it names this story's
   events (the gift, the timetable, the waving).
4. Sample conventions pass on the guided-practice paragraph:
   verbal — "waiting" in "I waited at the station for hours"
   is the main verb, not a verbal (no fix needed; note it);
   voice — "the train was late" is not passive (no agent is
   hidden); punctuation — the draft has no semicolons or
   colons, so the fix is to join two short sentences with a
   semicolon where the ideas are equal: "I waited at the
   station for hours and hours; the train was late." Reason: the
   two clauses are independent and closely related. (Accept any
   genuine fault found with a stated reason.)

**Applied task (sample memo)**

"Your draft's pacing is strong in the arrival scene and weak in
the waiting: I compressed three waiting sentences into one so
the arrival lands harder — the wait was not the story, the
arrival was. I caught one point-of-view slip: your first-person
narrator reported that Jon 'had decided not to come,' which she
could not know; I changed it to 'I was sure he had decided not
to come,' which is her guess, not his thought. One conventions
fix: I joined two short sentences with a semicolon ('The lamps
came on; the platform grew smaller') because the two ideas are
equal and closely related. Your old ending only reported the
boarding; the new conclusion says what Mara understands now —
that Jon had understood her leaving all along — which the old
ending never said."

**Exit check (sample)**

1. The four passes, in order: pacing, point of view,
   conclusion, conventions. Conventions come last because
   fixing commas on a scene that will be cut or restructured
   wastes effort — shape the story first, polish it last.
2. The test for a reflective conclusion: could this conclusion
   end a different story? If yes, rewrite it until it belongs
   only to this story.

## Practice A — Point-of-view identification

1. **1P.** The narrator is the character ("I had packed").
   Only the speaker's thoughts are heard.
2. **3L (Mara).** Only Mara's thoughts are heard ("she still
   was not sure").
3. **3O.** Mara's uncertainty *and* Jon's certainty are
   reported, plus the fact Mara does not know (Jon packed his
   bag the night before).
4. **1P (plural).** "We" — the narrator is one of the people
   waiting.
5. **3L (the keeper).** Only the keeper's knowledge is reported
   ("he knew which ones would board"); no other character's
   private thoughts appear.
6. **1P.** "I lifted my lantern" — the narrator is the keeper.
7. **3O.** The narrator reports a fact Mara does not know (Jon
   already bought her ticket) — knowledge no single character
   holds.
8. **3L (Jon).** Only Jon's perception is reported ("worried
   him," "he wondered what she was hiding"); Mara's smile is
   observed from outside.

Rewrites (samples): Passage 2 in first person — "I had packed
my suitcase twice and unpacked it once, and I still was not sure
I was doing the right thing." Passage 3 in third-person limited
to Mara — "Mara had packed her suitcase twice and unpacked it
once. The hallway was quiet, and Jon had not said goodbye. She
did not know whether he would come to the station at all."
(Note: Jon's secret packing cannot appear in the limited version
— Mara cannot know it. Dropping it is the point of the
exercise.)

## Practice B — Pacing rewrite

1. Sample expansion: "Mara stood at the door with her suitcase
   already in her hand. Her mother held the doorframe and did
   not let go of it. 'Write when you get there,' her mother
   said, 'even if it is only one line.' Mara nodded, and for a
   moment neither of them moved. Then her mother stepped back,
   and Mara walked out into the cold morning." Reason: the
   goodbye is the last one before a long journey — it changes
   the relationship, so it deserves a scene.
2. Sample expansion: "Jon's shoes slapped the wet pavement as
   he ran. The bus was already pulling away from the curb, its
   lights smearing in the rain. 'Wait!' he shouted, but the
   driver did not hear him, or pretended not to. He stopped
   running and stood in the rain, watching the taillights shrink."
   Reason: missing the bus decides whether he catches the train
   — the run is the turning point, so it deserves a scene.
3. Sample expansion: "At dusk the keeper took the matches from
   his pocket, the same pocket, the same hour, every evening. He
   struck one, cupped it against the wind, and touched it to the
   first lamp. The light caught, and the platform turned gold at
   the edges. The traveler watched from the bench and understood
   that this was not a chore — it was the keeper's way of saying
   the day was over." Reason: the ritual shows who the keeper is
   and how the traveler sees him — character, not background —
   so it deserves a scene.
4. Sample compression: "Mara woke at six, dressed in the dark,
   ate bread standing in the kitchen, put on her coat, walked to
   the station, and bought her ticket." (One sentence; all six
   facts kept.)
5. Sample compression: "Jon checked his pockets and his bag to
   make sure he had his ticket, his money, and his book." (One
   sentence; all three facts kept.)
6. Sample compression: "Mara pulled her coat tight and moved
   under the roof as the rain kept falling harder." (One
   sentence; all facts kept: rain fell, coat tight, moved under
   the roof, rain kept falling.)

## Practice C — Scene craft

1. Sample scene (market): "The market smelled of rain on hot
   stone and fresh bread. 'The last loaf is gone,' the baker
   said, wiping her hands on her apron. Mara counted her coins
   twice and set them on the counter. 'Then I will take the
   rolls,' she said, 'and you will save me a loaf tomorrow.' The
   baker laughed, a short, warm sound under the awning, and
   wrapped the rolls. *She had come for bread and was leaving
   with a promise — and with someone in this town who knew her
   name.*" (Second line changes the plan: rolls instead of a
   loaf, plus tomorrow's loaf. Smell: rain on stone, bread.
   Sound: the laugh. Reflection underlined.)
2. Sample showing: "Jon set his bag down hard on the bench and
   did not look at Mara. 'The tickets,' he said, very quietly.
   'I left them on the kitchen table.' He stared at the
   timetable without reading it, and his hands stayed in his
   pockets." (No *embarrassed*, *angry*, or *terrible* — the
   slammed bag, the quiet voice, and the averted eyes show it.)
3. Sample repair: "'Is the train coming?' Mara asked, stamping
   her cold feet. Jon folded the timetable and put it away.
   'It is late,' he said. 'We should wait inside.'" (Beat:
   folding the timetable. Changed line: "We should wait inside"
   gives a new plan, so the dialogue propels action.)
4. Sample reflection: "Jon held the timetable Mara had circled
   for him and understood that she had been planning his trip
   with him all along — he had thought he was leaving alone."
5. Sample dialogue: "'I sold the watch,' Jim said. / 'Then the
   chain is useless,' Della said. / 'No,' Jim said. 'The chain
   is the reason I sold it — I wanted you to have something
   worth the watch.' / Della sat down. 'Then we will keep the
   chain,' she said, 'until the watch comes back.'" (Second
   speaker reveals the sale; the first speaker's reply changes
   her mind — from "useless" to "we will keep it." *Said* used
   throughout; no adverbs.)
6. Sample: Walking home changes nothing — no choice, no truth,
   no relationship shift — so it can be told in a summary
   sentence. Deciding to leave changes everything, so it
   deserves a scene, where the reader can watch the decision
   happen. The rule is importance, not length.

## Practice R1 — "The Gift of the Magi"

1. **Third-person omniscient.** Della's thoughts: the narrator
   reports her counting ("Three times Della counted it"), her
   distress over the $1.87, and her plan to sell her hair.
   Jim's thoughts (or a fact neither knows): the narrator
   reports that Jim sold his watch to buy the combs — a fact
   Della does not know until the reveal — and closes by
   addressing the reader directly ("The magi, as you know…"),
   a voice no character has.
2. Before the final scene, the reader knows Della sold her hair
   to buy the chain — Jim does not know that. What Della does
   not know — that Jim sold his watch to buy the combs — the
   reader does *not* know either until the final scene; the
   reader learns it at the same moment Della does. That shared
   ignorance is deliberate: the reveal lands on the reader too.
3. Sample: An exact number makes Della's poverty concrete and
   countable — the reader can feel the gap between $1.87 and the
   price of a gift. "Della was poor" is vague and tells the
   reader what to think; $1.87 shows the reader the exact size
   of the problem.
4. Sample: Scene — the final reunion, when Jim comes home and
   the two exchange gifts, told moment by moment with dialogue
   (the hair reveal, the chain, the combs). Summary — the
   backstory of Jim's salary shrinking from $30 to $20 a week
   and the flat at $8 a week, compressed into a short passage of
   context. O. Henry gives the reunion a scene because it is the
   turning point (both sacrifices are revealed); he gives the
   backstory a summary because the reader only needs the facts
   of their poverty, not the months of it.

## Practice R2 — Discussion: "The Gift of the Magi"

Accept either position when defended with two details.

1. Sample (agree): The narrator is right — Della and Jim each
   gave up their greatest treasure (her hair, his watch) for the
   other, and the gifts were useless in practical terms but
   perfect in love; that is exactly what the magi did, giving
   valuable gifts to a child they did not know. Sample
   (disagree): The narrator is too generous — they destroyed
   the very things the gifts were for (the chain needs the
   watch; the combs need the hair), so the gifts were foolish,
   not wise; the "wisest" judgment ignores the real cost.
2. Sample: It is the same as dramatic irony because the reader
   knows something a character does not — the reader watches
   Della sell her hair while Jim does not know, and watches
   both act on partial knowledge. It is different because the
   reader does *not* know everything from the start: Jim's
   sacrifice is hidden from the reader too until the final
   scene, so the irony builds in two stages — first Della's
   secret, then Jim's. In Shakespeare's stage irony the
   audience usually knows the whole secret from early on.
3. Sample: A first-person Della version would lose (a) Jim's
   thoughts and reactions — the reader would only see him
   through Della's eyes — and (b) the narrator's closing
   judgment ("the magi"), which is an outside voice praising
   them both. It might gain immediacy: Della's feelings about
   her hair, in her own words, would hit harder than the
   narrator's report of them.

## Practice R3 — "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge"

1. (a) The opening on the bridge is a **scene**: moment by
   moment (the noose, the sergeant, the ticking of the watch,
   the drop), covering only a few minutes of story time — and
   the falling itself is expanded across many lines. (b) The
   middle is a **flashback**: how Farquhar came to the bridge —
   the stranger at his gate who told him about the bridge, his
   plan to burn it, and the trap. It answers the question the
   opening raises: why is this civilian being hanged? (c) The
   escape is a **scene with expanded time**: the flight through
   the river and woods feels like hours but is seconds — it is
   the most expanded passage in the story, slower even than the
   opening.
2. The withheld fact: Farquhar never escaped — he died on the
   bridge, and the whole escape was imagined in the instant of
   the hanging.
3. Sample: The falling — Farquhar feels the rope snap, plunges
   into the water, and his senses sharpen impossibly (he hears
   the ticking of his watch, sees individual leaves, feels the
   water's cold in detail). A few seconds of story time are
   stretched across pages. The expansion makes the reader feel
   the desperate vividness of a mind racing at the end of life —
   and makes the reader believe the escape, which is why the
   final line hits so hard.
4. Sample: The narrator tells us the stranger at Farquhar's
   gate was a Federal scout in disguise — a fact Farquhar never
   learns. It matters because it explains the hanging (he was
   trapped, not merely caught) and makes the ending inevitable:
   the story was never going to let him escape, because the trap
   was set before it began.

## Practice R4 — "The Story of an Hour"

1. **Third-person limited to Louise Mallard.** The narrator
   follows her thoughts and no one else's: "She was beginning
   to recognize this thing that was approaching to possess
   her," and her whispered "free, free, free!" Josephine's and
   Richards's feelings are shown from outside (Josephine's
   worry, Richards's care), never from inside their heads.
2. The hour in Louise's room alone is expanded into a scene:
   the open window, the spring day, her slow realization that
   she is free, the whispered "free, free, free!" The news of
   the death and Brently's return are compressed into quick
   scenes at the edges. An hour of story time is told in about
   a thousand words — and most of those words belong to the
   minutes in her room, because that is when everything changes.
3. The reader knows Louise feels free and alive — she whispers
   "free, free, free!" and drinks in "a very elixir of life" —
   while Josephine believes Louise is grieving and worries she
   will make herself ill.
4. The doctors do not know that Louise died of shock and
   disappointment — her new life vanished the moment Brently
   walked in alive. That gap is dramatic irony: the reader
   knows her death came from the loss of freedom, while the
   doctors (and Josephine) believe the opposite, that joy killed
   her.

## Unit assessment — 24 points

**Part 1 — Reading "The Last Train" (8 points, 2 each)**

1. **Third-person limited to Mara.** Two proving details (any
   two of these): "she was still deciding whether he would
   come" (Mara's private thought); "It was the first time all
   day she had been sure of anything" (Mara's inner state);
   "She had packed anyway" (her decision, reported from her
   side). The narrator never reports Jon's or the keeper's
   private thoughts — the keeper speaks only in dialogue.
2. **Scene:** the keeper's exchange — "'The last train is
   running late tonight,' he said" — dialogue, moment by
   moment. **Summary:** "Mara had waited at the station for two
   hours" (two hours in one sentence) or "She had packed
   anyway, and bought her ticket at noon" (a morning and noon
   compressed). Why: the wait's length is context, so it is
   compressed; the keeper's news changes Mara's plan, so it is
   expanded into a scene.
3. The keeper's line reveals the train will not come before
   seven. It changes Mara's plan: she will miss her connection
   in the city, so the journey must wait until morning — and she
   decides to wait with the keeper instead of waiting alone.
   (2 points: 1 for the revelation, 1 for the change.)
4. Transition into the flashback: "That morning, Jon had
   said…". Transition back to the present: "Now the lamps were
   coming on…". The flashback explains why Mara is waiting
   alone and unsure whether Jon will come: they argued that
   morning, she left anyway, and she does not know whether he
   has forgiven her.

**Part 2 — Pacing rewrite (6 points, 3 each)**

5. Sample expansion (4–6 sentences, facts kept, dialogue +
   sensory detail): "Jon's shoes slapped the wet pavement as he
   ran. The bus was already pulling away from the curb, its
   lights smearing in the rain. 'Wait!' he shouted, but the
   driver did not hear him, or pretended not to. He stopped
   running and stood in the rain, watching the taillights
   shrink. The train would leave without him — he already knew
   it." (Facts: Jon runs, misses the bus, is late for the
   train. Dialogue: "Wait!" Sensory detail: wet pavement,
   smearing lights, rain.)
6. Sample compression (one sentence, every fact kept): "Jon
   woke at six, dressed in the dark, ate bread standing in the
   kitchen, walked to the station in the cold, bought his
   ticket, and waited." (Facts: woke at six; dressed in the
   dark; ate standing; walked in the cold; bought his ticket;
   waited.)

**Part 3 — Narrative scene (10 points, 2 per criterion)**

- **Point of view named and used consistently (2):** the
  learner names first person, third-person limited to one
  character, or third-person omniscient at the top, and the
  scene never slips outside that narrator's knowledge. Deduct
  1 for one small slip (for example, a first-person narrator
  reporting another character's secret thought once).
- **Dialogue propels action (2):** at least two lines, and the
  second line changes the first speaker's plan or reveals a new
  fact. Deduct 1 if the dialogue is present but only reports
  speech (small talk with no change).
- **Sensory detail present and precise (2):** at least one
  sensory detail (sound, touch, smell, taste) that is specific
  (a cold handle, buzzing lamps) rather than vague ("nice," "bad").
- **Reflection sentence underlined and specific (2):** the
  underlined sentence says what this character understands now
  about this scene — not a general moral that could end any
  story.
- **Time shift with a clear transition (2):** a flashback or
  foreshadowing detail, with a transition into it (for example,
  "That morning…," "Last winter…") and, for a flashback, a
  return to the present. Deduct 1 if the shift is present but
  the transition is missing or unclear.

**Scoring:** 21–24 exceeds; 17–20 meets; 12–16 approaching
(reteach the missed skill, then revise); below 12 reteach with
Lessons 01–06 and use a parallel form.

## Project — model opening and craft-note paragraph

These are models, not the only correct responses. Any narrative
meeting the project's requirements is acceptable.

**Model opening — first person, as the traveler (Mara):**

"I had been standing on the platform so long the bench had gone
cold under my bag. The clock said ten minutes to six, but the
track was empty, and I did not know whether the train was late
or whether I had misread the timetable. The keeper was watching
me from the doorway, and I could not tell whether he was
worried or only waiting, the way I was. I checked the clock
again, though the hands had not moved."

**Model opening — first person, as the station keeper (same
facts, different knowledge):**

"The girl in the red coat had been standing on my platform for
two hours, and the telegraph had already told me what she did
not know: the last train was running an hour late. I watched her
check the clock for the third time and felt sorry for her, the
way I always felt sorry for the ones who waited without knowing.
I picked up my lantern and walked toward her, because thirty
years on this platform had taught me that bad news is easier to
hear from someone holding a light."

**Model craft-note paragraph (sample — the full note needs four
choices, 150–200 words):**

"I chose first person as Mara because the story is about not
knowing: the reader should share her uncertainty about whether
Jon will come, and a first-person narrator cannot know what Jon
is thinking — 'I did not know whether the train was late or
whether I had misread the timetable.' I expanded the wait into
a scene and compressed the morning into a summary ('I had
packed that morning and said goodbye to my mother') because the
waiting is where Mara decides, and the packing is only travel.
The keeper's line 'The last train is running late tonight' does
the most work in the story: it reveals the delay and forces
Mara to choose whether to wait with him. My conclusion reflects
rather than summarizes: Mara understands that Jon had
understood her leaving all along, which is something she did
not know at the start — and no other story ends with a circled
timetable."
