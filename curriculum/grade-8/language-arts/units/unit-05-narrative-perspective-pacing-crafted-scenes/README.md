# Unit 05 — Narrative: Perspective, Pacing, and Crafted Scenes

**Grade:** 8 · **Subject:** Language Arts · **Track weeks:** 19–22
**Backlog:** [issue #40](https://github.com/murderszn/open-tutor/issues/40), section U05
**Status:** validated draft (this unit); not educator-reviewed, not merged

Units 01–04 taught the learner to read other writers critically and to
revise their own sentences: to weigh evidence, track a theme, test an
argument, compare conflicting accounts, and choose voice, verbals, and
punctuation on purpose. This unit turns the learner into the writer of
a story. The question is not "what happens?" but "who tells it, how
fast does it move, and which moments deserve a full scene?" A story
told by the wrong narrator, or told at the wrong speed, is a different
story — the learner proves that by telling one scene two ways and by
writing a complete narrative of their own.

## Essential question

How do a narrator's point of view, the pacing of events, and the
craft of individual scenes change what a reader knows, feels, and
believes about a story?

## Prerequisites

From the diagnostic weeks and Units 01–04:

- Citing textual evidence and writing objective summaries (U01) —
  now applied to a narrator's claims: every judgment about a
  narrator needs a quoted detail behind it.
- Dialogue, incidents, and dramatic irony (U02) — now used as
  writing tools, not only as reading targets: the learner writes
  dialogue that propels action and plants irony on purpose.
- Argument evaluation and conflicting accounts (U03) — now applied
  to narrators: two characters can describe the same evening
  differently, and the reader must decide whom to trust.
- Grammar as revision (U04) — voice, verbals, mood, semicolons,
  colons, conjunctive adverbs — now applied in the revision
  workshop (Lesson 06): the learner edits their own narrative
  with the U04 toolkit and explains each change.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the unit the learner will be able to (adult-observed):

1. **Identify and compare first-person, third-person limited, and
   third-person omniscient narration**, and explain how the
   narrator's position limits or expands what the reader knows
   (RL.8.6, W.8.3.a).
2. **Evaluate narrator reliability** by testing a narrator's claims
   against details in the text, and explain how an unreliable
   narrator changes the reader's judgment of events and characters
   (RL.8.1, RL.8.6).
3. **Analyze how dialogue and incidents propel action, reveal
   character, or provoke decisions** in narrative scenes, in
   mentor texts and in the learner's own drafts (RL.8.3).
4. **Control pacing** by choosing scene, summary, and time shifts
   (flashback, foreshadowing) to develop experiences, events,
   and characters (W.8.3.b, W.8.3.c).
5. **Write a complete narrative** (600–900 words) with a chosen
   point of view, crafted scenes using dialogue, description,
   reflection, and sensory language, an event sequence that
   unfolds naturally, transitions that signal shifts in time and
   setting, and a conclusion that follows from and reflects on
   the narrated events (W.8.3.a–e, W.8.4, W.8.5, W.8.10, L.8.6).

All model passages in the lessons and practice sets are **original,
written for this unit**. The three mentor stories are public-domain
texts read in full at the linked sources (see the
[Resource Pack](resource-pack.md)): O. Henry's "The Gift of the
Magi" (1905), Ambrose Bierce's "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge"
(1890), and Kate Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (1894, read in the
Project Gutenberg Chopin collection). Brief quotations from them in
lessons and practice are short excerpts used for analysis; the unit
does not reproduce any story in full. The station-platform
illustration is AI-generated (see [assets/README.md](assets/README.md));
it is a picture to write about, not evidence about any real place.
No real learner work, names, or records appear anywhere in this unit.

**Adult preview note.** "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge" centers
on a hanging and an imagined escape; "The Story of an Hour" centers
on a reported death, a marriage, and a sudden death at the end. The
adult previews both stories and may substitute another public-domain
story that teaches the same skill (pacing/time handling for Bierce;
limited point of view and irony for Chopin) if either theme is not
right for this learner at this time. The lessons name the substitute
skill, not the story, so a swap does not break the sequence.

## 20-session pacing (4 weeks × 5 sessions, ~45 minutes each)

Sessions rotate: explicit narrative lesson → practice → reading or
discussion → writing workshop. **Lessons alone do not fill the
unit** — the named practice, reading, discussion, project, review,
quiz, and assessment sessions below are the rest of the 20. Week 22
is the revision and assessment week: the formative quiz checks
narrative technique and point-of-view effects, and the final
session is the unit assessment plus a portfolio note.

| Wk | Session | Activity | Success criterion |

## Differentiation, supports, and extensions

- **Reading access:** every lesson prints its passages in full;
  the adult reads any passage aloud on request, and the learner
  may answer orally, by dictation, or in writing. The mentor
  stories are available as free audiobook recordings (see the
  Resource Pack).
- **Response modes:** labeling, sorting, and charting count as full
  answers wherever a paragraph is not the skill being tested;
  speech-to-text is always allowed for longer writing.
- **Scaffolds:** every lesson gives sentence starters ("This
  narrator is ___ because the narrator knows ___ but not ___")
  and a worked rewrite before the learner rewrites alone;
  Practice B gives a partially expanded summary for learners
  who need it.
- **Extensions:** Lesson 02's extension writes the station
  scene from the point of view of an object (the clock, the
  bench) and explains what that POV can and cannot know;
  Lesson 05's extension compares the structures of "The Gift
  of the Magi" and "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge" in a
  short paragraph (RL.8.5); the project extension adds a
  foreshadowing detail that pays off in the final scene.
- **Reteaching signals:** if POV labels are still swapped after
  Lesson 01, return to its "who is speaking, and what can they
  know?" test before Lesson 02 (reliability builds on the same
  question); if pacing rewrites only add adjectives, return to
  Lesson 03's scene-vs.-summary test before Practice B; if
  dialogue only reports speech without propelling action, return
  to Lesson 04's "what changes because this was said?" question
  before Practice C.
