# Lesson 06 — Revision: Pacing Pass, Reflective Conclusion, and the U04 Toolkit

**Goal:** you will revise your own narrative the way an editor
revises: a pacing pass, a conclusion that reflects, and a
conventions pass with the grammar tools from Unit 04 — and you
will explain every change.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** your project draft; your
Unit 04 notes (verbals, voice, mood, punctuation).
**Readiness check:** read the first paragraph of your draft
aloud. Where did you stumble or rush? That stumble is your first
revision target — readers stumble where writers did.

## What is revision?

**Revision** is re-seeing the story, not fixing commas.
(W.8.5.) The conventions pass comes last; first come three
bigger passes, in this order:

1. **The pacing pass.** Read the draft and mark every passage
   **S** (scene) or **U** (summary). Then ask of each one: *does
   this moment change anything?* If a scene changes nothing,
   compress it into a summary. If a summary covers a choice, a
   truth, or a relationship shift, expand it into a scene.
   Cut one passage that only repeats what the reader already
   knows.
2. **The point-of-view pass.** Check every paragraph against
   the narrator you chose. A first-person narrator must not
   report another character's secret thoughts as fact. A
   limited narrator must not slip into another character's head
   for one sentence and slip back. Circle each slip and rewrite
   it inside the narrator's knowledge.
3. **The conclusion pass.** A reflective conclusion (W.8.3.e)
   follows from the events and reflects on them: the narrator
   (or a character) understands something now that they did not
   understand at the start. It is not a summary ("and then they
   went home") and not a moral ("and so I learned…"). Test it:
   *could this conclusion end a different story?* If yes,
   rewrite it until it belongs only to this story.

Only after those three passes comes the **conventions pass**
with the Unit 04 toolkit: read the draft once for verbals (is
every *-ing* word doing the job you meant?), once for voice
(active unless passive serves a purpose), once for mood
(conditional and subjunctive where the meaning is a wish or a
contrary-to-fact state), and once for punctuation (semicolons
only between independent clauses; colons only after a complete
thought). Fix each fault and write a one-phrase reason next to
it — the reason is the revision, not the correction alone.

## Worked example 1 — the pacing pass

> **Draft paragraph:** "Mara waited at the station. She waited
> a long time. The train was late. She was cold. She thought
> about the morning. She had argued with Jon. She had packed
> her suitcase. She had bought a ticket. Then Jon arrived."

Step 1 — mark it: this is all summary (U) — seven sentences of
telling, no scene. Step 2 — what matters here? The argument
with Jon (it explains the whole story) and Jon's arrival (it
resolves the wait). Step 3 — revise: compress the waiting into
one sentence, expand the argument into a short scene, and let
the arrival land as a scene:

> "Mara waited at the station until the lamps came on. That
> morning Jon had said, 'Leaving is a mistake,' and she had
> answered, 'Staying is worse.' She had packed anyway. Then
> she heard footsteps, and Jon was there, holding a small
> wrapped gift."

Step 4 — what changed? Two hours of waiting became one
sentence; the argument became dialogue; the arrival became an
action. The facts are the same; the speed now matches the
importance.

## Worked example 2 — the reflective conclusion

**Weak conclusion (summary + moral):**

> "Mara got on the train and went to her new home. She had
> learned that family is the most important thing."

Step 1: it summarizes (the reader knows she boarded) and
preaches (the moral could end any story). Step 2: what does
Mara understand *now*, specifically? That Jon's gift meant he
had understood her leaving all along — and that she had been
wrong about him.

**Revised conclusion (reflection):**

> "Mara unwrapped the gift on the train: a timetable, with her
> route already circled in pencil. Jon had known where she was
> going before she had told him. She had spent the whole day
> certain he did not understand — and the whole day, he had
> understood better than she did."

Step 1: it follows from the events (the gift, the circled
route). Step 2: it reflects (Mara's understanding of Jon
changes). Step 3: it belongs only to this story — no other
story ends with a circled timetable.

**Watch out — revising is not rewriting from scratch.** Keep
the sentences that work. Most drafts need three to six targeted
changes, not a new draft. If you find yourself rewriting every
sentence, stop: you are probably fixing style when the problem
is structure. Go back to the structure test (Lesson 05) first.

## Guided practice

Read this draft paragraph and do the three passes on paper
(write your marks and notes, not a full rewrite yet):

> "I waited at the station for hours and hours. I was very
> nervous and very cold. I thought about everything. Jon was
> probably not coming. The train was late. I had packed my
> suitcase that morning. I had said goodbye to my mother. Then
> the train came, and Jon was on it, and he was waving."

1. **Pacing pass:** mark each sentence S or U. Which moment
   deserves a scene, and which sentences should compress into
   one?
2. **Point-of-view pass:** the narrator is first person. Find
   one sentence that reports something the narrator could not
   know, or state that there is none.
3. **Conclusion pass:** this is the draft's ending. Does it
   reflect on the events, or only report them? Write one
   reflection sentence that could replace the last sentence.

*(Answers and reasoning: see the [answer key](../answer-key.md),
Lesson 06 guided practice.)*

## Independent practice

1. Take your project draft (or, if you have none, the paragraph
   in guided practice). Make **three** pacing changes: expand
   one summary into a scene of at least four sentences,
   compress one scene into a two-sentence summary, and cut one
   sentence that only repeats what the reader already knows.
   List each change with its before-and-after.
2. Run the point-of-view pass on your draft: list every
   paragraph's narrator knowledge in one phrase each ("Mara's
   thoughts only," "Mara and Jon's thoughts"). Rewrite any
   paragraph that breaks your chosen point of view.
3. Write a new reflective conclusion for your draft (two or
   three sentences). Test it with the question: *could this
   conclusion end a different story?* If yes, rewrite it until
   the answer is no.
4. Run the conventions pass on one paragraph of your draft:
   find and fix one verbal, one voice choice, and one
   punctuation fault (semicolon, colon, or conjunctive adverb).
   Write the reason for each fix in one phrase.

## Applied task — the editor's memo

Write a short memo (100–150 words) to a fellow writer about
your draft, as if you were their editor. The memo must name:

- one pacing change you made and why the moment deserved a
  scene (or a summary);
- one point-of-view slip you caught and how you fixed it;
- one conventions fix from the Unit 04 toolkit and its reason;
- what your reflective conclusion says that the draft's old
  ending did not say.

This memo is the model for the project craft note (see
[project.md](../project.md)).

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each: (1) What are the four
passes, in order, and why does the conventions pass come last?
(2) What is the test for a reflective conclusion?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if the pacing pass feels overwhelming, do it
  with the adult reading the draft aloud while you only listen
  and mark S or U — hearing the story reveals slow spots that
  reading silently hides.
- **Alternate response mode:** record the memo as a spoken
  note (or dictate it) instead of writing it; the adult checks
  that all four required points are named.
- **Extension:** take the revised conclusion from worked
  example 2 and write the *next* scene (the train journey, four
  or five sentences) in which Mara's new understanding changes
  how she acts — she writes Jon a note, or she keeps the
  timetable where she can see it. Show the changed
  understanding in action, not in a summary sentence.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "revision means fixing spelling." The
order of the passes is the lesson: structure and pacing first,
conventions last. If a learner spends the session on commas
while a scene is missing, redirect them to the pacing pass —
the conventions pass will still be there afterward, and it will
be faster on a well-shaped draft. Note guided practice item 2:
the paragraph has *no* point-of-view slip (a first-person
narrator reporting their own thoughts and guesses is fine —
"Jon was probably not coming" is a guess, not a fact about Jon's
mind). Do not let the learner "fix" a slip that is not there.
See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
