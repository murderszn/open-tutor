# Lesson 03 — Pacing: Scene, Summary, and the Speed of Time

**Goal:** you will control how fast a story moves — expanding
important moments into full scenes and compressing unimportant
time into summary — without changing the facts.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook; the pacing
diagram below; the station illustration.
**Readiness check:** which of these takes longer to *read* —
"Three years passed" or a three-page description of one minute?
Which takes longer in *story time*? (The sentence covers three
years of story time in four words; the three pages cover one
minute. Reading time and story time are different clocks.)

## What is pacing?

**Pacing** is how fast the story moves through time. A writer
controls it with two tools: **scene** and **summary**.

- A **scene** shows events as they happen, moment by moment —
  mostly dialogue and action, in something close to real time.
  A scene is slow to read and fast in feeling: the reader lives
  inside the moment.
- A **summary** tells events quickly, covering hours, days, or
  years in a sentence or a paragraph. A summary is fast to read
  and covers a lot of story time: the reader is told what
  happened, not shown it happening.

**Time compression** is squeezing a long stretch of story time
into a few words ("Three years passed"). **Time expansion** is
stretching a short moment across many words — a held breath, a
look, a decision — so the reader feels its weight. Writers
expand the moments that matter (a choice, a reunion, a loss)
and compress the time between them (travel, waiting, routine).
Pacing is a decision about *importance*, not about length: a
long scene of a dull errand is bad pacing, and a one-sentence
summary of the story's turning point is bad pacing too.

## Worked example 1 — the same wait, two speeds

**Summary version** (time compression — two hours in two
sentences):

> "Mara waited at the station for two hours. The train did not
> come, and the lamps came on one by one."

Step 1: Count the story time — two hours. Step 2: Count the
telling — two sentences. Step 3: What does the reader *not*
get? No dialogue, no detail, no moment to live in. That is
correct if the wait does not matter — but if the wait *is* the
story, this version wastes it.

**Scene version** (time expansion — ten minutes in a paragraph):

> "Mara checked the clock. Ten minutes to six. She moved her
> suitcase out of the wind and sat on the bench, where the wood
> was still warm from the afternoon sun. The station keeper
> lifted his lantern and looked down the track, then looked back
> at her, and said, 'It'll come.' Mara nodded, though she did
> not believe him. She counted the ties between the rails until
> the numbers ran out."

Step 1: Count the story time — about ten minutes. Step 2: Count
the telling — a full paragraph, with dialogue, a sensory detail
(the warm wood), and an action (counting ties). Step 3: What
does the reader get? The *feeling* of waiting. The writer chose
to expand this moment because the waiting — and whether Mara
believes the keeper — is the story.

**The test.** Ask: *does this moment change anything?* If a
choice is made, a truth is learned, or a relationship shifts —
expand it into a scene. If time only passes — compress it into
a summary.

## Worked example 2 — when a summary is the right choice

> "Jon practiced the route for three weeks. He walked it in the
> morning, walked it after school, and walked it once in the
> rain, until he could do it without thinking. On the fourth
> week, he ran it."

Step 1: Story time — three weeks. Step 2: Telling — three
sentences. Step 3: Why is summary right here? The practices are
all the same action, repeated. Showing each practice would bore
the reader and teach nothing new. The writer compresses the
repetition and saves the scene for the fourth week — the run
itself, where something can go wrong. **Rule of thumb:** repeat
an action once in summary; show it once in scene, at the moment
it matters most.

**Watch out — compression must not delete causes.** "Mara was
angry, so she left" compresses a whole argument into a clause.
If the reader needs to know *why* she was angry, the argument
deserves a scene. Compress time, never motive: the reader must
always be able to answer "why did this happen?" from what you
showed them.

## Guided practice

For each item, name the pacing move (scene, summary, time
compression, time expansion) and answer the question in one or
two sentences.

1. "The summer went by in a blur of long days and cut grass."
2. "She picked up the letter, read the first line, put it down,
   picked it up again, and read it a second time, slowly."
3. Rewrite item 1 as a scene of one summer afternoon (three or
   four sentences). Keep the facts: it is summer, the days are
   long, the grass is cut.
4. Rewrite item 2 as a summary (one sentence). Keep the facts:
   she read the letter twice.

## Independent practice

1. Expand this summary into a scene of four or five sentences:
   "Mara waited an hour for the bus in the rain." Add one line
   of dialogue, one sensory detail (sound, cold, wet), and one
   action that shows she is impatient — without writing the word
   *impatient*.
2. Compress this scene into a one-sentence summary. Keep the
   facts: "Jon woke at six. He dressed in the dark so he would
   not wake Mara. He ate bread standing up in the kitchen. He
   walked to the station in the cold and bought his ticket from
   the sleepy keeper. Then he waited."
3. Explain in two or three sentences why a writer would expand
   the moment a character makes a difficult decision and
   compress the journey to get there. Use the words *scene*,
   *summary*, and *matters*.
4. Find one scene and one summary in "The Gift of the Magi"
   (you read it in the last session). Quote or describe each in
   one sentence and say why O. Henry gave each moment its speed.

## Applied task — the wait, three ways

Use the station illustration from Lesson 01
([`../assets/station-platform-two-perspectives.png`](../assets/station-platform-two-perspectives.png)).
Write the traveler's wait **three ways**, keeping the same facts
(dusk, the clock, the bench, the lantern, the train not yet
there):

- **A — summary:** two sentences covering the whole two-hour
  wait.
- **B — scene:** a paragraph of at least six sentences covering
  ten minutes of the wait, with one line of dialogue and one
  sensory detail.
- **C — scene with a flashback:** take version B and interrupt
  it once — after the dialogue, insert a short flashback (two
  or three sentences) to the morning the traveler bought the
  ticket — then return to the platform.

Label each version. Under version C, write one sentence
explaining what the flashback adds that version B does not have.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each: (1) What is the
difference between a scene and a summary, in terms of story
time and telling time? (2) What is the test for deciding
whether a moment deserves a scene?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if expanding feels shaky, use the five-sense
  list: write one thing the character sees, one thing they
  hear, and one thing they feel (cold, weight, tiredness)
  before adding any dialogue. Three details make a scene; a
  list of adjectives does not.
- **Alternate response mode:** tell version B aloud to the
  adult as a spoken scene (with the dialogue in a different
  voice), then write down what you said; the adult checks that
  the facts match version A.
- **Extension:** take your version C flashback and move it —
  put it *before* the scene instead of inside it — and explain
  in two sentences how the change affects suspense (does the
  reader now know something the platform scene has not shown
  yet?).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "more words means better writing." The
lesson is the opposite: speed is a choice, and the right speed
depends on importance. Grade the *reason* for each pacing
choice, not the word count. Note independent practice item 2:
the correct summary keeps all five facts (woke at six, dressed
in the dark, ate standing, walked in the cold, bought the
ticket, waited) — a summary that drops the ticket or the wait
has changed the facts, not just the speed. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
