# Lesson 01 — Point of View: Who Is Telling This Story?

**Goal:** you will tell first-person, third-person limited, and
third-person omniscient narration apart — and explain what each
narrator can and cannot know.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook; the station
illustration in the applied task.
**Readiness check:** read this sentence aloud — "Mara stared at
the empty track and counted the minutes." Who is telling you
about Mara: Mara herself, or someone watching her? How do you
know? (Someone watching her: the sentence uses *she/her* and
names Mara from outside.)

## What is point of view?

**Point of view** is the position from which a story is told. It
decides two things: **whose eyes the reader sees through**, and
**how much the reader is allowed to know**. The **narrator** is
the voice telling the story. The narrator is not always the
author — the author chooses a narrator the way a director chooses
a camera position. Move the camera and the story changes, even
when the events stay the same.

There are three positions you will use all unit. Learn them by
the pronouns and by the knowledge test.

| Point of view | Pronouns | What the narrator knows | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First person** | I, me, my, we | Only what one character knows, feels, and sees — plus what that character guesses | Is the narrator a character in the story? Does the narrator say "I"? |
| **Third-person limited** | he, she, they | The thoughts and feelings of **one** character only, plus what anyone could observe | Does the narrator follow one character's mind and no one else's? |
| **Third-person omniscient** | he, she, they | The thoughts and feelings of **any** character — the narrator knows everything relevant | Does the narrator report two or more characters' private thoughts? |

**Second person** ("you") exists too — the narrator speaks
directly to the reader — but it is rare in fiction, and no mentor
story in this unit uses it. If you meet it, name it and move on.

**The knowledge test.** For any passage, ask two questions:
(1) *Whose thoughts am I hearing?* (2) *Whose thoughts am I
not hearing?* The answers name the point of view. A first-person
narrator can report only their own thoughts. A limited narrator
can report one character's thoughts. An omniscient narrator can
report anyone's — and often tells the reader things no character
knows yet.

## Worked example 1 — the same evening, three cameras

The same event: Mara waits for her brother Jon at the evening
train. Read each version and run the knowledge test.

> **A.** "I checked the clock for the third time. Jon was late
> again, and I was sure he had forgotten me — or worse, that he
> had decided not to come."

Step 1: Pronouns — *I*. Step 2: Whose thoughts? Only the
speaker's (Mara's): her worry, her guess about Jon. Step 3: What
can't the narrator know? Whether Jon actually forgot, or decided
not to come — she is guessing. This is **first person**.

> **B.** "Mara checked the clock for the third time. Jon was late
> again, and she was sure he had forgotten her — or worse, that
> he had decided not to come."

Step 1: Pronouns — *she/her*, and Mara is named from outside.
Step 2: Whose thoughts? Only Mara's: her worry, her guess.
Step 3: What can't the narrator know? Jon's thoughts — the
narrator never reports what Jon is thinking. This is
**third-person limited** (limited to Mara).

> **C.** "Mara checked the clock for the third time, sure Jon
> had forgotten her. Across town, Jon was running for the bus,
> his ticket clutched in one hand, furious at himself for losing
> track of the time — he had not forgotten her at all."

Step 1: Pronouns — *she*, *he*. Step 2: Whose thoughts? Mara's
*and* Jon's: her worry and his fury at himself. Step 3: The
narrator reports private thoughts from two characters, plus a
fact neither character knows (that Jon is across town running).
This is **third-person omniscient**.

Notice: version B and version C look alike at first — both use
*he/she*. Only the knowledge test separates them. Count the
minds, not the pronouns.

## Worked example 2 — spotting the limit in a real sentence

> "He was nervous; she was furious; neither knew what the other
> was thinking."

Step 1: Two characters' feelings are named — his nervousness,
her fury. Step 2: The sentence also reports something neither
character knows: that the other is hiding their feeling. A
limited narrator following one character could not report the
other character's private feeling as a fact. Step 3: This is
**third-person omniscient**. A limited version would stay inside
one head: "He was nervous, and he could not tell whether she was
furious or only tired." A first-person version would be: "I was
nervous, and I could not tell whether she was furious or only
tired."

**Watch out — the narrator is not the author.** When O. Henry
writes "One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all," the
voice telling you about Della is a narrator O. Henry chose — a
third-person narrator who knows Della's thoughts and Jim's
thoughts. Do not write "O. Henry thinks…"; write "the narrator
tells us…" or "the narrator knows…". Lesson 02 returns to this:
a narrator can be wrong, and the author can know it.

## Guided practice

Label each passage **first person (1P)**, **third-person limited
(3L)**, or **third-person omniscient (3O)**. For each, name whose
thoughts you hear and one thing the narrator cannot know — or,
for omniscient, one thing the narrator knows that no character
knows.

1. "I had never missed a train in my life, and I was not about
   to start tonight."
2. "Jon stared at the timetable. He had never missed a train in
   his life, and he was not about to start tonight."
3. "Jon stared at the timetable, determined not to miss his
   train. Mara, watching from the bench, was sure he would miss
   it — she had seen him miss three buses that month."
4. "We waited on the platform until the lamps came on."

## Independent practice

Label each passage and give the knowledge-test reason in one
sentence.

1. "Mara counted the coins in her pocket. Eighty-seven cents.
   It would have to be enough."
2. "I counted the coins in my pocket. Eighty-seven cents. It
   would have to be enough."
3. "Mara counted the coins in her pocket, hoping eighty-seven
   cents would be enough. Behind the counter, the shopkeeper
   was already wrapping the gift she wanted, certain she could
   not afford it."
4. "The rain came down hard, and we ran for the shelter of the
   station roof."
5. "Jon did not know that Mara had already bought his present.
   He only knew that she was smiling in a way that worried him."
6. "She smiled in a way that worried him, but he could not say
   why."

Then rewrite passage 1 in first person and passage 2 in
third-person omniscient (two or three sentences each). Keep the
facts the same; change only what the narrator can know.

**Caption:** AI-generated illustration for this unit. A traveler
waits on a station platform at dusk while the station keeper

view — it shows no real place, person, or event to cite.

**Text-only alternative:** On the left of the platform, a teenage
traveler in a red coat with a backpack stands looking down the
empty train tracks. In the middle, a wooden bench stands in front
of a tall post with a large round station clock; the clock face
has plain tick marks instead of numbers. On the right, an older
station keeper with gray hair, glasses, and a dark uniform and
cap holds a glowing lantern and watches the traveler. A station
building with warm lit windows stands behind the keeper. The sky
is deep blue evening with a few stars; lamps glow warm yellow.

Write the moment **twice**, three or four sentences each time:

- **Version A — first person, as the traveler:** what do you
  see, what are you thinking, what do you *not* know? (You do
  not know what the keeper is thinking, or whether the train is
  coming.)
- **Version B — third-person omniscient:** report the
  traveler's thoughts *and* the keeper's thoughts, plus one fact
  neither of them knows (for example, that the train is already
  late, or that the keeper has been waiting for this traveler
  all evening).

Label each version with its point of view. The two versions must
describe the same moment — the same platform, the same clock,
the same lantern — and differ only in what the narrator can
know.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each: (1) What is the knowledge
test, and what two questions does it ask? (2) A passage uses
*he* and *she* and reports only Mara's thoughts. Which point of
view is it, and why is it not omniscient?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if labeling feels shaky, circle every pronoun
  first, then underline every thought or feeling and write the
  name of the character it belongs to next to it. Count the
  names: one name (plus "I") means first person or limited;
  two or more names means omniscient.
- **Alternate response mode:** sort the guided-practice passages
  into three columns (1P / 3L / 3O) instead of writing labels;
  the adult checks the sort and asks for the knowledge test on
  two items.
- **Extension:** write the station moment a third time, from
  the point of view of the clock (or the bench) — first person,
  as the object. Then explain in two sentences what that
  narrator can know (the platform, the waiting, the time) and
  cannot know (anyone's thoughts, anything beyond the platform).

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "third person means omniscient." The
pronouns do not decide it — the *number of minds* does. Insist
on the knowledge test for every item, said aloud, until it is
automatic. Note item 5 of independent practice: "Jon did not
know that Mara had already bought his present" reports a fact
Jon does not know *and* Mara's secret — that is omniscient,
even though the sentence is about Jon. Item 6 stays inside one
head ("he could not say why"), so it is limited. See the
[teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
