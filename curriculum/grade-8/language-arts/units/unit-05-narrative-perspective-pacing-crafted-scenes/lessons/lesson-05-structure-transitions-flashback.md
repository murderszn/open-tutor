# Lesson 05 — Structure: Event Sequence, Transitions, Flashback, and Foreshadowing

**Goal:** you will organize events so they unfold naturally,
signal shifts in time and setting with transitions, and use
flashback and foreshadowing on purpose.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook.
**Readiness check:** put these events in the order they happened:
"Mara boarded the train. / Mara bought a ticket. / Mara packed
her suitcase." (Packed → bought → boarded. If a story gave them
in a different order, the writer chose that order for a reason.)

## What is structure?

**Structure** is the order in which a story gives the reader its
events. Most stories follow **chronology** — the order things
happened — but a writer can break chronology on purpose. The
question is never "is this in order?" but "does this order serve
the reader?" An **event sequence that unfolds naturally and
logically** (W.8.3.a) is one in which each event follows from
the one before it, so the reader can answer "why did this
happen?" at every step.

**Transitions** are the words, phrases, and clauses that signal
sequence and shifts in time or setting (W.8.3.c): *later*,
*the next morning*, *by then*, *meanwhile*, *years earlier*,
*while Mara waited*. A transition is a signpost: it tells the
reader "we are moving in time" or "we are moving to another
place." Without transitions, the reader trips over jumps they
cannot see. With too many, the story reads like a list. Use a
transition whenever the time, place, or speaker changes — and
only then.

## Flashback and foreshadowing

A **flashback** is a scene that interrupts the present action to
show something that happened earlier. It answers a question the
present has raised: *why is Mara afraid of trains?* — cut to
the morning she missed one. A flashback works when the past
explains the present; it fails when it only repeats what the
reader already knows.

**Foreshadowing** is a hint early in a story about what will
happen later — a detail that seems small on first reading and
significant on second reading. A ticking clock, a ticket bought
in a hurry, a keeper who looks twice at a suitcase: each plants
a question the ending will answer. Foreshadowing is a promise,
not a spoiler: it should make the ending feel *earned*, not
*given away*.

**The structure test.** After drafting, cover the story and
retell it in three sentences: what happened first, what changed,
what happened last. If the retelling is confused, the structure
is confused — no matter how good the sentences are.

## Worked example 1 — ordering for effect

These six events, in chronology:

1. Mara packs her suitcase in the morning.
2. Mara buys a ticket at noon.
3. Mara argues with Jon about leaving.
4. Mara waits on the platform at dusk.
5. Jon arrives with a gift.
6. Mara boards the train.

**Chronological version** (events 1–6 in order) is clear and
steady: the reader watches the day build toward the departure.
**Reordered version** — start at event 4 (the wait), flash back
to event 3 (the argument), then return to the platform for
events 5–6:

> "Mara checked the clock. Ten minutes to six, and Jon was
> not there. That morning they had argued — he had called her
> leaving a mistake, and she had called him afraid. She had
> packed anyway, bought her ticket at noon, and come to the
> platform alone. Now the lamps were coming on, and she was
> still deciding whether he would come. Then she heard
> footsteps, and Jon was there, holding a small wrapped gift."

Step 1: What does the reordered version gain? The wait becomes
the story's question (*will he come?*), and the argument
becomes the explanation the reader needs. Step 2: What does it
risk? Confusion — so the writer marks the flashback clearly
("That morning they had argued") and returns to the present
clearly ("Now the lamps were coming on"). Step 3: The rule —
**start with the moment that asks the story's question**, and
use flashback to answer the questions the opening raises.

## Worked example 2 — a transition doing two jobs

> "By the time the clock struck six, the platform was empty
> except for Mara. Meanwhile, across town, Jon was running for
> the bus."

Step 1: "By the time the clock struck six" signals a time shift
(we have moved forward to six o'clock) and a summary (the
waiting hours are compressed). Step 2: "Meanwhile, across town"
signals a place shift and a second point of view (we leave Mara
and follow Jon). Step 3: One sentence moves time, place, and
character — and sets up the dramatic irony the reader will
carry into the next scene (Mara thinks Jon is not coming; the
reader knows he is running). A good transition is invisible:
the reader feels the move without stopping to admire it.

**Watch out — the flashback trap.** A flashback that starts
with "Mara remembered when…" and then tells a whole childhood
is usually a summary wearing a flashback's clothes. A true
flashback is a *scene* from the past — with dialogue, action,
and sensory detail — not a paragraph of explanation. If your
flashback has no dialogue and no action, it is probably a
summary; either shorten it to a sentence or expand it into a
real scene.

## Guided practice

1. Put these events in chronological order: "Jon gives Mara
   the gift. / Mara argues with Jon. / Mara boards the train. /
   Jon buys the gift."
2. Write a transition that moves the reader from the platform
   at dusk to Mara's kitchen that morning. Use a time word and
   a place word.
3. Write a one-sentence foreshadowing detail for this story:
   Mara is about to board a train and never return. The detail
   must not say she will never return — it must hint at it.
4. A story opens with Mara already on the train, then flashes
   back to the argument with Jon. What question does the opening
   raise that the flashback answers? Answer in one sentence.

## Independent practice

1. Order these six events chronologically, then choose where
   you would start the story (which event first) and explain
   your choice in two sentences: (a) Mara finds an old ticket
   in a drawer. (b) Mara and Jon argue. (c) Mara packs. (d) Mara
   buys a new ticket. (e) Mara waits at the station. (f) The
   train arrives.
2. Write three transitions, one for each shift: (a) from night
   to the next morning; (b) from the station to the market
   across town; (c) from the present into a flashback of last
   winter. Each transition must be a single clause or sentence.
3. Write a flashback scene of four or five sentences: Mara
   remembers the last time she saw her grandmother at this
   station. The flashback must contain one line of dialogue and
   one sensory detail, and it must end with a sentence that
   returns the reader to the present platform.
4. Explain in two or three sentences how foreshadowing and
   dramatic irony are related and how they differ. Use the
   words *reader*, *knows*, and *hint* in your answer.

## Applied task — rebuild the evening

Take the six events from worked example 1 (pack, buy ticket,
argue, wait, gift, board). Write the evening **twice**:

- **Version A — chronological:** events 1–6 in order, in a
  paragraph of six to eight sentences, with one transition
  between events.
- **Version B — reordered:** start at event 4 (the wait),
  flash back to event 3 (the argument), then finish with
  events 5–6. Use one clear transition into the flashback and
  one clear transition back to the present.

Under version B, write two sentences explaining what the
reordering gains (what question does the opening raise?) and
what it risks (what could confuse the reader, and how did you
prevent it?).

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each: (1) What is the structure
test, and what three things does the retelling name? (2) What
is the difference between a flashback and a summary of the past?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if ordering feels shaky, write each event on its
  own slip of paper (or line), then physically reorder the slips
  until the story's question comes first. Number the slips when
  you are satisfied, then write from the numbered order.
- **Alternate response mode:** draw the story as a timeline with
  boxes for each event and arrows for flashbacks; the adult
  checks that every arrow is labeled with a transition word.
- **Extension:** compare the structures of "The Gift of the
  Magi" and "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge" in a short
  paragraph (RL.8.5): how does each story order its events, what
  does each order let the reader know and when, and which
  structure creates more suspense? Quote one detail from each
  story to support your comparison.
