# Lesson 02 — Narrator Reliability and Dramatic Irony

**Goal:** you will test whether a narrator can be trusted — and
explain how dramatic irony works when the reader knows more
than a character does.
**Time:** ~45 minutes. **Materials:** notebook; "The Gift of the
Magi" (for the reading session, not this lesson).
**Readiness check:** a narrator says, "I am the best player on
the team, and everyone knows it." What would make you doubt
that claim? (Evidence against it — for example, the narrator
sitting on the bench, or teammates laughing.)

## What is a reliable narrator?

A **reliable narrator** is one whose account the reader can
trust: their facts check out against the details in the story,
their judgments are fair, and they do not hide what they know.
An **unreliable narrator** is one whose account the reader has
reason to doubt. Unreliability is not lying on purpose — a
narrator can be unreliable because they are mistaken, because
they only saw part of what happened, because they want something
to be true, or because they are telling the story long after and
misremember.

**The reliability test.** Do not ask "is this narrator nice?"
Ask three questions and answer each with a detail from the text:

1. **Do the narrator's facts match the story's details?** If the
   narrator says "the room was empty" and the next sentence
   describes a dog sleeping in the corner, the narrator is wrong
   about a fact.
2. **Does the narrator report what they could not know?** A
   first-person narrator who reports another character's secret
   thoughts as fact is guessing or inventing — unless the other
   character told them.
3. **Does the narrator's judgment match the evidence?** If the
   narrator calls someone "my best friend" and then describes
   that person stealing from them twice, the judgment and the
   evidence do not match. Trust the evidence.

A narrator can be **partly reliable**: right about facts, wrong
about what they mean. That is the most common kind, and the most
interesting to write. When you judge a narrator, quote the
detail that proves your judgment — never judge on a feeling
alone (RL.8.1).

## What is dramatic irony?

**Dramatic irony** is the gap between what the reader (or
audience) knows and what a character knows. It is the effect
RL.8.6 names: differences in the points of view of the
characters and the reader create suspense or humor. The recipe
has three parts:

1. **The reader learns a fact.** (The narrator tells us, or
   another scene shows us.)
2. **A character does not know that fact.** (They act on a
   false belief.)
3. **The reader watches the character act** — and the tension
   or the humor comes from knowing what the character does not.

Dramatic irony needs a narrator who knows more than at least one
character. That is why it pairs with third-person narration so
often — and why a first-person narrator can create it only by
telling the reader something the *other* characters do not know,
or by admitting, "I did not know then what I know now."

## Worked example 1 — testing a narrator

> "I was the fastest runner in our school — everyone said so.
> On the morning of the race I felt wonderful. I took my time
> tying my shoes, because I knew I would win easily. When the
> whistle blew, I jogged off the line, smiling. By the halfway
> mark, three runners were ahead of me, and my legs felt like
> wet sand."

Step 1 — facts vs. details: the narrator claims to be the
fastest, but the story's own details show three runners ahead
and legs "like wet sand." The claim does not match the details.
Step 2 — could the narrator know it? The narrator reports only
their own feelings, so there is no impossible knowledge — but
Step 3 — judgment vs. evidence: "I would win easily" is a
judgment the evidence (three runners ahead) contradicts. This
narrator is **unreliable about their own ability** — not a liar,
but mistaken and overconfident. The reader's job is to trust the
details (three runners ahead, heavy legs) over the narrator's
claim (fastest in the school).

## Worked example 2 — building dramatic irony

> "Mara wrapped the scarf and hid it in her bag. It was Jon's
> birthday present, and she had spent her last coins on the
> wool. Jon, meanwhile, was at the market spending his last
> coins on a book of train timetables — for Mara, who loved
> trains and had none. Neither of them knew what the other had
> bought."

Step 1 — what does the reader know? Both purchases: the scarf
and the timetables. Step 2 — what does each character not know?
Mara does not know about the timetables; Jon does not know about
the scarf. Step 3 — the effect: the reader watches two people
spend their last coins on each other and feels warmth (and a
little worry — what if the gifts do not fit?). That is dramatic
irony: the reader holds both halves of the secret, and neither
character holds both. (This is the shape of O. Henry's "The
Gift of the Magi," which you will read in the next session:
Della sells her hair for a chain; Jim sells his watch for
combs. The reader knows both sacrifices before either character
does.)

**Watch out — irony is not the same as a surprise ending.** A
surprise ending hides a fact from the *reader* until the last
line. Dramatic irony gives the fact to the reader *early* and
lets the reader watch a character act without it. If you are not
sure which one you have, ask: "Who knew, and when?" If the
reader knew first, it is dramatic irony.

## Guided practice

For each item, name the narrator's reliability (reliable,
unreliable, or partly reliable) and quote the detail that proves
it; for item 4, also name what the reader knows that the
character does not.

1. "I have always been honest with my friends. Last Tuesday I
   told Mara I had finished the homework, though I had only
   done half of it."
2. "Jon counted the money twice. There was not enough for the
   train ticket. He told Mara the ticket was already bought."
3. "The house was silent. I listened for a full minute and
   heard nothing — no voices, no footsteps, no kettle. Then I
   heard the back door open, and I knew someone was inside."
4. "Mrs. Alvarez set the table for two, humming, and put the
   good plates out. The reader already knows her son's letter —
   he is not coming home tonight."

## Independent practice

1. Write a four-sentence first-person paragraph in which the
   narrator claims to be calm — but the details show they are
   frightened. (Give at least two details that contradict the
   claim: shaking hands, a racing heart, checking the door.)
2. Write a four-sentence paragraph in which a third-person
   limited narrator follows a character who is *wrong* about
   another character's feelings. Show the wrong belief and one
   detail the reader can use to see it is wrong.
3. Explain in two or three sentences why dramatic irony needs a
   narrator who knows more than at least one character. Use the
   words *reader*, *character*, and *knows* in your answer.
4. Read this sentence and answer: "Della finished her cry and
   attended to her cheeks with the powder rag." — from "The
   Gift of the Magi." Whose feelings does the narrator report,
   and does the narrator know more than Della does in this
   sentence? Explain in one sentence. (You will read the whole
   story next session; for now, judge only this sentence.)

## Applied task — the keeper's version

Return to the station illustration from Lesson 01
([`../assets/station-platform-two-perspectives.png`](../assets/station-platform-two-perspectives.png)).
Write two short paragraphs:

- **Paragraph 1 — the keeper, first person, unreliable:** the
  keeper insists the train is "never late" — but include two
  details that prove the train *is* late (the clock, the empty
  track, the cold lantern light, the traveler's waiting). The
  reader must be able to catch the keeper being wrong.
- **Paragraph 2 — dramatic irony, third person:** write three
  sentences in which the reader learns that the traveler is
  leaving home for good, while the keeper believes the traveler
  is only visiting for the weekend. Name, in a final sentence,
  what the keeper does not know.

## Exit check

Answer in one or two sentences each: (1) What are the three
questions of the reliability test? (2) What three parts make
dramatic irony, and which one must come first?

## Supports and extension

- **Support:** if judging reliability feels shaky, make a
  two-column chart — "narrator claims" on the left, "story
  details" on the right — and draw a line between any claim and
  the detail that matches or contradicts it. A claim with no
  matching detail is unproven; a claim with a contradicting
  detail is unreliable.
- **Alternate response mode:** act out item 4 of guided
  practice with the adult (one reads the narrator, one reads the
  character's lines) and stop after each sentence to say who
  knows what.
- **Extension:** rewrite worked example 1 so the same narrator
  is *reliable*: keep the race, keep the loss, but change the
  narrator's claims so they match the details ("I was fast, but
  not the fastest — and today my legs gave out"). Then explain
  in two sentences what changed and why the new version is
  trustworthy.

## Teacher note

Likely misconception: "unreliable means the narrator is lying."
Most unreliable narrators in grade-8 reading are mistaken,
limited, or self-deceived — not liars. Grade the evidence, not
the accusation: full credit requires a quoted detail, not just
the label "unreliable." Note guided practice item 3: the
narrator hears the door open after claiming silence — the
narrator is reliable (they report what they heard honestly and
correct the earlier impression), which is the opposite of the
trap answer. See the [teacher guide](../teacher-guide.md) and
[answer key](../answer-key.md).
