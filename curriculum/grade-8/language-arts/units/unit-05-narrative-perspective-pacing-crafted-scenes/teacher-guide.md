# Teacher Guide — Unit 05 (Adult / Educator Only)

This guide supports the adult guiding a single grade-8 learner.
It contains teaching notes and scoring guidance. Full solutions
are in the [answer key](answer-key.md), not here, so this file
can be read alongside the learner's work without giving answers
away in the lesson flow.

## Unit at a glance

- **Question:** how do a narrator's point of view, the pacing
  of events, and the craft of individual scenes change what a
  reader knows, feels, and believes about a story?
- **Core move:** the same moment told two ways. Every lesson
  returns to one habit — *change the narrator or the speed and
  the story changes* — and the project proves it: the same
  opening scene from two points of view. Grade the reason for
  each craft choice, not just the correction or the word count.
- **Mentor stories:** O. Henry's "The Gift of the Magi" (Week
  19), Bierce's "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge" (Week 20),
  and Chopin's "The Story of an Hour" (Week 21) — all public
  domain, all linked in the [Resource Pack](resource-pack.md).
  Preview Bierce and Chopin first (see the unit README's adult
  preview note); either can be swapped for another public-domain
  story teaching the same skill without breaking the sequence.

## Session-by-session notes

**Sessions 1–5 (Week 19, point of view and irony).** The
knowledge test is the unit's most important habit; insist the
learner says it aloud for the first dozen items. The "third
person means omniscient" error will recur all unit — name it
every time it appears, even in later weeks. Session 4's Magi
reading is the first mentor text: accept any POV answer that
names third-person omniscient with two details (one from
Della's mind, one from Jim's mind or a fact neither knows).
Session 5's discussion deliberately connects to Unit 02's
dramatic irony — the Magi irony and Shakespeare's irony share
the reader-knows-more shape, but the Magi irony is symmetric
(each character hides a sacrifice from the other) while stage
irony is usually one-sided.

**Sessions 6–10 (Week 20, pacing and scene craft).** Expect the
"more words means better writing" rule from prior schooling. Do
not fight it with a counter-rule; fight it with the importance
question: "does this moment change anything?" Session 9's
Bierce reading is the hardest text in the unit (Lexile ~1000,
a hanging, an imagined escape) — read it together, and let the
learner map the time handling on paper before discussing the
ending. If the ending confuses them, re-read the last paragraph
and the opening paragraph side by side: the story never leaves
the bridge.

**Sessions 11–15 (Week 21, structure and project).** The
structure test (retell in three sentences) is the fastest
diagnostic in the unit — run it on every draft. Session 14's
perspective-shift scene is where point-of-view slips show up
most: watch for a second-character version that changes the
*facts* instead of only the knowledge. Session 15's Chopin
reading is short (about one hour of story time) — use it to
show that a whole story can be one expanded hour.

**Sessions 16–20 (Week 22, revision and assessment).** Session
16's four passes have a strict order: pacing, point of view,
conclusion, then conventions. Do not let the learner spend the
session on commas while a scene is missing. Session 18's quiz
is formative: score, review the same day, reteach in Session
19. Session 20's portfolio note asks the learner to name one
strength and one next step — record the next step privately; it
becomes the U06 entry note.

## Misconceptions to expect

| Misconception | Correction |
|---|---|
| Third person means omniscient | The knowledge test: count the minds, not the pronouns |
| Unreliable means the narrator is lying | Most unreliable narrators are mistaken, limited, or self-deceived; grade the quoted evidence |
| More words means better writing | Speed is a choice; expand what matters, compress the rest |
| Good description means many adjectives | One precise sensory detail beats five vague ones |
| A flashback is telling the past | A flashback is a scene from the past, with dialogue and action — otherwise it is a summary |
| A reflective conclusion is a moral | Reflection is specific to this character and this moment; a moral could end any story |
| Revision means fixing spelling | Structure and pacing first, conventions last |

## Differentiation and reteaching

- **Reading access:** read any passage aloud on request;
  labeling, sorting, and charting are full answers where a
  paragraph is not the skill tested. The mentor stories have
  free audiobook alternatives (see the Resource Pack).
- **If POV labels are still swapped after Lesson 01:** return
  to the "who is speaking, and what can they know?" test with
  pronoun cards before Lesson 02.
- **If pacing rewrites only add adjectives:** return to Lesson
  03's scene-vs.-summary test with the pacing diagram before
  Practice B.
- **If dialogue only reports speech:** return to Lesson 04's
  "what changes because this was said?" question before
  Practice C.
- **If flashbacks are summaries:** return to Lesson 05's
  watch-out box and expand one flashback into a scene with
  dialogue before the project.
- **Extensions:** Lesson 01's object-POV writing and Lesson
  05's two-story structure comparison are the unit's extension
  tasks; both are optional and ungraded, or graded only on the
  explanation sentences.

## Scoring guidance

- **Formative quiz (14 points):** score, review the same day,
  and reteach in Session 19. Do not average it into the unit
  assessment; its purpose is diagnosis.
- **Unit assessment (24 points):** 21–24 exceeds; 17–20 meets;
  12–16 approaching (reteach the missed skill, then revise);
  below 12 reteach with Lessons 01–06 and use a parallel form
  (build it by changing the assessment's names, settings, and
  sentence topics while keeping the same skills — do not reuse
  the same sentences for a retake).
- **Project:** score with the rubric in
  [project.md](project.md). Part 3's craft note earns full
  credit only when each explanation names its choice, quotes
  the narrative's words, and states an effect on the reader.
- **What "meets" looks like:** the learner can label POV by the
  knowledge test, test a narrator's reliability with quoted
  details, control scene/summary pacing on purpose, write
  dialogue that propels action, use a flashback or foreshadowing
  with clear transitions, and revise a narrative to a reflective
  conclusion. Occasional slips on borderline cases (such as a
  narrator who is partly reliable) do not block "meets" when the
  reasoning is stated.
