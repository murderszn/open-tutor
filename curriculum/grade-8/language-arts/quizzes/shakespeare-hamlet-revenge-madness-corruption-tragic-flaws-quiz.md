# Grade 8 Language Arts — Shakespeare: Hamlet — Revenge, Madness, Corruption & Tragic Flaws Quiz

**Topic:** Shakespeare: Hamlet — Revenge, Madness, Corruption & Tragic Flaws  

This quiz assesses the broader dramatic architecture of *The Tragedy of Hamlet*, analyzing the motif of disease/corruption, real vs. feigned madness, and the ethics of revenge.

## 📋 Assessment Questions

**1.** What famous line uttered by Marcellus in Act I, Scene 4 establishes the motif of national moral decay in Denmark?

**2.** What is an **antic disposition**, and why does Hamlet decide to adopt one?

**3.** Analyze the contrast between **Hamlet's feigned madness** and **Ophelia's genuine madness** in Act IV.

**4.** How does Shakespeare use the motif of physical poison (from Old Hamlet's ear to the poisoned chalice and foil in Act V) as a symbol for moral corruption?

**5.** In Act III, Scene 3, Hamlet discovers Claudius kneeling alone in prayer, completely vulnerable. Why does Hamlet hesitate and refuse to kill him at that moment?

**6.** How does **Laertes** act as a character foil to Hamlet in his reaction to his father's (Polonius's) murder?

**7.** In Act V, Scene 1, Hamlet picks up the skull of **Yorick** in the graveyard. What universal truth about human mortality does this graveyard meditation express?

**8.** What is the theatrical function of the **play-within-a-play** (*The Mousetrap* / *The Murder of Gonzago*) in Act III, Scene 2?

**9.** Explain Hamlet's philosophical shift in Act V when he tells Horatio: *"There's a special providence in the fall of a sparrow. If it be now, 'tis not to come... The readiness is all."*

**10.** At the tragedy's climax, who survives to tell Hamlet's story, and what foreign prince arrives to restore legitimate political order to Denmark?

### Model Answers & Rubric (10 Points Total: 1 point per question)

**Question 1:**  
*"Something is rotten in the state of Denmark."*

**Question 2:**  
An 'antic disposition' is a mask of eccentric, erratic, or bizarre behavior (pretended madness) that Hamlet adopts to observe the royal court and conceal his knowledge of Claudius's crime.

**Question 3:**  
Hamlet's madness is calculated, theatrical, and laced with double-edged wit; Ophelia's madness is tragic, genuine insanity triggered by the grief of her father's murder and Hamlet's abandonment, culminating in her drowning.

**Question 4:**  
Just as a few drops of poison poured into King Hamlet's ear curdled his blood, Claudius's poisonous act of fratricide corrupts the entire Danish court, culminating in an ending where everyone is literally poisoned.

**Question 5:**  
Claudius appears to be praying for forgiveness; Hamlet fears that killing him mid-prayer will send Claudius's soul straight to heaven, which would be a reward rather than the damnable revenge he desires.

**Question 6:**  
Laertes demands immediate, reckless bloody vengeance without caring about morality, law, or his soul's salvation ('To hell, allegiance!'), whereas Hamlet deliberates, questions evidence, and delays.

**Question 7:**  
The skull of Yorick, the former court jester who once carried young Hamlet on his back, forces Hamlet to confront the reality that all human beings—great kings like Alexander or Caesar, and humble jesters alike—eventually rot into dust.

**Question 8:**  
To catch the conscience of King Claudius; Hamlet observes Claudius's physical guilt reaction when the actors reenact the murder to confirm the ghost's testimony before taking action.

**Question 9:**  
Hamlet abandons his anxious overthinking and accepts that human lives are guided by divine providence and destiny; he surrenders control and embraces readiness for whatever comes.

**Question 10:**  
Horatio survives to report Hamlet and his cause truly to the unsatisfied; Prince Fortinbras of Norway arrives to claim the Danish crown and restore political stability.
