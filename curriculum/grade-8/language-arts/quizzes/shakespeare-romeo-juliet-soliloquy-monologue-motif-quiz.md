# Grade 8 Language Arts — Shakespeare: Romeo & Juliet — Soliloquy, Monologue & Motif Quiz

**Topic:** Shakespeare: Romeo & Juliet — Soliloquy, Monologue & Motif  

This quiz explores dramatic speech formats, motifs of light and dark, and tragic flaws (*hamartia*) in *Romeo and Juliet*.

## 📋 Assessment Questions

**1.** What is the technical difference between a **soliloquy** and a **monologue** in drama?

**2.** What is an **aside**?

**3.** In Act II, Scene 2, Romeo observes: *"But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? It is the east, and Juliet is the sun."* What extended metaphor is being developed here?

**4.** How does Shakespeare invert traditional associations of **light and darkness** throughout *Romeo and Juliet*?

**5.** Give one example where daylight in Verona represents danger and violence, while darkness represents sanctuary and safety.

**6.** What is the definition of **oxymoron**? Identify two oxymorons from Romeo's speeches in Act I (*"O brawling love, O loving hate..."*).

**7.** Why does Friar Laurence caution Romeo: *"These violent delights have violent ends, and in their triumph die, like fire and powder, which as they kiss consume"*?

**8.** What is **hamartia** (tragic flaw)? What is Romeo's primary tragic flaw?

**9.** Explain the role of **apothecary scene** in Mantua: Why does Romeo target an impoverished apothecary to buy illegal poison?

**10.** What final resolution do Lord Capulet and Lord Montague arrive at upon seeing their children's bodies in the Capulet tomb?

### Model Answers & Rubric (10 Points Total: 1 point per question)

**Question 1:**  
A soliloquy is delivered by a character alone on stage to voice private internal thoughts directly to the audience. A monologue is a long, uninterrupted speech delivered by one character to other characters present on stage.

**Question 2:**  
An aside is a brief comment delivered by an actor directly to the audience (or to another specific character) that the other characters on stage are presumed not to hear.

**Question 3:**  
An extended metaphor comparing Juliet to the radiant celestial sun, whose warmth and brilliance banish the envious moon and illuminate Romeo's dark world.

**Question 4:**  
Traditionally, light symbolizes goodness and safety, while darkness represents evil. In *Romeo and Juliet*, darkness is the romantic sanctuary where the lovers can unite in secret, whereas harsh daylight brings street brawls, heat, exposure, and death.

**Question 5:**  
In Act III, Scene 1, the hot afternoon sun fuels the Capulet-Montague brawl where Mercutio and Tybalt are slain; at dawn in Act III, Scene 5, the arrival of morning light forces Romeo to flee Juliet's bedroom into exile.

**Question 6:**  
An oxymoron joins two contradictory, opposing terms to reveal a complex truth. Examples: *'brawling love'*, *'loving hate'*, *'heavy lightness'*, *'feather of lead'*, *'bright smoke'*.

**Question 7:**  
Friar Laurence warns that excessive, rushed passion burns out catastrophically fast, just like gunpowder exploding upon contact with fire; he counsels moderation.

**Question 8:**  
Hamartia is the fatal character flaw or error in judgment that leads to a tragic hero's downfall. Romeo's flaw is reckless emotional impulsivity and impatience—acting on extreme feeling without pause for rational reflection.

**Question 9:**  
Romeo knows selling lethal poison carries the death penalty in Mantua, but he exploits the apothecary's desperate poverty and starvation to purchase the poison.

**Question 10:**  
The grieving fathers finally end their ancient feud, clasp hands in peace, and pledge to construct pure gold statues of Romeo and Juliet in Verona to commemorate their sacrifice.
